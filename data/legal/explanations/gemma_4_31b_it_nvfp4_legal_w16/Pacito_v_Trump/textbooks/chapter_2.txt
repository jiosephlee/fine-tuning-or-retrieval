Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 2: # The Legal Standard for Stays Pending Appeal

When a district court issues a preliminary injunction—such as the orders in *Pacito v. Trump* that prohibited the government from suspending refugee admissions and defunding resettlement services—the government often asks an appellate court to "stay" that injunction. A stay pending appeal effectively pauses the lower court's order, allowing the status quo (or the challenged government policy) to remain in place until the appellate court reaches a final decision on the merits. In *Pacito*, the Ninth Circuit had to determine whether the district court's injunctions should be stayed while the court reviewed the legality of the President's executive orders.

## The Nken v. Holder Four-Factor Test

To determine whether to grant a stay, the Ninth Circuit applies a well-established four-factor framework derived from the Supreme Court case *Nken v. Holder*. The court considers: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing that they are likely to succeed on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured without a stay; (3) whether the stay will substantially injure other parties interested in the proceeding; and (4) where the public interest lies. While all four factors are relevant, the court emphasizes that the first two—likelihood of success on the merits and the threat of irreparable injury—are the "most critical" components of the analysis.

## Likelihood of Success on the Merits

The "likelihood of success on the merits" factor is a predictive analysis: the court asks whether the party seeking the stay is likely to win the case once a full decision is issued. In *Pacito*, the court found that the government's likelihood of success varied depending on which part of the challenged policy was being analyzed.

Regarding the suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) under Executive Order 14163, the court found the government was likely to prevail. The court relied on 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which grants the President broad authority to suspend the entry of any class of aliens deemed detrimental to the interests of the United States. Citing *Trump v. Hawaii*, the court noted that this statutory authority "exudes deference" to the President. Because the President had made a finding that the entry of refugees was detrimental, the court concluded the government had fulfilled the sole prerequisite of the statute.

However, the court found the government was *not* likely to prevail on the challenge regarding the defunding of resettlement services. The court pointed to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which specifies that the government "shall" provide certain reception and placement services (such as English language training and employment resources) to refugees admitted to the United States. Because this statutory language appeared mandatory, the court reasoned that the government could not unilaterally terminate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide these services.

## Irreparable Injury to the Applicant

The second critical factor is whether the applicant (in this case, the government) will suffer "irreparable injury" if the stay is denied. Irreparable injury refers to harm that cannot be undone or adequately compensated by money damages after the case is eventually decided. In *Pacito*, the court noted that the remaining factors—including irreparable injury—favored the United States. When the government is the applicant, this often involves the argument that the inability to enforce a national security or immigration policy causes immediate and lasting harm to the country's interests.

## Substantial Injury to Other Interested Parties

The third factor requires the court to weigh the harm to the applicant against the harm to other parties, such as the refugee plaintiffs and the organizations providing resettlement services. If a stay is granted, the plaintiffs are "injured" because the lower court's protection (the injunction) is removed. In *Pacito*, the court balanced these interests by granting a partial stay: it stayed the injunction regarding the suspension of refugee entry (favoring the government) but refused to stay the injunction regarding resettlement services (protecting the refugees already in the country).

## The Role of the Public Interest in Immigration and National Security

The final factor is the "public interest." In cases involving immigration and national security, the public interest is often tied to the Executive Branch's ability to manage the nation's borders and foreign affairs. The Ninth Circuit noted that the President is conferred "broad discretion" over domestic and foreign interests.

This deference is central to the court's reasoning. By applying the *Nken* factors, the court determined that the public interest in upholding the President's broad authority to restrict entry under § 1182(f) outweighed the plaintiffs' interests. Conversely, the public interest in complying with the statutory mandates of the Refugee Act regarding those already admitted to the U.S. necessitated the reinstatement of resettlement services.
