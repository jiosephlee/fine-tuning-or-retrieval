Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# The "Update" Loophole: How Terse Declarations Sustain FOIA Withholdings Over Time

The tension between the Freedom of Information Act’s (FOIA) transparency mandate and the government’s need for investigative secrecy often comes down to a question of timing. Under Exemption 7(A), the government can withhold records if their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are "pending or reasonably anticipated." But what happens when a FOIA lawsuit drags on for years, and the "pending" proceeding becomes a decades-long ghost? The D.C. Circuit recently addressed this in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, and in doing so, it created a potentially convenient path for agencies to maintain long-term secrecy with minimal evidentiary effort.

The case involved Foad Farahi, an Iranian national whose FBI file was subject to a massive withholding based on Exemption 7(A). The litigation spanned several years, leading the district court to correctly observe that the government’s initial justifications might be stale. The court required the FBI to update its assessment of whether the enforcement proceedings were still pending. The FBI responded with a declaration from Joseph Bender that was, by the court's own admission, "surprisingly terse." Bender essentially confirmed that the investigations remained ongoing, without providing the granular detail found in the agency's original, more comprehensive submissions.

Farahi argued that this conclusory "update" failed to meet the evidentiary standard set forth in *Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Washington (CREW) v. DOJ*, where the D.C. Circuit warned that vague assertions of ongoing investigations may be insufficient to sustain a withholding. However, the D.C. Circuit rejected this argument. The court reasoned that the Bender declaration should not be read in isolation. Instead, it viewed the update as a bridge back to the original, detailed declarations provided by a previous official, David Hardy. Because the initial evidence was robust, the court held that the agency only needed to provide a confirmation that the status quo persisted.

This reasoning establishes a "maintenance" standard for government declarations that may frustrate FOIA practitioners. By treating the update declaration as a supplement rather than a standalone piece of evidence, the court has effectively lowered the bar for agencies to refresh stale declarations. If an agency can get a detailed declaration into the record once, it may be able to shield those records for an indefinite period by providing minimalist updates that merely attest that "nothing has changed." 

The doctrinal implication here is a narrowing of the *CREW* holding. While *CREW* suggests that "vague" assertions are insufficient, *Farahi* suggests that vagueness is acceptable if the agency is simply updating a previously detailed record. This creates a strategic incentive for agencies to front-load their litigation with exhaustive (and often ex parte) declarations, knowing that the subsequent burden of proof to maintain the withholding over the life of the litigation will be significantly diminished.

Furthermore, the court's treatment of segregability in this case reinforces the difficulty plaintiffs face when dealing with national security files. The court deferred to the FBI’s assertion that non-exempt information was "inextricably intertwined" with exempt material, noting that even if individual words could be released, the government is not required to produce "meaningless" fragments of information. When combined with the court's willingness to rely on ex parte declarations to resolve these disputes, the result is a high wall of deference.

For practitioners, *Farahi* suggests that challenging "stale" government evidence requires more than pointing to the brevity of an update. To overcome this "update loophole," plaintiffs will likely need to provide specific evidence that the underlying circumstances of the investigation have fundamentally shifted, rather than relying on the court to demand a more detailed accounting from the agency. As it stands, the D.C. Circuit has signaled that once the government establishes a "reasonably anticipated" proceeding, it can keep the door closed with very little effort.



# Shadow Litigation: The Role of Ex Parte Declarations in FOIA Enforcement

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) is designed to be a window into the federal government, but in cases involving national security and law enforcement, that window is often replaced by a mirrored wall. The D.C. Circuit recently reaffirmed this reality in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, a case that highlights a troubling procedural paradox: while FOIA mandates transparency, the process for determining whether the government is *justified* in withholding information is often conducted in total secrecy.

In *Farahi*, the appellant—an Iranian national with a long history of immigration proceedings—sought his FBI file. The Bureau withheld the vast majority of the records under Exemption 7(A), which protects records compiled for law enforcement purposes if their release could reasonably be expected to interfere with "pending or reasonably anticipated" enforcement proceedings. The district court granted summary judgment to the FBI, and the D.C. Circuit affirmed. 

The core of the dispute was not merely whether the FBI had a valid reason to withhold documents, but *how* the FBI proved it. To satisfy the court, the government relied on a combination of public declarations and ex parte, under-seal submissions. When the district court asked for an update on whether the investigations were still ongoing years after the initial request, the FBI provided a supplemental declaration from an acting section chief. Farahi argued that this update was too terse and conclusory to be credible. 

The D.C. Circuit disagreed, holding that the "terse" update did not need to stand alone; it merely supplemented previous, detailed ex parte declarations that the court had already reviewed in private. The court further ruled that the FBI had sufficiently demonstrated that the records were not "reasonably segregable," meaning that any non-exempt information was too intertwined with the secret material to be extracted.

The most striking aspect of the opinion is the court’s candid admission regarding the procedural imbalance of this process. The court acknowledged that the use of ex parte declarations "handicaps" FOIA plaintiffs. In any other area of civil litigation, a party moving for summary judgment must provide evidence that the opposing party can challenge. Here, however, the "evidence" is a secret document that the plaintiff—and the public—will never see. The court dismissed this concern as an "inherent" part of FOIA cases where national security is at stake, suggesting that the only way to protect the secret is to keep the justification for the secret, secret as well.

From an analytical perspective, this approach risks transforming judicial review into a rubber-stamp exercise. While the court argues that it is providing oversight by reviewing the ex parte declarations, that oversight is fundamentally one-sided. The plaintiff cannot point to contradictions in the government's narrative, cannot suggest alternative interpretations of the "interference" risk, and cannot argue that the "pending proceedings" are a pretext for avoiding embarrassment. When the court relies on the government's own secret narrative to decide that the government's secret narrative is true, the "adversarial" nature of the litigation vanishes.

The doctrinal implications here are significant, particularly regarding the "reasonably anticipated" standard of Exemption 7(A). By allowing the government to maintain the "ongoing" status of an investigation via periodic, vague updates to secret filings, the court provides the FBI with a powerful tool to indefinitely shield records from public view. If a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" can be maintained simply by an agency's internal attestation—which the court will then verify using other secret attestations—the burden of proof effectively shifts from the government to the plaintiff, who must prove a negative without any access to the evidence.

Ultimately, *Farahi* reinforces a high wall of deference to the executive in counterterrorism contexts. While the court may be correct that disclosing the details of an investigation would jeopardize it, the reliance on a "shadow" record creates a dangerous precedent. It suggests that in the realm of national security, the government is granted a near-blank check to define the scope of its own transparency, leaving the judiciary to act not as a check on executive power, but as a confidential auditor of it.



# Information vs. Words: Parsing the "Inextricably Intertwined" Standard

In the world of Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) litigation, the battle is rarely over whether a record exists, but rather over how much of it the government is willing to let the public see. The tension usually centers on "segregability"—the statutory requirement that agencies release any reasonably segregable non-exempt portion of a record. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the D.C. Circuit has reinforced a high bar for plaintiffs seeking to force the government's hand, drawing a sharp, conceptual distinction between "meaningful information" and mere "words."

The case involves a massive FBI file—over 10,000 pages and 80 CDs—pertaining to Mr. Farahi, an Iranian national with alleged ties to terrorism. The FBI withheld the bulk of these records under Exemption 7(A), arguing that disclosure would interfere with ongoing law enforcement proceedings. While the court’s affirmation of the exemption itself follows a well-trodden path of deference to national security claims, the court's analysis of segregability provides a critical lesson in how the "inextricably intertwined" doctrine operates in practice.

At the heart of the dispute was Farahi’s intuitive argument: in a corpus of 10,000 pages, it is statistically improbable that not a single sentence or paragraph is free of exempt material. To a layperson, or even a lawyer, the idea that an agency can withhold tens of thousands of pages in their entirety without a single redaction feels like a violation of the spirit of FOIA. However, the D.C. Circuit leaned on the "inextricably intertwined" standard, which allows the government to withhold records when non-exempt portions are so blended with exempt ones that separation would be impractical or would reveal the very secrets the exemption is meant to protect.

The court’s reasoning here is particularly telling. Relying on *Mead Data Center, Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force*, the court asserted that the focus of FOIA is "information," not "simply words which the Government has written down." By this logic, if non-exempt material is "interspersed line-by-line" throughout a document, it does not constitute "meaningful information." Effectively, the court held that the government is not required to excise isolated words or phrases if they do not convey a coherent, independent piece of information.

This distinction creates a significant practical hurdle for FOIA plaintiffs. If "meaningful information" is the metric, the government can essentially argue that while a page may contain non-exempt facts, those facts are "noise" unless they can be extracted without compromising the "signal" of the exempt law enforcement data. When applied to massive files, this logic can transform the "inextricably intertwined" doctrine from a narrow exception into a broad shield. If the FBI can convince a judge—via ex parte declarations—that the exempt and non-exempt data are woven together at a granular level, the volume of the file becomes irrelevant. A 10,000-page file is treated no differently than a one-page memo if the "intertwining" is sufficiently dense.

The downstream effect of this ruling is a further solidification of the "black box" nature of national security FOIA litigation. Because the court relied on ex parte submissions—which Farahi did not oppose—the determination of what was "meaningful" versus what was merely "words" happened entirely behind closed doors. The plaintiff is left to trust the agency’s categorization and the court’s review of that categorization, with no way to challenge the actual level of "intertwining" present in the documents.

Ultimately, *Farahi* underscores a sobering reality for litigants: the statutory mandate to "segregate and release" is not a guarantee of a "search and rescue" mission for every non-exempt fact. By prioritizing "meaningful information" over literal text, the D.C. Circuit has signaled that as long as the government provides a reasonably specific justification for the entanglement of its data, the courts will not require agencies to perform the surgical precision that plaintiffs often hope for in massive record requests.
