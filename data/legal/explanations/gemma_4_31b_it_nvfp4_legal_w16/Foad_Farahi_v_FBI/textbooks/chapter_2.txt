Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 2: # The Legal Framework of FOIA Exemption 7(A)

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) establishes a general presumption of transparency, requiring federal agencies to disclose records to the public unless those records fall under a specific statutory exemption. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the central dispute involves Exemption 7(A), which allows an agency to withhold records "compiled for law enforcement purposes" if the agency can demonstrate that disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." To successfully invoke this exemption, the government must satisfy a two-pronged test: it must prove that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their release would likely interfere with a proceeding that is either pending or reasonably anticipated.

The first requirement—that records be "compiled for law enforcement purposes"—is not automatically satisfied simply because a law enforcement agency created the document. The government must establish a rational "nexus" between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties. Furthermore, there must be a demonstrable connection between the individual or incident being investigated and a possible security risk or a violation of federal law. In *Farahi*, the court found this requirement easily met. The FBI’s declarations indicated the records were compiled to investigate violations of national security and international terrorism, and the broader context—including the plaintiff’s own admissions regarding his alleged ties to terrorist organizations—confirmed the law enforcement nature of the file.

The second requirement focuses on the potential for interference with "enforcement proceedings." Under judicial interpretation, such proceedings must be "pending or reasonably anticipated." The court clarifies that ongoing criminal investigations qualify as proceedings for this purpose. The government can typically satisfy this requirement by attesting to the existence of the investigation. Interference is often established by showing that the release of records would "tip off" targets about the scope and nature of an investigation, thereby jeopardizing the government's ability to gather evidence for a future criminal case. In *Farahi*, the court noted that while the government's updated declarations were terse, they should be read in the context of earlier, more detailed ex parte submissions that established the pendency of the investigation.

The procedural burden in FOIA litigation rests with the government. The agency bears the burden of proving that a requested exemption applies to the withheld records. However, this burden is modulated by the doctrine of agency deference. Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, courts grant its determinations a "fair measure of deference." This deference is significantly heightened when the records in question implicate national security or counterterrorism investigations. The court in *Farahi* emphasized that the executive branch is entitled to substantial deference in these sensitive areas, which informs how the court evaluates the sufficiency of the agency's declarations.

Finally, even when an exemption applies, the government cannot withhold a document in its entirety if the exempt information is reasonably segregable. Under FOIA, agencies must take reasonable steps to separate and release nonexempt information. However, the government may withhold a record in full if the nonexempt portions are "inextricably intertwined" with the exempt portions. As applied in *Farahi*, this means that if nonexempt material is interspersed line-by-line throughout a document, it is not considered reasonably segregable, and the agency is not required to redact the document word-by-word.
