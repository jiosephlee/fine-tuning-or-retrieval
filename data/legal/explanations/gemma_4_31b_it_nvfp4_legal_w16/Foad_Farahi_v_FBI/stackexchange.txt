Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### How can a court rely on ex parte declarations in FOIA cases without violating due process?
Question:
I'm confused by the procedural aspect of this case. The FBI submitted declarations 'ex parte' and 'under seal,' meaning the plaintiff, Farahi, never saw the specific evidence the court used to grant summary judgment. In any other area of law, I thought the opposing party had a right to see the evidence against them. Is this standard for FOIA cases, and how does the court balance the agency's need for secrecy with the plaintiff's right to challenge the evidence?
Answer:
***

### How can a court rely on ex parte declarations in FOIA cases without violating due process?

The short answer is that in FOIA litigation, the court acts as a gatekeeper to balance the public's right to information against the government's need for secrecy. When the evidence required to justify a withholding is itself the secret information the agency seeks to protect, the court may rely on *ex parte* (one-sided) and sealed declarations to reach a decision without compromising national security or law enforcement operations.

Here is the detailed breakdown of the reasoning used in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*.

#### 1. The "Inherent Handicap" of FOIA Litigation
You are correct that in most civil litigation, a party has a right to see the evidence used against them. However, FOIA presents a unique procedural paradox: the plaintiff is suing to see documents, and the government is arguing that those documents must remain secret. 

As the court noted in *Farahi*, relying on *ex parte* declarations does "handicap" the plaintiff because they cannot challenge the specific facts contained in the sealed documents. However, the court holds that this problem "is inherent in those FOIA cases where... an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." In essence, the court decides that the risk of disclosing sensitive law enforcement data outweighs the plaintiff's right to see the evidence during the summary judgment phase.

#### 2. The Standard for Exemption 7(A)
In this case, the FBI relied on **Exemption 7(A)**, which protects records compiled for law enforcement purposes if disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." 

To satisfy this, the government must show the proceedings are "pending or reasonably anticipated." The court in *Farahi* explained that the government can meet this burden by simply attesting to the *existence* of an ongoing investigation. While the public declarations provided the general framework (e.g., that investigations were ongoing), the *ex parte* declarations provided the "considerable detail" necessary for the judge to verify that the FBI's claims were not merely "vague" or "conclusory."

#### 3. The Segregability Requirement
A key point of contention in FOIA cases is **segregability**: the requirement that agencies release any non-exempt portion of a record. 

The court may rely on government affidavits that show "with reasonable specificity" why documents cannot be further segregated (i.e., why the exempt and non-exempt parts are "inextricably intertwined"). Because describing why a document cannot be segregated often requires describing the secret contents of the document, the court routinely accepts these justifications *ex parte*. In *Farahi*, the district court reviewed the FBI's sealed submissions and determined that no "meaningful information" was reasonably segregable, a conclusion the Court of Appeals affirmed.

#### 4. Judicial Deference
Finally, the court’s reliance on these declarations is bolstered by a legal doctrine of **deference**. Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, the court grants its decisions a "fair measure of deference," which is increased when national security and counterterrorism are implicated. 

**Summary of the court's logic in *Farahi*:**
* **Due Process:** The plaintiff's right to challenge evidence is superseded by the necessity of protecting ongoing investigations.
* **Verification:** The court itself reviews the *ex parte* evidence to ensure the agency is not acting arbitrarily, even if the plaintiff cannot.
* **Procedural Waiver:** In this specific case, the court also noted that Farahi did not oppose the FBI's initial motion to file the declarations under seal and *ex parte*, further limiting his grounds for appeal on this procedural point.

### Does an 'ongoing investigation' always qualify as a 'proceeding' under Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
The court references the case 'CREW' to say that ongoing criminal investigations count as 'proceedings' that are 'pending or reasonably anticipated.' However, usually, a 'proceeding' implies something formal, like a lawsuit or an indictment. If the FBI is just 'gathering evidence' without a formal charge, how is that a 'proceeding'? I'm struggling to find the line between a general investigation and a 'concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.'
Answer:
***

### Short Answer: Yes, provided the investigation is actively gathering evidence for a potential future case.

Under Exemption 7(A) of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), a "proceeding" does not need to be a formal legal action, such as an indictment or a lawsuit, to qualify for protection. As the court explains in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, ongoing criminal investigations are considered "proceedings" so long as they are "**pending or reasonably anticipated**."

### The Legal Standard

The "line" you are looking for—between a general investigation and a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding"—is defined by the purpose and the potential impact of the disclosure. According to the court's analysis:

1.  **The Nature of the Activity:** Exemption 7(A) applies "so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case." Therefore, the act of gathering evidence itself constitutes a "proceeding" for the purposes of this exemption, even if no formal charges have been filed.
2.  **The Risk of Interference:** The agency must show that the premature release of evidence would jeopardize that potential future case. In *Farahi*, the court noted that a "widely accepted justification" for withholding records is that disclosure could "tip off the targets about the scope of the investigation."

### Application in *Farahi*

In this case, the FBI successfully argued that its investigation into Farahi's alleged ties to terrorism constituted a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding." The court grounded this conclusion in two types of evidence:

*   **Attestations of Existence:** The government may establish the pendency of a proceeding simply by attesting to its "existence" via declarations.
*   **Contextual Detail:** While the court acknowledged that a "vague" or "conclusory" assertion that an investigation is ongoing might be insufficient, it held that the FBI's claims were credible here because they were backed by detailed (and in some cases, *ex parte*) declarations and the known breadth of the investigation.

In summary, you do not need a docket number to have a "proceeding." If the government is actively gathering evidence for a possible future prosecution and disclosure would interfere with that process, it qualifies as a "pending or reasonably anticipated" proceeding under Exemption 7(A).

### What is the 'inextricably intertwined' standard for segregability in large record sets?
Question:
The FBI withheld over 10,000 pages, claiming that non-exempt material was 'inextricably intertwined' or 'interspersed line-by-line' with exempt material. The court seems to accept that no 'meaningful information' was segregable. If a document is 10,000 pages long, is it practically possible for every single page to be so intertwined that not even a few sentences can be released? I'm trying to understand how high the bar is for the government to avoid the duty to segregate.
Answer:
***

**The "Inextricably Intertwined" Standard and Segregability under FOIA**

Under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), agencies are generally required to take "reasonable steps" to segregate and release non-exempt information even if the record as a whole is protected. However, the "inextricably intertwined" standard provides a critical exception to this duty.

### The Legal Standard
The government may withhold records in their entirety when non-exempt portions are **"inextricably intertwined"** with exempt portions. *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008). This occurs when exempt and non-exempt information is so mixed—for example, "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document"—that the non-exempt material is not "reasonably segregable." *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, No. 23-5243 (D.C. Cir. 2025) (citing *Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force*, 566 F.2d 242, 261).

### "Meaningful Information" vs. "Words"
Your question asks whether it is practically possible for 10,000 pages to be so intertwined that not a few sentences can be released. The court in *Farahi* addresses this by distinguishing between **words** and **information**.

The court emphasizes that "the focus of the FOIA is information... not simply words which the Government has written down." *Farahi* (quoting *Mead*). Consequently, the bar for segregability is not whether a few isolated words or phrases can be released without harm, but whether any **meaningful information** can be extracted. If the non-exempt content consists only of fragments that do not constitute meaningful information, the government has satisfied its duty to segregate.

### The Evidentiary Bar for the Government
To avoid the duty to segregate, the government does not necessarily need to provide a public, page-by-page analysis. Instead:

1.  **Specificity:** The government must provide affidavits or declarations that show "with reasonable specificity" why the documents cannot be further segregated. *Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 61.
2.  **Ex Parte Submissions:** In sensitive cases (such as those involving national security or ongoing law enforcement investigations), the court may rely on *ex parte* declarations. The court in *Farahi* held that such declarations are often the only way to resolve segregability disputes without disclosing the very secret material the government is trying to protect.
3.  **Detailed Justification:** In *Farahi*, the court found the government met its burden because it provided a "detailed justification" in a supplemental *ex parte* submission explaining why the records were not reasonably segregable.

**Summary for your analysis:** The "bar" is not a mathematical one based on the volume of pages. Even in a 10,000-page set, the government can avoid segregation if it provides a reasonably specific justification—even if *ex parte*—that the only releasable elements are isolated words or phrases rather than "meaningful information."

### How does 'deference' to the FBI impact the government's burden of proof under FOIA?
Question:
The opinion states that the government bears the burden of showing that an exemption applies, but then immediately says that because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decisions are 'entitled to deference.' This feels contradictory. If the court defers to a 'terse' declaration (like Bender's) and accepts the agency's word that an investigation is still ongoing, hasn't the burden effectively shifted to the plaintiff to prove that the investigation is *not* ongoing?
Answer:
***

### How does 'deference' to the FBI impact the government's burden of proof under FOIA?

To understand the court's reasoning in *Farahi v. FBI*, it is important to distinguish between the **legal burden of proof** and the **standard of evidence** used to satisfy that burden.

**The Burden of Proof Remains with the Government**
As a matter of law, the burden of proof does not shift. Under U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B), the government always bears the burden of showing that a FOIA exemption applies. The court in *Farahi* explicitly reaffirmed this principle, stating that the FBI must show that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that disclosure would reasonably be expected to interfere with proceedings that are "pending or reasonably anticipated."

**The Role of Deference**
"Deference" does not change *who* carries the burden, but it influences *how much* evidence the court requires to satisfy that burden. Because the FBI possesses specialized expertise in law enforcement—particularly in matters of national security and counterterrorism—the court grants a "fair measure of deference" to its determinations (*Campbell v. DOJ*). 

In practice, this means the court is more likely to accept the agency's expert judgment and declarations as sufficient proof, provided there is a rational nexus between the agency's action and its law enforcement duties. Deference allows the agency to meet its burden without being forced to disclose sensitive operational details that would defeat the purpose of the exemption.

**Addressing the "Terse" Declaration**
You mentioned the "terse" nature of the Bender declaration. The court’s acceptance of that declaration was not a result of blindly shifting the burden to the plaintiff, but rather a result of **contextual evidence**. 

The court rejected the idea that the Bender declaration should be viewed "in isolation." Instead, it viewed the Bender declaration as a supplemental update to the previous Hardy declarations. The court found the government had met its burden because:
1. The **Hardy declarations** (including the ex parte submission) provided the "considerable detail" and the "rational nexus" required to establish the existence of the investigation.
2. The **Bender declaration** served merely to confirm that the status of the investigation remained unchanged.

Therefore, the court did not accept a conclusory statement in a vacuum; it accepted a brief confirmation of a factual foundation that had already been established by more detailed evidence. The burden remained on the government to provide that foundation, and once provided, deference allowed the court to accept the FBI's assertion that the investigation remained ongoing.

### What does it mean for a FOIA withholding category to be 'overbroad'?
Question:
In Section IV(C), the court mentions that when an agency withholds documents by category, those categories must be 'sufficiently distinct' so the court can determine if the exemption applies. Farahi argued the categories were overbroad, but the court said he 'forfeited' the argument because he didn't explain why. I don't understand what an 'overbroad category' looks like in practice. What is an example of a category that is 'sufficiently distinct' versus one that is too broad?
Answer:
***

# What does it mean for a FOIA withholding category to be 'overbroad'?

In the context of FOIA litigation, agencies often withhold documents by grouping them into "categories" (e.g., "investigative reports" or "internal memos") rather than providing a line-by-line justification for every single page. 

As explained in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, a withholding category is **overbroad** if it is not "**sufficiently distinct**" to allow a court to determine whether the claimed exemption actually applies to the documents in that group.

### The Legal Standard
The core requirement is that categories must be defined with enough specificity that a court can perform its judicial review. If a category is too vague or encompasses too many different types of records, the court cannot possibly determine if the agency is applying the exemption correctly.

According to the opinion, the standard (citing *CREW* and *Gallant*) is that categories must be:
> "...sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied to each category."

### Practical Application: "Sufficiently Distinct" vs. "Overbroad"
While the court in *Farahi* did not provide a list of examples (because the plaintiff "forfeited" the argument by failing to explain *why* the FBI's categories were problematic), the legal logic functions as follows:

*   **Sufficiently Distinct:** A category that describes a specific type of record or a specific functional group of documents. For example, if an agency creates a category for "records compiled for the purpose of a specific ongoing criminal investigation," a court can evaluate whether those records meet the requirements of Exemption 7(A).
*   **Overbroad:** A category that is so general it blends exempt and non-exempt material together without a clear distinction. For example, if an agency categorized documents simply as "all records pertaining to the subject's file," that would likely be overbroad because it doesn't allow the court to distinguish between law enforcement records (which may be exempt) and purely administrative records (which are generally not).

### Why the Argument Failed in *Farahi*
In this specific case, Farahi argued that the FBI's categories were overbroad. However, the court did not analyze whether the categories were actually too broad. Instead, it ruled that Farahi **forfeited** the argument. 

To succeed on this claim, a requester cannot simply label the categories "overbroad"; they must provide a "specific explanation" as to why the agency's definitions fail to meet the "sufficiently distinct" standard. Because Farahi failed to provide that explanation, the court declined to review the merits of the category definitions.
