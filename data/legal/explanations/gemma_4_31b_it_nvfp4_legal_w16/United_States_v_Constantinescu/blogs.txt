Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Beyond the Right to Control: The Fifth Circuit’s Boundary Between Information and Property

The federal fraud statutes are among the most powerful tools in the government's prosecutorial arsenal, but their reach has recently been the subject of intense scrutiny by the Supreme Court. At the heart of this tension is a fundamental question: what constitutes a "property interest" for the purposes of a fraud conviction? In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit has provided a critical clarification on this boundary, distinguishing between the deprivation of information and the fraudulent inducement of a financial transaction.

The case involves a classic "pump and dump" scheme executed via social media. The defendants allegedly used their platforms to mislead followers into buying specific securities, artificially inflating the prices so the defendants could sell their own holdings for a massive profit. The district court originally dismissed the indictment, reasoning that the government had merely alleged a scheme to deprive followers of "valuable economic information"—a theory the court believed was prohibited by the Supreme Court’s decision in *Ciminelli v. United States*.

To understand why the Fifth Circuit reversed this dismissal, one must navigate the narrow doctrinal gap between the "right-to-control" theory and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court rejected the right-to-control theory, which posits that a victim is defrauded if they are deprived of valuable information necessary to make a discretionary decision. The Court held that such a "right" is not a traditional property interest. However, the Fifth Circuit correctly notes that *Constantinescu* is not a case about the right to information, but about the movement of money. Under the fraudulent-inducement theory, validated in *Kousisis v. United States*, fraud occurs when a defendant uses misrepresentations to trick a victim into a contract or transaction that requires them to part with money or property.

The Fifth Circuit’s reasoning here is surgically precise. By inducing followers to purchase securities, the defendants were not merely withholding information; they were inducing a transfer of assets. The court clarifies that the act of tricking a victim into handing over money is a deprivation of a property interest, regardless of whether the victim receives something of equal value in return—such as a security traded at market price.

This distinction is reinforced by the court's application of *Kousisis*, which dismantled the argument that the government must prove a defendant intended to cause a net pecuniary loss to the victim. The defendants in *Constantinescu* argued that because they intended to profit for themselves rather than specifically "harm" their followers, they lacked the intent to defraud. The Fifth Circuit dismissed this as a "distinction without a difference." If the objective is to obtain money through deceit, the fraud is complete the moment the money is transferred under false pretenses.

The doctrinal implications of this ruling are significant, particularly in the era of "fin-fluencers" and social-media-driven markets. By firmly placing "pump and dump" schemes within the fraudulent-inducement framework, the Fifth Circuit has ensured that the *Ciminelli* restriction on "information-only" fraud does not become a shield for traditional securities fraud. For prosecutors, the lesson is clear: the indictment must move beyond the deprivation of a "right to know" and explicitly allege that the deception was the engine used to induce the transfer of money or property.

Downstream, this decision signals that the Fifth Circuit will not allow the recent narrowing of the mail and wire fraud statutes to erode the government's ability to police market manipulation. As long as there is a tangible exchange of assets triggered by a lie, the "traditional property interest" requirement will likely be satisfied, keeping the door open for the prosecution of complex financial schemes in the digital age.



# Profit Without Pecuniary Loss: Does 'Intent to Defraud' Require an Intent to Harm?

The Fifth Circuit recently weighed in on a critical question regarding the boundaries of federal fraud statutes: must a defendant intend to cause a victim a net economic loss to possess the "intent to defraud," or is the intent to simply obtain money through deceit sufficient? In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the court held the latter, reviving a securities fraud indictment against a group of social-media "finfluencers" accused of orchestrating a classic pump-and-dump scheme.

The case centers on a group of defendants who allegedly used their large social media followings to artificially inflate the price of securities they already owned. By posting false and misleading information to induce their followers to buy, the defendants were able to "dump" their shares at a profit, allegedly netting $114 million. The district court originally dismissed the indictment, reasoning that the government failed to allege a deprivation of a traditional property interest—essentially arguing that the victims were deprived of *information* rather than *money*—and that the defendants intended to profit rather than cause harm.

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal hinges on a sharp distinction between two theories of fraud: the "right-to-control" theory and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. The district court’s analysis relied heavily on *Ciminelli v. United States*, where the Supreme Court rejected the right-to-control theory, holding that the government cannot sustain a fraud conviction based solely on the deprivation of potentially valuable economic information. In *Ciminelli*, the "property" allegedly stolen was the victim's right to make an informed discretionary decision.

However, Judge Engelhardt correctly noted that the *Constantinescu* indictment does not rely on the right-to-control theory. Instead, it invokes the fraudulent-inducement theory, which the Supreme Court recently validated in *Kousisis v. United States*. Under this theory, a defendant is liable if they use a material misrepresentation to trick a victim into a contract or transaction that requires the victim to hand over money or property. The distinction is subtle but dispositive: while *Ciminelli* prevents the government from criminalizing the theft of *information*, *Kousisis* confirms that the government can still criminalize the theft of *money* obtained through lies.

The most provocative aspect of the court's reasoning is its treatment of "intent to defraud." The defendants argued that because they were selling securities—and the victims were receiving those securities in exchange for their money—there was no intent to cause the victims a net pecuniary loss. They essentially argued that if a victim buys a stock at market value, they haven't been "harmed" in the traditional sense, regardless of the lies told to induce the purchase.

The Fifth Circuit rejected this, leaning on *Kousisis* to clarify that the fraud statutes do not require a "mirror image" of loss and gain. The "intent to defraud" is satisfied when the defendant intends to deceive the victim to obtain their money or property, regardless of whether the defendant provides something of equal value in return. In the context of a pump-and-dump, the "injury" is the fraudulent inducement to part with money. The fact that the victim received a security in exchange does not sanitize the deception.

This holding has significant implications for the prosecution of digital-age securities fraud. By decoupling "intent to defraud" from "intent to cause net economic loss," the court has closed a potential loophole for social media promoters who might argue that their victims were not "harmed" as long as they received an asset in exchange for their cash. For "finfluencers" and traders, this serves as a stark reminder that the legal focus is on the integrity of the transaction and the honesty of the inducement, not just the final balance sheet of the victim.



# Finfluencers in the Crosshairs: Traditional Fraud Doctrines in the Social Media Age

The rise of the "finfluencer"—the social media personality who blends financial advice with lifestyle branding—has created a regulatory gray area. When a trader with a million followers tells their audience to buy a specific stock while secretly preparing to sell their own shares, is that merely "hyping" a trade or is it a federal crime? The Fifth Circuit’s recent decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* clarifies that traditional securities fraud statutes are more than capable of capturing the modern "pump and dump" scheme, even when the defendants claim their only goal was personal profit rather than victim harm.

The case centers on several defendants who allegedly used their social media platforms to mislead followers about their trading positions and the potential value of certain securities. By inducing their followers to buy, they artificially inflated the prices, allowing them to "dump" their own holdings for a reported $114 million profit. The district court originally dismissed the indictment, reasoning that the government was merely alleging a deprivation of "valuable economic information"—a theory the Supreme Court recently rejected in *Ciminelli v. United States*.

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal hinges on a critical distinction between the "right-to-control" theory and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court held that federal fraud statutes do not protect a victim's "right to control" their assets via the possession of honest information. However, as the Fifth Circuit correctly notes, *Constantinescu* is not about a right to information; it is about the inducement to part with money. Under the fraudulent-inducement theory—upheld in the recent *Kousisis* decision—the crime is committed when a defendant uses a material lie to trick a victim into a transaction involving money or property. By inducing followers to purchase securities under false pretenses, the defendants were not just withholding information; they were actively manipulating followers into handing over cash.

Perhaps more significant for future "finfluencer" prosecutions is the court's treatment of the "intent to defraud" element. The defendants argued that because they sought to enrich themselves rather than specifically intend to cause their followers a financial loss, they lacked the requisite criminal intent. The Fifth Circuit dismantled this argument using *Kousisis*, affirming that the fraud statutes do not require a "mirror image" of loss. In other words, the government does not need to prove that the defendant wanted to "hurt" the victim’s bottom line, only that the defendant intended to "obtain" money or property through deception. Even if a follower received a security of equivalent market value in the exchange, the act of inducing that purchase through a lie satisfies the statutory requirement.

This ruling creates a dangerous precedent for social media traders who operate in the space between "opinion" and "misrepresentation." By grounding the decision in *Kousisis* and *Ciminelli*, the Fifth Circuit has signaled that the government does not need to prove a net pecuniary loss to secure a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. The mere act of using a material misrepresentation to move a follower's money into a security is sufficient.

The downstream effect of *Constantinescu* is a broadening of the pathway for prosecuting the "finfluencer" economy. Many social media traders rely on the premise that their followers are taking a known risk and that the "pump" is simply part of the game. However, this decision clarifies that once a trader misrepresents their own position or the facts of a security to induce others to buy, they have crossed from marketing into federal fraud. For those who treat their social media following as a tool for market manipulation, the "right-to-control" defense is now officially dead.
