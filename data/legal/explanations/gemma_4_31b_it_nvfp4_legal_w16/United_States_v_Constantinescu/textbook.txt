Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## The 'Pump and Dump' Scheme: Social media influence and market manipulation

The case of *United States v. Constantinescu* centers on a coordinated effort by a group of social media influencers to manipulate the stock market through a "pump and dump" scheme. According to the government's allegations, the defendants—who maintained significant followings across various social media platforms—presented themselves to the public as expert stock traders. They leveraged this perceived authority to induce their followers to purchase specific securities.

The mechanics of the alleged scheme involved three primary steps. First, the defendants would purchase a security. Second, they would "pump" the price of that security by posting false and misleading information on social media, tricking their followers into buying the stock and thereby artificially inflating its market price. Finally, the defendants would "dump" the security by secretly selling their shares at the inflated price for a profit. The government alleges that the defendants realized approximately $114 million in profits from this conduct.

## The Superseding Indictment: Charges under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and 18 U.S.C. § 1349

Based on these allegations, the government issued a superseding indictment charging the defendants with multiple counts of securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and conspiracy to commit securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349. 

Section 1348 is the primary statute for securities fraud, prohibiting schemes to defraud any person in connection with a security or schemes to obtain money or property through false representations. Section 1349 is the general conspiracy statute that applies to the commission of the underlying fraud. While one defendant, Daniel Knight, pleaded guilty to the charges, the remaining defendants moved to dismiss the indictment, challenging the legal sufficiency of the government's claims.

## District Court Ruling: Dismissal based on the lack of a traditional property interest

The district court granted the defendants' motion to dismiss, concluding that the indictment failed to state a cognizable offense. The court's reasoning rested on the definition of what constitutes a "property interest" in a fraud case. 

The district court determined that the indictment merely alleged the defendants sought to deprive their followers of "potentially valuable economic information." The court reasoned that since the federal fraud statutes are intended to protect traditional property interests—such as money or tangible assets—the deprivation of information alone was insufficient to sustain a criminal charge of fraud. Because the court viewed the alleged harm as the loss of information rather than the loss of actual property, it dismissed the indictment.

## Procedural Status: The government's appeal to the Fifth Circuit

The government timely appealed the district court's dismissal to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit. The central issue on appeal is whether the indictment was sufficient to state a crime. 

To resolve this, the Fifth Circuit must apply a *de novo* standard of review, meaning it examines the indictment from scratch without deferring to the district court's legal conclusions. The appellate court's task is to take the allegations in the indictment as true and determine if they enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offenses, fairly inform the defendants of the charges, and provide a basis for a double jeopardy defense. Specifically, the Fifth Circuit must decide if the "fraudulent-inducement" alleged by the government—tricking followers into spending money to buy securities—constitutes a deprivation of a protected property interest.

# Standards for Reviewing Indictment Sufficiency

When a defendant moves to dismiss an indictment, the court must determine whether the charging document is legally sufficient to support a conviction if all the allegations are proven. In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit addressed this threshold issue after a district court dismissed an indictment for securities fraud, ruling that the government failed to allege the deprivation of a traditional property interest.

## De Novo Review: The Standard for Reviewing the Sufficiency of an Indictment

The Fifth Circuit reviews the sufficiency of an indictment *de novo*. This means the appellate court does not defer to the district court's legal conclusions; instead, it examines the indictment independently to determine if it meets the legal requirements for a valid charge. Because the question of whether an indictment states an offense is a legal determination, the appellate court applies the same standard of review it would use for any question of law.

## The 'Presumption of Truth': Treating Indictment Allegations as True

A critical aspect of this review is the "presumption of truth." When determining whether an indictment is sufficient, the court is not weighing evidence or deciding if the government's claims are actually true. Rather, the court must take the allegations in the indictment as true. If the facts alleged in the document, assuming they are accurate, describe a crime, the indictment is sufficient to proceed to trial. The court's role at this stage is not to evaluate the strength of the government's case, but simply to determine whether an offense has been stated.

## Three Pillars of a Sufficient Indictment: Prima Facie Elements, Notice to the Defendant, and Double Jeopardy Protection

To be legally sufficient under Fifth Circuit precedent, an indictment must satisfy three specific functions. First, it must enumerate each *prima facie* element of the charged offense. This ensures that the government has alleged every legal component required to prove the crime. Second, the indictment must fairly inform the defendant of the charges against them. This "notice" requirement is essential for the defendant to prepare an adequate legal defense. Third, the indictment must provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions. By specifying the acts and the timeframe of the alleged crime, the indictment prevents the government from charging the defendant again for the same conduct following an acquittal.

## The Material Element Requirement

Ultimately, these three pillars coalesce into a single requirement: an indictment must allege each material element of the offense. A material element is a necessary legal component of the crime that the government must prove to secure a conviction. 

In *Constantinescu*, the central dispute revolved around these material elements. The defendants argued that the indictment was insufficient because it failed to allege a scheme to deprive victims of a "traditional property interest," which they contended was a material element of fraud. The Fifth Circuit's analysis focused on whether the government's "fraudulent-inducement theory"—alleging that defendants tricked followers into spending money to buy securities—sufficiently alleged the material elements of a scheme and intent to defraud.

# The Legal Framework of Securities Fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1348)

## Statutory Language: Schemes to Defraud and the Use of False Pretenses in Security Transactions

At the center of *United States v. Constantinescu* is 18 U.S.C. § 1348, the federal securities fraud statute. This statute prohibits two primary types of conduct: schemes designed to "defraud any person in connection with... any security," and schemes intended "to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of... any security." 

In this case, the government alleged that the defendants engaged in a "pump and dump" scheme. Specifically, the defendants used their large social media followings to post false and misleading information about securities they already owned. By misrepresenting their trading positions and the potential price of these securities, they induced their followers to purchase the stocks, which artificially inflated the price and allowed the defendants to sell their holdings for a profit. To understand the court's analysis, one must see how these facts map onto the statutory language: the "false pretenses" were the social media posts, and the "money or property" obtained were the funds provided by investors who were tricked into purchasing the securities.

## Relationship to Mail and Wire Fraud: Borrowing Key Concepts and Judicial Treatment

Because there is relatively little direct case law interpreting § 1348 within the Fifth Circuit, the court relies on a common interpretive technique: looking to analogous statutes. The court notes that § 1348 "borrows key concepts" from the mail and wire fraud statutes. Because the language and objectives of these statutes are so similar, courts often apply the legal standards and precedents developed in mail and wire fraud cases to analyze securities fraud charges.

This relationship is critical for the *Constantinescu* court because it allows the court to import Supreme Court precedents—such as *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States*—which were decided in the context of wire fraud but apply here to determine whether the indictment sufficiently alleged a "scheme" and an "intent" to defraud.

## Defining 'Scheme to Defraud': Material Misrepresentation and the Objective of Obtaining Value

According to the court, the "scheme to defraud" element of § 1348 requires a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others to obtain something of value, such as money. The core of the legal dispute in this case was whether the defendants' actions constituted a "scheme" to deprive victims of a protected property interest.

The defendants argued that the indictment merely alleged they deprived followers of "valuable economic information," which they claimed was not a traditional property interest. The court rejected this by distinguishing between two theories of fraud. Under the "right-to-control theory," a defendant is liable if they deprive a victim of information necessary to make a decision; however, the Supreme Court in *Ciminelli* held that this theory is insufficient for a fraud conviction because information is not "property." In contrast, the government in *Constantinescu* advanced a "fraudulent-inducement theory." Under this theory, the defendant uses a misstatement to trick a victim into a contract—such as purchasing a stock—that requires the victim to hand over money or property. Because the defendants allegedly induced their followers to actually spend money to buy securities, the court held that the indictment properly alleged a scheme to defraud a traditional property interest.

## Defining 'Intent to Defraud': The Dual Requirement of Intent to Deceive and Intent to Cause Harm

The second requirement for a conviction under § 1348 is the "intent to defraud." The Fifth Circuit defines this as a dual requirement: the defendant must (1) intend to deceive, and (2) intend to cause some harm to result from that deceit. 

The defendants in this case argued that the indictment failed to allege an intent to cause harm because it only alleged that the defendants intended to *profit*. They contended that since the followers received securities (which ostensibly had market value) in exchange for their money, there was no intent to leave the victims economically worse off. 

The court rejected this argument by applying *Kousisis v. United States*, which clarifies that the fraud statutes do not require a victim to suffer a net pecuniary loss. The "harm" required by the law is the deprivation of money or property through deceit. The court reasoned that if a defendant intends to "obtain" the victim's money via a lie, the fraud is complete regardless of whether the defendant provides something of equal value in return. Therefore, the defendants' objective of obtaining money by fraudulently inducing followers to purchase securities was sufficient to allege an intent to cause harm.

# Property Interests: Right-to-Control vs. Fraudulent Inducement

In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit addresses a critical distinction in federal fraud law: what constitutes a "property interest" sufficient to support a criminal conviction. To understand the court's reasoning, a student must distinguish between the "right-to-control" theory of fraud and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. This distinction determines whether a defendant’s deceptive conduct is a federal crime or merely an unethical deprivation of information.

## The Right-to-Control Theory: Deprivation of Valuable Economic Information

The "right-to-control" theory is a legal framework previously used by some prosecutors to argue that a victim is defrauded if they are deprived of "potentially valuable economic information" necessary to make a discretionary decision. Under this theory, the "property" being stolen is not necessarily money or a physical asset, but rather the victim's *right to control* their own assets by having access to honest information. 

In the context of a "pump and dump" scheme, a defendant relying on this theory would argue that by posting misleading information about a stock, they deprived their followers of the truthful information needed to decide whether or not to invest. However, as the court explains, this theory focuses on the loss of information rather than the loss of a tangible asset.

## Analysis of Ciminelli v. United States: Why the Right-to-Control Theory is Insufficient

The Supreme Court fundamentally rejected the right-to-control theory in *Ciminelli v. United States*. In that case, the government attempted to convict a construction company owner for wire fraud because he used a lobbyist to help secure state-funded jobs, thereby depriving the state of the honest information necessary to make an informed decision about awarding contracts. 

The Supreme Court held that the federal fraud statutes protect only "traditional property interests." Because the "right to valuable economic information" is not a traditional property interest, it cannot form the basis for a fraud conviction. The Court's reasoning is that the fraud statutes are intended to protect money and property, not to police every instance of dishonesty or the deprivation of information. Therefore, if a government's case rests *solely* on the idea that a victim was denied information, it fails as a matter of law.

## The Fraudulent-Inducement Theory: Tricking Victims into Parting with Money or Property

In contrast to the right-to-control theory, the "fraudulent-inducement theory" focuses on the actual transfer of assets. This theory applies when a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into entering a contract or a transaction that requires the victim to hand over money or property. 

As the court notes via the Supreme Court’s decision in *Kousisis v. United States*, fraudulent inducement is not a "repackaging" of the right-to-control theory. The key difference is the object of the fraud: while the right-to-control theory seeks to protect the *decision-making process* (information), the fraudulent-inducement theory protects the *assets themselves* (money and property). Crucially, under *Kousisis*, a defendant can be liable under this theory even if the victim receives something of equal value in return, or if the defendant did not intend to cause a net pecuniary loss, so long as the defendant's object was to obtain the victim's money or property through deceit.

## Application to the Case: Why Inducing the Purchase of Securities Constitutes a Deprivation of a Traditional Property Interest

In *Constantinescu*, the defendants argued that the indictment was insufficient because it merely alleged they deprived followers of economic information. The district court agreed, effectively treating the indictment as if it were based on the right-to-control theory. However, the Fifth Circuit reversed this conclusion by analyzing the specific allegations in the indictment.

The court found that the indictment did not claim the victims were merely deprived of information; rather, it alleged that the defendants used false information to *induce* their followers to purchase securities. By inducing followers to buy stocks, the defendants were tricking them into parting with their money—a traditional property interest. 

The court clarified that the "pump and dump" scheme's goal was to obtain money by fraudulently inducing purchases. Because the indictment alleged that the defendants' object was to obtain money via deception, it successfully alleged a fraudulent-inducement scheme. This distinguished the case from *Ciminelli* and confirmed that the indictment sufficiently alleged both a scheme and an intent to defraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348.

# Intent and the Requirement of Economic Harm

In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the defendants challenged the sufficiency of their indictment by arguing that the government failed to allege a proper "intent to defraud." Their argument rested on a specific interpretation of "harm": they contended that because the government alleged they intended to profit from their "pump and dump" scheme, rather than specifically intending to cause their followers to lose money, the legal requirement for intent was not met.

## The 'Net Pecuniary Loss' Argument

The defendants argued that the indictment did not sufficiently allege an intent to defraud because it did not claim they intended to leave their victims economically worse off. In their view, the objective of the scheme was their own enrichment, not the financial injury of their social media followers. This argument suggests that for a defendant to possess the requisite "intent to defraud," the government must prove that the defendant specifically sought to "hurt the victim’s bottom line" or cause a net pecuniary loss.

## Analysis of Kousisis v. United States

The Fifth Circuit rejected this argument by relying on the Supreme Court’s decision in *Kousisis v. United States*. In *Kousisis*, a contractor used a "pass-through" entity to falsely satisfy a requirement to subcontract with a disadvantaged business in order to obtain government contracts. The Supreme Court in that case clarified the distinction between "obtaining" property and "hurting the bottom line."

The Court held that the federal fraud statutes do not require a victim to suffer an actual economic loss, nor do they require the defendant to intend that the victim suffer such a loss. Instead, the statutes are violated when a defendant schemes to "obtain" the victim's money or property through deceit. The focus is on the act of obtaining the property through fraud, not the ultimate financial balance sheet of the victim.

## The Role of Consideration

A key point of contention in *Constantinescu* was the nature of the transactions. The defendants induced their followers to purchase securities. Because the followers received securities in exchange for their money, the defendants argued that there was no intent to harm, as the victims were receiving an asset of potentially equal market value.

The court, applying the reasoning from *Kousisis*, explained that providing something of value in return—whether it be money, property, or services—does not negate the fraud. A defendant may be liable for securities fraud even if they provide the victim with something of equal value. The crime lies in the use of material misrepresentations to induce the victim to part with their money; the "fairness" of the exchange or the lack of a net loss does not erase the fraudulent nature of the inducement.

## Conclusion on Intent

The court concluded that the "intent to defraud" is satisfied when a defendant's object is to obtain money or property through deception. In this case, the indictment alleged that the defendants posted false information specifically so that investors would be induced to purchase securities. 

Under the "fraudulent-inducement theory," a fraud is complete the moment a defendant induces the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses. The court determined that the defendants' desire to profit from the "pump and dump" demonstrated that they "had money in mind," which is the essence of the intent required by the statute. Consequently, the lack of an intended net pecuniary loss to the victims did not render the indictment insufficient.
