Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Beyond the Right to Control: The Fifth Circuit’s Boundary Between Information and Property

The federal fraud statutes are among the most powerful tools in the government's prosecutorial arsenal, but their reach has recently been the subject of intense scrutiny by the Supreme Court. At the heart of this tension is a fundamental question: what constitutes a "property interest" for the purposes of a fraud conviction? In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit has provided a critical clarification on this boundary, distinguishing between the deprivation of information and the fraudulent inducement of a financial transaction.

The case involves a classic "pump and dump" scheme executed via social media. The defendants allegedly used their platforms to mislead followers into buying specific securities, artificially inflating the prices so the defendants could sell their own holdings for a massive profit. The district court originally dismissed the indictment, reasoning that the government had merely alleged a scheme to deprive followers of "valuable economic information"—a theory the court believed was prohibited by the Supreme Court’s decision in *Ciminelli v. United States*.

To understand why the Fifth Circuit reversed this dismissal, one must navigate the narrow doctrinal gap between the "right-to-control" theory and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court rejected the right-to-control theory, which posits that a victim is defrauded if they are deprived of valuable information necessary to make a discretionary decision. The Court held that such a "right" is not a traditional property interest. However, the Fifth Circuit correctly notes that *Constantinescu* is not a case about the right to information, but about the movement of money. Under the fraudulent-inducement theory, validated in *Kousisis v. United States*, fraud occurs when a defendant uses misrepresentations to trick a victim into a contract or transaction that requires them to part with money or property.

The Fifth Circuit’s reasoning here is surgically precise. By inducing followers to purchase securities, the defendants were not merely withholding information; they were inducing a transfer of assets. The court clarifies that the act of tricking a victim into handing over money is a deprivation of a property interest, regardless of whether the victim receives something of equal value in return—such as a security traded at market price.

This distinction is reinforced by the court's application of *Kousisis*, which dismantled the argument that the government must prove a defendant intended to cause a net pecuniary loss to the victim. The defendants in *Constantinescu* argued that because they intended to profit for themselves rather than specifically "harm" their followers, they lacked the intent to defraud. The Fifth Circuit dismissed this as a "distinction without a difference." If the objective is to obtain money through deceit, the fraud is complete the moment the money is transferred under false pretenses.

The doctrinal implications of this ruling are significant, particularly in the era of "fin-fluencers" and social-media-driven markets. By firmly placing "pump and dump" schemes within the fraudulent-inducement framework, the Fifth Circuit has ensured that the *Ciminelli* restriction on "information-only" fraud does not become a shield for traditional securities fraud. For prosecutors, the lesson is clear: the indictment must move beyond the deprivation of a "right to know" and explicitly allege that the deception was the engine used to induce the transfer of money or property.

Downstream, this decision signals that the Fifth Circuit will not allow the recent narrowing of the mail and wire fraud statutes to erode the government's ability to police market manipulation. As long as there is a tangible exchange of assets triggered by a lie, the "traditional property interest" requirement will likely be satisfied, keeping the door open for the prosecution of complex financial schemes in the digital age.
