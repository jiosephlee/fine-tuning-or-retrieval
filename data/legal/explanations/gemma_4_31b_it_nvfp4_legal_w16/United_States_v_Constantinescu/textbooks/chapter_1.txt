Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural Posture

## The 'Pump and Dump' Scheme: Social media influence and market manipulation

The case of *United States v. Constantinescu* centers on a coordinated effort by a group of social media influencers to manipulate the stock market through a "pump and dump" scheme. According to the government's allegations, the defendants—who maintained significant followings across various social media platforms—presented themselves to the public as expert stock traders. They leveraged this perceived authority to induce their followers to purchase specific securities.

The mechanics of the alleged scheme involved three primary steps. First, the defendants would purchase a security. Second, they would "pump" the price of that security by posting false and misleading information on social media, tricking their followers into buying the stock and thereby artificially inflating its market price. Finally, the defendants would "dump" the security by secretly selling their shares at the inflated price for a profit. The government alleges that the defendants realized approximately $114 million in profits from this conduct.

## The Superseding Indictment: Charges under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and 18 U.S.C. § 1349

Based on these allegations, the government issued a superseding indictment charging the defendants with multiple counts of securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and conspiracy to commit securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349. 

Section 1348 is the primary statute for securities fraud, prohibiting schemes to defraud any person in connection with a security or schemes to obtain money or property through false representations. Section 1349 is the general conspiracy statute that applies to the commission of the underlying fraud. While one defendant, Daniel Knight, pleaded guilty to the charges, the remaining defendants moved to dismiss the indictment, challenging the legal sufficiency of the government's claims.

## District Court Ruling: Dismissal based on the lack of a traditional property interest

The district court granted the defendants' motion to dismiss, concluding that the indictment failed to state a cognizable offense. The court's reasoning rested on the definition of what constitutes a "property interest" in a fraud case. 

The district court determined that the indictment merely alleged the defendants sought to deprive their followers of "potentially valuable economic information." The court reasoned that since the federal fraud statutes are intended to protect traditional property interests—such as money or tangible assets—the deprivation of information alone was insufficient to sustain a criminal charge of fraud. Because the court viewed the alleged harm as the loss of information rather than the loss of actual property, it dismissed the indictment.

## Procedural Status: The government's appeal to the Fifth Circuit

The government timely appealed the district court's dismissal to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit. The central issue on appeal is whether the indictment was sufficient to state a crime. 

To resolve this, the Fifth Circuit must apply a *de novo* standard of review, meaning it examines the indictment from scratch without deferring to the district court's legal conclusions. The appellate court's task is to take the allegations in the indictment as true and determine if they enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offenses, fairly inform the defendants of the charges, and provide a basis for a double jeopardy defense. Specifically, the Fifth Circuit must decide if the "fraudulent-inducement" alleged by the government—tricking followers into spending money to buy securities—constitutes a deprivation of a protected property interest.
