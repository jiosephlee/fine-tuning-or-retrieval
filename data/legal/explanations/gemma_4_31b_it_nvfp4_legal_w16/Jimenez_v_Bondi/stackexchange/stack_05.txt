Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### What constitutes 'reasonable' internal relocation if the persecutor has national-level political ties?
Question:
The court suggests that because the petitioners spent three weeks in the Bogota countryside and their father still works for the government, relocation within Colombia is feasible. But if the persecutor (Don Rafa) is aligned with the sitting President of the country, doesn't that imply a level of reach that makes relocation anywhere in the country risky? I'm struggling to see how the court balances the 'reasonableness' of relocation against the political reach of the persecutor.
Answer:
***

### Short Answer
In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit balances the alleged political reach of a persecutor against the "reasonableness" of internal relocation by focusing on the **scope of the threats** and **empirical evidence of safety** rather than theoretical risks. The court found relocation feasible because the threats were localized to a specific neighborhood and the petitioners had already successfully resided in another area of the country without incident.

### Analysis
Under 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i), the government can rebut a claim of well-founded fear if it demonstrates that an applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of their home country. In this case, the court determined that internal relocation was reasonable based on several key factors:

**1. The Scope of the Threat**
The court placed significant weight on the specific nature of the persecutors' demands. The court noted that the men who threatened the petitioners told them to "move out of the neighborhood," not to "move out of the country." This indicated that the threat was geographically limited rather than a national mandate for the petitioners' removal or death regardless of their location within Colombia.

**2. Empirical Evidence of Safety**
The court looked to the petitioners' own history to determine feasibility. The record showed that the family had already lived with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before departing for the United States. Because they were able to reside there safely, the court viewed this as evidence that relocating within the country was a viable option.

**3. Government Action vs. Political Alignment**
While the petitioners argued that Don Rafa's alignment with the President made relocation risky, the court found this argument unsupported by the facts. Specifically, the court pointed out that the Colombian government was already aware of Don Rafa’s criminal activities and had "attempted several searches and seizures" of his property. This evidence countered the claim that Don Rafa possessed such absolute political protection that the government would be unable or unwilling to protect the petitioners in other parts of the country.

**4. Family Ties and Institutional Stability**
The court also noted that the petitioners' family members continued to live in Colombia unharmed and that Jimenez’s father remained employed within the criminal justice system. This suggested that the "political reach" of the persecutor did not extend to a level that rendered the entire national infrastructure—or the petitioners' immediate family—unsafe.

### Conclusion
For a law student analyzing this, the takeaway is that the court treats "reasonable relocation" as a fact-intensive inquiry. The court essentially ruled that a general allegation of a persecutor's national political ties is insufficient to overcome specific evidence that the threat is localized and that the government is actively engaging in law enforcement actions against that same persecutor.
