Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### At what point do threats and psychological harm cross the threshold from 'harassment' to 'persecution'?
Question:
In this case, the court acknowledged that the petitioners suffered death threats and a terrifying encounter with their child, but still ruled that this didn't amount to 'past persecution' because there was no lasting physical harm. If physical violence isn't strictly required, what specific markers is the court looking for to distinguish between 'psychologically unsettling' events and actual persecution?
Answer:
***

To understand the threshold between "harassment" (or mere psychological distress) and "persecution" under the Tenth Circuit's analysis in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, it is necessary to look at how the court defines the "infliction of suffering."

### The Legal Standard for Persecution
Because the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) does not define "persecution," the Tenth Circuit relies on case law to establish the boundary. The court holds that persecution requires the infliction of harm that is **"regarded as offensive"** and must **"entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty."** (*Wiransane v. Ashcroft*).

While the court does not look at incidents in isolation—instead considering the **cumulative effects** of multiple incidents—there are specific markers the court uses to determine if that cumulative effect reaches the level of persecution.

### The Role of Threats and Psychological Harm
The court distinguishes between events that are "psychologically unsettling" and those that constitute legal persecution using the following markers:

**1. Threats alone are insufficient.**
The court explicitly states that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution." (*Karki v. Holder*). Even repeated threats—such as the four telephone threats and one vague in-person threat in *Chhetri v. Rosen*—do not cross the threshold.

**2. Psychological harm is rarely a standalone basis for asylum.**
The court acknowledges that certain experiences can be "physically stressful or psychologically unsettling," but concludes that fears and emotional distress stemming from threats are generally insufficient. In *Jimenez*, the court noted it has "rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone."

**3. The "Offensiveness" and Severity Threshold.**
While physical harm is not strictly required to satisfy the persecution prong, the harm must be more than "mere restrictions or sporadic threats." (*Brandy v. Holder*). To illustrate this high bar, the court cited examples of harm that **did not** rise to the level of persecution:
*   A 10-hour arrest and detention combined with threats (*Brandy*).
*   Six robberies and threats made with machetes, where the threats were contingent on compliance with a demand (*Carias-Mejia v. Garland*).

### Application to the Case
In *Jimenez*, the petitioners suffered death threats and a "terrifying encounter" where a man grabbed their son and covered his mouth. Despite the psychological trauma of these events, the court found they did not amount to past persecution because:
*   The petitioners conceded there was **no lasting physical harm**.
*   The events, even when viewed cumulatively, remained in the category of being "psychologically unsettling" rather than "offensive" suffering that transcends mere threats.

**Summary:** For a law student analyzing this, the "marker" is not simply the *intensity* of the fear or the *terrifying nature* of the encounter, but whether the harm transcends "threats to life and liberty" to become an actual infliction of offensive suffering—typically evidenced by lasting physical harm or severe restrictions on liberty.
