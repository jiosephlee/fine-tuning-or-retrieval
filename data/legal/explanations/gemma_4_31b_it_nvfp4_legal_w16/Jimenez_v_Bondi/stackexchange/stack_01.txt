Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### How does the 'substantial evidence' standard of review affect an asylum appeal in the Tenth Circuit?
Question:
I'm confused about the standard of review used in Jimenez v. Bondi. The court mentions that findings of fact are reviewed under a 'substantial evidence' standard and are conclusive unless any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude otherwise. Does this mean the appellate court cannot reconsider the facts even if they find the IJ's logic flawed, as long as there is some evidence in the record to support the conclusion?
Answer:
***

**How does the 'substantial evidence' standard of review affect an asylum appeal in the Tenth Circuit?**

In the Tenth Circuit, the "substantial evidence" standard is highly deferential and prevents the appellate court from substituting its own judgment for that of the Immigration Judge (IJ) or the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA).

### The Legal Standard
As stated in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, findings of fact are reviewed under a substantial-evidence standard, meaning they are "conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary." 

This is a critical distinction from a *de novo* review. The court is not asking if the BIA's conclusion was the "best" or "most correct" interpretation of the facts, but rather whether the conclusion was reasonable based on the available record.

### Application to Your Question
To answer your specific question: **Yes, the appellate court will generally not reconsider the facts even if you believe the IJ's logic is flawed, provided there is evidence in the record that could support the conclusion.**

The court in *Jimenez* explicitly clarifies that it is "not [its] prerogative to reweigh the evidence." Instead, the court's only role is to decide if substantial evidence supports the decision. 

You can see this applied in the "Nexus" section of the opinion. The Petitioners argued that they were targeted because of their political opinions. While the record did contain evidence of political affiliations and some political threats, the IJ and BIA found that the *predominant* motive was actually a property dispute and Jimenez's professional involvement with "Don Rafa." 

The Tenth Circuit refused to "reweigh" the political evidence to see if it outweighed the property dispute evidence. Because the record contained facts that a reasonable adjudicator could use to conclude that the property dispute was the primary driver—specifically that the threats escalated only after the property dispute began—the court found "substantial evidence" to support the BIA's finding.

### Summary for the Law Student
If you are arguing an appeal under this standard, you cannot simply argue that the IJ "got it wrong" or that a different set of facts is more persuasive. To succeed, you must demonstrate that the record is so devoid of supporting evidence that **any** reasonable adjudicator would be **compelled** to reach a different result.
