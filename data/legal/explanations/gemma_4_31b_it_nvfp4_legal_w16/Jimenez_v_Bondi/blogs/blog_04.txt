Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Safe Havens or Danger Zones? The Feasibility of Internal Relocation

In the complex architecture of asylum law, the "internal relocation" analysis often serves as the final hurdle for petitioners who have otherwise established a fear of persecution. The question is deceptively simple: if a petitioner is unsafe in their hometown, is there another part of their home country where they could live safely? In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit recently reaffirmed a stringent approach to this inquiry, highlighting the difficult tension between localized threats and the perceived reach of powerful criminal organizations.

The case of the Jimenez family illustrates the steep climb asylum seekers face when their trauma is primarily psychological or based on threats rather than physical violence. The petitioners, Colombian nationals, alleged they were targeted by "Don Rafa," a narco-trafficking leader with ties to the current Colombian presidency. The conflict stemmed from a volatile mix of politics—the family supported the Liberal Party—and a legal dispute over a property used for trafficking and prostitution, which the lead petitioner, a provisional lawyer, had investigated.

The Tenth Circuit’s denial of the petition for review rested on three primary pillars: the lack of physical persecution, a missing "nexus" to a protected ground, and the feasibility of internal relocation. On the first two points, the court adhered to a strict reading of the law. It held that threats alone, even when repeated and frightening, do not constitute persecution without accompanying physical harm or extreme restrictions on liberty. More importantly, the court found that the "nexus" for the harm was not the petitioners' political opinion, but rather their interference with Don Rafa’s business interests. In the eyes of the court, the political affiliation was tangential; the real driver was a professional dispute.

However, the court's analysis of internal relocation is where the decision becomes most provocative. To deny asylum, the government must show that a petitioner could avoid future persecution by relocating within their own country. Here, the court pointed to two key facts: first, the family had lived safely in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before fleeing to the U.S.; second, the persecutors had specifically told the petitioners to leave their *neighborhood*, not the country.

From a doctrinal standpoint, the court’s reasoning is logically consistent with the administrative record. By focusing on the specific geography of the threats, the court treated the danger as a localized phenomenon. If the threat is "get out of this neighborhood," then moving to a different city or region is a reasonable solution.

Yet, this "localized" lens arguably clashes with the systemic reality of narco-trafficking organizations. The petitioners argued that Don Rafa’s alignment with the Colombian presidency meant that no part of the country was truly a safe haven. In many asylum contexts, the reach of a "cartel" or a politically connected gang is national, not municipal. By dismissing the petitioners' concerns about the president’s ties to the persecutors as "hypothetical," the Tenth Circuit reinforced a high evidentiary bar: petitioners must prove not just that a persecutor is powerful, but that their power is omnipresent and inescapable across every square inch of the national territory.

The downstream effects of *Jimenez* are significant. It signals that the Tenth Circuit is unlikely to infer a nationwide danger from the mere existence of political ties between a criminal actor and a head of state. Instead, the court requires a tighter, more evidence-based link between the persecutor's reach and the specific areas where the petitioner might relocate. 

Furthermore, the court's treatment of "government protection" serves as a warning to practitioners. The court noted that the petitioners failed to report the threats to the broader Colombian government, relying instead on a few conversations with unhelpful local officers. By emphasizing that a "one-off conversation" does not prove a systemic failure of the state, the court has reiterated that the failure to exhaust official police channels in the home country can be fatal to an asylum claim.

Ultimately, *Jimenez v. Bondi* underscores a rigorous application of the INA that prioritizes tangible physical harm and localized geography over systemic fears of political corruption. For those seeking refuge from powerful non-state actors, the "safe haven" required for internal relocation is becoming an increasingly narrow target to hit.
