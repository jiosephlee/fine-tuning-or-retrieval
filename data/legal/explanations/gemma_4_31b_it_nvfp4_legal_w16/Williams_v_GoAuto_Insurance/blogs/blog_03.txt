Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# The Certification Gamble: Federalism and the Reluctance to Ask State Courts

In the complex intersection of federal jurisdiction and state law, the process of "certification"—where a federal court asks a state's highest court to interpret an ambiguous statute—is often viewed by litigants as a vital safeguard. However, as the Fifth Circuit recently demonstrated in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, federal courts often view this tool not as a default safety valve, but as a scarce resource to be guarded. The decision to decline certification in this case underscores a persistent tension in our federalist system: the balance between judicial efficiency and the accuracy of state law application.

The dispute in *Williams* centered on the minutiae of Louisiana’s insurance-premium finance statutes. Under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550, a finance company must "certify" certain facts to an insurer before a policy can be canceled for nonpayment. The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto’s process—which relied on an automated email trigger and delayed the physical signing of documents until after the cancellation occurred—violated the "strict adherence" required by Louisiana law. Specifically, they contended that "certifying" implies a signature and that "receipt" requires physical possession.

The Fifth Circuit’s reasoning was rooted in a modern, functionalist interpretation of the text. Judge Southwick correctly noted that the statute does not explicitly require a signature. By refusing to read a signature requirement into the word "certifying," the court avoided adding a layer of bureaucracy not present in the legislative text. Similarly, the court’s treatment of "receipt" as encompassing a computer-to-computer "web service" call reflects a pragmatic acknowledgment that modern commerce has outpaced the traditional definitions found in dictionaries like Black’s.

However, the more significant legal question lies in the court's refusal to certify these interpretations to the Louisiana Supreme Court. The plaintiffs argued that because these terms were undefined and critical to the insurance industry, the state's highest court should provide the definitive word. The Fifth Circuit declined, citing a "compelling reason" standard and describing the availability of certification as an "important resource" that it would not "risk... going to too often."

This reluctance to certify creates a precarious doctrinal situation. When a federal court interprets an ambiguous state statute without the state court's guidance, it is essentially betting that its interpretation aligns with the state’s intent. If the Louisiana Supreme Court were to later rule that "certifying" does indeed require a signature, the Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Williams* would stand as a federal interpretation of state law that is fundamentally "wrong" by the state's own standards, yet binding on the parties involved.

The "compelling reason" threshold effectively raises the bar for certification so high that it is rarely cleared unless the state law is in a state of total chaos or the outcome of a massive class of cases depends on a single word. By treating certification as a finite resource, federal courts prioritize their own docket efficiency over the precision of state law application. 

Downstream, this approach may lead to a fragmented landscape where state laws are applied differently in state courts than in federal courts sitting in diversity. While the Fifth Circuit’s interpretation of GoAuto’s automated processes is logically sound and consistent with modern business practices, the refusal to certify means that the "strict adherence" mandated by Louisiana law is now being defined by a federal circuit rather than the sovereign state it governs. In the long run, this "certification gamble" may undermine the very predictability that the Louisiana statute sought to provide for insureds and insurers alike.
