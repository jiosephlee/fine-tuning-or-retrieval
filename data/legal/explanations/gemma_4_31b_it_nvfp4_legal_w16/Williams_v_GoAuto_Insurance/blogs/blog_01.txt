Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Strict Adherence is Not an Invitation to Legislate: Textualism in Insurance Cancellations

In the realm of insurance litigation, "strict adherence" to statutory notice requirements is usually a potent weapon for plaintiffs. When a statute mandates a specific procedure for cancelling a policy—particularly when a third-party finance company is exercising a power of attorney—courts typically view any deviation as a fatal flaw that renders the cancellation void. In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Fifth Circuit was asked to decide whether "strict adherence" to a Louisiana statute required a physical signature on a certification of default, even if the statute itself mentioned no such signature.

The court’s answer was a definitive no, providing a sharp reminder that "strict adherence" to a text is not a license for judges to read additional requirements into that text.

## The Conflict Between Process and Text

The case centered on Louisiana Rev. Stat. § 9:3550, which governs how an insurance-premium finance company (in this case, APAC) can request that an insurer (GoAuto) cancel a policy due to nonpayment. The statute requires the finance company to send the insurer a notice and a statement "certifying" several facts, including the existence of a valid power of attorney and that the default has not been cured.

The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto's process was defective because the certifications were generated automatically by a computer system and were not signed by a human being until *after* the policy had already been cancelled. They contended that the act of "certifying" inherently requires a signature to be valid.

Judge Southwick, writing for the court, rejected this interpretation. The court noted that while Louisiana jurisprudence emphasizes strict adherence to the statute's procedures, this means following the words on the page—not adding new ones. Because § 9:3550 does not explicitly require a signature, the court refused to imply one. This is a classic textualist move: the court declined to transform a general functional requirement ("certifying") into a specific formal requirement ("signing") in the absence of legislative direction.

## Modernizing "Receipt"

The court also tackled the definition of "receipt" in the digital age. The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto did not "receive" the cancellation requests because the process was handled by a web service—essentially a "listener" computer program—rather than a person taking physical possession of a document.

By relying on Black’s Law Dictionary, the Fifth Circuit concluded that a computer system’s "receipt" of an electronic call satisfies the statutory requirement. This is a pragmatic and necessary interpretation for modern commerce. If the court had required a human being to physically touch a piece of paper for a cancellation to be effective, it would have rendered the statute's own allowance for "electronic mail" almost meaningless in a high-volume corporate environment.

## Analytical Perspective: The "Imaginative" District Court

One of the more striking moments in the opinion is the court’s treatment of the district court's reasoning. The lower court had held that GoAuto complied with the law because the APAC employee who delivered the documents "neatly fits the definition of private carrier." 

The Fifth Circuit dismissed this logic as "imaginative and unconventional," essentially rebuking the district court for attempting to bend a legal definition (private carrier) to fit a set of facts just to reach a "correct" result. By pivoting away from the "private carrier" theory and focusing on the e-mail and web service, the Fifth Circuit restored a more disciplined approach to statutory interpretation.

## Doctrinal Implications

The downstream effect of *Williams* is a cautionary tale for litigants who rely on "strict adherence" standards. There is a common misconception that "strict adherence" allows a party to argue that a process was "too informal" or "too automated" to be legal. This ruling clarifies that "strict adherence" is a double-edged sword: it prevents companies from skipping statutory steps, but it also prevents plaintiffs from inventing new steps that the legislature never included.

For the insurance industry, this is a victory for automation. It confirms that as long as the substantive requirements of a notice statute are met, the move toward "headless" or automated compliance—where software interacts with software—will not be invalidated simply because a human signature is missing or delayed. The court has signaled that it will not treat the transition from paper to pixels as a gap in statutory compliance.
