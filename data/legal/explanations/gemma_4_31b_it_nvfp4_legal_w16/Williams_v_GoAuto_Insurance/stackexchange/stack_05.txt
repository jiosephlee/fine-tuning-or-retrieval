Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Does the term 'certifying' in a statute inherently imply a signature requirement?
Question:
The core of the dispute is whether the word 'certifying' in La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) requires a signature. The court finds that because the statute doesn't explicitly say 'signed,' a signature isn't required. Is this a general rule of statutory interpretation (that implied requirements are ignored if not explicit), or is this specific to insurance law?
Answer:
***

### Does the term 'certifying' in a statute inherently imply a signature requirement?

**Short Answer:** No. Based on the court's reasoning in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the term "certifying" does not inherently imply a signature requirement if the statute does not explicitly call for one. The court held that "strict adherence" to a statute prevents a court from adding requirements—such as a signature—that are not expressly written in the text.

**Detailed Explanation**

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the court addressed whether La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a), which requires a premium finance company to provide a "statement certifying" specific facts to cancel an insurance policy, necessitates a signature. 

The court's reasoning follows these primary points of statutory interpretation:

**1. Absence of Explicit Text**
The court began with the plain language of the statute, noting that the law "makes no explicit reference to a signature." Because the statute did not use the word "signed," the court determined that any requirement for a signature would have to be implied from the word "certifying."

**2. Rejection of Implied Requirements via "Strict Adherence"**
The plaintiffs argued that Louisiana law requires "strict adherence" to the procedures for cancelling financed insurance policies. However, the court interpreted this standard not as a mandate to imply additional safeguards, but as a limitation on judicial discretion. The court explicitly stated:
> " 'Strict adherence' to the procedures for cancellation is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures."

Essentially, the court reasoned that if the legislature intended for a signature to be a prerequisite for a valid certification, it would have included that requirement in the text.

**3. Analysis of Precedent**
The court reviewed Louisiana case law to see if "certifying" had been interpreted to require a signature in the past. It found that:
* Most cases merely referred to the requirement as a "statement certifying compliance" or a "statement from the premium finance company" without mentioning signatures.
* In *Benitez v. Elsayed*, a court mentioned a lack of signature as one of several shortcomings of a notice, but the court in *Williams* noted that *Benitez* did not hold that the absence of a signature would *by itself* disqualify a cancellation if the other statutory elements were present.

**Conclusion**
The court concluded that a company can "certify" information without a named employee being identified or a signature being affixed. Because the statute identifies "the insurance premium finance company" (the entity) as the party performing the certification, the court found that the electronic delivery of the certification satisfied the law.
