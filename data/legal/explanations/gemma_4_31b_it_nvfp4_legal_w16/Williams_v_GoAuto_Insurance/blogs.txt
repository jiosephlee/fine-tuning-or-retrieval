Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Strict Adherence is Not an Invitation to Legislate: Textualism in Insurance Cancellations

In the realm of insurance litigation, "strict adherence" to statutory notice requirements is usually a potent weapon for plaintiffs. When a statute mandates a specific procedure for cancelling a policy—particularly when a third-party finance company is exercising a power of attorney—courts typically view any deviation as a fatal flaw that renders the cancellation void. In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Fifth Circuit was asked to decide whether "strict adherence" to a Louisiana statute required a physical signature on a certification of default, even if the statute itself mentioned no such signature.

The court’s answer was a definitive no, providing a sharp reminder that "strict adherence" to a text is not a license for judges to read additional requirements into that text.

## The Conflict Between Process and Text

The case centered on Louisiana Rev. Stat. § 9:3550, which governs how an insurance-premium finance company (in this case, APAC) can request that an insurer (GoAuto) cancel a policy due to nonpayment. The statute requires the finance company to send the insurer a notice and a statement "certifying" several facts, including the existence of a valid power of attorney and that the default has not been cured.

The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto's process was defective because the certifications were generated automatically by a computer system and were not signed by a human being until *after* the policy had already been cancelled. They contended that the act of "certifying" inherently requires a signature to be valid.

Judge Southwick, writing for the court, rejected this interpretation. The court noted that while Louisiana jurisprudence emphasizes strict adherence to the statute's procedures, this means following the words on the page—not adding new ones. Because § 9:3550 does not explicitly require a signature, the court refused to imply one. This is a classic textualist move: the court declined to transform a general functional requirement ("certifying") into a specific formal requirement ("signing") in the absence of legislative direction.

## Modernizing "Receipt"

The court also tackled the definition of "receipt" in the digital age. The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto did not "receive" the cancellation requests because the process was handled by a web service—essentially a "listener" computer program—rather than a person taking physical possession of a document.

By relying on Black’s Law Dictionary, the Fifth Circuit concluded that a computer system’s "receipt" of an electronic call satisfies the statutory requirement. This is a pragmatic and necessary interpretation for modern commerce. If the court had required a human being to physically touch a piece of paper for a cancellation to be effective, it would have rendered the statute's own allowance for "electronic mail" almost meaningless in a high-volume corporate environment.

## Analytical Perspective: The "Imaginative" District Court

One of the more striking moments in the opinion is the court’s treatment of the district court's reasoning. The lower court had held that GoAuto complied with the law because the APAC employee who delivered the documents "neatly fits the definition of private carrier." 

The Fifth Circuit dismissed this logic as "imaginative and unconventional," essentially rebuking the district court for attempting to bend a legal definition (private carrier) to fit a set of facts just to reach a "correct" result. By pivoting away from the "private carrier" theory and focusing on the e-mail and web service, the Fifth Circuit restored a more disciplined approach to statutory interpretation.

## Doctrinal Implications

The downstream effect of *Williams* is a cautionary tale for litigants who rely on "strict adherence" standards. There is a common misconception that "strict adherence" allows a party to argue that a process was "too informal" or "too automated" to be legal. This ruling clarifies that "strict adherence" is a double-edged sword: it prevents companies from skipping statutory steps, but it also prevents plaintiffs from inventing new steps that the legislature never included.

For the insurance industry, this is a victory for automation. It confirms that as long as the substantive requirements of a notice statute are met, the move toward "headless" or automated compliance—where software interacts with software—will not be invalidated simply because a human signature is missing or delayed. The court has signaled that it will not treat the transition from paper to pixels as a gap in statutory compliance.



# From Filing Cabinets to API Calls: Redefining 'Receipt' for the Digital Age

In the world of insurance law, "strict adherence" to statutory notice requirements is often the difference between a valid policy cancellation and a multimillion-dollar bad faith claim. In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Fifth Circuit was asked to decide whether a digital-first cancellation process—driven by automated web services rather than signed papers—satisfied a Louisiana statute that seems to envision a world of physical documents and filing cabinets.

The case centers on the interaction between an insurance company (GoAuto) and a premium finance company (APAC). Under Louisiana law, when a policyholder defaults on their financing, the finance company can trigger a cancellation by sending the insurer a notice "certifying" specific facts. The insurer then cancels the policy "upon receipt" of that notice. The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto’s process was a legal fiction: the "certification" was unsigned at the moment of cancellation, and the "receipt" was merely an automated API call between two computers, not a physical delivery of a document.

The court’s reasoning on the "certification" requirement is a textbook example of judicial restraint in statutory interpretation. The plaintiffs argued that the word "certifying" implies a signature. However, the court noted that the statute itself never mentions a signature. While one lower court had previously mentioned a lack of signature as a flaw, that case involved a document that didn't even purport to certify the necessary facts. Here, the court held that "strict adherence" to a statute is not a license for courts to read additional requirements into the text. If the legislature wanted a signature, it should have required one.

More significant, however, is the court's treatment of "receipt." The plaintiffs leaned heavily on the traditional definition of "receipt" as taking "physical possession" of an object. This is the kind of argument that often succeeds in rigid jurisdictions: if the document isn't in a human's hand or a physical mailbox, it hasn't been "received."

The Fifth Circuit rejected this formalist approach in favor of a pragmatic, modern reading. By acknowledging that a "web service is like a listener" that performs an activity when called, the court effectively updated the definition of "possession" for the digital age. The court reasoned that in a modern statutory framework, an e-mail or an automated data transfer satisfies the requirement of receipt. This is a crucial distinction; the court refused to treat the law as a time capsule, insisting instead that "receipt" must be interpreted in the context of how current business is actually conducted.

Interestingly, the Fifth Circuit had to walk back some of the district court's more creative reasoning. The lower court had attempted to bridge the gap by labeling the APAC employee who delivered the documents as a "private carrier." Judge Southwick correctly dismissed this as "imaginative and unconventional." By abandoning the "private carrier" theory and focusing on the electronic transmission, the Fifth Circuit provided a much cleaner, more scalable precedent for the digital economy.

The doctrinal implications here are broad. As more business-to-business transactions migrate to APIs and automated triggers, courts will increasingly encounter "receipt" and "delivery" requirements written for the era of certified mail. *Williams* signals that the Fifth Circuit will not allow antiquated definitions of "physical possession" to invalidate modern automated processes, provided the substantive requirements of the statute are met. For practitioners, the takeaway is clear: while "strict adherence" remains the standard, that adherence is measured by the functional reality of the technology used, not by a nostalgic requirement for ink and paper.



# The Certification Gamble: Federalism and the Reluctance to Ask State Courts

In the complex intersection of federal jurisdiction and state law, the process of "certification"—where a federal court asks a state's highest court to interpret an ambiguous statute—is often viewed by litigants as a vital safeguard. However, as the Fifth Circuit recently demonstrated in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, federal courts often view this tool not as a default safety valve, but as a scarce resource to be guarded. The decision to decline certification in this case underscores a persistent tension in our federalist system: the balance between judicial efficiency and the accuracy of state law application.

The dispute in *Williams* centered on the minutiae of Louisiana’s insurance-premium finance statutes. Under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550, a finance company must "certify" certain facts to an insurer before a policy can be canceled for nonpayment. The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto’s process—which relied on an automated email trigger and delayed the physical signing of documents until after the cancellation occurred—violated the "strict adherence" required by Louisiana law. Specifically, they contended that "certifying" implies a signature and that "receipt" requires physical possession.

The Fifth Circuit’s reasoning was rooted in a modern, functionalist interpretation of the text. Judge Southwick correctly noted that the statute does not explicitly require a signature. By refusing to read a signature requirement into the word "certifying," the court avoided adding a layer of bureaucracy not present in the legislative text. Similarly, the court’s treatment of "receipt" as encompassing a computer-to-computer "web service" call reflects a pragmatic acknowledgment that modern commerce has outpaced the traditional definitions found in dictionaries like Black’s.

However, the more significant legal question lies in the court's refusal to certify these interpretations to the Louisiana Supreme Court. The plaintiffs argued that because these terms were undefined and critical to the insurance industry, the state's highest court should provide the definitive word. The Fifth Circuit declined, citing a "compelling reason" standard and describing the availability of certification as an "important resource" that it would not "risk... going to too often."

This reluctance to certify creates a precarious doctrinal situation. When a federal court interprets an ambiguous state statute without the state court's guidance, it is essentially betting that its interpretation aligns with the state’s intent. If the Louisiana Supreme Court were to later rule that "certifying" does indeed require a signature, the Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Williams* would stand as a federal interpretation of state law that is fundamentally "wrong" by the state's own standards, yet binding on the parties involved.

The "compelling reason" threshold effectively raises the bar for certification so high that it is rarely cleared unless the state law is in a state of total chaos or the outcome of a massive class of cases depends on a single word. By treating certification as a finite resource, federal courts prioritize their own docket efficiency over the precision of state law application. 

Downstream, this approach may lead to a fragmented landscape where state laws are applied differently in state courts than in federal courts sitting in diversity. While the Fifth Circuit’s interpretation of GoAuto’s automated processes is logically sound and consistent with modern business practices, the refusal to certify means that the "strict adherence" mandated by Louisiana law is now being defined by a federal circuit rather than the sovereign state it governs. In the long run, this "certification gamble" may undermine the very predictability that the Louisiana statute sought to provide for insureds and insurers alike.
