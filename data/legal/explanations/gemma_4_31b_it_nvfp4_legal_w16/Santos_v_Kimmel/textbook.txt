Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

The dispute in *Santos v. Kimmel* arises from a series of comedic segments aired on the late-night television show *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* (JKL). The plaintiff, former Congressman George Santos, alleged that Jimmy Kimmel used various fictitious identities to solicit personalized videos from him through Cameo, a platform where users pay for celebrity shout-outs. After Santos fulfilled these requests and provided the videos, Kimmel aired them on JKL as part of a mocking series titled “Will Santos Say It?” Santos subsequently filed suit, asserting copyright infringement and several state law claims, including breach of contract and fraudulent inducement.

The parties to this litigation include George Santos as the plaintiff and three defendants: Jimmy Kimmel, the American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (ABC), which airs JKL, and The Walt Disney Company, the parent company of ABC. By naming both the creator of the content and the corporate entities responsible for broadcasting it, Santos sought to hold the entire production chain liable for the use of his videos.

The case first proceeded in the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York. The defendants moved to dismiss the complaint under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. A Rule 12(b)(6) motion asserts that the plaintiff has failed to state a claim upon which relief can be granted; essentially, it argues that even if all the factual allegations in the complaint are true, there is no legal basis for a recovery. The District Court granted the motion, concluding that the copyright claims were barred by the fair use doctrine and that the state law claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act or otherwise failed to state a valid legal claim.

On appeal to the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, the court applied a *de novo* standard of review. In the context of a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal, *de novo* review means the appellate court examines the legal issues from scratch, giving no deference to the District Court’s legal conclusions. To conduct this review, the Second Circuit considered the facts alleged in the complaint, any exhibits attached to it, and documents incorporated by reference. Additionally, the court utilized judicial notice to verify copyright registrations as published in the Copyright Office’s registry, a procedural tool that allows the court to accept certain undisputed official facts as true without requiring formal evidence.

# Copyright Infringement and the Fair Use Doctrine

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the court addressed whether the unauthorized broadcasting of personalized videos on a late-night talk show constituted copyright infringement or was protected by the fair use doctrine. Because the defendants moved to dismiss the complaint under Rule 12(b)(6), the court had to determine if the fair use defense was so clearly established by the allegations in the complaint that the copyright claims could be barred even at the pleading stage.

## The four-factor test under 17 U.S.C. § 107

Fair use is an affirmative defense that allows for the use of copyrighted material without the owner's permission to avoid the "rigid application" of the Copyright Act, which might otherwise stifle creativity. To determine whether a use is "fair," courts apply a non-exclusive four-factor test codified in 17 U.S.C. § 107: (1) the purpose and character of the use; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.

## Factor 1: Purpose and character of the use (Transformative use and objective observer standard)

The first factor focuses primarily on whether the secondary use is "transformative"—meaning it adds something new, with a further purpose or different character, rather than merely superseding the original work. In this case, the court found the use highly transformative. While George Santos argued that the videos were not transformative because Kimmel "instigated" their creation, the court rejected this, noting that the purpose of the *use* on the show *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* (JKL) was to comment on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money.

Crucially, the court clarified that transformativeness is determined by an objective observer standard. Santos argued that his own purpose in making the videos was the same as Kimmel's (to show a willingness to say absurd things for money). However, the court held that the subjective intent of the copyright holder or the user is irrelevant. Instead, the court looked at what a reasonable observer would perceive. Because the original videos themselves conveyed "hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity," the use of those videos to mock Santos was fundamentally different from the original purpose and thus transformative.

## The role of 'good faith' and 'fair dealing' in fair use analysis

Santos attempted to defeat the fair use defense by arguing that Kimmel acted in bad faith by using fictitious names to deceive him into filming the videos. This argument relied on a statement from the Supreme Court in *Harper & Row* suggesting that fair use presupposes "good faith and fair dealing."

The Second Circuit rejected this argument, noting that the Supreme Court has since distanced itself from that requirement. More importantly, the court found that the "bad faith" in this instance did not result in the defendants attempting to "supplant" Santos’s commercially valuable right to the videos. Because the defendants were motivated by sarcastic criticism and commentary—core purposes of fair use—the deceptive nature of the solicitation did not nullify the defense.

## Factors 2 and 3: Nature of the copyrighted work and amount of the portion used

The court spent less time on the second and third factors, as they did not weigh heavily against a finding of fair use. Regarding the nature of the work, the court noted that the copyright registrations indicated the videos were published on the day they were created. Regarding the amount used, the court found that the use of the videos in the "Will Santos Say It?" segments was justified by the transformative purpose of the commentary.

## Factor 4: Market effect (Distinguishing between market substitution and critical devaluation)

The fourth factor examines whether the secondary use harms the potential market for the original work. The court highlighted a critical distinction here: fair use analysis does not ask if the secondary work *damages* the market for the original (for example, by making the original look ridiculous through parody), but whether it *usurps* the market by acting as a "competing substitute."

In this case, the court found no market harm. Kimmel’s segments were not substitutes for personalized Cameo videos; no one would watch a mocking late-night comedy segment instead of purchasing a personalized greeting. Because the devaluation of the videos resulted from criticism and lampooning rather than market substitution, the fourth factor supported a finding of fair use.

## Applying fair use at the pleading stage

Typically, affirmative defenses are decided after discovery. However, the court affirmed that if the facts alleged in a complaint (and documents incorporated by reference) make the fair use defense "so clearly established," a court may dismiss a copyright infringement claim under Rule 12(b)(6). Because the nature of the JKL segments and the original videos were clear from the pleadings, the court concluded that fair use barred the claims as a matter of law without the need for further litigation.

# State Law Contractual Claims

After addressing the copyright infringement claims, the court turned to the state law claims brought by George Santos, specifically those involving breach of contract and breach of implied contract. These claims were dismissed at the district court level and affirmed on appeal, providing a study in the requirements of contractual privity, the standards for pleading implied contracts, and the necessity of mutual assent.

## Copyright Act Preemption of State Law Claims

The District Court initially dismissed Santos’s breach of contract and implied contract claims on the grounds that they were preempted by the Copyright Act. Preemption occurs when a federal statute, such as the Copyright Act, overrides a state law claim because the state claim effectively seeks the same remedy for the same right that the federal law governs. Santos argued that his claims were "qualitatively different" from a copyright claim and therefore should not be preempted. However, the Second Circuit declined to resolve this technical legal question. The court determined that the state law claims were insufficient as a matter of law regardless of whether they were preempted, meaning they failed to meet basic contractual requirements under state law.

## Direct Breach of Contract: Third-Party Beneficiary Status under Illinois Law

Santos alleged that the defendants breached the Terms of Service (ToS) of the Cameo platform. Because the ToS are governed by Illinois law, the court applied Illinois legal standards to determine if Santos had the standing to sue for their breach. A primary issue here is privity—the legal relationship between the parties to a contract. Santos was not a party to the agreement between the user (Kimmel) and the platform (Cameo).

Under Illinois law, there is a strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties. For a third party to enforce a contract, the implication that the contract applies to them must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration. The court found that the Cameo ToS contained no such declaration for Santos. In fact, the court noted that other sections of the ToS *did* contain explicit third-party beneficiary language, which demonstrated that when the parties intended to allow third parties to enforce a provision, they did so expressly. Because there was no express intent to benefit Santos in the relevant provisions, his direct breach of contract claim failed.

## Implied-in-Fact Contracts under New York Law

Unable to rely on the written Terms of Service, Santos also argued that an "implied-in-fact" contract existed between him and the defendants. Unlike an express contract, an implied-in-fact contract is formed by the conduct of the parties rather than written or spoken words. This specific claim was governed by New York law.

To survive a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6), a plaintiff must allege more than mere conclusions; they must plead the essential terms of the agreement in nonconclusory language. The court found that Santos’s complaint failed this pleading standard. He did not specify the essential terms of the putative contract or the specific provisions that the defendants allegedly breached. Because the complaint lacked these factual specifics, it failed to state a plausible claim for an implied-in-fact contract.

## The 'Meeting of the Minds' Requirement for Contractual Agreements

Beyond the pleading of terms, the court addressed the fundamental requirement of any contract: the "meeting of the minds." This refers to mutual assent, where both parties agree to the same essential terms at the same time. 

The court found no evidence in the complaint that there was a meeting of the minds between Santos and Kimmel regarding the Terms of Service or any other binding agreement. Specifically, there was no indication that Kimmel had agreed to adhere to the platform's Terms of Service in a manner that created a binding contractual obligation toward Santos. Without a demonstrated agreement or mutual assent, the court concluded that no enforceable contract existed, leading to the affirmation of the dismissal.

# Fraudulent Inducement and Final Disposition

## Elements of a fraudulent inducement claim in New York

In addition to his copyright and contract claims, George Santos alleged that the defendants engaged in fraudulent inducement. This claim arose from the fact that Jimmy Kimmel used fictitious names to deceive Santos into recording the personalized videos on the Cameo platform. Under New York law, which governed this specific claim, a plaintiff must meet strict pleading requirements to survive a motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim.

## The 'out-of-pocket loss' requirement for fraud recovery

A critical element of a fraudulent inducement claim in New York is the requirement that the plaintiff suffer an actual financial loss. The court emphasizes the "out-of-pocket loss" rule, which stipulates that a plaintiff cannot recover damages for fraud unless they can demonstrate that they suffered a tangible economic loss as a direct result of the fraud. This means the plaintiff must show that they gave up something of value or paid money they would not have otherwise paid, resulting in a net loss.

## Analysis of why the complaint failed to allege actual loss

In applying this standard to the facts, the Second Circuit affirmed the District Court’s dismissal of Santos's fraudulent inducement claim. While Santos alleged that he was deceived by Kimmel's use of fake identities to procure the videos, the complaint failed to allege any corresponding out-of-pocket loss. 

Because the videos were created through the Cameo platform—where Santos was paid to provide the content—the court found no evidence that Santos had suffered an actual financial detriment. The mere fact that he was tricked into performing a service does not constitute a legal "loss" if he was compensated for that service and did not expend his own funds in reliance on the misrepresentation. Consequently, the claim failed to state a viable cause of action under New York law.

## Final affirmation of the District Court's judgment

The court's analysis of the fraudulent inducement claim served as the final piece of the broader litigation. Having already determined that the copyright infringement claims were barred by the fair use doctrine—specifically because the use of the videos was transformative and did not usurp the market for the original works—and that the contract claims were either preempted by federal law or lacked essential terms and standing, the court reached its final conclusion. 

Finding that all of Santos's claims—including the claim for fraudulent inducement—failed to state a claim for relief, the Second Circuit affirmed the District Court's final judgment in its entirety.
