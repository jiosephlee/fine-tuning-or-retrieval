Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Terms of Service and the Third-Party Beneficiary Trap

The Second Circuit recently handed down a decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* that, while providing ample fodder for late-night comedians, offers a sobering lesson in contract law for the modern "creator economy." The case arose after Jimmy Kimmel used fictitious names to commission personalized videos from George Santos via the Cameo platform, only to air those videos on his show to mock Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. While the court’s analysis of fair use is a predictable victory for satirists, the ruling on the contract claims exposes a dangerous legal blind spot for creators who rely on platform-level agreements for protection.

The copyright portion of the opinion is straightforward: the court found that Kimmel’s use of the videos was transformative. By shifting the purpose of the videos from "positivity and encouragement" to a commentary on Santos’s character, the use fell squarely within the bounds of fair use. The court notably rejected Santos’s argument that Kimmel’s deceptive tactics—using fake names to procure the videos—nullified the fair use defense. In doing so, the court reaffirmed that "bad faith" in the procurement of a work does not necessarily strip a user of the fair use defense, particularly when the resulting work is a piece of social commentary.

However, the more doctrinally significant aspect of the ruling lies in the dismissal of Santos’s breach of contract claims. Santos attempted to sue Kimmel for violating Cameo’s Terms of Service (ToS). The problem, as the court pointed out, is a fundamental one of privity: the contract was between the user (Kimmel) and the platform (Cameo). Santos was not a party to that agreement. To recover, Santos had to establish that he was a third-party beneficiary—a legal hurdle that is notoriously high in both Illinois and New York.

Under Illinois law, which governed the ToS, there is a "strong presumption" against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties. The court noted that for a third party to enforce a contract, the intent to do so must be so strong as to be "practically an express declaration." Because Cameo’s ToS did not explicitly state that creators could sue users for breaches of the terms, Santos was left as a legal stranger to the agreement. This creates a "third-party beneficiary trap": creators often operate under the assumption that a platform’s "community guidelines" or "terms of use" act as a protective shield for everyone in the ecosystem, when in reality, those terms are designed primarily to protect the platform itself.

The court’s refusal to find an implied-in-fact contract further closes the door on this strategy. To establish such a contract under New York law, a plaintiff must show a "meeting of the minds" and specific essential terms. The court found that Santos failed to allege any such agreement between himself and Kimmel. Furthermore, his claim for fraudulent inducement failed because he could not demonstrate an actual "out-of-pocket loss," a strict requirement under New York law that prevents plaintiffs from recovering based on the "benefit of the bargain" alone.

The downstream implications of *Santos v. Kimmel* are significant for the broader gig economy. Whether it is a creator on Cameo, a freelancer on Upwork, or an artist on Patreon, the takeaway is clear: do not assume that the platform's Terms of Service provide you with a private right of action against a bad actor. If a creator wants the ability to sue a user for a breach of specific conduct rules, they cannot rely on the platform's boilerplate agreement; they must establish their own independent contractual relationship or insist that the platform include an express third-party beneficiary clause. Until then, the "terms" that creators rely on for safety are often legally invisible when they actually need them.
