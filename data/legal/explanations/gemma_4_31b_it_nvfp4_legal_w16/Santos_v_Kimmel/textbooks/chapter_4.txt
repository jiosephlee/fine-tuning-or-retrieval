Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 4: # Fraudulent Inducement and Final Disposition

## Elements of a fraudulent inducement claim in New York

In addition to his copyright and contract claims, George Santos alleged that the defendants engaged in fraudulent inducement. This claim arose from the fact that Jimmy Kimmel used fictitious names to deceive Santos into recording the personalized videos on the Cameo platform. Under New York law, which governed this specific claim, a plaintiff must meet strict pleading requirements to survive a motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim.

## The 'out-of-pocket loss' requirement for fraud recovery

A critical element of a fraudulent inducement claim in New York is the requirement that the plaintiff suffer an actual financial loss. The court emphasizes the "out-of-pocket loss" rule, which stipulates that a plaintiff cannot recover damages for fraud unless they can demonstrate that they suffered a tangible economic loss as a direct result of the fraud. This means the plaintiff must show that they gave up something of value or paid money they would not have otherwise paid, resulting in a net loss.

## Analysis of why the complaint failed to allege actual loss

In applying this standard to the facts, the Second Circuit affirmed the District Court’s dismissal of Santos's fraudulent inducement claim. While Santos alleged that he was deceived by Kimmel's use of fake identities to procure the videos, the complaint failed to allege any corresponding out-of-pocket loss. 

Because the videos were created through the Cameo platform—where Santos was paid to provide the content—the court found no evidence that Santos had suffered an actual financial detriment. The mere fact that he was tricked into performing a service does not constitute a legal "loss" if he was compensated for that service and did not expend his own funds in reliance on the misrepresentation. Consequently, the claim failed to state a viable cause of action under New York law.

## Final affirmation of the District Court's judgment

The court's analysis of the fraudulent inducement claim served as the final piece of the broader litigation. Having already determined that the copyright infringement claims were barred by the fair use doctrine—specifically because the use of the videos was transformative and did not usurp the market for the original works—and that the contract claims were either preempted by federal law or lacked essential terms and standing, the court reached its final conclusion. 

Finding that all of Santos's claims—including the claim for fraudulent inducement—failed to state a claim for relief, the Second Circuit affirmed the District Court's final judgment in its entirety.
