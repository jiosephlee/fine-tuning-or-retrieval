Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# The 'Reasonable Observer' and the Art of the Prank: Transformative Use in Santos v. Kimmel

The Second Circuit recently handed down a decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* that serves as a sharp reminder of the resilience of the fair use doctrine when applied to political satire. While the facts of the case—former Congressman George Santos suing Jimmy Kimmel for using "trickery" to obtain Cameo videos for a mocking late-night segment—are predictably comedic, the legal implications are substantive. The court's ruling reinforces a critical shift in copyright jurisprudence: the "transformative" nature of a work is determined by the perspective of the reasonable observer, not the subjective intentions of the creator or the deceptive tactics of the user.

The core of the dispute centered on whether Kimmel’s "Will Santos Say It?" segments constituted fair use of the videos Santos recorded. Santos argued that the use was not transformative because Kimmel, by posing as various fictitious personas to request the videos, essentially "designed" the original works. Under this theory, Kimmel wasn't transforming the videos but rather executing a plan he had already authored. However, the Second Circuit rejected this approach, leaning heavily on the Supreme Court’s reasoning in *Andy Warhol Foundation for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*.

The court clarified that the "purpose and character" of a use is not an exercise in mind-reading or a search for the "designer's" intent. Instead, the court asks what a reasonable observer would perceive. While Santos may have intended the videos to convey "hope, strength, and positivity," the reasonable observer watching *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* saw something entirely different: a commentary on a former politician's willingness to say absurd things for money. By shifting the focus from the internal intent of the parties to the external perception of the audience, the court reaffirmed that satire and commentary often derive their transformative power precisely by stripping a work of its original intent and repurposing it for a critical end.

Equally significant is the court's treatment of "bad faith." Santos attempted to nullify the fair use defense by pointing to Kimmel's use of false representations to solicit the videos. In earlier copyright jurisprudence, specifically *Harper & Row*, the Supreme Court suggested that fair use presupposes "good faith and fair dealing." The Second Circuit, however, noted that the Court has since distanced itself from that requirement. The ruling suggests that as long as the resulting use is transformative and does not seek to "supplant" the market for the original work, the deceptive means used to acquire the content are largely irrelevant to the fair use analysis. This is a potent takeaway for media defendants; it suggests that the "how" of acquisition does not necessarily taint the "why" of the subsequent use.

The court also swiftly dispatched the market harm argument. Applying the standard from *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.*, the court noted that a parody or lampoon does not "usurp" the market for the original work by offering a substitute. There is no plausible world in which a consumer seeks out a Jimmy Kimmel roast as a replacement for a personalized, positive Cameo video.

Beyond copyright, the decision underscores the difficulty of asserting contract claims when the plaintiff is not a party to the agreement. Santos’s attempt to sue for breach of the Cameo Terms of Service failed because he was not a signatory to the contract between the user and the platform. The court's refusal to recognize Santos as a third-party beneficiary—absent an express declaration in the terms—serves as a cautionary tale regarding the limits of platform-based "protections" for creators.

Ultimately, *Santos v. Kimmel* solidifies the *Warhol* framework in the Second Circuit, prioritizing the objective "purpose" of a work over the subjective "intent" of its participants. For the media and the arts, it ensures that the "reasonable observer" remains the primary lens through which the boundaries of free expression and copyright are viewed, even when the content in question is the result of a prank.



# Terms of Service and the Third-Party Beneficiary Trap

The Second Circuit recently handed down a decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* that, while providing ample fodder for late-night comedians, offers a sobering lesson in contract law for the modern "creator economy." The case arose after Jimmy Kimmel used fictitious names to commission personalized videos from George Santos via the Cameo platform, only to air those videos on his show to mock Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. While the court’s analysis of fair use is a predictable victory for satirists, the ruling on the contract claims exposes a dangerous legal blind spot for creators who rely on platform-level agreements for protection.

The copyright portion of the opinion is straightforward: the court found that Kimmel’s use of the videos was transformative. By shifting the purpose of the videos from "positivity and encouragement" to a commentary on Santos’s character, the use fell squarely within the bounds of fair use. The court notably rejected Santos’s argument that Kimmel’s deceptive tactics—using fake names to procure the videos—nullified the fair use defense. In doing so, the court reaffirmed that "bad faith" in the procurement of a work does not necessarily strip a user of the fair use defense, particularly when the resulting work is a piece of social commentary.

However, the more doctrinally significant aspect of the ruling lies in the dismissal of Santos’s breach of contract claims. Santos attempted to sue Kimmel for violating Cameo’s Terms of Service (ToS). The problem, as the court pointed out, is a fundamental one of privity: the contract was between the user (Kimmel) and the platform (Cameo). Santos was not a party to that agreement. To recover, Santos had to establish that he was a third-party beneficiary—a legal hurdle that is notoriously high in both Illinois and New York.

Under Illinois law, which governed the ToS, there is a "strong presumption" against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties. The court noted that for a third party to enforce a contract, the intent to do so must be so strong as to be "practically an express declaration." Because Cameo’s ToS did not explicitly state that creators could sue users for breaches of the terms, Santos was left as a legal stranger to the agreement. This creates a "third-party beneficiary trap": creators often operate under the assumption that a platform’s "community guidelines" or "terms of use" act as a protective shield for everyone in the ecosystem, when in reality, those terms are designed primarily to protect the platform itself.

The court’s refusal to find an implied-in-fact contract further closes the door on this strategy. To establish such a contract under New York law, a plaintiff must show a "meeting of the minds" and specific essential terms. The court found that Santos failed to allege any such agreement between himself and Kimmel. Furthermore, his claim for fraudulent inducement failed because he could not demonstrate an actual "out-of-pocket loss," a strict requirement under New York law that prevents plaintiffs from recovering based on the "benefit of the bargain" alone.

The downstream implications of *Santos v. Kimmel* are significant for the broader gig economy. Whether it is a creator on Cameo, a freelancer on Upwork, or an artist on Patreon, the takeaway is clear: do not assume that the platform's Terms of Service provide you with a private right of action against a bad actor. If a creator wants the ability to sue a user for a breach of specific conduct rules, they cannot rely on the platform's boilerplate agreement; they must establish their own independent contractual relationship or insist that the platform include an express third-party beneficiary clause. Until then, the "terms" that creators rely on for safety are often legally invisible when they actually need them.



# Deception Without Damage: The Out-of-Pocket Requirement for Fraud in New York

In the courtroom of public opinion, the facts of *Santos v. Kimmel* might look like a textbook case of a "scam." Jimmy Kimmel used fictitious identities to trick former Congressman George Santos into filming a series of Cameo videos, which Kimmel then aired on his late-night show to mock the former lawmaker’s willingness to say anything for a fee. While the Second Circuit’s decision to affirm the dismissal of this suit covers a broad range of copyright and contract issues, the disposition of the fraudulent inducement claim offers a sharp reminder of the gap between moral deception and legal injury under New York law.

The core of the dispute regarding the fraud claim is not whether Kimmel lied—the complaint essentially concedes that he did—but whether that lie resulted in a cognizable legal harm. Santos alleged that he was fraudulently induced into recording the videos. However, the Second Circuit, adhering to New York precedent, held that the complaint failed to allege any actual out-of-pocket loss. This is a critical distinction in the law of fraud: being the victim of a lie is not, by itself, a cause of action for damages.

The court relied on the strict standard articulated in *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, which requires a plaintiff to demonstrate a concrete financial loss. In the context of fraudulent inducement, New York follows the "out-of-pocket" rule rather than the "benefit-of-the-bargain" rule. Under the latter, a plaintiff might sue for the difference between the value of what they were promised and the value of what they actually received. But New York’s out-of-pocket rule is more exacting; it requires the plaintiff to show that they are actually worse off financially because of the fraud.

In this case, the irony is that Santos was actually paid for the videos through the Cameo platform. Because he received payment for his services and did not allege that he spent more money to produce the videos than he earned from them, he suffered no "out-of-pocket" loss. The court’s reasoning is logically sound: if a party is paid to perform a task and completes that task, the fact that the payer had a secret, mocking motive for the request does not translate into a financial loss for the performer.

This ruling underscores a rigorous doctrinal boundary in New York tort law. By refusing to allow a fraud claim to proceed without a showing of actual loss, the court prevents the "fraud" label from becoming a catch-all for any deceptive behavior that causes emotional distress or reputational harm. If Santos wanted to recover for the mockery or the deceptive nature of the requests, he would have had to plead a different theory of liability—though, as the court noted, his other state law claims were similarly deficient.

The downstream effect of this decision is a reinforcement of the high pleading bar for fraud in the Second Circuit. Litigants cannot simply allege a "scheme" or a "lie" to survive a motion to dismiss; they must map out a specific financial deficit. For practitioners, *Santos v. Kimmel* serves as a cautionary tale that in the eyes of the New York courts, a deceptive bargain is not necessarily a legal injury, especially when the plaintiff is paid to participate in the deception.
