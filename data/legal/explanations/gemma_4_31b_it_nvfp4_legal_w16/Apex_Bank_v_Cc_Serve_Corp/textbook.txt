Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Case Overview and Procedural Posture

To understand the resolution of *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, one must first understand the roles of the parties and the specific procedural path a trademark application takes before it reaches a federal appellate court. This case centers on a dispute over the registration of marks that the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) found to be confusingly similar.

## The Parties: CC Serve Corp. (Opposer) and Apex Bank (Applicant)

The dispute involves two entities operating in the financial services sector. CC Serve Corp. is the "Opposer," meaning it is the party seeking to prevent another's mark from being registered. CC Serve owns a federal registration for the word mark "ASPIRE," which it has used since at least 1996. CC Serve’s business model involves partnering with banks to issue "ASPIRE"-branded credit cards and managing the associated accounts.

Apex Bank is the "Applicant," a retail bank based in Tennessee. While Apex Bank provides traditional retail services—such as checking and savings accounts, mortgages, and loans—it does not currently offer credit cards. However, Apex Bank sought to expand its reach by launching an internet bank under the brand "ASPIRE BANK."

## Comparing the Marks and Scope of Services

The legal conflict arises from the overlap between the marks and the services they represent. CC Serve’s mark is the single word "ASPIRE," used specifically for credit card services. Apex Bank sought to register both word and design marks for "ASPIRE BANK" to cover "banking and financing services." 

In trademark law, the "scope of services" is critical because a mark does not receive exclusive rights to a word in every possible context, but rather in connection with specific goods or services. Here, the tension lies in whether "credit card services" (CC Serve) and "banking and financing services" (Apex Bank) are sufficiently similar that a consumer would believe they come from the same source.

## Procedural Timeline: From Application to the TTAB

The path to litigation began when Apex Bank filed "intent-to-use" (ITU) applications with the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO). An ITU application allows a party to reserve a mark before they have actually started using it in commerce. During the prosecution of these applications, CC Serve submitted a "letter of protest," which is an informal way to alert the USPTO examining attorney that a proposed mark may conflict with an existing registration.

Despite the protest, the examining attorney approved the "ASPIRE BANK" marks for publication. Once a mark is published, other parties have a window to file a formal "opposition" to the registration. CC Serve initiated this opposition in February 2020, arguing that there was a likelihood of confusion between its "ASPIRE" mark and Apex’s "ASPIRE BANK" marks under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act. 

Section 2(d) is the primary mechanism for refusing a trademark registration when a prior user’s mark is so similar that it is likely to cause consumer confusion. After reviewing the evidence, the TTAB sustained the opposition, concluding that confusion was likely and refusing the registration of Apex Bank's marks.

## Jurisdiction of the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit

Following the TTAB's adverse decision, Apex Bank appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. Because the TTAB is an administrative body within the USPTO, its decisions are reviewed by the courts. 

The Federal Circuit has specialized jurisdiction over these matters under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B), which grants it the authority to hear appeals from the TTAB. The court's role in this appeal is not to restart the trial, but to determine if the Board’s legal conclusions were correct (reviewed *de novo*) and if its findings of fact were supported by "substantial evidence."

# The Legal Framework for Likelihood of Confusion

## Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act: The 'Likelihood of Confusion' Standard

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the dispute centered on whether Apex Bank could register its "ASPIRE BANK" marks given the prior registration of the word mark "ASPIRE" by CC Serve Corp. The legal basis for the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board's (the Board) refusal to register the marks is Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d). Under this section, a trademark registration must be refused if a "likelihood of confusion" exists because an applicant and a prior user are using similar marks on their respective goods or services.

The core of the inquiry is whether the concurrent use of the marks would lead consumers to be mistaken about the source of the services. In this case, the Board determined that consumers would likely be confused between CC Serve’s credit card services and Apex Bank’s proposed banking and financing services, leading it to sustain CC Serve's opposition to Apex Bank's application.

## The DuPont Factors: The Multi-Factor Test for Determining Confusion

To determine whether a likelihood of confusion exists, courts and the Board utilize a multi-factor test known as the *DuPont* factors. While there are thirteen factors in total, the court clarifies that the Board does not need to consider every single one; it only needs to address those that are "relevant and of record." 

The *DuPont* analysis creates a specific legal bridge: the individual factors are treated as findings of fact, but the ultimate determination of "likelihood of confusion" is a question of law based on those factual findings. In *Apex Bank*, the court focused on three specific factors:
*   **The First Factor:** The similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties regarding appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression.
*   **The Second Factor:** The similarity of the parties' goods or services, including whether the products are related or marketed in a way that suggests they emanate from the same source.
*   **The Sixth Factor:** The number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods, which helps determine if a mark is "weak" (entitled to a narrow scope of protection) or "strong."

## Standards of Review: De Novo Review for Legal Conclusions vs. Substantial Evidence for Findings of Fact

When a case is appealed from the Board to the Federal Circuit, the court applies two different standards of review depending on whether it is examining a legal conclusion or a factual finding.

Legal conclusions—such as the ultimate determination of whether there is a likelihood of confusion—are reviewed *de novo*. This means the appellate court examines the issue from scratch, giving no deference to the Board's legal reasoning. In contrast, the Board's findings of fact regarding the individual *DuPont* factors are reviewed under the "substantial evidence" standard. Substantial evidence is defined as relevant evidence that a "reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." 

This distinction is critical in *Apex Bank*. For example, the court used the substantial evidence standard to affirm the Board's factual finding that the services were highly similar (the second factor), but applied *de novo* review to determine that the Board had committed a legal error in how it defined the scope of similar marks for the sixth factor.

## The Role of the Board in Selecting Relevant DuPont Factors

The Board possesses discretion in deciding which *DuPont* factors are necessary to reach a conclusion. As noted in the opinion, the Board is not required to engage in a mechanical analysis of all thirteen factors. Instead, it identifies the factors most pertinent to the specific facts of the case. 

In this matter, the Board focused its analysis on the similarity of the marks (Factor 1), the similarity of the services (Factor 2), and the presence of third-party marks (Factor 6). The court's review focused on whether the Board applied the legal standards correctly within those selected factors. The court emphasized that the Board must remain consistent across these factors; for instance, if the Board finds services are "highly similar" under the second factor, it cannot then apply a more restrictive or narrow definition of "similarity" when evaluating third-party marks under the sixth factor.

# Analysis of the Second DuPont Factor: Similarity of Services

In trademark disputes, the determination of whether a likelihood of confusion exists is governed by a set of factors known as the *DuPont* factors. This chapter focuses on the court's analysis of the second *DuPont* factor: the similarity of the parties' goods or services. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the court affirmed the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board's finding that the services provided by the two parties were highly similar, creating a significant risk that consumers would confuse the source of the services.

## Defining 'Similarity': The Standard for Related Goods and Services

Under the second *DuPont* factor, the court does not require the services in question to be identical to find a likelihood of confusion. The legal standard is broader: the evidence must simply establish that the respective products are "related in some manner" or that the circumstances of their marketing are such that they could lead a consumer to the "mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source." The core inquiry is whether the services are sufficiently related that a consumer would logically assume they are offered by the same entity.

## The Board's Interpretation of 'Credit Card Services' and 'Banking Services'

In this case, the Board had to compare CC Serve’s registration for "credit card services" with Apex Bank’s application for "banking and financing services." Because the term "credit card services" had been deleted from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve's registration, the Board first had to define the scope of those services. The Board determined that "credit card services" encompassed a wide array of activities, including issuing cards to finance purchases, processing payments, managing accounts, and providing access to credit scores.

The Board then compared this definition to the services proposed by Apex Bank. By looking to dictionary definitions, the Board found that the terms "banking," "bank," and "finance" all encompass the act of extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards. 

## The Concept of Services Being 'Legally Identical, In Part'

Based on the overlapping dictionary definitions, the Board reached a critical legal conclusion: the services provided by Apex Bank and CC Serve were "legally identical, in part." This means that the scope of "banking and financing services" is broad enough to encompass the specific activities that constitute "credit card services." When services are found to be even partially identical, the degree of similarity is considered very high, which weighs heavily in favor of a finding of likelihood of confusion.

## The Use of Third-Party Registrations to Establish Common Sources

To support the finding that these services are related, the Board looked at third-party registrations. The Board identified other trademarks that covered both credit card services and general banking and financing services under a single mark. This evidence is used to demonstrate that, in the actual marketplace, these types of services frequently "emanate from a single source." By showing that other companies offer both sets of services under one brand, the Board established a factual basis for the conclusion that consumers would expect a bank and a credit card service provider to potentially be the same entity.

## Why Apex Bank's Argument Regarding CC Serve's Business Model Was Rejected

Apex Bank attempted to distinguish the services by arguing that they were not directly competitive. Specifically, Apex pointed out that CC Serve does not provide traditional banking services itself; instead, it partners with banks to manage credit card programs. Apex argued that this distinction in business models meant the services were not the same.

The court found this argument unavailing. The focus of the second *DuPont* factor is not whether the parties are direct competitors or whether their business models are identical, but whether the *services* are related enough to cause consumer confusion. Because the Board's findings regarding the nature of the services were supported by substantial evidence—including dictionary definitions and third-party registrations—the court affirmed that the services were highly similar regardless of CC Serve's specific partnering arrangements.

# Analysis of the Sixth DuPont Factor: Third-Party Use and Commercial Strength

In trademark disputes, courts and the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) use the "DuPont factors" to determine whether there is a likelihood of confusion between two marks. One of the most critical components of this analysis is the sixth DuPont factor, which examines the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods or services. This chapter examines how the Federal Circuit in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* corrected a legal error in the Board's application of this factor.

## The Purpose of the Sixth Factor: Assessing Commercial Strength and the 'Crowded Field' Theory

The primary objective of the sixth DuPont factor is to determine the "commercial strength" of a mark. In trademark law, the strength of a mark dictates the scope of protection it receives. A mark that is unique in its field is considered strong and is entitled to a broader scope of protection. Conversely, if many other parties use similar marks on similar goods, the mark is considered "weak" and is entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.

This is known as the "crowded field" theory. The reasoning behind this theory is that when consumers are exposed to numerous similar marks within a specific industry, they become more adept at distinguishing between them. In a crowded field, consumers are less likely to be confused by a new, similar mark because they have been "educated" to look for small differences to identify the source of the goods or services. Therefore, evidence of extensive third-party use of a similar mark can be a powerful tool for an applicant to argue that a prior registration should not block their own mark.

## The Board's Narrow Definition of the 'Relevant Public'

In *Apex Bank*, the applicant, Apex Bank, attempted to demonstrate that the mark "ASPIRE" was commercially weak by presenting evidence of 42 third-party marks using "Aspire" or "Aspire-formative" terms within the financial services industry. However, the Board disregarded the majority of this evidence. 

The Board limited its analysis to a very narrow "relevant public": consumers of "credit card services." By restricting the scope to this specific niche, the Board excluded marks used for broader banking and financing services. This resulted in the Board counting only nine similar marks. The Board then concluded that nine marks were not "considerable" or "ubiquitous" enough to prove that CC Serve’s mark was weak. Consequently, the Board granted CC Serve the "normal scope of protection" accorded to inherently distinctive marks, rather than the narrow protection associated with a weak mark in a crowded field.

## The Conflict Between the Board's Findings in the Second and Sixth Factors

The Federal Circuit found a glaring inconsistency in the Board's reasoning. To understand this conflict, one must look at the Board's analysis of the second DuPont factor, which assesses the similarity of the parties' goods and services.

Under the second factor, the Board had determined that CC Serve’s "credit card services" and Apex Bank’s "banking and financing services" were "highly similar" and, in some respects, "legally identical." The Board used this high degree of similarity to conclude that the second factor weighed heavily in favor of a likelihood of confusion. 

The court noted that the Board essentially adopted two different definitions of "similarity" depending on which factor it was analyzing. For the second factor, the Board viewed credit card services and general banking services as nearly the same. But for the sixth factor, the Board suddenly viewed them as distinct enough to exclude general banking marks from the "crowded field" analysis.

## Legal Requirement for Consistency in Defining 'Similarity'

The Federal Circuit emphasized that the legal standard for "similarity" must be applied consistently across all relevant DuPont factors. The court clarified that the sixth factor does not require the third-party marks to be used on *identical* goods or services—only *similar* ones.

If a tribunal makes a factual finding that two types of services (such as credit card services and general banking services) are highly similar for the purpose of finding a likelihood of confusion, it must maintain that same scope of similarity when evaluating the number of third-party marks in the field. The court held that there is no legal justification for imposing a more stringent or narrow standard of similarity under the sixth factor than was used under the second factor.

## The Impact of Excluding Broader Financial Service Marks on the Determination of Mark Strength

The Board's decision to exclude the 33 additional marks used in the broader financial services industry had a decisive impact on the outcome. By narrowing the field, the Board shifted the mark's status from potentially "weak" (due to a crowded field of 42 marks) to "normal" (based on only 9 marks).

Because the commercial strength of a mark influences the "overall commercial impression" analyzed under the first DuPont factor, the Federal Circuit vacated the Board's findings on both the sixth and first factors. The court remanded the case, requiring the Board to reconsider the "crowded field" analysis using the same broad definition of similarity it had previously applied to the services themselves. This ensures that the strength of the mark—and the resulting scope of its protection—is based on the actual competitive landscape of the relevant industry.

# The First DuPont Factor and Final Disposition

## The First DuPont Factor: Comparison of Appearance, Sound, Connotation, and Commercial Impression

The first *DuPont* factor is a foundational element of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis. It requires the court to examine the similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties, specifically focusing on their appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. Rather than analyzing a mark in isolated pieces, this factor emphasizes the "overall commercial impression" created by the marks. The central question is whether the total effect of the marks—how they look, sound, and what they suggest to a consumer—is likely to result in confusion as to the source of the services offered.

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board initially analyzed the first factor to determine if "ASPIRE BANK" and "ASPIRE" created a confusingly similar commercial impression. However, the Federal Circuit determined that this analysis could not stand on its own because the commercial impression of a mark is not analyzed in a vacuum; it is heavily influenced by the mark's commercial strength.

## How the Sixth Factor's Determination of Commercial Strength Informs the First Factor

To understand why the court revisited the first factor, one must understand the role of the sixth *DuPont* factor: the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods. The sixth factor is used to determine a mark's "commercial strength." When a particular word or design is used frequently by many different third parties in a specific industry (a "crowded field"), the mark is considered relatively weak. The theory is that if consumers see many different businesses using the word "Aspire," they become more adept at distinguishing between those businesses and are less likely to assume that two different "Aspire" marks emanate from the same source.

In this case, the Board had limited its sixth-factor analysis to marks used specifically for "credit card services," ignoring dozens of other marks used for broader banking and financing services. The Federal Circuit found this too restrictive. Because the Board had already determined under the second *DuPont* factor that banking and credit card services are highly similar (and in some ways legally identical), the court held that the Board should have used that same broad scope when looking for third-party marks under the sixth factor. If the court finds that the "Aspire" mark is commercially weak due to a crowded field of similar marks, that weakness directly informs the "commercial impression" analyzed under the first factor.

## The Logic Behind Vacating the First Factor Analysis

The Federal Circuit’s decision to vacate the Board's analysis of the first factor was a matter of logical necessity. Because the Board’s analysis of the sixth factor was legally flawed—by excluding relevant third-party marks—the resulting determination of the mark's commercial strength was potentially incorrect. 

Since the commercial strength of a mark (Factor 6) helps define the scope of protection the mark is entitled to and shapes the overall commercial impression (Factor 1), the court could not leave the first factor intact. If the remanded analysis of the sixth factor reveals that "ASPIRE" is weaker than the Board originally thought, the "commercial impression" of the marks may be viewed differently, potentially reducing the likelihood of confusion. Therefore, the court vacated both the sixth and first factors to ensure the Board’s analysis was internally consistent and based on a correct understanding of the relevant market.

## Final Outcome: Affirming-in-Part, Vacating-in-Part, and Remanding

The final disposition of this case illustrates how an appellate court handles a complex multi-factor test. The court did not overturn the entire Board decision, but rather targeted the specific legal errors.

First, the court **affirmed** the Board's finding on the second *DuPont* factor. It agreed that there was substantial evidence to support the conclusion that "credit card services" and "banking and financing services" are highly similar.

Second, the court **vacated** the findings regarding the sixth and first *DuPont* factors. The Board erred by applying a more stringent standard of similarity to third-party marks than it did to the parties' own services.

Finally, the court **remanded** the case to the Board. The Board must now reconsider the number and nature of similar marks in use (Factor 6) using a consistent definition of similarity, and then re-evaluate the appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression of the marks (Factor 1) in light of that updated strength analysis.
