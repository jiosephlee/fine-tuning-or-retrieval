Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# The Pivoting Expert: When Contradictory Testimony Fails the Substantial Evidence Test

In patent litigation, the expert witness is often the bridge between the dense technical specifications of a patent and the jury’s understanding of the accused product. However, as the Federal Circuit recently demonstrated in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, that bridge can collapse if the expert attempts to "pivot" their theory in real-time during cross-examination. 

The result of this particular collapse was the reversal of a $166 million damages award. The case serves as a stark reminder that while juries are given wide latitude to weigh evidence, a verdict cannot stand on "substantial evidence" if the primary technical testimony is self-contradictory or fails to actually map the claim limitations to the accused device.

## The Sampling Problem

The dispute centered, in part, on U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134, which describes a method for mitigating interference in radios. A key limitation of the patent requires "over-sampling... a passband of received signals" that includes both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." To win, Finesse had to prove that AT&T’s radios actually sampled both types of signals.

Finesse’s expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, relied on a Nokia technical document to argue that the radios infringed. He testified that the radio's analog-to-digital converter (ADC) sampled the "signals of interest" (which he mapped to the downlink transmit reference) and the "interference generating signals" (which he mapped to the "modeled PIM path").

The problem was a matter of basic sequencing: the "modeled PIM path" is generated *after* the sampling occurs. It is logically impossible to sample a signal that hasn't been generated yet. 

## The Danger of the "Pivot"

When AT&T’s counsel pointed out this temporal impossibility during cross-examination, Dr. Wells did not concede; instead, he pivoted. He began referring to "x1 and x2"—different variables in the technical documentation—suggesting there were two signals on the path. 

However, Dr. Wells never actually performed the necessary legal work of "mapping." He never explicitly testified that x1 and x2 *were* the "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." He continued to oscillate between his original, flawed mapping and these new variables, leaving the record in a state of confusion.

The district court initially excused this, essentially guessing that Dr. Wells *meant* to map x1 and x2 to the claims. The Federal Circuit, however, rejected this judicial guesswork.

## Substantial Evidence and the "Contradictory" Standard

The Federal Circuit’s reasoning leans on the "substantial evidence" standard. While a court doesn't re-weigh evidence at the JMOL (Judgment as a Matter of Law) stage, it must ensure that a reasonable jury *could* have reached the verdict based on the evidence presented.

The court pointed to *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.* to establish a critical rule: when a party rests its case on expert testimony that is self-contradictory, that evidence may be deemed insufficient. In *Finesse*, the court found that Dr. Wells’ "confusing change of course" was not enough to support a verdict. Because the expert failed to provide a consistent, logical mapping of the claim limitation to the hardware, there was no substantial evidence of infringement.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

This decision reinforces a rigorous requirement for expert testimony in patent cases: the "mapping" must be explicit and logically sound. It is not enough for an expert to point to a general area of a technical schematic and suggest that "the signals are in there somewhere."

For practitioners, the downstream effects are clear:

First, "fixing" a theory on appeal is impossible. Finesse attempted to clarify the mapping in its briefs—arguing that the expert was merely "laboring under a misimpression" during trial. The Federal Circuit flatly rejected this, noting that arguments not presented to the jury cannot be used to sustain a verdict.

Second, the court’s treatment of the ’775 patent in the same opinion doubles down on this requirement. In that instance, the expert pointed to three multiplications in a document to satisfy a claim requiring seven. Again, the court refused to allow the plaintiff to "fill in the gaps" on appeal.

Ultimately, *Finesse Wireless* is a cautionary tale about the perils of the "pivoting expert." When an expert witness contradicts their own primary theory under cross-examination, they don't just lose credibility—they may inadvertently strip the plaintiff of the "substantial evidence" required to survive a JMOL motion. For a $166 million verdict, that is a catastrophic failure of translation.



# Identifiable vs. Unique: The Nuances of Counting Signals in Patent Claims

In the high-stakes world of patent litigation, a single word—or the absence of one—can determine whether a defendant owes nothing or millions of dollars. The Federal Circuit’s recent decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* provides a masterclass in the distinction between "separately identifiable" and "unique" components within a claim, while serving as a cautionary tale about the perils of expert testimony that fails to bridge the gap between a technical document and a claim limitation.

The case centered on two patents aimed at reducing interference in radios. While the court ultimately reversed a massive damages award and found noninfringement for both patents, the reasoning regarding the ’775 patent is particularly instructive for patent practitioners. The core of the dispute was whether a method requiring "three signals S1, S2 and S3" necessarily required three *distinct* or *unique* input signals, or whether a system using only two unique signals could still satisfy the claim.

The Federal Circuit upheld the district court’s construction that S1, S2, and S3 must be "separately identifiable," but not necessarily "unique." In practical terms, this means that if a device uses only two unique signals (x1 and x2), it can still meet a "three signal" limitation if one of those signals is used twice (e.g., S1=x1, S2=x1, S3=x2). The court relied on the fact that the intrinsic evidence did not explicitly require uniqueness and credited inventor testimony that these variables represented signals generally.

This distinction is doctrinally significant because it touches upon the "clear and unambiguous" standard for claim disavowal. AT&T argued that Finesse had surrendered the scope of "two signals" during prosecution when it amended the claims from "two or three signals" to simply "three signals" to overcome a prior art rejection. Typically, such an amendment is viewed as a narrowing of scope. However, the Federal Circuit held that this specific amendment did not rise to the level of a clear and unambiguous disavowal of products using only two unique inputs. This suggests that adding a numerical limitation does not automatically preclude a finding of infringement if the "identifiable" variables in the claim can be mapped to a smaller set of unique physical inputs.

However, winning the battle over claim construction does not always win the war of infringement. Despite the lenient "identifiable vs. unique" construction, Finesse lost the ’775 patent case on the facts. The claim required seven specific third-order multiplications. The evidence—specifically a Nokia technical document—only showed three distinct multiplications. The plaintiff’s expert, Dr. Wells, failed to provide a coherent mapping explaining how those three distinct multiplications satisfied the seven claimed operations. 

The court’s refusal to allow Finesse to "fix" this mapping on appeal underscores a fundamental rule of trial practice: arguments made to the appellate court that were not presented to the jury are irrelevant. When an expert relies on a document that does not explicitly show the claimed steps, and then fails to explain the mapping during testimony, the verdict lacks "substantial evidence."

For the broader legal community, *Finesse Wireless* highlights a critical tension in patent drafting. While the court was permissive regarding the number of unique signals, it was rigorous regarding the mathematical execution of the claimed steps. Practitioners should take note: an expansive construction of a term like "three signals" is of little value if the accompanying functional limitations (such as the "seven multiplications") create a technical hurdle that the evidence cannot clear. The decision reinforces that while the court may be flexible on the "what" (the inputs), it remains strict on the "how" (the process).



# The Mapping Gap: Why Technical Documentation is Not a Substitute for Expert Proof

In the high-stakes arena of patent litigation, there is often a tempting shortcut for plaintiffs: presenting a technical document of the accused device and asking an expert to "map" the claim limitations onto that document. If the document looks similar enough to the claim, a jury may be inclined to find infringement. However, the Federal Circuit’s recent decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* serves as a stark reminder that "close enough" is not a legal standard. By reversing a $166 million damages award, the court has reaffirmed that a patentee cannot use appellate briefing to cure a failure of proof at trial.

The core of the dispute centered on two patents aimed at reducing interference in radios. While the court found issues with both, the reversal regarding the ’775 patent is particularly instructive for practitioners. The ’775 patent claimed a specific method for generating interference cancellation signals that required seven specific third-order multiplications of three signals (S1, S2, and S3). To prevail, Finesse had to prove that the accused Nokia radios actually performed these seven multiplications.

The failure of Finesse’s case lay in the gap between the technical documentation and the expert testimony. Finesse’s expert, Dr. Wells, pointed to a Nokia technical document to prove infringement. However, that document only showed three distinct multiplications. Dr. Wells testified that these three multiplications equated to the seven claimed, but he failed to explain *how*. He didn't provide a one-to-one mapping, nor did he explain how the complex conjugate and modulus operations in the Nokia document satisfied the specific multiplication requirements of the patent.

This is where the court drew a hard line. Finesse attempted to "fill the gaps" during the appeal, arguing that the Nokia documents actually showed ten multiplications, which would more than cover the seven required by the claim. The Federal Circuit rejected this approach. The court's reasoning is doctrinally sound: a jury verdict must be supported by *substantial evidence* presented at trial. If the expert fails to provide a clear mapping during testimony, the patentee cannot supplement that deficiency with a new theory in an appellate brief. The court essentially ruled that the "mapping" is the evidence, not the underlying document itself.

A similar failure plagued Finesse’s arguments regarding the ’134 patent. There, the expert mapped the "interference generating signals" to a "modeled PIM path." The problem was a matter of basic physics and sequencing: the modeled PIM path was generated *after* the sampling process occurred. You cannot sample a signal that has not yet been generated. When confronted with this, the expert pivoted to other variables ("x1 and x2") but never clearly mapped them to the claimed limitations. The court characterized this as "confusing and unclear" testimony, which is insufficient to sustain a verdict.

The implications of *Finesse Wireless* are significant for the "mapping" phase of patent litigation. It signals that the Federal Circuit will not tolerate "black box" expert testimony where a witness asserts that a document proves infringement without detailing the precise operational steps. For plaintiffs, the lesson is clear: the technical document is merely the map; the expert’s testimony is the journey. If the expert cannot walk the court through every single step of the claim—specifically regarding the number of operations or the sequence of events—the case is vulnerable to a JMOL motion.

Ultimately, this decision reinforces the necessity of rigorous, granular proof in complex technical cases. It warns patentees that relying on the perceived complexity of a technology to gloss over missing claim elements is a dangerous strategy. Precision in the trial record is the only way to ensure a verdict survives the scrutiny of the Federal Circuit.
