Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Confusion over the dual standards of review for JMOL and jury findings in Federal Circuit appeals
Question:
I'm reading the discussion section where the court says they review the denial of JMOL under Fifth Circuit law (de novo, reversing only if evidence points 'strongly and overwhelmingly' to one party) but then says they review the jury's finding of infringement for 'substantial evidence.' I'm confused—isn't the JMOL motion essentially asking the court to review the jury's finding? Why are there two different standards of review being applied to the same outcome (the infringement verdict)?
Answer:
***

### Why are there two different standards of review for JMOL and jury findings?

It is common for law students to find this confusing because, as you noted, both standards are ultimately being used to determine if the infringement verdict should stand. However, the two standards address different "layers" of the legal process: the **procedural decision of the judge** and the **factual finding of the jury**.

Here is the breakdown of why the court applies both in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*.

#### 1. The Review of the District Court’s Decision (The "Legal" Layer)
When the Appellants appeal the **denial of a JMOL motion**, they are asking the appellate court to review a legal ruling made by the district court judge. 

The district court judge's role at the JMOL stage is to decide: *"As a matter of law, is there enough evidence here that a reasonable jury could possibly find for the plaintiff?"* Because this is a legal determination about the sufficiency of the evidence, the Federal Circuit reviews the judge's decision **de novo**. 

Per the opinion, the court follows Fifth Circuit law, which dictates that it will reverse the denial of JMOL only if the evidence points **"so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party"** that no reasonable juror could have reached a contrary conclusion.

#### 2. The Review of the Jury’s Verdict (The "Factual" Layer)
While the *denial of the motion* is reviewed *de novo*, the **jury’s actual finding of fact** (that the patents were infringed) is subject to a different standard: **substantial evidence**. 

The "substantial evidence" standard is the baseline for factual findings. As the court explains, a finding is supported by substantial evidence if **"a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial."**

#### How they work together in this opinion
The appellate court uses the "substantial evidence" standard as the tool to determine if the district court judge was wrong to deny the JMOL. 

In the *Finesse* case, the court analyzed the evidence (specifically Dr. Wells' contradictory testimony regarding the ’134 patent and the lack of mapping for the ’775 patent) and concluded that the evidence was insufficient. The court's logic follows this path:
1. **Factual Analysis:** The court found that the jury's infringement verdict was **not supported by substantial evidence** (the "factual layer").
2. **Legal Conclusion:** Because there was no substantial evidence to support the verdict, the district court's decision to deny the JMOL was incorrect.
3. **Outcome:** Under the *de novo* review of the JMOL denial, the court **reversed** the district court’s ruling.

**Summary:** The "substantial evidence" standard is used to evaluate the **evidence**, while the "de novo/strongly and overwhelmingly" standard is used to evaluate the **judge's decision** to let the jury's verdict stand.

### How does 'sampling' work in the context of the '134 patent's non-infringement ruling?
Question:
In the '134 patent analysis, the court reverses because the 'modeled PIM path' is generated *after* the RF ADC sampling occurs. The court says signals cannot be sampled before they are generated. For a non-lawyer/student, I'm struggling to understand why this is a fatal blow to the infringement claim. If the system eventually processes these signals, why does the specific sequence of sampling vs. generation matter so much for the 'interference generating signals' limitation?
Answer:
### Answer

To understand why the sequence of sampling versus generation is "fatal" to the infringement claim, you have to look at the specific language of the claim limitation and the requirement for literal infringement.

**The Legal Standard: Meeting Every Limitation**
In patent law, for a device to literally infringe a claim, it must meet every single limitation of that claim. If even one limitation is missing, there is no literal infringement.

**The Specific Limitation at Issue**
The '134 patent’s Claim 1[a] requires:
> "over-sampling... a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, **wherein the received signals include** signals of interest and **interference generating signals**..."

The key phrase here is *"wherein the received signals include."* This means that at the exact moment and location where the sampling occurs, the signals being sampled must already exist and must include the "interference generating signals."

**The "Fatal Blow" in the Accused Device**
The court's analysis focused on the physical and temporal architecture of the accused Nokia radios. The mapping provided by Finesse’s expert, Dr. Wells, created a logical impossibility based on the following facts:

1.  **The Location of Sampling:** The sampling occurs at the RF ADC (Radio Frequency Analog-to-Digital Converter).
2.  **The Identity of the Signal:** Dr. Wells mapped the "interference generating signals" to the "modeled PIM path."
3.  **The Sequence of Generation:** The court found (and the expert admitted under cross-examination) that the "modeled PIM path" is generated **downstream** of the RF ADC.

Because the modeled PIM path is generated *after* the sampling occurs, it is physically impossible for the RF ADC to sample a passband that *includes* that path. As the court noted, "signals cannot be sampled before they are generated."

**Why "Eventually Processing" the Signals is Irrelevant**
You asked why it matters if the system eventually processes these signals. In a patent infringement analysis, the court is not asking "does this machine achieve the same result?" but rather "does this machine perform the specific steps described in the claim?"

The claim does not say "the system shall eventually process interference generating signals"; it says the system must **sample a passband that includes them**. 

If the accused device generates a signal digitally *after* the analog-to-digital conversion (sampling) has already happened, it is performing a fundamentally different operation than what is claimed. The "interference generating signal" in the accused device is a product of the system's internal processing, whereas the '134 patent claims a system that samples those signals as part of the *incoming* received passband.

**Conclusion**
Because Finesse's expert mapped the "interference generating signals" to a path that did not exist until after the sampling stage, the accused radios failed to meet limitation 1[a]. Since that limitation was not met, the claim was not infringed.

### What is the distinction between 'unique' signals and 'separately identifiable' signals in claim construction?
Question:
Regarding the '775 patent, the court held that 'three signals S1, S2 and S3' does not require the signals to be 'unique,' only 'separately identifiable.' The court then suggests that using two unique signals plus a copy of one of them satisfies this. I don't understand the legal or technical distinction here. If S3 is just a copy of S1, isn't that still just two unique signals? How can a copy be 'separately identifiable' without being 'unique'?
Answer:
### The Distinction Between "Unique" and "Separately Identifiable" Signals

The distinction the court is making centers on the difference between **distinct input sources** (uniqueness) and **distinct instances within a formula** (identifiability).

**The Legal Distinction**
In claim construction, "unique" typically refers to the nature of the source. If a claim requires three *unique* signals, the accused device must utilize three different input sources. However, the district court in this case construed "three signals S1, S2 and S3" as "**separately identifiable, but not unique**." 

This means that while the claim requires three "slots" to be filled for the purpose of the mathematical operations described (the seven multiplications), those slots do not necessarily need to be filled by three different sources.

**How a Copy is "Separately Identifiable"**
To answer your specific question: Yes, if S3 is a copy of S1, you only have two *unique* signals. However, in the context of a mathematical operation (like $S1 \times S2 \times S3$), you still have three *separately identifiable* signals because there are three distinct positions in the equation.

The court grounded this interpretation in two pieces of evidence:
1.  **Intrinsic Evidence:** The court found that the patent's own text did not limit the signals to unique inputs.
2.  **Extrinsic Evidence:** The inventor testified that S1, S2, and S3 "represent signals generally and can be the same signal or different signals."

Therefore, if a system takes two unique input signals ($x1$ and $x2$) and uses $x1$ as S1, $x2$ as S2, and then uses a copy of $x1$ as S3, it has provided three signals that can be separately identified and mapped to the variables in the claim's formula, even though only two unique sources exist.

**Application to the Case**
The Federal Circuit agreed that Finesse's amendment to "three signals" (rather than "two or three signals") did not constitute a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal of devices using only two unique input signals. The amendment merely required that three signals be *identifiable* for the multiplications to occur.

Notably, while this distinction helped the plaintiff's argument regarding the *inputs*, it did not save the infringement verdict. The court ultimately reversed because the accused radios did not actually perform the **seven specific multiplications** required by the claim, regardless of whether the signals used were unique or copies.

### Why wasn't the amendment from 'two or three signals' to 'three signals' considered a clear disavowal of scope?
Question:
In the '775 patent section, Finesse amended their claims during prosecution to change 'two or three signals' to 'three signals' to overcome a prior art rejection (Filipovic). Usually, when a patentee narrows a claim to get it allowed, they are seen as surrendering the surrendered scope. Why did the court find that this amendment did *not* rise to the level of a 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal of products using only two unique input signals?
Answer:
### Why wasn't the amendment from "two or three signals" to "three signals" considered a clear disavowal of scope?

In patent prosecution, narrowing a claim to overcome a prior art rejection can sometimes be interpreted as a "disavowal" of the surrendered scope. However, in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit held that amending the '775 patent to recite "three signals" instead of "two or three signals" did not constitute a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal of products using only two unique input signals.

The court's reasoning rests on two primary factors: the context of the prosecution history and the technical distinction between "identifiable" and "unique" signals.

**1. The amendment was not the sole basis for overcoming the prior art**
The court noted that the amendment was not the only way Finesse overcame the *Filipovic* rejection. Finesse argued to the examiner that *Filipovic* was limited to mitigating "active" intermodulation products (IMPs), whereas the claimed method addressed "passive" IMPs. Because the rejection was overcome *in part* by this distinction between active and passive IMPs, the change from "two or three" to "three" signals did not necessarily signal a total surrender of all two-signal configurations.

**2. "Separately identifiable" does not mean "unique"**
The court distinguished between the number of signals required by the claim and the number of *unique input sources* required. The district court had construed "three signals S1, S2 and S3" to mean "separately identifiable," but not necessarily "unique." This was supported by inventor testimony stating that S1, S2, and S3 represent signals generally and could be the same signal or different signals. 

Consequently, the court found that the amendment required three *separately identifiable* signals for the purpose of the multiplications, but it did not explicitly require three *unique input* signals. 

**Conclusion**
Under the legal standard cited in *Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, a disavowal must be "clear and unambiguous." The court concluded that because the amendment could be satisfied by using two unique signals (where one signal is used more than once to act as a separate identifiable signal), the amendment did not rise to the level of a clear surrender of all two-unique-signal products.

### Does a patent claim for 'seven multiplications' require an exact 1:1 mapping even if the device performs more than seven?
Question:
At oral argument, Finesse claimed the accused radios actually perform ten multiplications, which is more than the seven required by the '775 patent. However, the court still found non-infringement because Finesse couldn't map a specific subset of those ten to the seven claimed multiplications. If a device does *more* than what the patent requires, why is the failure to provide a precise mapping of the subset sufficient to reverse the infringement verdict?
Answer:
### Answer

The short answer is **yes**. In patent infringement analysis, the patentee must prove that the accused device meets **every limitation** of the asserted claim. Performing *more* than the required number of operations does not automatically satisfy a claim limitation if the specific operations required by the patent cannot be identified within the accused device's process.

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the court held that Finesse failed to prove infringement of the '775 patent because it could not demonstrate that the accused radios performed the **seven specific multiplications** required by claim 1[d].

#### The Legal Standard: Meeting Every Limitation
To establish infringement, a plaintiff must show that the accused device contains every element of the claimed invention. In this case, the '775 patent did not simply claim "seven multiplications" in a general sense; it claimed seven **specific** mathematical combinations of three signals ($S_1, S_2, S_3$):
1. $S_1 \times S_1 \times S_2$
2. $S_1 \times S_2 \times S_2$
3. $S_1 \times S_2 \times S_3$
4. $S_1 \times S_1 \times S_3$
5. $S_2 \times S_2 \times S_3$
6. $S_1 \times S_3 \times S_3$
7. $S_2 \times S_3 \times S_3$

#### Why "More than Seven" Was Insufficient
Finesse argued at oral argument that the accused radios performed ten multiplications, which is numerically greater than the seven required. However, the court rejected this "more is better" logic for two primary reasons:

1. **Lack of Mapping:** The court noted that "nowhere do Finesse or Dr. Wells explain which subset of the ten multiplications... corresponds to the seven claimed multiplications." Simply proving that a device performs ten operations is irrelevant if the patentee cannot point to seven of those operations and show they are exactly the seven specified in the claim.
2. **Flawed Mapping:** The evidence presented at trial (a Nokia technical document) only showed three distinct multiplications ($x_1 \cdot |x_1| \cdot |x_1|$, $x_1 \cdot x_1 \cdot x_2'$, and $x_1 \cdot |x_2| \cdot |x_2|$). Finesse's attempt to map these to the claimed seven was found to be "flawed" because it required mapping a single signal ($S_3$) to three different things simultaneously: the signal $x_2$, its complex conjugate $x_2'$, and its modulus $|x_2|$.

#### Conclusion
The court concluded that because there was no evidence that the accused radios performed the specific seven multiplications required by the claim—and no valid mapping from the ten actual multiplications to the seven claimed ones—no reasonable jury could have found the devices infringing. In essence, performing ten random multiplications does not satisfy a claim for seven specific ones.

### What is the procedural effect of 'vacating' the damages award versus 'reversing' it?
Question:
The court reverses the denial of JMOL on non-infringement and then 'vacates' the damages award. I'm confused by the terminology. Since the court found there was no infringement, the damages logically cannot exist, but why use the term 'vacate' for the damages while using 'reverse' for the JMOL ruling? Does this have different legal implications for the finality of the judgment?
Answer:
***

### The difference between "reversing" a ruling and "vacating" an award

In appellate practice, the terms "reverse" and "vacate" describe different procedural actions. While they often appear together in a final judgment, they target different types of lower-court outputs: legal rulings and resulting orders.

**Reversing the JMOL Ruling**
To **reverse** is to change a legal determination made by the lower court. In this case, the district court had issued a legal ruling by *denying* the defendants' motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) of non-infringement. By reversing this denial, the Federal Circuit is correcting a legal error—specifically, the court's conclusion that there was substantial evidence to support the jury's infringement verdict.

Because the court found that "no reasonable jury could have found" infringement for either the ’134 or ’775 patents, it reversed the district court's decision. The procedural effect of this reversal is that the legal finding of infringement is overturned, and a finding of non-infringement is established.

**Vacating the Damages Award**
To **vacate** is to void, cancel, or set aside a specific order or judgment. Unlike a legal ruling (which is "right" or "wrong"), a damages award is a consequential remedy. 

The court does not "reverse" the $166,303,391 lump-sum damages award because it is not correcting a legal interpretation of the award itself; rather, it is removing the award's legal basis. As the opinion states: *"Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement... we vacate the damages award."* 

Once the finding of infringement was reversed, the damages award became legally unsupported. Vacating the award wipes the slate clean, effectively treating the award as if it had never been entered.

**Summary of Procedural Effect**
| Term | Target | Procedural Effect |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Reverse** | Legal Ruling (Denial of JMOL) | Overturns the legal conclusion; substitutes the lower court's "Wrong" with "Right." |
| **Vacate** | Resulting Order (Damages Award) | Renders the specific monetary judgment void; removes the remedy. |

Regarding finality: both actions contribute to the finality of the appellate judgment. By reversing the JMOL denial and vacating the damages, the Federal Circuit has fully resolved the merits of the infringement claims and stripped the plaintiff of the financial judgment, leaving no further basis for the damages to be recovered.
