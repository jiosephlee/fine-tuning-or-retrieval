Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Procedural Rights Are Not Concrete Injuries: The DC Circuit Reaffirms Standing Rigor

In the realm of administrative law, there is often a tempting temptation for litigants to conflate a government agency's statutory duty with a private citizen's right to sue. The D.C. Circuit’s recent decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* serves as a sharp reminder that a statutory mandate—even one phrased in the imperative "shall"—does not automatically grant a plaintiff a ticket into federal court. By dismissing the case for lack of Article III standing, the court reaffirmed that a "procedural right in vacuo" is insufficient to trigger judicial power.

The dispute centered on the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) and its role in policing the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). America First Legal (AFL) sought to compel the OSC to investigate the Department of Justice’s (DOJ) alleged policy of refusing to search employee emails without consent. AFL relied on 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which states that the OSC "shall... conduct an investigation" of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. On its face, the statute seems to command action. However, the D.C. Circuit focused not on whether the OSC had a legal obligation to investigate, but on whether AFL had suffered a concrete, particularized injury that a court could actually redress.

The court's analysis of AFL's two standing theories provides a masterclass in the rigor of the *Lujan* framework. First, AFL argued that the OSC’s failure to investigate prevented AFL from obtaining the very records it sought from the DOJ. The court rejected this as a "speculative chain" of causation. For AFL to get its records, the OSC would have to investigate, the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) would have to sanction DOJ employees, and the DOJ would then have to decide to produce the records. To the court, this was too many "unfettered choices" by independent actors. It is a critical distinction: the injury (not getting records) was traceable to the DOJ, not the OSC.

Second, AFL attempted to claim an "informational injury," arguing that it was entitled to the fruits of the investigation itself. The court dismantled this theory by distinguishing between a mandate for an agency to *act* and a mandate for an agency to *provide information*. While § 1216 may require the OSC to investigate, it does not create a statutory entitlement for the complainant to receive the results. Because AFL could not point to a specific legal right to receive information, the "procedural right" to an investigation remained a right without a concrete interest—precisely the "right in vacuo" the Supreme Court rejected in *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*

From an analytical perspective, the court is signaling a refusal to allow statutory "watchdog" provisions to bypass the Constitution's case-or-controversy requirement. Litigants often attempt to use "procedural standing" to lower the bar for imminence and redressability. Here, the court clarified that while procedural standing relaxes certain requirements, it does not eliminate the need for a baseline injury-in-fact. If the outcome of a court order (forcing an investigation) does not logically and predictably result in a concrete benefit to the plaintiff, the court has no business intervening.

The doctrinal implications of *Greer* are significant for practitioners challenging agency inaction. It reinforces the high hurdle for "informational standing," which now requires a clear statutory entitlement to the data in question, rather than a general interest in the agency's compliance with the law. Downstream, this decision limits the ability of advocacy groups to use the judiciary to force internal government oversight. In short, the D.C. Circuit has made it clear: a statutory violation is not an injury, and a government duty is not a private right to relief.



# Breaking the Chain: Redressability and the Speculative Nature of Inter-Agency Action

In the administrative state, the path from a legal violation to a concrete remedy is rarely a straight line. Often, it is a complex series of dominoes involving different agencies, different statutory mandates, and vast amounts of prosecutorial discretion. In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the D.C. Circuit has reinforced a critical boundary of Article III standing: courts will not entertain a "domino theory" of redressability where the requested relief relies on a speculative chain of actions by independent government actors.

The dispute began with a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request. The America First Legal Foundation (AFL) alleged that the Department of Justice (DOJ) arbitrarily withheld records by refusing to search employee emails without their consent. When the Office of Special Counsel (OSC)—an independent investigative agency—declined to investigate this policy under 5 U.S.C. § 1216, AFL sued to compel that investigation. While the district court found that the OSC had legally erred in its refusal to investigate, the D.C. Circuit vacated that judgment on standing grounds, focusing specifically on the "redressability" element of the *Lujan* framework.

The court’s reasoning regarding AFL’s first theory of standing—that an OSC investigation would eventually lead to the production of FOIA records—is the most instructive part of the opinion. AFL argued that if the court ordered the OSC to investigate, the OSC might pursue disciplinary action, which might lead the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) to sanction DOJ employees, which might finally persuade the DOJ to release the emails. 

Judge Katsas correctly identified this as a "speculative chain." For a plaintiff to have standing, a favorable judgment must be "likely" to redress the injury. Here, the outcome depended on the unfettered choices of two separate entities not before the court: the MSPB and the DOJ. By requiring a plaintiff to guess how one agency's action might influence another's discretionary decision, the court signaled that it will not allow the judiciary to become a manager of inter-agency pressure.

From a doctrinal perspective, this decision serves as a stern reminder that procedural rights are not self-executing. AFL attempted to frame its injury as a "procedural right" to an investigation. However, the court reiterated that a procedural right "in vacuo"—one not tied to a concrete, particularized interest—is insufficient for standing. Even under the relaxed standards for procedural injuries, the plaintiff must still show a plausible causal link to a concrete outcome. The court's refusal to engraft the notice requirements of one section of the Civil Service Reform Act (§ 1214) onto another (§ 1216) further underscores the court's commitment to textualism over a "fairness-based" reading of agency obligations.

The downstream effects of this ruling are significant for oversight litigation. It creates a high hurdle for plaintiffs seeking to compel "watchdog" agencies to act. If the ultimate goal of a lawsuit is to force a primary agency (like the DOJ) to change its behavior, suing a secondary oversight agency (like the OSC) may be a dead end if the secondary agency lacks the direct power to compel the primary agency's compliance.

Ultimately, *Greer* clarifies that the "redressability" prong of standing is not merely a formality, but a substantive check on judicial power. The court is not in the business of imagining hypothetical scenarios where a legal victory might "persuade" the government to do the right thing; it requires a direct, legally cognizable path from the court's order to the plaintiff's relief. By breaking the speculative chain, the D.C. Circuit has ensured that the doors to the courthouse remain closed to those who seek a legal remedy based on a series of "might-have-beens."



# The FOIA Enforcement Gap: Navigating the Tension Between § 1216 and § 552

For decades, FOIA requesters have faced a recurring frustration: agencies often provide records only after a court order, yet the individual officials who delayed the production rarely face personal consequences. While FOIA allows a court to find that an agency acted "arbitrarily or capriciously," the bridge between a judicial finding and actual employee discipline is narrow and difficult to cross. In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the D.C. Circuit has effectively reinforced that bridge’s instability, not by deciding the statutory conflict on the merits, but by deploying a rigorous standing analysis to keep the judiciary out of the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) business.

The case centers on a tension between two provisions of the U.S. Code. On one hand, 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) appears to be a broad mandate, stating that the OSC “shall… conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information” prohibited by FOIA. On the other hand, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) creates a much higher bar, triggering an OSC proceeding only after a court has ordered production, awarded attorney’s fees, and specifically questioned the agency’s conduct.

The America First Legal Foundation (AFL) argued that § 1216 is a standalone duty. In their view, if a requester can allege an arbitrary withholding policy—such as the DOJ’s alleged refusal to search employee emails without consent—the OSC must investigate, regardless of whether the strict predicates of § 552 have been met. This interpretation would transform the OSC into a more accessible watchdog, allowing private parties to trigger disciplinary investigations into federal employees without first winning a full-blown FOIA lawsuit.

However, the D.C. Circuit avoided the merits of this statutory clash entirely, ruling that AFL lacked Article III standing. The court’s reasoning highlights a critical distinction in administrative law: the difference between a "procedural right" and a "concrete interest." AFL attempted to argue that the OSC’s failure to investigate constituted a procedural injury. But as Judge Katsas noted, a procedural right in a vacuum is not an injury in fact. To establish standing, AFL had to show that the lack of an investigation caused a concrete harm.

AFL’s attempt to link the OSC’s inaction to the non-production of records by the DOJ failed on the grounds of causation and redressability. The court identified a "speculative chain" of events: a court order to investigate would lead to an OSC finding, which would lead to a Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) sanction, which *might* eventually persuade the DOJ to release the records. The court correctly noted that judicial remedies cannot rely on the "unfettered choices" of independent actors not before the court. Essentially, the court reminded us that the OSC is an enforcement agency, not a records-production agency. If you want documents, you sue the agency under FOIA; you cannot use the OSC as a back-door mechanism to compel production.

The doctrinal implications of this decision are significant. By denying standing, the court has preserved a wide berth of prosecutorial discretion for the OSC. If the court had reached the merits and held that § 1216 is a mandatory, non-discretionary duty triggered by a mere allegation, it would have opened the floodgates for "watchdog" litigation. Every disgruntled FOIA requester could potentially compel the OSC to launch investigations into the conduct of federal employees, effectively turning the judiciary into a manager of the OSC’s investigative docket.

Furthermore, the court’s rejection of AFL’s second theory—that they had an informational interest in the *results* of the investigation—closes a potential loophole. Because § 1216 does not explicitly entitle a complainant to receive the fruits of an investigation, and because the notice requirements of § 1214 are discretionary in this context, there is no "statutory entitlement" to information.

Ultimately, *Greer* reinforces the reality that holding federal employees personally accountable for FOIA violations remains an uphill battle. The "enforcement gap" remains wide: unless a requester can navigate the grueling path to a specific judicial finding under § 552, the OSC’s general duty under § 1216 is unlikely to be a viable tool for private litigants. For now, the court has signaled that it will not use its equitable powers to force the OSC to act, ensuring that the decision of whom to investigate remains firmly within the executive branch's discretion.
