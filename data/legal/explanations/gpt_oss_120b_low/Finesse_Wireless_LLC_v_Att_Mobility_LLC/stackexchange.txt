Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### What standard does the Federal Circuit apply when reviewing a district court’s denial of a JMOL of noninfringement?
Question:
In the opinion, the court says it reviews denials of JMOL de novo under Fifth Circuit law, citing Wi‑LAN and Apple cases. I’m confused about how the de novo standard works in practice: does the appellate court look at the evidence itself, or only at whether the district court made a legal error? How does the “substantial evidence” test interact with the de novo review?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Federal Circuit applies **de novo review** to a district court’s denial of a Rule 50(a) motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of non‑infringement, but the *substantial‑evidence* standard still governs the underlying factual findings that the district court relied on.  

### 1. De novo review of the JMOL decision  

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the court states:

> “We review a district court’s denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit, here the Fifth Circuit. The Fifth Circuit reviews denials of JMOL **de novo**, reversing ‘only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.’”  
> *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016) (citing Fifth Circuit law).

“De novo” means the appellate court makes its own legal determination about whether the elements of the claim are satisfied. It does **not** defer to the district court’s legal analysis of the claim‑construction or the burden‑of‑proof questions. The Federal Circuit therefore asks:

1. **Did the plaintiff meet the burden of proving each claim limitation?**  
2. **If the plaintiff’s evidence is insufficient, would a reasonable jury, viewing the record in the light most favorable to the verdict, have been compelled to find infringement?**

If the answer to (2) is “no,” the appellate court reverses the denial of JMOL.

### 2. How the appellate court treats the evidence  

Although the legal conclusion is reviewed de novo, the **evidence itself is not re‑examined in the abstract**. The Federal Circuit looks at the record **as the jury saw it**—the testimony, expert reports, and exhibits—to decide whether a reasonable jury could have reached the infringement verdict. This is the “substantial‑evidence” step.

The opinion explains the interaction:

> “We review a jury’s finding of infringement for **substantial evidence**. ‘A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.’”  
> *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN Inc.*, 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022).

Thus, the appellate court:

* **Applies the de novo legal standard** (e.g., the meaning of “signals of interest” or the requirement that three separately identifiable signals be multiplied seven times).  
* **Simultaneously asks whether the factual record meets the substantial‑evidence threshold** for each of those legally‑defined elements. If the record is so lacking that no reasonable jury could have found infringement, the denial of JMOL is reversed—even though the court is not “re‑weighing” the evidence in the way a trial judge would.

### 3. Practical effect in *Finesse*  

For the ’134 patent, the Federal Circuit examined Dr. Wells’s testimony and concluded that the expert “never testified that x1 and x2 correspond to the ‘signals of interest’ and ‘interference generating signals.’” Because the record did not contain **substantial evidence** that the accused radios sampled both types of signals, the court found that a reasonable jury could not have reached an infringement verdict and therefore reversed the denial of JMOL.

For the ’775 patent, the court looked at the expert’s explanation of the seven required multiplications. It held that the evidence was “confusing and contradictory” and that “no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications.” Again, the legal issue (whether the claim requires seven distinct multiplications) was decided de novo, but the factual determination that the evidence did not meet the substantial‑evidence standard drove the reversal.

### 4. Summary of the standard  

| Step | Standard | What the appellate court does |
|------|----------|-------------------------------|
| **Legal determination** (claim construction, element‑by‑element analysis) | **De novo** | Analyzes the law afresh, independent of the district court’s legal reasoning. |
| **Factual support for each element** | **Substantial‑evidence** (Fifth Circuit) | Reviews the trial record to see whether a reasonable jury could have found the element satisfied. If the evidence is insufficient, the JMOL denial is reversed. |

In short, the Federal Circuit’s de novo review of a JMOL denial means it decides the legal questions itself, but it still requires that the underlying evidentiary record contain **substantial evidence** that would have allowed a reasonable jury to reach the infringement conclusion. If that evidentiary threshold is not met, the appellate court will reverse the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL.

### How should the claim term “signals of interest” be construed under the ’134 patent, and why was the expert’s testimony considered insufficient?
Question:
The court discusses the expert mapping the downlink TX reference to “signals of interest” and the modeled PIM path to “interference generating signals.” It then says the expert never actually linked x1/x2 to those claim terms. What is the proper construction of “signals of interest” under the patent, and why does the lack of a direct link cause the jury’s finding to lack substantial evidence?
Answer:
**Answer**

The proper claim construction of “signals of interest” in the ’134 patent is the **over‑sampled down‑link transmit (DL‑TX) reference that is actually present in the radio’s received pass‑band before the analog‑to‑digital conversion**.  The Federal Circuit’s opinion makes clear that the term is limited by claim‑language 1[a] to “received signals … that include **signals of interest** and **interference‑generating signals**,” and that the alleged infringer must **sample** both kinds of signals in the same pass‑band.  The patent does not define “signals of interest” more broadly than the signals that the receiver is trying to recover (the desired down‑link communications), and the court repeatedly notes that the expert, Dr. Wells, *only* identified the DL‑TX reference as the “signal of interest” when he was relying on the Nokia document’s legend (“Red – DL(TX) reference & modeled PIM path”).

Why the jury’s finding of infringement is unsupported is because the expert never **connected** the concrete hardware signals that enter the RF‑ADC (the two inputs labelled *x1* and *x2*) with the claim terms.  The district court’s JMOL analysis, affirmed by the Federal Circuit, hinges on two requirements:

1. **Sampling requirement ( limitation 1[a] )** – The accused device must sample a pass‑band that contains both the “signals of interest” **and** the “interference‑generating signals.”  The court observes that the “modeled PIM path” (the expert’s only explicit mapping of an “interference‑generating signal”) is **generated downstream of the ADC**; it does **not exist** at the point of sampling.  Consequently, if the “modeled PIM path” is taken as the “interference‑generating signal,” the ADC never samples it, and limitation 1[a] is not satisfied.

2. **Linkage requirement** – For an expert’s opinion to satisfy the burden of proof, the expert must **directly associate** each claim element with a corresponding element in the accused device.  The opinion states that Dr. Wells “never testified that *x1* and *x2* map onto the ‘signals of interest’ and ‘interference‑generating signals’” (see J.A. 15010‑19).  Instead, he equivocated, saying the red path contains “two signals” and that those two signals are the DL‑TX reference **and** the modeled PIM path, but he never said *x1* = DL‑TX reference or *x2* = modeled PIM path.  The court calls this “confusing and unclear” testimony and notes that Finesse’s inference that the two inputs must correspond to the claim terms required “piecing together parts of Dr. Wells’ testimony over ninety pages apart.”

Because the expert’s testimony fails to make the **necessary one‑to‑one correspondence**, the evidence is **self‑contradictory** and does not rise to the level of “substantial evidence” required to support a jury’s verdict.  The Federal Circuit cites *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, emphasizing that where the party with the burden of proof rests its case on an expert whose opinion is internally inconsistent, a reasonable jury could not have found infringement.  Accordingly, the court reversed the district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) and held that the jury’s finding of infringement of the ’134 patent is not supported by substantial evidence.

**Bottom line**

- “Signals of interest” = the down‑link transmit reference that is present in the received pass‑band **before** sampling.
- The expert never linked the actual ADC inputs (*x1*, *x2*) to that term (or to the “interference‑generating signals”), and his only explicit mapping (the modeled PIM path) cannot satisfy the sampling limitation because it is generated **after** sampling.
- That lack of a direct, unambiguous link means the expert’s opinion does not meet the burden of proof, so the jury’s infringement finding lacks substantial evidence and must be set aside.

### Why does the Federal Circuit consider the amendment to “three signals S1, S2, S3” in the ’775 patent not a clear disavowal of two‑signal embodiments?
Question:
Finesse amended the claims during prosecution to recite three signals instead of “two or three signals.” The court says this is not a clear and unambiguous disavowal. What legal standard governs whether a claim amendment disavows prior claim scope, and how did the court apply it here?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Federal Circuit applies the “clear‑and‑unambiguous disavowal” test when it decides whether a claim amendment during prosecution surrenders (i.e., disavows) prior claim scope.  The standard is found in *Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019), which holds that an amendment disavows earlier scope only if the change is **express, unmistakable, and leaves no room for the broader reading** that existed before the amendment.  Mere narrowing of language is not enough; the patentee must “make it clear to a person of ordinary skill in the art that the inventor no longer wishes to claim the broader subject matter.”  The Federal Circuit treats the question as a matter of claim construction, applying *KSR*‑style “clear‑and‑unambiguous” language rather than a simple “any narrowing” rule.

### How the court applied the test to the ’775 patent

1. **What the amendment said**  
   During prosecution the claims were changed from “digitally multiplying **two or three** signals” to recite “digitally multiplying **three** signals S1, S2, S3” (see the record at J.A. 1006‑10).  The amendment was therefore a *re‑phrasing* that added the requirement that the three signals be “separately identifiable” (J.A. 968‑69) but did **not** add any language indicating that the three signals must be *distinct* input signals.

2. **What the “clear‑and‑unambiguous” test looks for**  
   The court asks whether the amendment contains language that *explicitly* removes the possibility of two‑signal embodiments.  In *Cont’l Circuits*, the Federal Circuit emphasized that a disavowal must be “express and unmistakable,” such as “the claim now recites ‘three distinct signals’” or a limitation that “the three signals must be different from one another.”  Absent such language, the broader two‑signal reading remains part of the claim’s ordinary meaning.

3. **Why the amendment did not satisfy the test**  
   - The amendment **only** required “three signals S1, S2, S3” to be *separately identifiable*; it **did not** say they must be *different* or *unique* (J.A. 968‑69).  
   - The prosecution history (the examiner’s rejection, the applicant’s response, and the Notice of Allowance) never contain a statement that the invention is limited to three *unique* input signals.  The applicant merely argued that the prior art (Filipovic) taught only two signals and therefore the claim was narrowed to overcome the obviousness rejection, but it never said “we no longer claim two‑signal embodiments.”  
   - The court therefore concluded that the amendment “does not rise to the level of ‘clear and unambiguous’ disavowal of claim scope over products…with two unique input signals” (see the opinion, *cont’l Circuits* citation, 915 F.3d at 798).

4. **Resulting construction**  
   Because the amendment failed the disavowal test, the claim was construed to require “three separately identifiable signals” but **without** a requirement that those signals be distinct.  Consequently, the plaintiff could not rely on the amendment to defeat a non‑infringement argument that the accused radios use only two unique signals (x1 and x2) plus a copy of one of them.

### Bottom line

The governing standard is the “clear‑and‑unambiguous disavowal” test from *Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel*.  The Federal Circuit applied it by looking for express language in the amendment (or in the specification or prosecution history) that unmistakably eliminated two‑signal embodiments.  Because the amendment merely required three “separately identifiable” signals and said nothing about distinctness, the court held that the amendment **did not** constitute a clear disavowal, and thus the claim scope still covered two‑signal configurations.

### What is the requirement for the “seven third‑order multiplications” limitation in the ’775 patent, and how did the court determine the evidence was insufficient?
Question:
The opinion notes the patent requires seven specific multiplications of three signals, but the Nokia document only shows three distinct multiplications (and ten total). How must a party prove that the accused device performs the seven multiplications, and why did the court find the expert’s mapping inadequate?
Answer:
**Answer**

The ’775 patent’s independent claim requires that the accused device **digitally multiply three signals (S1, S2, S3) in *seven* distinct third‑order products** in order to generate the intermodulation‑product‑cancellation (ICS) signals.  The limitation is found in claim 1(d):

> “…generating an n‑th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering **S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3** and **S2×S3×S3**, where n is an integer.”  (Fed. Cir. opinion, ¶ B‑B)

Thus, to satisfy the claim a party must show:

1. **Three separately identifiable signals** are present in the receiver (the court held that “separately identifiable” does not require the signals to be *unique*; they may be copies of one another).  Id. at 115‑14.  
2. **Each of the seven third‑order products listed above is actually computed** by the accused radio.  The claim does not permit the patentee’s “seven” to be satisfied by a smaller set of distinct multiplications that are merely *re‑used* or *re‑ordered*; the specification teaches that each product is a separate operation that contributes to the cancellation algorithm.  Id. at 16:54‑17:6.

### How a party proves the requirement

Proof must be **positive, concrete evidence** that the accused device performs all seven products.  In practice this means:

* **A sworn expert testimony** (or other admissible evidence) that identifies the internal signal‑processing chain and points to the exact hardware or firmware blocks that compute each of the seven products.  
* **A mapping** that links each product in the claim to a corresponding operation in the device (e.g., “the block that multiplies x₁ × x₁ × x₂ implements the claim’s S1×S1×S2”).  The mapping must be *unambiguous*; the expert cannot rely on speculation or on implicit equivalence.

### Why the court found the evidence insufficient

The Court of Appeals held that the evidence offered by Finesse’s expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, **did not satisfy either of these evidentiary burdens** for the ’775 patent:

1. **The Nokia document only displayed three distinct multiplications** – namely  
   *x₁·|x₁|·|x₁|*, *x₁·x₁·x₂′*, and *x₁·|x₂|·|x₂|* – and **listed a total of ten multiplications** (p(0) through p(9)).  The record contains **no indication** that any of the three distinct multiplications are *repeated* to generate the seven specific products required by the claim.  Id. at 26486 (reproduced diagram).

2. **No expert testimony linked the three distinct multiplications to the seven claim limitations**.  Dr. Wells merely asserted that “the three distinct multiplications correspond to the seven claimed multiplications” but **failed to explain the mapping**.  The opinion notes that he “did not testify how those three distinct multiplications map onto the seven claimed multiplications” (Id. at 14966‑70).  Without a clear one‑to‑one correspondence, a jury could not reasonably conclude that the accused radios performed all seven products.

3. **The ten‑multiplication list in the Nokia document was never tied to the claim’s seven products**.  Finesse argued on appeal that the device actually performs ten multiplications, so “seven of them must be present.”  However, **the expert never identified which ten, or which subset of ten, correspond to the seven enumerated in the patent** (Id. at 15025‑29).  The Court therefore found the argument to be speculative.

Because the burden of proof rests on the plaintiff (Finesse) to show infringement, and because the expert’s testimony was **confusing, contradictory, and lacking the required detailed mapping**, the Court concluded that **no reasonable jury could have found the accused radios meet the seven‑multiplication limitation**.  The Court applied the “substantial‑evidence” standard for JMOL and held that the record **lacked the necessary evidence** (Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp., 543 F.3d at 1349), prompting reversal of the district court’s JMOL denial.

**In short:** To satisfy the ’775 claim a party must produce evidence that the accused device actually computes each of the seven third‑order products listed in the claim.  The expert testimony in this case fell short because it identified only three distinct multiplications, offered no concrete mapping to the seven required products, and never explained how the ten products shown in the Nokia document related to the claim.  Consequently, the Court held the evidence insufficient and granted JMOL of non‑infringement.

### How does the Federal Circuit treat expert testimony that is internally contradictory when assessing infringement?
Question:
The court cites Johns Hopkins v. Datascope for rejecting contradictory expert testimony. In this case, Dr. Wells gave conflicting explanations about which signals correspond to the claim elements. What is the legal precedent for evaluating contradictory expert testimony, and what threshold must be met for a jury’s verdict to be supported by substantial evidence?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Federal Circuit follows two well‑settled doctrines when it confronts expert testimony that is internally inconsistent:

1. **The “no reasonable jury” standard for contradictory expert opinions** – see *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, 543 F.3d 1342, 1349 (Fed. Cir. 2008).  
2. **The “substantial‑evidence” test for a jury’s factual findings** – see *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN, Inc.*, 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022) (quoting *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016)).

The opinion in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, 2025 WL … (Fed. Cir. Sep. 24 2025), applies both of those doctrines to the expert testimony of Dr. Jonathan Wells.

---

### 1. Legal precedent for evaluating contradictory expert testimony  

The Court explicitly relies on *Johns Hopkins* for the rule that “when the party with the burden of proof rests its case on an expert’s self‑contradictory testimony, we may conclude the evidence is insufficient to satisfy that standard.” 543 F.3d at 1349.  The *Johns Hopkins* decision held that a jury cannot be instructed to accept a conclusion that is directly contradicted by the expert’s own testimony, because no reasonable juror could reconcile the inconsistency. The Federal Circuit therefore treats such testimony as **insufficient to meet the plaintiff’s burden of proving infringement**.

In *Finesse*, the Court points out that Dr. Wells repeatedly identified the “signals of interest” as the down‑link transmit reference **and** the “interference‑generating signals” as the modeled PIM path, only to later claim that the two signals on the red path (x1 and x2) are the relevant inputs without ever mapping x1 or x2 onto the statutory terms.  The opinion calls this “confusing change of course” and states that it “is not sufficient to support the jury verdict.”  The Court therefore follows *Johns Hopkins* and treats Wells’s contradictory statements as fatal to the infringement finding.

---

### 2. Threshold for a jury’s verdict to be supported by substantial evidence  

The second issue is the evidentiary standard the Court uses to decide whether a jury’s verdict can stand.  The Federal Circuit adopts the “substantial‑evidence” test: a factual finding is supported by substantial evidence when “a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.”  *Apple* at 969 (citing *Wi‑LAN* at 461).  This is a **low‑threshold, deferential standard** – the jury’s view need not be the only possible view, only one that is reasonable given the record.

Applying that standard, the Court in *Finesse* concludes that the record **lacked substantial evidence** that the accused radios sampled both “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals.”  The only testimony linking a claimed limitation to a device feature was Dr. Wells’s inconsistent identification of the same physical signals in two different ways.  Because the underlying expert evidence was contradictory, a reasonable jury could not have concluded infringement.  Consequently, the jury’s verdict fails the substantial‑evidence test, and the Court grants JMOL of non‑infringement.

---

### Take‑aways for you

* **Contradictory expert testimony** – The Federal Circuit treats it under *Johns Hopkins*: if an expert’s own statements negate the factual element the plaintiff must prove, no reasonable jury can be found to have relied on that testimony.  
* **Substantial‑evidence threshold** – Even after a jury’s verdict, the appellate court asks whether the evidence as a whole would permit a reasonable jury to reach that conclusion.  If the expert’s testimony is self‑contradictory, the record will not contain the requisite substantial evidence, and JMOL is appropriate.

Thus, in *Finesse* the Court combined the *Johns Hopkins* rule on contradictory expert opinions with the *Apple*/*Wi‑LAN* substantial‑evidence standard to reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL.

### What is the proper procedural basis for granting a new trial on damages after a JMOL of noninfringement is entered?
Question:
The district court denied a new trial on damages, but the appellate court vacated the damages award after reversing the JMOL. If a claim is ultimately found non‑infringing, does a new trial on damages become moot, or are there separate standards for vacating a damages award?
Answer:
**Answer**

When a district court’s judgment‑as‑a‑matter‑of‑law (JMOL) of non‑infringement is reversed on appeal, the damages award that was based on the jury’s finding of infringement is automatically vacated.  The Federal Circuit’s opinion in **Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC**, 2025 WL ‑‑ (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24 2025), makes this clear.

### 1.  The procedural link between infringement and damages

The court explains that a damages award “rests on” the jury’s finding that the defendant infringed the patent.  (See **Finesse**, ¶ II.)  Because the appellate court reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of non‑infringement for *all* asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, it concluded that the jury’s infringement verdict was not supported by substantial evidence.  Consequently, the judgment‑as‑a‑matter‑of‑law that should have been entered in the district court would have been *no infringement* and therefore *no damages*.

The Federal Circuit therefore *vacated* the $166‑million damages award.  The opinion does not describe a separate “new‑trial on damages” analysis; it simply states that once infringement is found to be absent, the damages award must be set aside.

### 2.  Separate standards do exist, but they become moot once non‑infringement is entered

- **JMOL of non‑infringement** – reviewed de novo; the appellate court looks at whether, viewing the evidence most favorably to the verdict, a reasonable juror could have found infringement.  (**Finesse**, ¶ I.A & ¶ I.B).  The Federal Circuit found the evidence was insufficient for both patents and reversed the denial of JMOL.

- **New‑trial on damages** – reviewed for abuse of discretion.  (**Finesse**, ¶ II).  The district court’s denial of a new trial on damages was therefore reviewed under a less stringent standard.

The opinion makes it clear that the *abuse‑of‑discretion* review of a new‑trial motion is *irrelevant* once the underlying infringement finding is overturned.  Because the appellate court entered a judgment of non‑infringement, the damages award “has no basis” and is vacated without further analysis of the new‑trial standard.

### 3.  Practical consequence for the moving party

If you are seeking a new trial on damages after a JMOL of non‑infringement is entered, you should:

1. **Ask the appellate court to vacate the damages award** (as the Federal Circuit did).  
2. **Do not rely on a new‑trial‑on‑damages argument** because the damages claim is legally dead once infringement is eliminated.

In short, under **Finesse**, the proper procedural basis is a *vacatur* of the damages judgment that follows directly from the reversal of the JMOL of infringement.  A separate new‑trial analysis is unnecessary and, in effect, moot.

### Why did the Federal Circuit reverse the district court’s JMOL denial for both the ’134 and ’775 patents, and what does ‘substantial evidence’ mean in this context?
Question:
The opinion repeatedly states the jury’s verdict was not supported by substantial evidence for both patents. I’m trying to understand what level of evidence is required to survive a JMOV (judgment as a matter of view) challenge and how the court applied that standard to the expert’s testimony for each patent.
Answer:
**Answer**

The Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s denial of judgment‑as‑a‑matter‑of‑law (JMOL) for both the ’134 and ’775 patents because the jury’s finding of infringement was *not* supported by the amount of evidence that the law requires—namely, “substantial evidence.”  The opinion explains that the district court’s factual determinations are reviewed for “substantial evidence,” not for a higher “preponderance” or “beyond a reasonable doubt” standard.  

Below is a step‑by‑step breakdown of the court’s reasoning and the meaning of “substantial evidence” in this context.

---

### 1. The legal standard for JMOL review

1. **De‑novo review of the legal question** – The Federal Circuit reviews the district court’s denial of JMOL de novo, but the *factual* basis for the jury’s verdict is examined under the “substantial evidence” test.  (¶ I, **Wi‑LAN** citation.)

2. **Substantial‑evidence test** – A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence when “a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.”  The court emphasizes that the evidence must be “so strong and overwhelming” that no reasonable jury could reach a contrary conclusion.  (¶ I‑A, **Apple v. Wi‑LAN** citation.)

3. **Burden of proof** – The plaintiff (Finesse) must prove each claim limitation, including that the accused radios **sample both “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals.”** (¶ A, ’134 patent, limitation 1[a]).  

---

### 2. Why the ’134 patent verdict lacked substantial evidence

* **Expert testimony was contradictory and incomplete.**  
  - Dr. Wells identified two signals on the RF‑ADC input (“the red path”) and called them “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals.” (J.A. 15010‑11).  
  - However, when pressed, he was unable to map the abstract claim terms to concrete elements of the radios. He repeatedly shifted between calling the **DL‑TX reference** the “signal of interest” and the **modeled PIM path** the “interference‑generating signal,” even after being told that the modeled PIM path is **generated downstream of the ADC** and therefore cannot be sampled. (J.A. 15010‑19).  

* **The district court’s “sufficient evidence” determination was misplaced.**  
  - The court concluded the jury could infer that the ADC sampled both signals because it was “persuaded” by the plaintiff’s argument that the modeled PIM path was *not* the interference‑generating signal. (J.A. 7‑16).  
  - The Federal Circuit rejected that inference, holding that the only explicit mapping in the record was **the modeled PIM path → interference‑generating signals**. Since the PIM path is created *after* sampling, the essential limitation 1[a] is not satisfied.  

* **Result:** Because the expert’s testimony did not convincingly establish that the radios actually sampled the required signals, the evidence fell far short of the “overwhelming” level required for substantial evidence. Accordingly, the jury’s infringement finding for the ’134 patent was unsupported. (¶ B, ’134 patent.)

---

### 3. Why the ’775 patent verdict lacked substantial evidence

* **Claim limitation at issue:** The patent requires **seven third‑order multiplications of three separately identifiable signals (S1, S2, S3).** (¶ B, ’775 patent.)

* **Expert’s evidence was insufficient to show the accused radios performed those seven multiplications.**  
  - Dr. Wells testified that the radios multiply “three separately identifiable signals” and tried to equate the three distinct multiplications shown in Nokia’s document with the seven claimed multiplications. (J.A. 14965‑70).  
  - The Nokia document, however, lists **only three distinct multiplications** (e.g., *x1·|x1|·|x1|*, *x1·x1·x2'*, *x1·|x2|·|x2|*) and does **not** demonstrate that any of them are repeated to reach the required seven. (J.A. 26486).  
  - Neither Dr. Wells nor Finesse offered a mapping from those three multiplications (or from the ten multiplications later cited) to the seven claimed ones. (J.A. 15025‑29; oral argument).  

* **The district court’s “reasonable jury” conclusion was unsupported.**  
  - The court said the jury could rely on the expert’s inference that “each of those three distinct multiplications corresponds to two or three of the seven claimed multiplications.” (J.A. 38‑39).  
  - The Federal Circuit found that inference was speculative; without a concrete explanation linking the three (or ten) documented multiplications to the statutory seven, a reasonable jury could not have found infringement.  

* **Result:** The evidence did not rise to the “overwhelming” threshold needed for substantial evidence, so the verdict on the ’775 patent was also reversed. (¶ B, ’775 patent.)

---

### 4. What “substantial evidence” means here

In the context of a JMOL review, **“substantial evidence” is a low‑threshold evidentiary standard**—but it is not a “rubber‑stamp.” The evidence must be:

1. **Sufficiently strong** that a reasonable jury could have reached the verdict *in light of the entire record*; and  
2. **Not merely speculative or inconclusive**.   Evidence that hinges on unanswered “what‑if” questions, contradictory testimony, or missing factual links (as in both patents) fails the test.

The Federal Circuit therefore looked at the **totality** of Dr. Wells’s testimony, the technical documents, and the parties’ own admissions. Because the expert never definitively tied the accused radios to the patented limitations, the record lacked the “overwhelming” character required for a jury verdict to be upheld.

---

### 5. Bottom line

* The Federal Circuit reversed because **the expert evidence was contradictory, incomplete, and failed to meet the “substantial evidence” threshold** for each patent.  
* “Substantial evidence” here means **evidence that would allow a reasonable jury to infer infringement without speculation**—something the record did not provide for either the ’134 or the ’775 patents.  

Thus, the JMOL denials were improper, and the appellate court properly entered non‑infringement judgments for both patents.
