Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Chapter 1: Factual and Procedural Foundations  

## The Patents: U.S. Pat. Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775 – Technical Field and Inventorship  

Finesse Wireless LLC (“Finesse”) is the assignee of two patents that spring from the same technological niche: the mitigation of intermodulation‑product interference in radio‑frequency (RF) devices.  Patent 7,346,134 (the “’134 patent”) and Patent 9,548,775 (the “’775 patent”) were both filed by inventors employed by Finesse and later assigned to the company.  The ’134 patent claims a method that samples a received RF passband, isolates the “signals of interest” from “interference‑generating signals,” computes an estimate of the intermodulation products (IMPs) that would arise from the latter, and then cancels those IMPs in a closed‑loop fashion.  The ’775 patent, issued later, recites a similar overall goal—cancelling passive IMPs—but focuses on the digital manipulation of three baseband signals (labeled S1, S2, and S3) in a series of seven third‑order multiplications to generate interference‑cancellation signals (ICSs).  

Both patents belong to the field of RF engineering, specifically to equipment that both transmits and receives on the same platform (e.g., a handset) and therefore must manage unwanted spurious signals that arise when two or more transmit signals mix in nonlinear components.  The technical problem addressed is the creation of a “PIM‑C” (passive intermodulation cancellation) feature that can identify and suppress those spurious products in real time.

## Parties: Patent Owner and Alleged Infringers  

Finesse, as the exclusive owner of the ’134 and ’775 patents, brought the case in the Eastern District of Texas.  AT&T Mobility LLC (“AT&T”) was named a direct defendant because its commercial cellular radios, sold under the AT&T brand, were alleged to embody the claimed methods.  Nokia of America Corporation (“Nokia”) intervened as a necessary party because the radios at issue were designed and manufactured by Nokia and incorporated the so‑called PIM‑C feature.  Both AT&T and Nokia are collectively referred to as the “Appellants” on appeal.

## Key Technical Concepts  

### Intermodulation Products (IMP)  

When two or more RF signals travel simultaneously through a nonlinear element (such as a passive metal connector, a rusted joint, or any component that does not obey a perfectly linear voltage‑current relationship), the signals can mix and create new frequencies that are linear combinations of the originals.  For example, with input frequencies f₁ and f₂, a third‑order product might appear at 2f₁ − f₂.  These spurious frequencies are called **intermodulation products** (IMPs).  

### Passive Intermodulation (PIM)  

If the nonlinear element is **passive**—meaning it does not actively amplify the signal (e.g., a metal fence, a connector, or a rusted joint)—the resulting IMPs are termed **passive intermodulation products** (PIM).  PIM is especially problematic in dense cellular environments because the spurious frequencies often fall within the allocated band for the “signals of interest,” degrading reception and throughput.  

### PIM Cancellation (PIM‑C)  

Modern radios can mitigate PIM by digitally estimating the expected IMPs and injecting an opposite‑phase signal that cancels them out.  This process requires the radio to (1) sample the incoming RF spectrum, (2) separate the desired communication signals from the potential interference‑generating signals, (3) compute an estimate of the resulting IMPs, and (4) subtract that estimate from the received waveform in a closed‑loop control system.  The two patents at issue each describe a different implementation of this general concept.  

## Trial‑Court Proceedings: Jury Verdict and Post‑Trial Motions  

The case proceeded to a bench‑trial in the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas.  After a full trial, the jury returned a **unanimous verdict** finding that:  

1. All asserted claims of the ’134 patent (claims 1‑3) were **valid** and **infringed** by the accused Nokia radios; and  
2. All asserted claims of the ’775 patent (claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, 36) were **valid** and **infringed**.  

The jury then awarded **$166,303,391** in lump‑sum damages, representing the present value of royalty payments for the remaining life of both patents.  

Following the verdict, the Appellants filed several post‑trial motions:  

* **Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) of Non‑infringement** – arguing that, as a matter of law, the evidence did not satisfy essential claim limitations, particularly the requirement that the accused radios sample both “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals” (for the ’134 patent) and that they perform the seven third‑order multiplications of three distinct signals (for the ’775 patent).  

* **Motion for JMOL on Damages** – contending that the jury’s damages calculation was unsupported.  

* **Motion for a New Trial on Damages** – seeking a fresh determination of the appropriate award.  

The district court **denied** all three motions.  The court held that the expert testimony of Dr. Jonathan Wells, who linked the technical description in a Nokia internal document to the patent limitations, created a sufficient evidentiary basis for the jury’s findings.  Consequently, the verdict and damages award stood.

## Appeal: Jurisdiction and Issues Presented  

Under **28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1)**, the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit has exclusive jurisdiction over appeals from final decisions of district courts in patent cases.  The Appellants therefore appealed the district court’s denials of JMOL and new‑trial motions to the Federal Circuit.  

The **notice of appeal** identified two principal issues:  

1. **Whether the district court erred in denying JMOL of non‑infringement** for each asserted claim of the ’134 and ’775 patents.  This issue required the appellate court to evaluate the adequacy of the expert testimony and the logical connection (or lack thereof) between the accused radios’ architecture and the patent claim language.  

2. **Whether the district court abused its discretion in denying a new trial on damages** after finding infringement.  Because the court eventually reversed the infringement findings, the damages issue became moot, but it was still raised and addressed on the record.  

The Federal Circuit’s review approach follows Fifth‑Circuit precedent (the controlling regional circuit for the Eastern District of Texas).  For JMOL denials, the appellate court reviews **de novo** the legal question of whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict, can support a jury finding beyond a reasonable doubt.  Substantive factual findings—such as the credibility of expert testimony—are reviewed for **substantial evidence**.  The court’s analysis therefore hinges on whether a reasonable jury could have concluded that the accused radios met each claim limitation, given the expert’s statements and the underlying technical record.  

By establishing the factual backdrop—patents, parties, technical concepts, trial outcomes, and the procedural path to the Federal Circuit—this chapter equips the reader with the necessary context to follow the appellate court’s reasoning on infringement and damages in the subsequent portions of the opinion.

# Chapter 2: Legal Framework for JMOL and Claim Construction  

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* the Federal Circuit was called upon to review the district court’s denial of a judgment‑as‑a‑matter‑of‑law (JMOL) motion and the court’s claim‑construction rulings.  Understanding the standards that govern those reviews is essential for any law student who must parse the opinion’s reasoning.  This chapter explains the applicable JMOL standard, the burden of proof that plaintiff‑incumbents face in infringement cases, the mechanics of claim construction, the Fifth Circuit precedents that shaped the panel’s analysis, and the way expert testimony fits into the factual record.  

## JMOL Standard: De Novo Review of the Evidence and the “Substantial Evidence” Test for Jury Findings  

A motion for JMOL under Rule 50(a) asks the trial court to enter judgment for a party notwithstanding the jury’s verdict.  The Federal Circuit, following Fifth Circuit precedent, reviews a district court’s denial of a JMOL motion **de novo** with respect to the legal conclusions (e.g., claim construction) and with respect to the evidentiary record using the “substantial evidence” test for factual findings.  *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016).  

*De novo* review means the appellate court does not defer to the trial court’s assessment of what the evidence “means”; it looks at the evidence anew, applying the governing legal standards.  However, when the district court has already drawn a factual conclusion—such as whether a particular expert testimony proves that a device meets a claim limitation—the appellate court must ask whether that conclusion is **supported by substantial evidence**.  Substantial evidence is “such relevant evidence that a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party,” *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN Inc.*, 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022).  

In *Finesse*, the Federal Circuit applied this two‑step approach.  First, it examined the claim‑construction rulings (a legal question) de novo.  Second, it evaluated whether the jury’s finding of infringement was supported by substantial evidence.  The court concluded that the evidence—principally the conflicting testimony of Dr. Jonathan Wells—was so ambiguous that a reasonable jury could not have reliably concluded that the accused radios satisfied the asserted claim limitations.  Accordingly, the jury verdict lacked substantial evidence, justifying reversal of the JMOL denial.  

## Burden of Proof for Infringement – Plaintiff’s Responsibility to Prove Each Claim Limitation  

In patent infringement actions, the plaintiff bears the **entire burden of proving every limitation of each asserted claim** by a preponderance of the evidence.  The plaintiff must show that the accused device (or method) includes every element of the claim as construed.  *Bayer Int’l Inc. v. Solis Biol. Comp. Inc.*, 471 F.3d 1268, 1272 (Fed. Cir. 2006).  

If even a single limitation is not satisfied, the claim is not infringed.  The burden does not shift to the defendant to prove non‑infringement; the plaintiff simply must establish infringement.  In *Finesse*, the plaintiff (Finesse) was required to prove that the radios sampled **both** “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals” (the first limitation of the ’134 patent) and that the radios performed **seven distinct third‑order multiplications** of three separately identifiable signals (the pivotal limitation of the ’775 patent).  The Federal Circuit emphasized that the plaintiff’s reliance on an expert who gave contradictory explanations failed to meet this burden.  Because the expert could not definitively map the alleged “signals of interest” or the seven multiplications onto the accused hardware, the plaintiff did not demonstrate that the accused radios satisfied those claim elements.  

## Claim Construction Principles: Markman De Novo Review, Role of Intrinsic and Extrinsic Evidence, “Clear and Unambiguous” Disavowal Doctrine  

Claim construction—often called a “Markman” hearing after *Markman v. Westview Instruments, Inc.*, 517 U.S. \(1996\)—is a legal question.  The court must **interpret the patent’s language**, constructing each disputed term **de novo**.  The primary source of meaning is the **intrinsic record**: the patent’s claims, specification, and prosecution history.  Extrinsic evidence—such as expert testimony, dictionaries, or prior art—may be considered, but only to aid interpretation and never to override a clear intrinsic meaning.  *Phillips v. AWH Corp.*, 415 F.3d 1303, 1311 (Fed. Cir. 2005).  

When the patentee’s amendments or statements during prosecution **clearly and unambiguously** disavow a particular scope, the court must interpret the claim accordingly.  This “clear‑and‑unambiguous” disavowal doctrine prevents the patentee from later asserting a broader interpretation than was surrendered to obtain the patent.  *Clear‑and‑unambiguous* disavowal is a high bar; ordinary prosecutorial arguments or amendments that lack explicit language do not automatically constitute a surrender of claim scope.  *Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019).  

In the present case, the Fifth Circuit had previously construed the ’775 claim term “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable” signals, but not “unique” signals.  The Federal Circuit upheld that construction because the intrinsic record (the specification and prosecution history) did not contain language expressly limiting the three signals to be distinct physical inputs.  The court also upheld the district court’s construction of the ’134 claim, which required the ADC to sample a passband containing both categories of signals.  Because those constructions were legal determinations, the Federal Circuit reviewed them **de novo**, confirming they were consistent with the intrinsic record.  

## Fifth Circuit Precedent on JMOL and Claim Construction Reviewed by the Federal Circuit  

The Federal Circuit’s approach in *Finesse* draws heavily on Fifth Circuit authority, which governs the Eastern District of Texas.  The Fifth Circuit’s JMOL standard requires that, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, **the evidence must point “so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.”**  *Wi‑LAN*, 811 F.3d at 461.  This is a stringent test; it is not enough that the plaintiff’s case is weak— the evidence must be essentially foreclosed to the defendant’s position.  

On claim construction, the Fifth Circuit follows the Federal Circuit’s Markman framework but adds that **“any underlying factual findings supporting the court’s construction are reviewed for clear error.”**  *Wi‑LAN*, 811 F.3d at 461.  Thus, while the construction itself is de novo, the factual basis (e.g., statements in the file history) is examined for clear error.  

In *Finesse*, the Federal Circuit acknowledged that the district court’s claim constructions were made pursuant to Fifth Circuit law, and it affirmed them because the court’s reasoning was consistent with both the intrinsic record and Fifth Circuit precedent.  The appellate review of the JMOL denial, however, applied the more demanding “substantial evidence” standard, concluding that the jury’s verdict was not supported by such evidence.  

## Interaction Between Expert Testimony and the Court’s Factual Findings  

Expert testimony is often the linchpin of a patent infringement case because it translates technical subject matter into the language of the patent claims.  Yet an expert’s opinion is **subject to the same evidentiary standards** as any other fact.  The jury may accept an expert’s conclusions only if the testimony is **reliable** (Daubert) and **sufficiently clear** to allow a factfinder to apply the claim limitations.  

In *Finesse*, Dr. Jonathan Wells testified that the accused radios sampled “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals” and that the radios performed the requisite seven multiplications.  However, his testimony was **self‑contradictory** and **indeterminate**.  On cross‑examination, he conceded that the “modeled PIM path” (the alleged interference‑generating signal) was generated **after** the ADC’s sampling point, making it impossible for the ADC to have sampled that signal.  Moreover, his explanation of how three signals were multiplied into seven specific products was vague, and he failed to map the three distinct multiplications identified in the Nokia document onto the seven claimed multiplications.  

The Federal Circuit treated these deficiencies as fatal to the plaintiff’s burden of proof.  Because the expert could not provide a **coherent factual link** between the accused device’s operation and each claim limitation, the expert’s testimony did not constitute **substantial evidence** of infringement.  The court therefore concluded that the jury’s verdict could not be supported by the record, justifying reversal of the JMOL denial.  

### Take‑aways for Students  

1. **JMOL Review is Dual‑layered:** Legal questions (e.g., claim construction) receive de novo review; factual findings receive the substantial‑evidence test.  
2. **Plaintiff Bears the Full Burden:** Every claim limitation must be proved by a preponderance of the evidence; contradictory expert testimony cannot satisfy that burden.  
3. **Claim Construction Is Legal, Not Factual:** The court interprets the patent’s language using intrinsic evidence; only clear‑and‑unambiguous disavowals narrow the scope.  
4. **Fifth Circuit Standards Influence Federal Circuit Review:** The “overwhelming evidence” standard for JMOL and the de novo approach to claim construction guide the appellate analysis.  
5. **Expert Testimony Must Be Clear and Consistent:** Courts will not treat ambiguous or self‑contradictory expert opinions as substantial evidence; the expert must bridge the gap between the technology and the claim language in a way the jury can reliably rely on.  

By mastering these principles, a law student will be equipped to follow the Federal Circuit’s reasoning in *Finesse Wireless* and to apply the same framework in future patent‑infringement appeals.

# Chapter 3: Infringement Analysis of the ’134 Patent  

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit was asked to determine whether the accused Nokia radios infringed claim 1 of Finesse’s ’134 patent, which recites a method for mitigating intermodulation‑product (IMP) interference by **sampling** a passband that contains both “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals.”  The court’s decision turns on the plaintiff’s burden of proof, the expert testimony offered by Dr. Jonathan Wells, and the application of the “substantial evidence” standard to the jury’s verdict.  This chapter walks through the court’s analysis step‑by‑step, showing how the appellate court concluded that the jury’s finding of infringement was not supported by the record.

---

## Claim‑1 Language of the ’134 Patent  

Claim 1 is the representative limitation for the ’134 patent.  It can be broken down into six functional steps:

1. **Oversampling** a passband of received signals at a desired frequency to produce a digital bit stream.  The passband must contain **both** (i) “signals of interest” (the desired communication signals) and (ii) “interference‑generating signals” that are capable of producing IMPs within the band of the signals of interest.  
2. **Isolating** the signals of interest in the bit stream by means of one or more decimating filters.  
3. **Isolating** the source signals that generate the IMPs (the interference‑generating signals) using one or more decimating filters.  
4. **Computing** an estimate of each IMP from the isolated source signals.  
5. **Cancelling** the in‑band IMPs by subtracting the computed estimate from the received signal.  
6. **Performing phase and amplitude adjustment** in a closed‑loop fashion, including sub‑sample phase shifts, to refine the cancellation.

The first step—oversampling a passband that includes *both* categories of signals—is essential.  If the accused device does not sample the interference‑generating signals, the claim limitation is not met, and there can be no literal infringement.  The patent’s construction of “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals” was adopted by the district court and was not altered on appeal.

---

## Finesse’s Burden: Proof that the Accused Radios Sample Both Signal Types  

In a patent‑infringement action, the patentee bears the burden of proving each claim limitation by a preponderance of the evidence.  For claim 1, the critical factual assertion is that the accused radios’ analog‑to‑digital converter (the RF ADC) **samples** a passband that contains the interference‑generating signals *before* they are generated by any downstream processing.

Because the burden rests on Finesse, the Federal Circuit reminded the parties that the district court must view the evidence “in the light most favorable to the verdict” but must also ensure that a reasonable jury could have reached the same conclusion.  The standard for reviewing a jury verdict on infringement is **substantial evidence**: a reasonable jury could have found for the prevailing party based on the evidence presented.  If the record fails to contain sufficient evidence that the required sampling occurs, the court must grant JMOL of non‑infringement.

---

## Expert Dr. Jonathan Wells’s Testimony – Mapping of DL(TX) Reference and Modeled PIM Path  

Finesse relied principally on the testimony of its infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells.  Dr. Wells introduced a Nokia technical document that depicted the signal flow of the accused radios.  The document’s legend identified a red pathway comprising two items:

* **DL(TX) reference** – the downlink transmit reference signal.  
* **Modeled PIM path** – a signal that represents the PIM that would be generated downstream.

Dr. Wells testified that the **DL(TX) reference** corresponds to the patent’s “signals of interest,” and that the **modeled PIM path** corresponds to the “interference‑generating signals.”  He repeatedly affirmed this mapping in direct examination, for example stating, “The downlink TX reference is the signal of interest” and “the modeled PIM path is the interference generating signal.”  His expert report echoed the same identification.

The purpose of this testimony was to satisfy limitation 1[a]—that the RF ADC samples a passband containing both signal types.  If the two items on the red path truly enter the ADC, the sampling requirement would be met.

---

## Cross‑Examination Revelations: The Modeled PIM Path Is Generated Downstream of the ADC  

Appellants’ counsel seized on a technical inconsistency.  During cross‑examination, they pointed out that the **modeled PIM path** is *generated* **after** the RF ADC, inside the GROOT field‑programmable gate array (FPGA).  In other words, the PIM signal is a **product of digital processing**, not a radio‑frequency signal present at the antenna input.  Because an ADC can only sample signals that exist **prior to** its conversion stage, a downstream‑generated PIM cannot be part of the sampled passband.

When confronted with this point, Dr. Wells altered his explanation.  He claimed that the red path contained “two signals … because we know that because there’s the x1 and the x2.”  However, he never linked **x1** or **x2** back to the patent’s “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals.”  Instead, he continued to identify the red path as the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path, without clarifying whether the ADC actually sees the latter.

This shift created a **conflict** in Dr. Wells’s testimony:

1. **Original mapping**: DL(TX) = signal of interest; modeled PIM = interference‑generating signal.  
2. **Cross‑examination correction**: the modeled PIM path cannot be sampled; therefore, the ADC does not receive an interference‑generating signal.  
3. **Subsequent clarification**: “x1 and x2 are the two inputs …” but no explicit association of x1/x2 to the patent terms.

The appellate court noted that the expert never provided a clear, consistent explanation that the ADC sampled both required signal types.  The testimony remained ambiguous even after the cross‑examination challenge.

---

## Court’s Assessment of the Contradictory and Ambiguous Testimony  

The Federal Circuit emphasized that **expert testimony must be clear enough to satisfy the factual element of infringement**.  The district court had found that Dr. Wells’s testimony “clearly” identified the two signals entering the ADC, but the appellate court disagreed.  The court evaluated the record and concluded:

* The only explicit identification of an “interference‑generating signal” was the **modeled PIM path**, which, by its own description, is **generated downstream** of the ADC.  
* Dr. Wells’s later attempt to salvage the testimony by invoking “x1 and x2” did not connect those labels to the claim terms; the expert never testified that x1 = signal of interest or x2 = interference‑generating signal.  
* The mapping relied on a **piecing‑together** of statements spread across a wide swath of the transcript, rather than a single, coherent explanation.

Because the burden of proof rested on Finesse, the court held that **confusing, self‑contradictory testimony cannot satisfy that burden**.  The Federal Circuit cited *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, underscoring that an expert’s contradictory statements can render an inference of infringement unreasonable.

---

## Application of the “Substantial Evidence” Standard  

Having identified the evidentiary gaps, the court turned to the standard for reviewing a jury verdict.  Under Fifth Circuit law, a jury’s finding of infringement must be supported by **substantial evidence**—evidence that a reasonable jury could rely upon to reach the same conclusion.  The appellate court asked: *Is there any reliable evidence that the RF ADC samples both a signal of interest and an interference‑generating signal?*

The answer was **no**:

* The only evidence of an interference‑generating signal was the modeled PIM path, which cannot be sampled.  
* No other expert testimony or documentary evidence established that the ADC receives a distinct interference‑generating signal.  
* The expert’s ambiguous statements do not rise to the level of “substantial” because they do not directly satisfy the claim limitation.

Consequently, the court concluded that a reasonable jury **could not** have found infringement on claim 1[a] based on the record.  The lack of substantial evidence required a judgment as a matter of law of non‑infringement.

---

## Resulting Reversal of the JMOL Denial for the ’134 Patent  

Because the evidence failed to meet the plaintiff’s burden, the Federal Circuit **reversed** the district court’s denial of JMOL of non‑infringement for the ’134 patent.  The appellate court vacated the jury’s infringement verdict for all asserted claims of that patent and, by implication, eliminated the associated damages award.

The decision illustrates two key principles for infringement analysis:

1. **Technical fidelity matters** – an expert must accurately describe the physical operation of the accused device relative to each claim limitation.  Mischaracterizing a downstream digital artifact as a pre‑sampling RF signal defeats the claim element.  
2. **Clear, consistent testimony is required** – when an expert’s testimony is internally contradictory, the court will not allow the jury to infer infringement; the burden remains on the patentee to provide a coherent factual foundation.

Understanding how the Federal Circuit applied claim construction, burden allocation, expert evaluation, and the substantial‑evidence standard provides a roadmap for analyzing infringement issues in future cases.

# Chapter 4: Infringement Analysis of the ’775 Patent  

In this chapter we walk a law student through the Federal Circuit’s reasoning on the second patent at issue in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*.  The focus is the claim‑construction and infringement analysis of the ’775 patent, specifically the contested limitation that the method must use **three separately identifiable signals** (S1, S2, S3) and must perform **seven third‑order multiplications** of those signals.  By unpacking the parties’ arguments, the expert testimony, and the court’s application of the “substantial evidence” standard, the student will see why the appellate court concluded that the jury’s finding of infringement was unsupported.  

---

## Claim‑1 Language of the ’775 Patent – Generation of IMP Cancellation Signals Using Three Signals and Seven Multiplications  

Claim 1 of the ’775 patent is directed to a method of cancelling passive intermodulation products (PIM) in a receiver that is co‑located with a transmitter.  The claim recites, in abbreviated form:  

1. **Generating IMP cancellation signals (ICSs) continuously and near real time** by using *copies* of transmitter signals;  
2. **Specifying that the passive IMPs are generated in passive components downstream of the high‑powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter**;  
3. **Describing a power‑series‑based, non‑linear model** for the IMP generation; and crucially,  

> “generating an n‑th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering  
> S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n is an integer.”

The claim therefore imposes **two technical requirements**:

* **Three separately identifiable signals** (S1, S2, S3) must be present in the digital processing step.  
* **Seven distinct third‑order multiplications**—the seven products listed above—must be performed on those signals to generate the cancellation signals.  

Both requirements are functional limitations; the plaintiff (Finesse) bears the burden of proving that the accused radios satisfy each limitation.  The Federal Circuit’s analysis hinges on how “separately identifiable” was construed and whether the expert testimony demonstrated the seven multiplications.

---

## Construction of “Three Separately Identifiable Signals” – Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Evidence  

### The district court’s construction  

At the summary‑judgment stage the district court interpreted “three separately identifiable signals” to mean **three signals that can be distinguished from one another** but **need not be distinct sources**.  The court relied on:

* **Intrinsic evidence** – the patent specification and the prosecution record, which described S1, S2, S3 as “signals” without requiring each to be a different physical input; and  
* **Extrinsic evidence** – the inventor’s testimony that the three signals could be the same signal duplicated or could be different signals.  

The court concluded that the limitation does **not** prohibit the accused device from using only two unique input signals, provided that the device can treat them as three “separately identifiable” entities (for example, by copying one of the inputs).

### Why the construction mattered  

If the construction required **three unique physical signals**, the accused radios—identified in the record as having only two inputs (x1 and x2)—could not satisfy the limitation, and the infringement question would be resolved in favor of the defendants.  The court’s broader construction, however, opened the possibility that the accused could meet the limitation with only two inputs, shifting the focus to the **seven‑multiplication** requirement.

---

## Finesse’s Argument on Claim Scope and Alleged Surrender of Two‑Signal Coverage  

Finesse argued that the patent’s claim scope was **not** surrendered during prosecution, despite the fact that the applicant amended the claims to recite “three signals” rather than “two or three signals.”  The appellant’s line of reasoning was:

1. **Prosecution History Context** – The original rejection (Filipovic) taught cancellation using **two** inputs; Finesse overcame that rejection by emphasizing that Filipovic dealt with *active* IMPs, whereas the ’775 patent addresses *passive* IMPs.  
2. **Amendment Purpose** – The amendment added the “three signals” limitation to overcome the obviousness rejection, but it did **not** make an explicit, unmistakable disavowal of two‑signal implementations.  In Federal Circuit precedent (*Cont’l Circuits v. Intel*, 915 F.3d at 798), a clear disavowal requires an unmistakable statement that the patentee intends to limit the claim to a narrower scope; the record lacks such a statement.  
3. **Resulting Construction** – Consequently, “three separately identifiable signals” should be read to require **identifiability**, not distinctness, preserving the possibility that the accused device uses two inputs and a copy of one.

The Federal Circuit accepted this argument, holding that the amendment did not rise to a “clear and unambiguous” surrender of the two‑signal alternative.

---

## Expert Testimony on the Nokia Document – Identification of Three Distinct Multiplications Only  

Finesse’s infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, testified that the accused radios infringe the ’775 patent by virtue of a Nokia internal technical document (the “Nokia document”).  The testimony can be broken down into two key components:

1. **Signal Identification** – Dr. Wells identified three signals in the document (x1, x2, and a copy of x1) and asserted that they correspond to S1, S2, S3.  This satisfied the court’s “separately identifiable” construction because the copy of x1 could be treated as a distinct signal for the purpose of the claim.  

2. **Multiplication Mapping** – The Nokia document listed **three distinct third‑order products**:
   * x1 · |x1| · |x1|  
   * x1 · x1 · x2′ (the complex conjugate of x2)  
   * x1 · |x2| · |x2|  

   Dr. Wells testified that these three products collectively **covered** the seven products required by the claim, arguing that each claimed product could be “mapped” onto one of the three documented products, sometimes by noting that the document performed extra operations (e.g., modulus, complex conjugate) that “effectively” duplicate the required products.

The transcript shows that Dr. Wells **never identified** the specific seven products listed in the claim within the Nokia document, nor did he explain how the three documented products correspond to each of the seven.  His inference that “three distinct multiplications” suffice was left unsupported.

---

## Failure to Show How Three Distinct Multiplications Map onto the Seven Claimed Multiplications  

The Federal Circuit emphasized that **claim‑level infringement requires a literal or equivalent performance of each limitation**.  In this context, the “seven multiplications” limitation is *structural*: the accused device must actually execute each of the seven products or a non‑literal equivalent that performs the same function, achieves the same result, and is performed in the same way (*Warner‑Lambert v. Philips*, 765 F.3d at 490 (Fed. Cir. 2014)).

The record contains **no evidence** that the accused radios:

* Perform **any of the six products** that are not explicitly listed in the Nokia document (e.g., S1×S2×S3 or S2×S3×S3); **and**  
* Repeat any of the three documented products to reach the total of seven multiplications.

Finesse’s post‑trial briefs attempted to argue that the Nokia document actually listed **ten** multiplications (a polynomial expansion p(n) = a₀ through a₉), but the expert never pointed to which ten correspond to the claim’s seven, nor did the record explain why only a subset would be sufficient.  Without a clear mapping, the jury could not have reasonably concluded that the accused radios satisfied the “seven multiplications” limitation.

---

## Assessment of the Plaintiff’s Failure to Present Evidence of the Required Ten or Seven Multiplications  

Because the plaintiff’s expert testimony was **incomplete and ambiguous**, the appellate court applied the **substantial‑evidence** standard.  Under Fifth Circuit law—as reiterated by the Federal Circuit—a factual finding (including a jury’s infringement verdict) is reviewed for substantial evidence: the evidence must be such that a reasonable jury could find in favor of the prevailing party when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to that party.  (*Apple v. Wi‑LAN*, 25 F.4th at 969).

The court found that, even when viewing the record in the plaintiff’s favor, **the evidence fell short** of satisfying the seven‑multiplication requirement:

* The **only** documented multiplications were three, not seven.  
* The expert **did not** explain how the three could be “counted” as seven, nor did he provide any demonstration that the accused device repeats any multiplication.  
* No **independent** testimony, demonstrative exhibits, or extrinsic evidence showed the presence of the missing products.

Consequently, the record did not contain “substantial evidence” supporting a reasonable conclusion that the accused radios performed all seven required multiplications.  The Federal Circuit therefore held that the jury’s infringement verdict on the ’775 patent **was not supported by substantial evidence**.

---

## Application of the “Substantial Evidence” Standard and Reversal of the JMOL Denial for the ’775 Patent  

Having determined that the district court’s denial of a judgment‑as‑a‑matter‑of‑law (JMOL) of non‑infringement lacked a substantial‑evidence foundation, the Federal Circuit **reversed** that denial.  The court’s logical sequence was:

1. **Identify the claim limitation** at issue (seven multiplications of three signals).  
2. **Summarize the expert evidence** (three multiplications shown, no mapping to the seven).  
3. **Apply the substantial‑evidence test**: a reasonable jury could not have found infringement because the evidence did not demonstrate that the accused radios satisfied every limitation.  
4. **Conclude** that the jury’s verdict is unsupported; therefore, a JMOL of non‑infringement is appropriate.  

Because the court reversed the JMOL denial for the ’775 patent (and also for the ’134 patent), it vacated the $166 million damages award, as the underlying infringement findings were no longer viable.

---

### Takeaways for the Reader  

* **Claim construction matters**: The court’s decision to read “separately identifiable” broadly allowed the infringement analysis to focus on the seven‑multiplication limitation.  
* **Literal performance is required**: When a claim recites a specific number of operations, the plaintiff must produce evidence of each operation or a clear, justified equivalence.  
* **Expert testimony must be precise**: An expert who cannot definitively map the accused device’s steps onto each claim limitation creates a evidentiary gap that defeats an infringement theory.  
* **Substantial evidence is the gatekeeper**: Even if a jury is persuaded, the appellate court will overturn a verdict lacking a solid evidentiary foundation for each claim element.  

Understanding how the Federal Circuit dissected the ’775 patent’s limitations provides a concrete illustration of the interplay between claim construction, expert evidence, and the substantive evidentiary standard that governs JMOL reviews.

# Chapter 5: Damages, Remedy, and Significance  

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit’s decision to reverse the district court’s denial of a judgment‑as‑a‑matter‑of‑law (JMOL) of non‑infringement inevitably affected the jury’s $166 million damages award.  This chapter explains why the district court’s post‑verdict motions were reviewed the way they were, what legal standards the appellate courts applied, and why the award was vacated.  It also highlights the policy reasons behind the court’s approach and the practical lessons for future patent cases—especially those involving complex technical patents such as the PIM‑cancellation inventions at issue here.

---

## District Court’s Post‑Verdict Motions: JMOL on Damages and a New‑Trial Request  

After the jury found the asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents infringed and entered a lump‑sum verdict of $166,303,391, the appellants (AT&T and Nokia) filed two separate post‑verdict motions:

1. **JMOL on Damages** – A request that the court determine, as a matter of law, that the jury’s damages calculation was legally insufficient.  The motion argued, for example, that the jury lacked a proper evidentiary basis for the $166 million figure because the underlying infringement findings were unsupported.  

2. **New‑Trial Motion on Damages** – The alternative request that the court set aside the verdict and order a new trial on damages, giving the parties another chance to present proof of reasonable royalty or other measures of loss.  

Both motions were denied by the Eastern District of Texas.  The district court’s reasoning, as reflected in the record, was that the parties had presented the requisite expert testimony and that the jury’s award was therefore entitled to the benefit of the doubt.  The appellate record shows the court did not find any glaring error in the jury’s calculation; rather, it concluded that, given the expert testimony, the verdict was not “clearly erroneous.”  (See J.A. 42‑57, 58‑65.)

---

## Fifth Circuit/Federal Circuit Standard for Reviewing Denial of a New Trial – Abuse of Discretion  

The Federal Circuit’s review of a district court’s denial of a new‑trial motion is governed by the jurisprudence of the Fifth Circuit, because the district court sits within that circuit.  The controlling standard is **abuse of discretion**.  The Fifth Circuit has repeatedly held that a trial court’s decision to grant—or deny—a new trial is “a matter of trial court discretion, reviewed only for abuse of discretion” (see *Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016); *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN Inc.*, 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022)).  In practice, this means the appellate court will not overturn the trial court’s ruling unless it can demonstrate that the district court’s decision was arbitrary, unreasonable, or not based on the evidentiary record.

To make that determination, the appellate court asks:

* **Did the district court consider the proper legal standard?**  
* **Were the facts and expert testimony in the record sufficient to support the trial court’s conclusion that a new trial was unwarranted?**  
* **Is there a clear error that would render the verdict indefensible?**  

If the answer to the first two questions is “yes,” the appellate court will uphold the denial, even if it might have reached a different result.

In *Finesse*, the Federal Circuit first examined the underlying infringement findings because a new‑trial motion on damages is predicated on the existence of a valid infringement finding.  The court concluded that the jury’s infringement verdict was **not supported by substantial evidence**—the standard for upholding a jury’s factual finding (see *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN*, 25 F.4th 960, 969).  Because the infringement findings failed, the damages award was rendered baseless.  Consequently, the district court’s denial of a new‑trial motion on damages could not be deemed a correct exercise of discretion; it rested on a factual premise that the appellate court found legally untenable.  This logical chain is why the Federal Circuit vacated the award rather than simply affirming the denial as an abuse‑of‑discretion question.

---

## Effect of Reversing the JMOL Denials on the Damages Award – Vacatur and Costs  

When a federal appellate court reverses a district court’s JMOL of non‑infringement, the underlying judgment of infringement is nullified.  The doctrine of **claim‑splitting** precludes the parties from simply re‑asserting the same infringement claim on appeal; instead, the case returns to the status it would have had if the jury’s verdict had never been entered.

In *Finesse*, the Federal Circuit’s reversal of the JMOL denials meant that, as a matter of law, no infringement occurred.  Without infringement, **no damages can flow** under 35 U.S.C. § 284.  Accordingly, the court **vacated** the $166 million lump‑sum award and entered a judgment of **zero** for the appellants.  The judgment also included an award of costs to the defendants (AT&T and Nokia) because they were the prevailing parties on the issue of liability and damages.  The cost award follows the typical “costs to the prevailing party” rule in civil cases (Rule 11 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure), and it reflects the appellate court’s view that the plaintiffs’ case was fundamentally defective.

---

## Policy Considerations: Holding Parties to Clear Expert Testimony, Preventing Speculative Infringement Findings  

The Federal Circuit’s decision underscores two broader policy principles that guide patent infringement litigation:

1. **Reliance on Clear, Consistent Expert Testimony** – Patent cases frequently hinge on technical experts who translate complex engineering concepts into legal claim language.  The court emphasized that **self‑contradictory or speculative testimony** cannot satisfy the plaintiff’s burden of proving infringement.  In *Finesse*, Dr. Wells’s inability to definitively map the “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals” to the claimed limitations, and his equivocal explanation of the seven required multiplications, rendered his testimony insufficient.  The Federal Circuit signaled that a plaintiff cannot rely on an expert’s “piecing together” of disparate statements to fill gaps in the record.  The requirement for **coherent, precise testimony** promotes fairness and prevents juries from being swayed by guesswork.

2. **Prevention of Damages Awards Based on Unsubstantiated Infringement** – By vacating the damages award after finding the infringement verdict unsupported, the court reinforced that **damages are a derivative remedy**—they flow only from a legally sound finding of infringement.  Allowing a massive award to stand while the underlying liability is questionable would undermine the integrity of the judicial process and incentivize plaintiffs to press weak infringement theories merely to extract large settlements.

These policies collectively serve to keep patent litigation grounded in technical accuracy and legal certainty, discouraging parties from pursuing “speculative” claims that could lead to runaway damages without a solid evidentiary foundation.

---

## Implications for Future PIM‑Related Patent Cases and Claim‑Scope Amendments  

The *Finesse* opinion offers several practical takeaways for attorneys handling patents that involve sophisticated signal‑processing claims:

* **Meticulous Claim Construction Is Critical** – The court’s analysis hinged on whether the accused radios actually performed the steps recited in the claims (e.g., sampling both “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals,” or performing seven distinct multiplications).  Parties must ensure that claim language is **precisely drafted** during prosecution, and that they retain **clear definitions** of key terms (such as “three signals”) to avoid later disputes over scope.

* **Preserve Evidentiary Support for Claim Elements** – When amending claims to overcome prior art, as Finesse did by adding the “three signals” limitation, counsel should **document the rationale** for the amendment and preserve any statements indicating that the amendment does **not** eliminate coverage for embodiments using fewer unique inputs.  Failure to do so can invite arguments that the amendment constitutes a “clear and unambiguous” surrender of claim scope, potentially narrowing the patent’s enforceability.

* **Expert Testimony Must Directly Correlate Technical Features to Claim Limitations** – In PIM‑related cases, the distinction between internal and external intermodulation, and between signals generated before versus after sampling, can be subtle.  Experts should be prepared to **trace the signal flow** in the accused device step‑by‑step, demonstrating that each claim limitation is met *exactly* as written, rather than relying on analogies or “reasonable equivalents” that may be disputed.

* **Strategic Use of Post‑Verdict Motions** – Defendants should consider filing a **JMOL on damages** early, especially when the infringement finding appears tenuous.  Even if the court denies the motion, the existence of a JMOL request creates a record that appellate courts can evaluate, as demonstrated in *Finesse*.  Conversely, plaintiffs should ensure that their damages theory is **anchored in reliable royalty data** and that the jury’s calculation can survive an appellate scrutiny of the underlying infringement.

* **Future Litigation over PIM‑Cancellation Technology** – The Federal Circuit’s emphasis on the physical impossibility of sampling a signal before it exists (as with the “modeled PIM path”) may influence how manufacturers design and document PIM‑cancellation features.  Companies may need to **explicitly disclose** the point in the signal chain where cancellation occurs, to pre‑empt arguments that the alleged “interference‑generating signals” are not actually sampled.

---

### Closing Thoughts  

The reversal of the district court’s denial of JMOL and the consequent vacatur of the $166 million award in *Finesse* illustrate the intertwined nature of infringement and damages in patent law.  An appellate court will not merely look at the size of a verdict; it will dissect whether the jury’s finding rests on a solid evidentiary foundation.  For law students and future litigators, the case teaches that **clear claim language, rigorous expert testimony, and a strategic posture on post‑verdict motions** are essential tools for both protecting patent rights and avoiding costly, ultimately vacated judgments.
