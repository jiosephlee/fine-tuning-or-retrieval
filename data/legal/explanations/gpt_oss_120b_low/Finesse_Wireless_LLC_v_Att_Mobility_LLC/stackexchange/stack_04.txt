Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### What is the requirement for the “seven third‑order multiplications” limitation in the ’775 patent, and how did the court determine the evidence was insufficient?
Question:
The opinion notes the patent requires seven specific multiplications of three signals, but the Nokia document only shows three distinct multiplications (and ten total). How must a party prove that the accused device performs the seven multiplications, and why did the court find the expert’s mapping inadequate?
Answer:
**Answer**

The ’775 patent’s independent claim requires that the accused device **digitally multiply three signals (S1, S2, S3) in *seven* distinct third‑order products** in order to generate the intermodulation‑product‑cancellation (ICS) signals.  The limitation is found in claim 1(d):

> “…generating an n‑th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering **S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3** and **S2×S3×S3**, where n is an integer.”  (Fed. Cir. opinion, ¶ B‑B)

Thus, to satisfy the claim a party must show:

1. **Three separately identifiable signals** are present in the receiver (the court held that “separately identifiable” does not require the signals to be *unique*; they may be copies of one another).  Id. at 115‑14.  
2. **Each of the seven third‑order products listed above is actually computed** by the accused radio.  The claim does not permit the patentee’s “seven” to be satisfied by a smaller set of distinct multiplications that are merely *re‑used* or *re‑ordered*; the specification teaches that each product is a separate operation that contributes to the cancellation algorithm.  Id. at 16:54‑17:6.

### How a party proves the requirement

Proof must be **positive, concrete evidence** that the accused device performs all seven products.  In practice this means:

* **A sworn expert testimony** (or other admissible evidence) that identifies the internal signal‑processing chain and points to the exact hardware or firmware blocks that compute each of the seven products.  
* **A mapping** that links each product in the claim to a corresponding operation in the device (e.g., “the block that multiplies x₁ × x₁ × x₂ implements the claim’s S1×S1×S2”).  The mapping must be *unambiguous*; the expert cannot rely on speculation or on implicit equivalence.

### Why the court found the evidence insufficient

The Court of Appeals held that the evidence offered by Finesse’s expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, **did not satisfy either of these evidentiary burdens** for the ’775 patent:

1. **The Nokia document only displayed three distinct multiplications** – namely  
   *x₁·|x₁|·|x₁|*, *x₁·x₁·x₂′*, and *x₁·|x₂|·|x₂|* – and **listed a total of ten multiplications** (p(0) through p(9)).  The record contains **no indication** that any of the three distinct multiplications are *repeated* to generate the seven specific products required by the claim.  Id. at 26486 (reproduced diagram).

2. **No expert testimony linked the three distinct multiplications to the seven claim limitations**.  Dr. Wells merely asserted that “the three distinct multiplications correspond to the seven claimed multiplications” but **failed to explain the mapping**.  The opinion notes that he “did not testify how those three distinct multiplications map onto the seven claimed multiplications” (Id. at 14966‑70).  Without a clear one‑to‑one correspondence, a jury could not reasonably conclude that the accused radios performed all seven products.

3. **The ten‑multiplication list in the Nokia document was never tied to the claim’s seven products**.  Finesse argued on appeal that the device actually performs ten multiplications, so “seven of them must be present.”  However, **the expert never identified which ten, or which subset of ten, correspond to the seven enumerated in the patent** (Id. at 15025‑29).  The Court therefore found the argument to be speculative.

Because the burden of proof rests on the plaintiff (Finesse) to show infringement, and because the expert’s testimony was **confusing, contradictory, and lacking the required detailed mapping**, the Court concluded that **no reasonable jury could have found the accused radios meet the seven‑multiplication limitation**.  The Court applied the “substantial‑evidence” standard for JMOL and held that the record **lacked the necessary evidence** (Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp., 543 F.3d at 1349), prompting reversal of the district court’s JMOL denial.

**In short:** To satisfy the ’775 claim a party must produce evidence that the accused device actually computes each of the seven third‑order products listed in the claim.  The expert testimony in this case fell short because it identified only three distinct multiplications, offered no concrete mapping to the seven required products, and never explained how the ten products shown in the Nokia document related to the claim.  Consequently, the Court held the evidence insufficient and granted JMOL of non‑infringement.
