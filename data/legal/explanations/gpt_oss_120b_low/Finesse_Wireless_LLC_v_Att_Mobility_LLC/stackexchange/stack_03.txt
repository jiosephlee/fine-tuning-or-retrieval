Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Why does the Federal Circuit consider the amendment to “three signals S1, S2, S3” in the ’775 patent not a clear disavowal of two‑signal embodiments?
Question:
Finesse amended the claims during prosecution to recite three signals instead of “two or three signals.” The court says this is not a clear and unambiguous disavowal. What legal standard governs whether a claim amendment disavows prior claim scope, and how did the court apply it here?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Federal Circuit applies the “clear‑and‑unambiguous disavowal” test when it decides whether a claim amendment during prosecution surrenders (i.e., disavows) prior claim scope.  The standard is found in *Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019), which holds that an amendment disavows earlier scope only if the change is **express, unmistakable, and leaves no room for the broader reading** that existed before the amendment.  Mere narrowing of language is not enough; the patentee must “make it clear to a person of ordinary skill in the art that the inventor no longer wishes to claim the broader subject matter.”  The Federal Circuit treats the question as a matter of claim construction, applying *KSR*‑style “clear‑and‑unambiguous” language rather than a simple “any narrowing” rule.

### How the court applied the test to the ’775 patent

1. **What the amendment said**  
   During prosecution the claims were changed from “digitally multiplying **two or three** signals” to recite “digitally multiplying **three** signals S1, S2, S3” (see the record at J.A. 1006‑10).  The amendment was therefore a *re‑phrasing* that added the requirement that the three signals be “separately identifiable” (J.A. 968‑69) but did **not** add any language indicating that the three signals must be *distinct* input signals.

2. **What the “clear‑and‑unambiguous” test looks for**  
   The court asks whether the amendment contains language that *explicitly* removes the possibility of two‑signal embodiments.  In *Cont’l Circuits*, the Federal Circuit emphasized that a disavowal must be “express and unmistakable,” such as “the claim now recites ‘three distinct signals’” or a limitation that “the three signals must be different from one another.”  Absent such language, the broader two‑signal reading remains part of the claim’s ordinary meaning.

3. **Why the amendment did not satisfy the test**  
   - The amendment **only** required “three signals S1, S2, S3” to be *separately identifiable*; it **did not** say they must be *different* or *unique* (J.A. 968‑69).  
   - The prosecution history (the examiner’s rejection, the applicant’s response, and the Notice of Allowance) never contain a statement that the invention is limited to three *unique* input signals.  The applicant merely argued that the prior art (Filipovic) taught only two signals and therefore the claim was narrowed to overcome the obviousness rejection, but it never said “we no longer claim two‑signal embodiments.”  
   - The court therefore concluded that the amendment “does not rise to the level of ‘clear and unambiguous’ disavowal of claim scope over products…with two unique input signals” (see the opinion, *cont’l Circuits* citation, 915 F.3d at 798).

4. **Resulting construction**  
   Because the amendment failed the disavowal test, the claim was construed to require “three separately identifiable signals” but **without** a requirement that those signals be distinct.  Consequently, the plaintiff could not rely on the amendment to defeat a non‑infringement argument that the accused radios use only two unique signals (x1 and x2) plus a copy of one of them.

### Bottom line

The governing standard is the “clear‑and‑unambiguous disavowal” test from *Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel*.  The Federal Circuit applied it by looking for express language in the amendment (or in the specification or prosecution history) that unmistakably eliminated two‑signal embodiments.  Because the amendment merely required three “separately identifiable” signals and said nothing about distinctness, the court held that the amendment **did not** constitute a clear disavowal, and thus the claim scope still covered two‑signal configurations.
