Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 4: # Chapter 4: Infringement Analysis of the ’775 Patent  

In this chapter we walk a law student through the Federal Circuit’s reasoning on the second patent at issue in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*.  The focus is the claim‑construction and infringement analysis of the ’775 patent, specifically the contested limitation that the method must use **three separately identifiable signals** (S1, S2, S3) and must perform **seven third‑order multiplications** of those signals.  By unpacking the parties’ arguments, the expert testimony, and the court’s application of the “substantial evidence” standard, the student will see why the appellate court concluded that the jury’s finding of infringement was unsupported.  

---

## Claim‑1 Language of the ’775 Patent – Generation of IMP Cancellation Signals Using Three Signals and Seven Multiplications  

Claim 1 of the ’775 patent is directed to a method of cancelling passive intermodulation products (PIM) in a receiver that is co‑located with a transmitter.  The claim recites, in abbreviated form:  

1. **Generating IMP cancellation signals (ICSs) continuously and near real time** by using *copies* of transmitter signals;  
2. **Specifying that the passive IMPs are generated in passive components downstream of the high‑powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter**;  
3. **Describing a power‑series‑based, non‑linear model** for the IMP generation; and crucially,  

> “generating an n‑th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering  
> S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3, where n is an integer.”

The claim therefore imposes **two technical requirements**:

* **Three separately identifiable signals** (S1, S2, S3) must be present in the digital processing step.  
* **Seven distinct third‑order multiplications**—the seven products listed above—must be performed on those signals to generate the cancellation signals.  

Both requirements are functional limitations; the plaintiff (Finesse) bears the burden of proving that the accused radios satisfy each limitation.  The Federal Circuit’s analysis hinges on how “separately identifiable” was construed and whether the expert testimony demonstrated the seven multiplications.

---

## Construction of “Three Separately Identifiable Signals” – Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Evidence  

### The district court’s construction  

At the summary‑judgment stage the district court interpreted “three separately identifiable signals” to mean **three signals that can be distinguished from one another** but **need not be distinct sources**.  The court relied on:

* **Intrinsic evidence** – the patent specification and the prosecution record, which described S1, S2, S3 as “signals” without requiring each to be a different physical input; and  
* **Extrinsic evidence** – the inventor’s testimony that the three signals could be the same signal duplicated or could be different signals.  

The court concluded that the limitation does **not** prohibit the accused device from using only two unique input signals, provided that the device can treat them as three “separately identifiable” entities (for example, by copying one of the inputs).

### Why the construction mattered  

If the construction required **three unique physical signals**, the accused radios—identified in the record as having only two inputs (x1 and x2)—could not satisfy the limitation, and the infringement question would be resolved in favor of the defendants.  The court’s broader construction, however, opened the possibility that the accused could meet the limitation with only two inputs, shifting the focus to the **seven‑multiplication** requirement.

---

## Finesse’s Argument on Claim Scope and Alleged Surrender of Two‑Signal Coverage  

Finesse argued that the patent’s claim scope was **not** surrendered during prosecution, despite the fact that the applicant amended the claims to recite “three signals” rather than “two or three signals.”  The appellant’s line of reasoning was:

1. **Prosecution History Context** – The original rejection (Filipovic) taught cancellation using **two** inputs; Finesse overcame that rejection by emphasizing that Filipovic dealt with *active* IMPs, whereas the ’775 patent addresses *passive* IMPs.  
2. **Amendment Purpose** – The amendment added the “three signals” limitation to overcome the obviousness rejection, but it did **not** make an explicit, unmistakable disavowal of two‑signal implementations.  In Federal Circuit precedent (*Cont’l Circuits v. Intel*, 915 F.3d at 798), a clear disavowal requires an unmistakable statement that the patentee intends to limit the claim to a narrower scope; the record lacks such a statement.  
3. **Resulting Construction** – Consequently, “three separately identifiable signals” should be read to require **identifiability**, not distinctness, preserving the possibility that the accused device uses two inputs and a copy of one.

The Federal Circuit accepted this argument, holding that the amendment did not rise to a “clear and unambiguous” surrender of the two‑signal alternative.

---

## Expert Testimony on the Nokia Document – Identification of Three Distinct Multiplications Only  

Finesse’s infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, testified that the accused radios infringe the ’775 patent by virtue of a Nokia internal technical document (the “Nokia document”).  The testimony can be broken down into two key components:

1. **Signal Identification** – Dr. Wells identified three signals in the document (x1, x2, and a copy of x1) and asserted that they correspond to S1, S2, S3.  This satisfied the court’s “separately identifiable” construction because the copy of x1 could be treated as a distinct signal for the purpose of the claim.  

2. **Multiplication Mapping** – The Nokia document listed **three distinct third‑order products**:
   * x1 · |x1| · |x1|  
   * x1 · x1 · x2′ (the complex conjugate of x2)  
   * x1 · |x2| · |x2|  

   Dr. Wells testified that these three products collectively **covered** the seven products required by the claim, arguing that each claimed product could be “mapped” onto one of the three documented products, sometimes by noting that the document performed extra operations (e.g., modulus, complex conjugate) that “effectively” duplicate the required products.

The transcript shows that Dr. Wells **never identified** the specific seven products listed in the claim within the Nokia document, nor did he explain how the three documented products correspond to each of the seven.  His inference that “three distinct multiplications” suffice was left unsupported.

---

## Failure to Show How Three Distinct Multiplications Map onto the Seven Claimed Multiplications  

The Federal Circuit emphasized that **claim‑level infringement requires a literal or equivalent performance of each limitation**.  In this context, the “seven multiplications” limitation is *structural*: the accused device must actually execute each of the seven products or a non‑literal equivalent that performs the same function, achieves the same result, and is performed in the same way (*Warner‑Lambert v. Philips*, 765 F.3d at 490 (Fed. Cir. 2014)).

The record contains **no evidence** that the accused radios:

* Perform **any of the six products** that are not explicitly listed in the Nokia document (e.g., S1×S2×S3 or S2×S3×S3); **and**  
* Repeat any of the three documented products to reach the total of seven multiplications.

Finesse’s post‑trial briefs attempted to argue that the Nokia document actually listed **ten** multiplications (a polynomial expansion p(n) = a₀ through a₉), but the expert never pointed to which ten correspond to the claim’s seven, nor did the record explain why only a subset would be sufficient.  Without a clear mapping, the jury could not have reasonably concluded that the accused radios satisfied the “seven multiplications” limitation.

---

## Assessment of the Plaintiff’s Failure to Present Evidence of the Required Ten or Seven Multiplications  

Because the plaintiff’s expert testimony was **incomplete and ambiguous**, the appellate court applied the **substantial‑evidence** standard.  Under Fifth Circuit law—as reiterated by the Federal Circuit—a factual finding (including a jury’s infringement verdict) is reviewed for substantial evidence: the evidence must be such that a reasonable jury could find in favor of the prevailing party when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to that party.  (*Apple v. Wi‑LAN*, 25 F.4th at 969).

The court found that, even when viewing the record in the plaintiff’s favor, **the evidence fell short** of satisfying the seven‑multiplication requirement:

* The **only** documented multiplications were three, not seven.  
* The expert **did not** explain how the three could be “counted” as seven, nor did he provide any demonstration that the accused device repeats any multiplication.  
* No **independent** testimony, demonstrative exhibits, or extrinsic evidence showed the presence of the missing products.

Consequently, the record did not contain “substantial evidence” supporting a reasonable conclusion that the accused radios performed all seven required multiplications.  The Federal Circuit therefore held that the jury’s infringement verdict on the ’775 patent **was not supported by substantial evidence**.

---

## Application of the “Substantial Evidence” Standard and Reversal of the JMOL Denial for the ’775 Patent  

Having determined that the district court’s denial of a judgment‑as‑a‑matter‑of‑law (JMOL) of non‑infringement lacked a substantial‑evidence foundation, the Federal Circuit **reversed** that denial.  The court’s logical sequence was:

1. **Identify the claim limitation** at issue (seven multiplications of three signals).  
2. **Summarize the expert evidence** (three multiplications shown, no mapping to the seven).  
3. **Apply the substantial‑evidence test**: a reasonable jury could not have found infringement because the evidence did not demonstrate that the accused radios satisfied every limitation.  
4. **Conclude** that the jury’s verdict is unsupported; therefore, a JMOL of non‑infringement is appropriate.  

Because the court reversed the JMOL denial for the ’775 patent (and also for the ’134 patent), it vacated the $166 million damages award, as the underlying infringement findings were no longer viable.

---

### Takeaways for the Reader  

* **Claim construction matters**: The court’s decision to read “separately identifiable” broadly allowed the infringement analysis to focus on the seven‑multiplication limitation.  
* **Literal performance is required**: When a claim recites a specific number of operations, the plaintiff must produce evidence of each operation or a clear, justified equivalence.  
* **Expert testimony must be precise**: An expert who cannot definitively map the accused device’s steps onto each claim limitation creates a evidentiary gap that defeats an infringement theory.  
* **Substantial evidence is the gatekeeper**: Even if a jury is persuaded, the appellate court will overturn a verdict lacking a solid evidentiary foundation for each claim element.  

Understanding how the Federal Circuit dissected the ’775 patent’s limitations provides a concrete illustration of the interplay between claim construction, expert evidence, and the substantive evidentiary standard that governs JMOL reviews.
