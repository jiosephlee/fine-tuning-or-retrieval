Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 3: # Chapter 3 – Evidentiary and Procedural Challenges  

In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit affirmed a district court that had allowed a series of evidentiary rulings favored by the government and rejected the defendant’s objections.  This chapter walks you through each objection, the legal standards that govern it, and how the appellate court applied those standards.  Understanding the mechanics of these rulings is essential for any law student learning to parse an appellate opinion that hinges on evidentiary discretion and harmless‑error analysis.  

---  

## Rule 404(b) Propensity Issue – Prior Possession Testimony as Intrinsic Evidence  

### The procedural posture  

Before trial, Cutbank moved to exclude testimony from two witnesses, D.F. and C.F., that they had previously seen him with the sawed‑off Marlin .22 rifle.  He argued that the testimony was “propensity evidence” barred by Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b), which generally prohibits using prior bad acts to prove a defendant’s character in order to show conduct in conformity with that character.  

### Legal rule  

Rule 404(b) does **not** bar evidence of prior conduct when that conduct is **intrinsic** to an element of the charged offense.  The Supreme Court, in *Hudson v. United States*, 522 U.S. 527 (1998), explained that “the admissibility of prior conduct evidence is determined by balancing the probative value of the evidence against the danger of unfair prejudice.”  When the prior act is **directly relevant** to an essential element—such as ownership, possession, or control of a firearm—it is considered **intrinsic** and therefore admissible despite its potentially prejudicial nature.  

The Eighth Circuit has applied this principle in *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), holding that “prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.”  

### How the court applied the rule  

The district court concluded that the witnesses’ testimony about Cutbank’s earlier encounters with the sawed‑off rifle was **intrinsic** to the possession element of 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e).  The appellate panel agreed, citing *Battle* and reaffirming that the prior‑possession evidence was not offered to show “that Cutbank is a dangerous person,” but to demonstrate **continuous control** of the firearm in question.  

Because the evidence passed the Rule 404(b) intrinsic‑test, the court correctly admitted it and did not err.  Cutbank offered no authority to the contrary, and the appellate standard of review for “abuse of discretion” gives the trial court broad latitude when it correctly applies the rule.

## Confrontation‑Clause and Hearsay Concerns – D.F.’s Statements to Police and Jury Instructions  

### The objection  

Cutbank demanded a limiting instruction that the jury could not consider D.F.’s statements to police until she testified at trial.  He argued that admitting the statements would violate the Sixth Amendment’s Confrontation Clause and the hearsay rule.  

### Governing standards  

1. **Confrontation Clause** – The Supreme Court in *Crawford v. Washington*, 541 U.S. 36 (2004), held that testimonial statements are admissible only when the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity for cross‑examination.  However, statements made to police **outside of a formal interrogation** are often treated as **non‑testimonial**, and even when testimonial, they are permissible if the declarant testifies later.  

2. **Hearsay** – Federal Rule of Evidence 801(a) defines hearsay as an out‑of‑court statement offered for the truth of the matter asserted.  The rule contains numerous exceptions, and the **admission of a statement for a purpose other than the truth of the matter asserted** (e.g., to show the police’s state of mind) is not hearsay.  

3. **Limiting Instructions** – The Eighth Circuit follows *United States v. Bennett*, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), which emphasizes that a trial court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.”  The court may give a **general instruction** that the jury must consider only evidence proven beyond a reasonable doubt and that all testimony is subject to the Confrontation Clause.  

### Court’s reasoning  

The district court found that D.F.’s statements to police were **non‑testimonial** and, even if testimonial, were later corroborated by her in‑court testimony, satisfying the Confrontation Clause.  Moreover, the statements were introduced **not to prove the truth of the events she described**, but to give context for the police’s actions and to explain the chain of evidence.  Consequently, the statements were **not hearsay**.  

The appellate panel affirmed, relying on *Bennett* and *Charboneau*, 613 F.3d at 861, which held that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied “when the hearsay declarants … actually appear in court and testify in person.”  Because D.F. testified, the constitutional concern was resolved, and the district court’s refusal to issue a limiting instruction was within its discretion.

## Admission of Prior‑Inconsistent Statements and the Relevance of D.F.’s Sister’s Death  

### The factual dispute  

During cross‑examination, D.F. explained that she had previously given statements claiming she did not remember seeing the gun.  She then testified that her reluctance stemmed from a personal history—her sister had been killed by a gun.  Cutbank moved to strike that portion of her testimony, arguing it was irrelevant and unfairly prejudicial.  

### Evidentiary framework  

1. **Prior‑Inconsistent Statements** – Under Rule 801(d)(1), a prior inconsistent statement is **non‑hearsay** when used to impeach a witness’s credibility.  The statement must be **confronted** on cross‑examination, which occurred here.  

2. **Relevance of Explanation** – Evidence that explains a witness’s inconsistent statements is admissible if it helps the trier of fact assess **credibility**.  The probative value of understanding a witness’s bias or emotional state may outweigh any minimal prejudice.  

3. **Balancing Under Rule 403** – Even relevant evidence may be excluded if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusion, or misleading the jury.  The Supreme Court in *United States v. McCall*, 709 F.2d 1140 (8th Cir. 1983), stressed that courts must weigh these factors case‑by‑case.  

### Appellate analysis  

The district court allowed D.F. to mention her sister’s death, finding it relevant to the **explanation** for her earlier inconsistent statements and to possible bias.  The Eighth Circuit agreed, noting that the testimony was **not offered to prove the truth of the gun‑killing incident**, but solely to explore D.F.’s credibility.  The court applied Rule 403 and found the **prejudicial effect was slight** compared with the probative value of understanding the witness’s demeanor and potential bias.  

Cutbank offered no authority to suggest the statement should have been excluded, and the appellate standard—abuse of discretion—does not permit reversal where the trial court’s judgment falls within the range of permissible outcomes.

## Authentication Requirements for Extrinsic Impeachment Evidence (C.F.’s Police Report)  

### The procedural wrinkle  

Cutbank sought to cross‑examine C.F. about statements she allegedly made to a police investigator.  When the defense attempted to read portions of the police report into evidence, the government objected, arguing the report was not authored by C.F. and therefore lacked proper authentication.  

### Legal principle  

Under Rule 902, self‑authenticating documents include official government publications and public records, but a **police report** is **not self‑authenticating** and must be authenticated under Rule 902(6) or Rule 901.  Moreover, when using extrinsic evidence to impeach a witness on a prior inconsistent statement, **Rule 801(d)(1)(C)** requires that the prior statement be **authentic** and that the witness have had an opportunity to adopt or reject it.  The Eighth Circuit in *United States v. Cumbie*, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022), emphasized that “extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such [evidence] must still be authenticated.”  

### Court’s application  

The district court correctly required the defense to **authenticate** the police report before admitting it.  Because the report was not signed by C.F., the defense could not lay a foundation establishing that the report accurately reflected her statements.  The appellate panel affirmed, noting that the district court’s demand for authentication was a routine exercise of discretion, not an error.  Cutbank’s failure to preserve a specific objection to the lack of authentication further weakened any claim of reversible error.

## Harmless‑Error Doctrine – Evaluation of Cumulative Prejudice and Relevant Case Law  

### The doctrine’s contours  

Even if an evidentiary ruling is erroneous, the conviction or sentence will stand unless the error is **not harmless**.  The Eighth Circuit follows the standard articulated in *United States v. DeMarce*, 564 F.3d at 997, which requires the appellate court to consider whether “the jury may have been substantially swayed” by the improper evidence.  The burden rests on the party asserting that the error was **harmful**.  The court evaluates the **totality of the record**, assessing the weight of the excluded or admitted evidence relative to the other proof presented.  

### How the court assessed prejudice in *Cutbank*  

The appellate panel surveyed each of Cutbank’s alleged errors:  

* **Rule 404(b) evidence** – admitted as intrinsic; no error.  
* **Confrontation‑Clause instruction** – no error.  
* **Prior‑inconsistent statements about D.F.’s sister** – admitted; any prejudice was minimal.  
* **Authentication of the police report** – proper discretion; no error.  

Having found no reversible error, the court turned to the harmless‑error analysis for the few rulings it deemed marginally questionable (e.g., the admission of D.F.’s sibling‑death testimony).  Citing *United States v. Keys*, 918 F.3d at 985, the panel noted that “we will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is substantial prejudice to the defendant.”  The record showed **substantial** direct and physical evidence—DNA matching Cutbank to the firearm, the victim’s contemporaneous testimony, and the recovered sawed‑off rifle.  The appellate court concluded that any alleged prejudice from the disputed evidence was **de minimis** and could not have altered the verdict.  

Thus, under the harmless‑error doctrine, the district court’s rulings—whether correct or only minimally erroneous—did not require reversal.  The conviction and sentence were affirmed.

## Synthesis  

Cutbank’s appeal illustrates the layered analysis an appellate court employs when evaluating evidentiary objections:  

1. **Identify the governing rule** (Rule 404(b) intrinsic evidence, Confrontation Clause, Rule 401/403, authentication requirements).  
2. **Apply the rule** to the factual context (prior possession testimony, D.F.’s statements, C.F.’s police report).  
3. **Determine the standard of review** (abuse of discretion for trial‑court evidentiary rulings; harmless‑error for alleged mistakes).  
4. **Assess prejudice** in the aggregate, using the court’s established harmless‑error framework.  

By mastering this step‑by‑step approach, you will be equipped to dissect similar evidentiary disputes in future appellate opinions, recognizing when a trial court’s discretion is properly preserved and when an error is sufficiently serious to merit reversal.
