Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### How does the Eighth Circuit apply the "harmless error" doctrine to evidentiary mistakes in a firearms case?
Question:
The opinion repeatedly references United States v. DeMarce and United States v. Keys, stating that the district court’s evidentiary rulings were not reversible because the error was harmless. Can someone explain the specific test the Eighth Circuit uses to determine harmlessness of evidentiary errors, especially when the error involves the admission of potentially prejudicial testimony about a victim’s family member’s death?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Eighth Circuit applies the “harmless error” doctrine to evidentiary mistakes by using a two‑step test that is spelled out in *United States v. DeMarce*, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009), and reiterated in *United States v. Keys*, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019).  

1. **Identify the error and its legal significance.**  
   The court first determines whether the trial court’s ruling was an error – i.e., a departure from the Federal Rules of Evidence, the Constitution, or a governing statute. In *Cutbank* the district court’s rulings involved (a) admitting testimony that the victim’s sister had been killed by a gun, (b) allowing prior‑possession evidence, and (c) refusing a limiting instruction. The opinion notes that each of those rulings “did not violate any rule of evidence or constitutional right” and therefore qualifies as a *potential* error.  

2. **Assess whether the error was “harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.”**  
   After the error is identified, the appellate court asks whether the error **had a substantial and injurious effect or influence on the jury’s verdict**. The *DeMarce* language makes clear that reversal is warranted only “if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence.” The court then looks to the strength of the government’s remaining proof. If the prosecution’s case was “substantial” and the error “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict,” the error is deemed harmless.  

   *Keys* refines this inquiry by emphasizing the “cumulative effect” analysis. The court will not overturn a conviction “unless there is **substantial prejudice** to the defendant,” meaning that the probative value of the error, viewed in the context of all the other evidence, must be such that the error could have caused a different outcome.  

In *Cutbank* the panel applied those standards as follows:

* **Strength of the government’s evidence.**  The government offered eyewitness identification of the saw‑ed‑off rifle, DNA linking the defendant to the weapon, and the victim’s contemporaneous testimony of the assault. This “substantial” body of proof, the opinion says, overwhelms any limited prejudice that might have arisen from the admission of the victim’s sister‑death commentary.  

* **Effect on the jury.**  The court concluded that the error “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict,” citing *DeMarce* at 998 and *Keys* at 985. Because the jury already heard numerous independent pieces of proof of the defendant’s possession and use of the firearm, the appellate court found no reasonable likelihood that the mistaken admission altered the verdict.

**Therefore, the Eighth Circuit’s test for harmless error in evidentiary contexts is:**  

1. **Determine that an evidentiary ruling was erroneous;**  
2. **Then ask whether the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt by evaluating (a) the overall strength of the government’s case and (b) whether the error likely had a substantial, injurious effect on the verdict.**  

If the answer to (2) is negative—i.e., the error was not “substantially prejudicial”—the conviction stands, as it did in *United States v. Justin Cutbank*.
