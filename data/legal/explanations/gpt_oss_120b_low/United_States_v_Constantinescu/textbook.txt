Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Background and Procedural History  

The United States v. Constantinescu case provides a contemporary illustration of how federal securities‑fraud statutes are applied to modern, internet‑based “pump‑and‑dump” schemes. Understanding the factual backdrop, the charges asserted, and the procedural journey from the district court to the Fifth Circuit is essential for reading the opinion’s analysis of indictment sufficiency.

## The Alleged Pump‑and‑Dump Scheme on Social‑Media Platforms  

The government’s complaint focuses on a coordinated effort by eight defendants—Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight—to manipulate the market price of publicly traded securities through false statements on social‑media sites. Each defendant maintained a sizable following on platforms such as Instagram, TikTok, and Twitter, where they cultivated an image of successful, “insider” traders.  

According to the superseding indictment, the scheme unfolded in three steps:

1. **Acquisition** – The defendants purchased a target security, establishing a genuine ownership interest that permitted them to profit from a price rise.  
2. **Pump** – While holding the security, they posted or streamed material that misrepresented their actual trading position, the security’s intrinsic value, and the likelihood of future price appreciation. The posts were crafted to appear credible and to induce followers to buy the same security, thereby creating artificial demand.  
3. **Dump** – After the price was inflated by the influx of follower purchases, the defendants sold their holdings at the elevated price, realizing a profit. The indictment alleges that the defendants collectively earned roughly **$114 million** from these transactions.

The government characterizes the conduct as a classic “pump‑and‑dump” fraud, adapted to the digital age: the false statements constituted the “pump,” and the subsequent sale of the security constituted the “dump.” By using their social‑media influence to induce others to part with money, the defendants allegedly obtained money or property of the victims through fraudulent pretenses, the very conduct prohibited by the securities‑fraud provisions of the criminal code.

## Count of Conspiracy and Securities‑Fraud Charges (18 U.S.C. §§ 1349, 1348)  

The indictment charges the defendants with two distinct statutory offenses:

1. **Conspiracy to Commit Securities Fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1349)** – This count alleges an agreement among the eight defendants to engage in a scheme that violates §§ 1348 (securities fraud) and §§ 1342 (wire fraud), and that each defendant knowingly participated in that agreement. Conspiracy requires proof of an overt act in furtherance of the illegal agreement, which the government points to the repeated false postings and the coordinated timing of the securities purchases and sales.

2. **Securities Fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1348)** – The indictment contains multiple counts under this provision. Section 1348 criminalizes any scheme “to defraud any person…in connection with any security” and any scheme “to obtain…money or property…in connection with the purchase or sale of any security” by means of false or fraudulent pretenses. The core elements the government must plead are:  
   * a **scheme to defraud**,  
   * the **use of false or misleading statements**,  
   * the **intent to deceive**, and  
   * **obtaining money or property** (or the object of obtaining such) from the victims.  

The indictment’s allegations—false social‑media statements, induced purchases by followers, and the defendants’ secret profit from the ensuing price rise—are framed to satisfy each of these statutory components.

## Outcome in District Court: Dismissal for Failure to Allege Deprivation of Property  

Defendant Daniel Knight entered a guilty plea, but the remaining seven defendants moved to dismiss the superseding indictment. The district court granted the motion, holding that the indictment “failed to state an offense by merely alleging that defendants sought to deprive their followers of potentially valuable economic information instead of a traditional property interest.” The court’s reasoning hinged on the Supreme Court’s decision in **Ciminelli v. United States**, which held that fraud statutes do not protect a “right‑to‑control” theory of ownership—that is, a claim that the victim’s valuable economic information, rather than money or property, was the target of the fraud.

The district court concluded that the indictment did not allege that the defendants intended to obtain the followers’ money or property; rather, it portrayed the alleged harm as the deprivation of information. Consequently, the court dismissed the indictment on the ground that it did not articulate a scheme to appropriate a protected property interest.

## The Government’s Timely Appeal and the Standard of Review for Indictment Sufficiency  

Following the dismissal, the United States filed an appeal. Under the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, an appeal from a district‑court order dismissing an indictment is timely if filed within 30 days after service of the order; the government met this deadline, making the appeal procedurally proper.

The Fifth Circuit’s review of an indictment’s sufficiency is **de novo**. As the opinion repeats, *United States v. Rafoi*, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023) states that the appellate court must “take the allegations of the indictment as true and determine whether an offense has been stated.” This standard obliges the reviewing court to focus exclusively on the factual allegations set forth in the charging document, without weighing the sufficiency of the evidence or the credibility of witnesses. The court asks whether the indictment **(1)** enumerates each prima facie element of the charged offense, **(2)** fairly informs the defendant of the charges, and **(3)** supplies a basis for a double‑jeopardy defense against future prosecutions. (*United States v. Gaytan*, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996)).

Thus, the appellate task is to determine whether, **assuming the truth of the indictment’s factual statements**, the government has alleged every element of §§ 1348 and 1349. The Fifth Circuit’s analysis therefore centers on the indictment’s language describing (a) the defendants’ false statements, (b) the induced purchases by followers, (c) the defendants’ secret sales, and (d) the resulting profit, and whether these allegations collectively satisfy the statutory requirements of a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud” that obtains victims’ money or property.  

In the following sections of the opinion, the Fifth Circuit evaluates those allegations against the Supreme Court’s recent interpretations of the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory (as distinguished from the rejected “right‑to‑control” theory) and ultimately reverses the district court’s dismissal, holding that the indictment does, in fact, sufficiently allege a scheme and intent to defraud.  

---  

By appreciating the factual underpinnings of the alleged pump‑and‑dump operation, the specific statutory charges, the district court’s rationale for dismissal, and the de novo standard applied on appeal, a law student can follow the Fifth Circuit’s reasoning and understand how modern securities‑fraud prosecutions are analyzed.

# Legal Standards Governing Indictment Sufficiency  

The Fifth Circuit’s approach to determining whether a federal indictment “states an offense” is a cornerstone of criminal procedure.  In *United States v. Constantinescu* the court applied this framework to decide whether the government’s charging document met the constitutional and statutory requirements for a viable fraud prosecution.  This chapter unpacks the Fifth Circuit’s de novo review of indictment sufficiency, outlines the three statutory requirements that an indictment must satisfy, and shows how those requirements are applied through the circuit’s leading cases.  Finally, we connect the analysis to Rule 7(b) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, which codifies the elements that must appear in a charging document.  

---  

## De novo Review of Indictment Sufficiency  

When a defendant moves to dismiss an indictment on the ground that it fails to state an offense, the appellate court conducts a **de novo** review.  This means the court examines the indictment **as if it were the first fact‑finder**, taking every factual allegation in the charging document as true and determining whether, on that face, the required elements of the charged offense are alleged.  The Fifth Circuit articulated this standard in *United States v. Rafoi*, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023):  

> “The court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated.”  

De novo review is distinct from a more deferential standard that might be applied to a trial court’s discretionary rulings.  Because the adequacy of an indictment is a jurisdictional issue—​the government must first have a valid charging document before any trial can proceed—​the appellate court does not give weight to the district court’s credibility determinations or evidentiary findings.  The focus is purely on **whether the statutory elements are pleaded**.  

---  

## The Three Statutory Requirements for a Sufficient Indictment  

The Fifth Circuit derives its sufficiency test from the language of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) and the Supreme Court’s “strict pleading” rule in *Bell v. United States*, 432 U.S. 106 (1977).  The circuit has distilled the requirement into three core components.  

1. **Enumerating Each Prima Facie Element of the Charged Offense**  
   An indictment must set forth every element that the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt.  If any element is omitted, the indictment is facially deficient.  This includes both the actus reus (the prohibited conduct) and the mens rea (the required mental state).  In *United States v. Gaytan*, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996), the court explained that omission of an element “constitutes a failure to allege a material element of the offense.”  

2. **Providing Fair Notice to the Defendant**  
   The charging document must “fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him,” a principle rooted in the Sixth Amendment’s Confrontation Clause and the Due Process Clause.  The indictment need not be a narrative of every factual detail, but it must be clear enough that the defendant can understand the essential nature of the accusation and can prepare a defense.  *Gaytan* emphasizes that “fair notice” is satisfied when the defendant can reasonably infer what conduct is alleged.  

3. **Protecting Against Double Jeopardy**  
   The indictment must also give the defendant “a double‑jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.”  This means the charging document must be sufficiently specific that the government cannot later bring a new indictment for the same conduct under a different theory without violating the Fifth Amendment.  The Supreme Court articulated this double‑jeopardy protection in *Blockburger v. United States*, 284 U.S._⊥_ 299 (1932), and the Fifth Circuit has applied it repeatedly, requiring that each count allege a distinct element set.  

When an indictment satisfies all three prongs, the Fifth Circuit concludes that the government has properly stated an offense, and the indictment survives a motion to dismiss.  The analysis in *Constantinescu* follows this template: the court examined whether the indictment alleged the elements of securities fraud (scheme to defraud, intent to defraud, and obtaining money or property) and found it did.  

---  

## Fifth Circuit Cases Defining Indictment Adequacy  

The circuit’s jurisprudence provides concrete illustrations of how the three requirements operate in practice.  

### United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545 (5th Cir. 1996)  

*Gaytan* is the seminal Fifth Circuit authority on the three‑part test.  The court held that an indictment that merely recites the statutory language without linking it to specific conduct fails the “fair notice” requirement.  The decision underscores that **mere legal conclusions are insufficient**; the prosecution must articulate factual allegations that give life to each element.  

### United States v. Guzman‑Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233 (5th Cir. 2000)  

In *Guzman‑Ocampo*, the Fifth Circuit emphasized that an indictment must “enumerate each prima facie element” and that the failure to do so is fatal on a motion to dismiss.  The court rejected a government pleading that left the mental‑state element of “knowledge” undeveloped, holding that the omission amounted to a failure to allege a material element.  This case is frequently cited when evaluating whether a fraud indictment includes the requisite intent element.  

### United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982 (5th Cir. 2023)  

*Rafoi* clarified the **de novo** nature of the review.  The Fifth Circuit reiterated that the appellate court “takes the allegations of the indictment as true” and does not weigh the credibility of the evidence supporting those allegations.  The decision is useful for students because it illustrates the strict, mechanical approach courts take: if the indictment’s language, read literally, captures all statutory elements, the court will reverse a dismissal even if, in reality, the government’s evidence would be weak.  

---  

## Interaction with Rule 7(b) of the Federal Criminal Rules  

Rule 7(b) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure mirrors the statutory requirements and provides a procedural checklist for prosecutors:  

> “A pleading (information, indictment, or information‑and‑complaint) must contain the **essential elements** of the offense charged, **including** a short and plain statement of the nature of the charge, and **identification of the required elements** of the offense with particularity.”  

The rule’s language aligns closely with the three prongs described above:  

| Rule 7(b) Requirement | Corresponding Fifth Circuit Prong |
|----------------------|-----------------------------------|
| “Essential elements of the offense charged” | **Element enumeration** – every statutory element must be alleged. |
| “Short and plain statement … that the defendant will understand the charge” | **Fair notice** – the defendant must be able to prepare a defense. |
| “Particularity” (including “specific facts”) | **Double‑jeopardy protection** – prevents the government from later reprosecuting on a different theory. |

In practice, a Fifth Circuit court will read the indictment, compare its allegations to the statutory text, and ask whether Rule 7(b) is satisfied.  If the indictment lists the statutory elements but does not tie them to factual conduct (e.g., merely says “defendant committed securities fraud” without describing the “pump‑and‑dump” scheme), the court will find a **failure to provide fair notice**.  If the indictment omits the required mental‑state language—such as “intent to defraud” in a securities‑fraud count—the court will deem the charging document deficient for **not enumerating an element**.  Finally, if the indictment is so vague that the government could later tack on a new theory (for example, adding a “mail fraud” count based on the same conduct), the court will view this as a **double‑jeopardy risk**.  

---  

## Applying the Standards in *United States v. Constantinescu*  

In *Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit applied the de novo test, took the indictment’s allegations as true, and asked whether the three statutory requirements were met:  

1. **Element enumeration** – The indictment alleged (a) a scheme to “pump” securities by posting false statements, (b) the defendants’ deception (material misrepresentations), and (c) the objective of obtaining money/property (profit of $114 million).  Each of these maps onto the elements of 18 U.S.C. § 1348 (scheme to defraud, intent to defraud, obtaining money/property).  

2. **Fair notice** – By describing the “pump‑and‑dump” mechanism and the defendants’ social‑media conduct, the indictment gave the defendants clear notice of the conduct alleged—namely, using false statements to induce purchases.  The defendants could therefore prepare a defense concerning the truthfulness of their posts and the existence of a “scheme.”  

3. **Double‑jeopardy protection** – The indictment listed specific counts (conspiracy under § 1349 and multiple fraud counts under §§ 1348 & 2).  Each count pleaded a distinct statutory violation, preventing the government from later reprosecuting on a different fraud theory without new charges.  

Because the indictment satisfied all three prongs, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the district court erred in dismissing the case and reversed.  

---  

### Takeaways for the Law Student  

* **De novo review** means the appellate court does not weigh evidence; it only checks whether the charging document, on its face, contains all statutory elements.  
* An indictment must **enumerate every element**, give the defendant **fair notice**, and protect the defendant from **double jeopardy**.  
* The Fifth Circuit’s leading cases—*Gaytan*, *Guzman‑Ocampo*, and *Rafoi*—provide the doctrinal backbone for evaluating indictment sufficiency.  
* **Rule 7(b)** acts as a procedural embodiment of these principles; reading the rule in concert with the circuit’s case law ensures that an indictment is both legally and procedurally sound.  

Understanding these standards equips you to assess whether an indictment survives a motion to dismiss and to anticipate how appellate courts will handle challenges to charging documents in future cases.

# Statutory Framework: Securities Fraud and Its Relationship to Wire/Fraud Statutes  

In United States v. Constantinescu the Fifth Circuit confronted a fundamental question: does the federal securities‑fraud provision, 18 U.S.C. § 1348, require the same “scheme‑to‑defraud” and “intent‑to‑defraud” elements that Congress embedded in the older mail‑ and wire‑fraud statutes?  The court’s answer—yes—rests on a close reading of the text of § 1348, an understanding that the provision borrows its key definitions from §§ 1341‑1343, and a body of circuit precedent that applies those definitions to the “material‑misrepresentation” and “fraudulent‑inducement” theories of liability.  This chapter breaks down each of those components so that you can see how the opinion fits the statutory scheme and why the Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment was sufficient.

## The Text of 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and Its Core Elements  

Section 1348 criminalizes two distinct types of conduct in connection with any security:

1. **A scheme or artifice to defraud any person** in connection with a security, **and**  
2. **A scheme to obtain money or property** by false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises **in connection with the purchase or sale of any security**.  

Both subsections require, at minimum, **(a) a “scheme to defraud”** and **(b) an “intent to defraud.”**  The statute does not enumerate additional elements such as loss to the victim; the focus is on the defendants’ deceptive conduct and their purpose to obtain something of value.  In Constantinescu the court stressed that the indictment must allege each of these material elements in order to survive a motion to dismiss.

## Borrowed Definitions from the Mail‑ and Wire‑Fraud Statutes ( §§ 1341‑1343 )  

Congress drafted §§ 1341 (mail fraud) and 1343 (wire fraud) earlier, providing detailed definitions of “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud.”  Because § 1348 “borrows key concepts” from those statutes, courts treat the language as interchangeable unless the securities statute offers a different meaning.

- **Scheme to Defraud** (see § 1341(4)): a “plan or design which, if carried out, would result in the deprivation of money or property.”  The Fifth Circuit reads this as requiring a **material misrepresentation or omission** that is intended to deceive a victim into parting with money or property.

- **Intent to Defraud** (see § 1341(4)): the **purpose to deceive** and **cause a victim to suffer a deprivation of money or property**.  The intent element is satisfied when the defendant **knows** the falsity of the statement and **purposefully** uses it to obtain the victim’s money or property.

Because § 1348 does not redefine these terms, the Fifth Circuit applies the mail‑ and wire‑fraud definitions directly.  This doctrinal shortcut is reflected in the opinion’s citation to Greenlaw, which notes that “courts have given the terms similar treatment” when interpreting securities‑fraud charges.

## Material‑Misrepresentation and Fraudulent‑Inducement Theories  

The Fifth Circuit’s analysis in Constantinescu turns on two recognized fraud theories that satisfy the “scheme” and “intent” elements.

### 1. Material‑Misrepresentation Theory  

Under this theory, a defendant makes **a false statement of fact that is material**—i.e., a fact that a reasonable investor would consider important in deciding whether to buy or sell a security.  The misrepresentation must be **intended to deceive** the victim, and the defendant must **seek to obtain money or property** from the victim as a result.

- *Key case*: **United States v. Evans**, 892 F.3d 692 (5th Cir. 2018).  Evans defined the scheme element as “a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the entity to be deceived.”  The Fifth Circuit in Constantinescu adopts this language verbatim, showing that the indictment’s allegation that defendants posted “false and misleading information” about a security satisfies the material‑misrepresentation prong.

### 2. Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory  

Here the focus is not on the falsity of a statement about the security itself, but on the **use of a material false statement to induce a victim to enter into a contract or transaction** that requires the victim to hand over money or property.  The victim’s loss is not a prerequisite; the crime is complete when the defendant successfully induces the victim to part with money or property under false pretenses.

- *Key case*: **Kousisis v. United States**, 145 S.Ct. 1382 (2025).  The Supreme Court clarified that the fraudulent‑inducement theory is **not a repackaging of the “right‑to‑control” theory** rejected in Ciminelli.  Instead, it protects “money and property” and does not require proof of the victim’s pecuniary loss.  The Constantinescu opinion leans heavily on Kousisis, emphasizing that the indictment’s allegation that defendants “induced” followers to purchase securities fits squarely within this theory.

Both theories satisfy the statutory “scheme to defraud” requirement because they each involve a **deceptive plan that, if carried out, deprives victims of money or property**.  They also meet the “intent to defraud” element because the defendants are alleged to have **purposefully used false statements to obtain the victims’ funds**.

## Circuit Precedent Interpreting the Elements  

The Fifth Circuit has a modest but growing body of cases that apply the mail‑ and wire‑fraud definitions to securities‑fraud prosecutions.  Two decisions are especially instructive for Constantinescu.

### *United States v. Greenlaw*, 84 F.4th 325 (5th Cir. 2023)  

Greenlaw treats § 1348 as a “straightforward borrowing” of the fraud‑statute language.  The court notes that where the statute is silent, courts may look to §§ 1341‑1343 for guidance on what constitutes a “scheme” and an “intent.”  This precedent gives the Constantinescu court authority to import the well‑developed definitions from mail‑ and wire‑fraud jurisprudence.

### *United States v. Evans*, 892 F.3d 692 (5th Cir. 2018)  

Evans is the leading Fifth Circuit exposition of the “material misrepresentation” component of a fraud scheme.  The court emphasized that **the misrepresentation must be material and the defendant must intend to obtain money or property**.  Constantinescu’s opinion directly cites Evans for the proposition that the indictment’s alleged false social‑media posts satisfy the material‑misrepresentation requirement.

Together, Greenlaw and Evans provide the analytical scaffolding the Fifth Circuit uses to conclude that the Constantinescu indictment alleged both required elements.  The court does not need to invent new categories; it simply applies the established definitions to the facts alleged in the superseding indictment.

## How the Framework Operates in Constantinescu  

Applying the statutory framework:

1. **Scheme to Defraud** – The indictment alleges that defendants posted false statements (“pump” posts) to induce followers to buy securities.  This is a **material misrepresentation** (the price and trading position were false) intended to cause followers to part with money.  Alternatively, under the fraudulent‑inducement theory, the false posts are the “means” by which the defendants induced the purchase contract.

2. **Intent to Defraud** – The indictment further alleges that defendants “secretly sold” the securities for profit, showing a **purpose to obtain money or property**.  The Supreme Court’s Kousisis decision confirms that a defendant’s **profit motive** alone satisfies the intent element; proving an intent to *harm* the victim is unnecessary.

Because the indictment contains factual allegations that meet both elements, the Fifth Circuit held that the complaint was legally sufficient, reversing the district court’s dismissal.

---

**Take‑away for the student:** When reading a securities‑fraud indictment, always ask whether the complaint alleges (a) a **material misrepresentation** or a **fraudulent inducement** that would cause a victim to give up money or property, and (b) the defendant’s **purpose** to obtain that money or property.  The Fifth Circuit (and the Supreme Court) treat § 1348 as a direct import of the mail‑ and wire‑fraud definitions, so the same analytical roadmap applies.  If both prongs are pleaded with enough factual detail, the indictment survives a motion to dismiss, as it did in United States v. Constantinescu.

# Supreme Court Guidance: Ciminelli and Kousisis  

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal of the district court’s dismissal in **United States v. Constantinescu** rests heavily on two recent United States Supreme Court decisions: *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States*.  Both cases interpret the federal fraud statutes—wire fraud, mail fraud, and the securities‑fraud provisions of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1341, 1343, 1348—by clarifying what “property” means under the statutes and by confirming that a defendant’s intent to obtain money or other property is sufficient for liability even when the victim does not suffer a net pecuniary loss.  Understanding these holdings is essential for analyzing whether an indictment sufficiently alleges a “scheme to defraud” and the requisite “intent to defraud” in the Constantinescu case.

## Ciminelli: Rejection of the Right‑to‑Control Theory and Protection of Traditional Property Interests  

### The Court’s Holding  

In *Ciminelli v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), the Supreme Court considered a wire‑fraud prosecution that rested on the “right‑to‑control” theory.  The government alleged that the defendant, a construction firm, obtained a lobbyist’s services to secure state‑funded contracts and that the fraud consisted of depriving the state of the “right to control” its own assets by withholding valuable economic information.  The Court held that the federal fraud statutes protect **traditional property interests**—interests that the law historically treats as property, such as money, tangible assets, or legal claims to money—but **do not protect a mere “right to valuable economic information.”**  The Court therefore rejected the government’s reliance on the right‑to‑control theory and dismissed the indictment.  

### Traditional Property Interest vs. Valuable Economic Information  

*Traditional property interest* is a term the Court uses to encompass “property” in the ordinary sense of the word—money, securities, real or personal property, and legal rights that are recognized as property under statutes and case law.  The key features are:

1. **Legally cognizable ownership or entitlement** – The interest can be bought, sold, or transferred.  
2. **Protected by property law** – The interest is safeguarded by the usual remedies for conversion, theft, or trespass.  

In contrast, *valuable economic information*—for example, insider tips, market forecasts, or data that enables better investment decisions—does not, by itself, constitute a property right.  While it may be **economically valuable**, the law does not treat the information itself as property that can be “taken” or “stolen.”  Consequently, a scheme that merely withholds such information, without depriving a victim of money or a legally protected asset, cannot satisfy the “scheme to defraud” element of the fraud statutes.

### Relevance to Constantinescu  

Defendants in *Constantinescu* attempted to argue that the district court’s dismissal was proper because the indictment only alleged that the defendants deprived followers of “potentially valuable economic information” rather than a property interest.  The Fifth Circuit, following *Ciminelli*, rejected that argument: the indictment does **not** rely on a right‑to‑control theory.  Instead, it alleges that defendants **induced followers to part with their money** by purchasing securities under false pretenses.  Because the alleged misrepresentations were aimed at obtaining **money or securities** from the victims, the conduct falls squarely within the “traditional property” category that *Ciminelli* preserves for fraud prosecutions.

## Kousisis: affirmation of the fraudulent‑inducement theory and the irrelevance of victim’s net loss  

### The Court’s Holding  

In *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1388 (2025), the Court addressed a fraud prosecution based on the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory.  The defendant obtained state contracts by misrepresenting that his company would meet a statutory subcontracting requirement.  The Court held that a conviction is proper when the defendant **uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires the victim to hand over money or property**, regardless of whether the defendant’s conduct actually causes the victim a net economic loss.  The Court emphasized that the fraud statutes are satisfied by the **objective acquisition of the victim’s money or property**, not by a subjective desire to harm the victim’s bottom line.

### Why Victim’s Loss Is Not an Element  

The fraudulent‑inducement theory distinguishes itself from the right‑to‑control theory by focusing on the **transactional element** of the fraud: the victim is induced to **part with money or property** in exchange for something of purported value (e.g., a contract, a security, a service).  The Court in *Kousisis* clarified two points:

1. **Intent to Obtain Money or Property Is Sufficient** – The defendant’s purpose must be to obtain the victim’s money or property; the defendant does not need to **intentionally cause a loss** beyond the mere exchange.  
2. **Actual Loss May Be Absent** – Even if the victim later receives something of equal or greater market value, the fraud is complete once the false statement causes the victim to part with money or property.  The statute does not require proof of a “mirror‑image” loss.

### How Kousisis Shapes the Analysis in Constantinescu  

The indictment in *Constantinescu* alleges that the defendants posted false statements to **induce followers to purchase securities**—a classic example of fraudulent inducement.  The defendants’ profit of $114 million demonstrates an **intent to obtain money** from the victims.  Under *Kousisis*, the government need not prove that each follower suffered a net loss; it is enough that the followers were persuaded to spend money on securities based on deceit.  Consequently, the Fifth Circuit’s determination that the indictment sufficiently alleges both a **scheme to defraud** (the coordinated “pump‑and‑dump” plan) and an **intent to defraud** (the purposeful misrepresentations designed to obtain followers’ money) follows directly from *Kousisis*.

## How These Decisions Shape the Analysis of Scheme and Intent in Constantinescu  

1. **Scheme to Defraud** – The *Ciminelli* decision tells us that a scheme must target a **traditional property interest**.  The indictment’s allegations that defendants caused followers to **hand over money** to buy securities satisfy this requirement.  The “pump‑and‑dump” plan is presented as a coordinated effort to **create a market distortion** that compels victims to part with money; therefore, the scheme element is met.

2. **Intent to Defraud** – *Kousisis* confirms that the government need only show the defendants **intended to obtain money or property** through deception.  The indictment’s description of the defendants’ profit motive—purchasing securities, inflating their price, then selling at a profit—demonstrates the requisite intent, even if the defendants claim that they did not intend to “hurt” their followers.  The Supreme Court’s language that a fraud is complete when the defendant **“schemes to obtain the victim’s money or property, regardless of whether he seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss”** directly supports the Fifth Circuit’s conclusion.

3. **Impact on the Sufficiency Review** – Because the Supreme Court has drawn a clear line between **invalid right‑to‑control theories** and **valid fraudulent‑inducement theories**, the Fifth Circuit’s de novo review of the indictment focuses on whether the complaint’s facts invoke the latter.  The *Constantinescu* indictment does so; therefore, the district court’s dismissal—predicated on a mischaracterization of the alleged injury—fails under the guidance of *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*.

In sum, *Ciminelli* bars reliance on a theory that treats the deprivation of information as “property,” while *Kousisis* opens the door for convictions where the defendant’s deception causes victims to part with money or other traditional property, irrespective of the victim’s ultimate economic outcome.  These holdings together provide the doctrinal foundation for the Fifth Circuit’s reversal: the indictment in *United States v. Constantinescu* adequately alleges a **scheme to defraud** and an **intent to defraud** within the parameters set by the Supreme Court.

# Court’s Application of Law to the Facts  

In this chapter we dissect the Fifth Circuit’s reasoning that the superseding indictment in *United States v. Constantinescu* sufficiently alleged both a “scheme to defraud” and the requisite “intent to defraud.”  The court’s analysis pivots on the distinction between a mere deprivation of information and an actionable fraud‑inducement scheme, the application of the Supreme Court’s “fraudulent‑inducement” theory, and the rejection of the defendants’ arguments that the indictment failed to identify a protected property interest or a victim‑loss requirement.  By following the court’s step‑by‑step logic, law students can see how appellate courts translate statutory elements into a factual matrix and how precedent shapes that translation.

## Why the Indictment Goes Beyond Depriving Followers of Information  

The defendants contended that the indictment merely alleged they “deprived their followers of potentially valuable economic information,” a characterization that, under the Supreme Court’s decision in *Ciminelli v. United States* (2023), would not satisfy a fraud charge because it does not target a traditional property interest.  The Fifth Circuit rejected that view for two interconnected reasons.

First, the indictment does not limit its allegations to the withholding of information.  Paragraphs 13–14 of the superseding indictment describe a concrete conduct pattern: the defendants **purchased a security**, **posted false and misleading statements** about that security on social‑media platforms, **induced other investors to buy the security**, and then **secretly sold (dumped) the security for a profit**.  Each of those steps is a factual allegation that the defendants *caused* other people to part with their **money** (or, equivalently, their property interest in cash).  The key phrase “induced … to purchase the security” signals that the fraud was not about the mere loss of information but about the **transactional relinquishment of money** in exchange for a security whose price was artificially inflated.

Second, the indictment’s language mirrors the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory articulated by the Supreme Court in *Kousisis v. United States* (2025).  Under that theory, a fraud claim arises when a defendant **uses a material misrepresentation to trick a victim into a contract that requires the victim to hand over money or property**.  The indictment’s allegations satisfy each element of that theory:

* **Material misrepresentation:** “false and misleading information about the security” (¶ 13).  
* **Inducement:** Followers were “induced … to purchase the security” (¶ 13).  
* **Transfer of money/property:** The purchase requires the followers to pay cash, thereby transferring money to the defendants (or to the market, which the defendants then capture by dumping).  

Thus, the court concluded that the indictment does more than allege the deprivation of intangible information; it alleges a concrete scheme that coerces victims into surrendering money—clearly a protected property interest under the fraud statutes.

## Application of the Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory to the Social‑Media “Pump” Conduct  

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Kousisis* is central to its analysis.  *Kousisis* clarified that the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory is **not** a repackaging of the disfavored “right‑to‑control” theory rejected in *Ciminelli*.  Instead, it focuses on the defendant’s **goal of obtaining money or property** from the victim, regardless of whether the victim receives something of equal face value in return.

Applying that framework to the “pump” conduct, the court identified the following factual correspondences:

| Fraud Element (Kousisis) | Alleged Conduct in Constantinescu |
|--------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Material misrepresentation** | Defendants posted “false and misleading information” about the securities’ value and their own positions. |
| **Use of misrepresentation to induce a contract** | The false posts were intended to cause followers to **enter into purchase contracts** for the securities. |
| **Victim’s transfer of money/property** | Each purchase required the follower to **pay cash**, transferring money to the market (and ultimately to the defendants when they dumped). |
| **Defendant’s objective to obtain money/property** | The indictment states that defendants “pump … and dump … to profit” (¶ 13–14). |

Because the indictment contains factual predicates that map neatly onto each element of the fraudulent‑inducement theory, the court deemed the statutory “scheme to defraud” element satisfied.  The presence of social‑media platforms does not alter the analysis; the platforms are merely the medium through which the false statements were disseminated.  The essential inquiry remains whether the defendants **intended** to induce victims to part with money, and the indictment’s allegations answer that in the affirmative.

## Analysis of Intent: Profit Motive Satisfies “Intent to Obtain Money or Property”  

The defendants also argued that the indictment fails to allege an “intent to defraud” because it emphasizes the defendants’ own profit rather than any intended injury to victims.  The Fifth Circuit, guided by *Kousisis*, rejected this argument by emphasizing that the fraud statutes **do not require proof of a victim‑loss motive**.  The relevant Supreme Court language is that a defendant “schemes to obtain the victim’s money or property, regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.”

The indictment’s language provides at least two independent sources of the requisite intent:

1. **Profit motive:** Paragraph 13 states that the defendants’ scheme was designed to “pump … and dump … to profit.”  The phrase “to profit” plainly indicates an intention to **obtain money** for themselves.  Under the statutory language of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 1349, the “intent to obtain” is satisfied when the defendant seeks monetary gain, even if that gain is derived from the victims’ purchases.

2. **Deceptive act aimed at victims:** The indictment also alleges that the defendants “posted false … information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.”  The purpose of the false statements was **to cause the victims to send money**, not merely to mislead them for the defendants’ own satisfaction.  The combination of (a) a false statement, (b) the intention that the statement cause the victim to act, and (c) the expectation that the victim’s action results in a transfer of money, fulfills the “intent to defraud” requirement.

The court noted that the **profit motive itself is a sufficient manifestation of intent** because it demonstrates that the defendants consciously pursued an economic objective achieved through deceit.  There is no need to prove that the defendants *desired* the victims to be poorer; the law is satisfied once the defendants **purposefully sought the victims’ money** as the object of their scheme.

## Rejection of the Defendants’ “No Property Interest” and “No Victim Harm” Arguments  

Having established that the indictment alleges a fraudulent inducement of money, the Fifth Circuit turned to the defendants’ two remaining contentions:

### 1. No Property Interest  

The district court’s dismissal relied on the premise that the only alleged victim loss was the deprivation of “potentially valuable economic information,” which is not a property interest under *Ciminelli*.  The Fifth Circuit clarified that the indictment **does not rely on such a theory**.  By alleging that followers **purchased securities**—thereby exchanging cash for an overvalued asset—the indictment identifies a **clear property right**: the followers’ **right to their own money**.  The loss of that money, however temporarily, constitutes a deprivation of a traditional property interest that the fraud statutes protect.

### 2. No Victim Harm  

The defendants argued that because the victims ultimately received market‑value securities, there is no “economic loss,” and therefore no fraud.  The court again pointed to *Kousisis*, which rejected a “mirror‑image” requirement—that the victim’s loss must equal the defendant’s gain.  The Fifth Circuit emphasized that the fraud statutes focus on the **transfer of money or property** obtained through deceit, not on the net result of the transaction.  Even if the victims later realized a market‑value security, the **initial act of inducing them to part with cash based on false statements** is sufficient to constitute fraud.  This reasoning aligns with longstanding precedent that a fraud is complete at the moment the victim partakes of the defendant’s scheme and surrenders money, irrespective of subsequent restitution or lack of loss.

### Synthesis  

By methodically aligning the indictment’s factual allegations with the Supreme Court’s fraudulent‑inducement framework, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment **clearly states a fraud offense**.  The indictment enumerates:

1. A **scheme** (false statements, social‑media dissemination, induced purchases).  
2. An **intent** (profit motive, purposeful deception to obtain money).  
3. A **victim’s surrender of money** (purchase of securities).  

Consequently, the district court’s dismissal for failure to allege a property interest or victim harm was untenable, and the appellate court reversed and remanded.

---

**Takeaway for the Reader:**  
When evaluating the sufficiency of an indictment for fraud, focus on whether the charging document **explicitly ties deceptive conduct to a victim’s transfer of money or property**.  The presence of a profit motive and the use of false statements to induce purchases are sufficient to satisfy both the “scheme” and “intent” elements, even if the alleged victims do not ultimately suffer a net financial loss.  The Fifth Circuit’s reasoning in *Constantinescu* demonstrates how appellate courts apply the Supreme Court’s fraudulent‑inducement theory to modern, technology‑driven schemes.

# Holding, Remedy, and Broader Significance  

In United States v. Constantinescu the Fifth Circuit affirmed that the superseding indictment sufficiently alleged the statutory elements of securities fraud, thereby overturning the district court’s dismissal.  The opinion not only resolves the parties’ immediate dispute but also sends a clear signal to prosecutors, defense counsel, and judges about how fraud statutes will be applied to schemes that employ social‑media platforms.  This chapter dissects the appellate court’s disposition, the practical directives on remand, and the broader doctrinal ramifications for future securities‑fraud litigation.  

## Reversal of the District Court’s Dismissal and Remand for Further Proceedings  

The Fifth Circuit’s analysis concluded that the indictment met the “prima facie” requirements of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 1349.  By holding that the government adequately alleged (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) the requisite “intent to defraud,” the court determined that the district court erred in granting a motion to dismiss.  Consequently, the appellate court **reversed** the dismissal judgment and **remanded** the case.  

The remand order is straightforward: the district court must proceed as if the indictment had never been dismissed.  The government may move forward with discovery, pre‑trial motions, and ultimately trial on the fraud counts.  No new indictment is required because the existing superseding indictment already satisfies the pleading standards articulated in *United States v. Rafoi*, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023).  The docket now returns to the procedural posture it occupied before the dismissal—i.e., the defendants remain “awaiting trial” on the conspiracy and securities‑fraud charges.  

## Practical Effect on Indictment Drafting for Online‑Based Fraud Schemes  

The opinion underscores two drafting imperatives for prosecutors pursuing fraud schemes that leverage modern communication tools such as TikTok, Instagram, or Discord:  

1. **Explicitly Tie Misrepresentations to the Transfer of Money or Property.**  The court emphasized that an indictment must allege that the defendant’s false statements were intended to induce victims to *part with* money, securities, or other property.  Merely alleging deception about “valuable economic information” is insufficient, as articulated in *Ciminelli v. United States*.  To avoid dismissal, counsel should describe how the misstatements prompted followers to purchase securities, thereby surrendering funds.  

2. **Articulate the Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory Rather Than the Right‑to‑Control Theory.**  The Fifth Circuit accepted the government’s reliance on the fraudulent‑inducement framework (the “money‑or‑property” theory) and rejected the district court’s focus on the right‑to‑control theory.  Indictments that invoke the fraudulent‑inducement theory should explicitly state that the defendant “used a material misstatement to trick victims into a contract or transaction requiring the delivery of money or property.”  This language satisfies the statutory “object” element identified in *Kousisis v. United States* and shields the indictment from attacks that the alleged injury is limited to informational loss.  

In practice, these guidelines translate into fact‑pleading that couples the deception with the victim’s act of purchasing (or otherwise transferring) an asset.  For example, a well‑crafted count might read: “Defendant … knowingly made false statements … that caused victims to *purchase* XYZ stock, thereby *transferring* their money to Defendant’s account.”  Such specificity ties the misrepresentation directly to the deprivation of a protected property interest.  

## Potential Impact on Prosecutorial Strategy and Defense Arguments in the Fifth Circuit  

### Prosecutorial Strategy  

The decision expands the prosecutorial toolkit for tackling fraud that occurs on digital platforms.  After *Constantinescu*, prosecutors can confidently bring securities‑fraud charges against “influencer” schemes without fearing dismissal on the basis that the alleged injury is merely informational.  The appellate court’s reliance on Supreme Court precedent affirms that the fraud statutes’ protective reach includes any transaction in which a victim is induced to surrender money or property, regardless of the actual loss incurred.  

Consequently, we can anticipate:  

* **More aggressive charging decisions** in cases involving cryptocurrency “pump‑and‑dump,” meme‑stock coordination, or “shill‑posting” on social media.  
* **Increased use of the fraudulent‑inducement theory** in indictments where the government can trace a flow of funds from victims to defendants, even if the victims later receive market‑value securities.  
* **Greater coordination with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** to obtain transaction records that demonstrate the monetary pathway from follower to defendant, bolstering the “money‑or‑property” element.  

### Defense Arguments  

Defendants in the Fifth Circuit will need to adjust their “information‑theft” line of attack.  After *Constantinescu*, a motion to dismiss on the ground that the indictment fails to allege deprivation of a “traditional property interest” is unlikely to succeed unless the pleading truly omits any link between the deception and a transfer of money or securities.  

Defense counsel may instead focus on:  

* **Challenging the materiality of the alleged misrepresentations**, arguing that the statements were opinions or puffery rather than false statements of fact.  
* **Questioning the causation element**, i.e., whether the victims actually relied on the defendants’ posts when purchasing the securities.  
* **Highlighting any legitimate services rendered**, such as genuine trade advice, to argue that the inducement was not “fraudulent.”  

These arguments shift the battle from pleading sufficiency to evidentiary disputes, aligning defense strategy with the appellate court’s emphasis on substantive rather than definitional defenses.  

## Relation to Ongoing Evolution of Fraud Jurisprudence Post‑Ciminelli/Kousisis  

*United States v. Constantinescu* sits at the intersection of two Supreme Court rulings that have reshaped federal fraud law.  

* **Ciminelli** barred convictions based solely on the deprivation of “right‑to‑control” information.  The Fifth Circuit’s opinion reaffirms that *Ciminelli* does not preclude convictions when the government alleges a classic “money‑or‑property” injury.  

* **Kousisis** broadened the reach of the fraudulent‑inducement theory, expressly stating that a victim need not suffer a net pecuniary loss for a fraud conviction to lie.  The appellate court applied *Kousisis* to the “pump‑and‑dump” context, confirming that the statute’s “object” element—obtaining the victim’s money or property—is satisfied even when the victim receives an item of comparable value (e.g., market‑value securities).  

By integrating these precedents, the Fifth Circuit signals that the fraud statutes are being interpreted in a manner consistent with the Supreme Court’s trend toward a **functional** rather than **formalistic** view of property.  The court’s reasoning suggests that any scheme that leverages false statements to cause an economic **transaction**—the act of parting with money or assets—falls squarely within the ambit of federal fraud laws.  

This doctrinal trajectory has two notable implications:  

1. **Broadening the class of prosecutable conduct**.  Activities previously viewed as “informational” misconduct may now be pursued if they can be linked to a monetary transaction, expanding the net of federal enforcement.  

2. **Increasing the importance of the “object” element** in pleading and trial practice.  Prosecutors will need to demonstrate, at least in the indictment, that the defendant’s objective was to obtain money or property, while courts will continue to scrutinize whether the indictment merely alleges informational deprivation.  

In sum, the Fifth Circuit’s holding in *Constantinescu* crystallizes the post‑*Ciminelli*/*Kousisis* landscape: fraud prosecutions must focus on the **exchange of value**, not merely the **withholding of knowledge**.  The decision thus serves as a practical guide for drafting indictments, a strategic touchstone for both sides, and a doctrinal milestone in the evolution of federal fraud jurisprudence.
