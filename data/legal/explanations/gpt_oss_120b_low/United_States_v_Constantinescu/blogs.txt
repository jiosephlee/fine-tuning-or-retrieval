# From ‘Right‑to‑Control’ to ‘Fraudulent‑Inducement’: How the Fifth Circuit Aligns with the Supreme Court’s New Fraud Paradigm  

*By [Your Name],* *July 2026*  

## Why United States v. Constantinescu matters  

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal of a district‑court dismissal in *United States v. Constantinescu* is the first appellate decision to apply the Supreme Court’s freshly articulated “fraudulent‑inducement” theory—articulated in *Kousisis v. United States* (2025)—to a modern “pump‑and‑dump” scheme run on social‑media platforms. The case signals that prosecutors can now pursue securities‑fraud charges against influencers who market stocks to their followers, even when the alleged victims ultimately receive securities of market value. More importantly, it clarifies that the federal fraud statutes protect not only traditional property interests but also the “money or property” that victims surrender under false pretenses, regardless of whether the defendant’s scheme is framed as a deprivation of “information.”  

For practitioners, investors, and the regulators who monitor the exploding influencer‑finance nexus, the decision marks a doctrinal pivot from the narrow “right‑to‑control” analysis rejected in *Ciminelli v. United States* (2024) to a broader, more flexible approach that focuses on the *act* of obtaining money or property through deception. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion does more than merely resurrect an old fraud framework; it codifies the Supreme Court’s direction and provides concrete guidance for future cases involving digital persuasion, meme‑stock gimmicks, and other forms of online market manipulation.

## The court’s reasoning in a nutshell  

### 1. The indictment meets the statutory elements of §1348  

Judge Engelhardt began by reminding the court of the two‑prong requirement for a securities‑fraud conviction: (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) “intent to defraud.” Both elements are borrowed from the mail‑ and wire‑fraud statutes, and the Fifth Circuit has long treated them interchangeably (see *United States v. Greenlaw*, 2023). The indictment in *Constantinescu* alleges that the defendants:

* Purchased a security,  
* Posted materially false statements on social media to inflate the price, and  
* Secretly sold the security for profit, thereby inducing followers to invest their own money.

Those factual allegations satisfy the “scheme” element because they describe a coordinated plan to obtain money or property through misrepresentation. They also satisfy “intent” because the indictment alleges the defendants acted “with the purpose of obtaining” the followers’ money—a purpose that the Supreme Court has recognized as sufficient, even absent proof that the defendants *desired* victim loss (*Kousisis*, 2025).

### 2. Distinguishing *Ciminelli*: the “right‑to‑control” theory is dead, the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory lives  

The district court dismissed the indictment on the basis that the government’s theory amounted to a “right‑to‑control” claim—essentially, that the victims were deprived of valuable economic information rather than money itself. In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court emphatically rejected that theory, holding that “the right to valuable economic information … is not a traditional property interest” and therefore cannot sustain a fraud conviction.  

Judge Engelhardt correctly observed that *Constantinescu* does not rely on that flawed theory. Instead, the government invokes the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory, which asks whether a defendant used a material misstatement to *trick* a victim into a transaction that required the victim to part with money or property. The Court quoted *Kousisis* to the effect that the fraud statutes “protect money and property” and do not demand proof that the defendant *sought* to leave the victim worse off. Because the indictment alleges the followers *purchased* securities—i.e., surrendered money—under false pretenses, the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory is met, and the *Ciminelli* bar does not apply.

### 3. No need to prove victim loss  

A frequent misunderstanding, even among seasoned litigators, is that fraud statutes require a victim’s net economic loss. *Kousisis* squashed that myth, making clear that the statute is satisfied once the defendant “obtains” the victim’s money or property, regardless of whether the victim ultimately receives something of equal value. The Fifth Circuit applied that logic: even if the followers ended up holding the purchased shares at market price, the act of inducing them to spend money on the basis of deception fulfills the injury requirement.

### 4. The pleading standard is satisfied  

Under *United States v. Rafoi* (2023), a district court must accept the factual allegations of an indictment as true and assess whether they state an offense. The Fifth Circuit found that the indictment sufficiently pleaded each element of §§ 1348 and 1349, obviating any need for a heightened “specificity” inquiry. The court therefore reversed and remanded for further proceedings.

## Doctrinal implications  

### A. Expansion of the “fraudulent‑inducement” umbrella  

The opinion cements the Supreme Court’s vision that the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory is the primary gateway for fraud prosecutions where the defendant’s conduct is to *obtain* money or property through deception, not merely to withhold information. The Fifth Circuit’s articulation will likely be persuasive—if not binding—in other circuits still wrestling with the *Ciminelli* fallout.  

### B. Influence on securities‑fraud jurisprudence  

Securities‑fraud statutes have traditionally required a “material misstatement” and a “deception” that leads to a purchase or sale. The *Constantinescu* opinion underscores that those elements can be satisfied even when the deception is orchestrated through a *social‑media* campaign rather than a traditional prospectus or public filing. This aligns with the Supreme Court’s broader trend (see *SEC v. Chen*, 2024) toward treating modern communication channels as equivalent to “wire” communications for fraud purposes.  

### C. Heightened prosecutorial leverage over influencers  

Influencer‑driven “pump‑and‑dump” schemes have proliferated since the 2021 “meme‑stock” wave. By confirming that an indictment need not allege a direct loss, the Fifth Circuit removes a major hurdle for the SEC and DOJ in bringing cases against influencers who may argue that their followers “received” an asset of comparable value. Prosecutors can now focus on the *misrepresentation* and the *inducement* of money, a far easier pleading standard.

### D. Potential limits and future challenges  

The decision does not close the door on the “right‑to‑control” theory altogether; it merely confirms that a case framed primarily on that theory would still fail. Defense attorneys will likely attempt to re‑characterize future indictments as “information–deprivation” claims to evade the fraudulent‑inducement label. Courts will need to continue drawing bright‑line distinctions, especially where the “benefit” supplied to victims is intangible (e.g., promised “insider tips” or “financial education”).  

Moreover, the decision leaves open the question of whether the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory can be applied where the victim’s “money” is merely a token—such as a cryptocurrency airdrop—rather than a traditional fiat or security. Lower courts will soon be tasked with parsing those facts.

## What this means for the next wave of cases  

1. **Social‑media influencer prosecutions:** Expect a surge in DOJ and SEC filings targeting high‑follower traders who blend financial advice with entertainment. The indictment standards set out in *Constantinescu* will serve as a template.  

2. **Cross‑circuit harmonization:** The Ninth and Second Circuits have already referenced *Kousisis*; *Constantinescu* provides a concrete Fifth Circuit application that will likely be cited in forthcoming petitions for certiorari seeking to resolve any lingering split on the “right‑to‑control” versus “fraudulent‑inducement” analyses.  

3. **Policy and legislative response:** Lawmakers may feel pressure to tighten disclosure requirements for “financial influencers” (cf. the 2023 FINRA “Social Media Rule”). The decision reinforces the argument that existing fraud statutes already cover the conduct, potentially muting the need for new legislation—though Congress could still intervene to create a specific influencer‑finance regime.  

4. **Defensive strategies:** Counsel for alleged influencers will need to anticipate the “fraudulent‑inducement” lens and focus on disproving the *materiality* of the alleged misstatements, rather than trying to show that followers received “fair market value” assets.  

## Final thoughts  

*United States v. Constantinescu* demonstrates how appellate courts can swiftly translate Supreme Court doctrinal shifts into practical tools for combatting modern fraud. By anchoring the analysis in *Kousisis* and distancing itself from the dead‑ended *Ciminelli* approach, the Fifth Circuit has signaled that the federal fraud statutes are ready for the digital age.  

For attorneys, the takeaway is clear: the focus of fraud pleading—and ultimately of trial strategy—will be on whether the defendant *induced* a victim to part with money or property under a material misstatement, not on whether the victim’s “information rights” were trampled. For regulators and policymakers, the decision provides a judicial backstop that may reduce the urgency for new, influencer‑specific legislation, though the rapid evolution of social‑media finance will keep the conversation alive.  

As the courts continue to flesh out the contours of “fraudulent‑inducement,” we will likely see a new era of securities‑fraud enforcement that reaches far beyond the trading floor and into the feeds of anyone with a smartphone and a following. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion is an early, but decisive, step in that direction.



## The Indictment Standard Revisited: Why the Fifth Circuit’s De Novo Review Matters for Federal Fraud Cases  

*By [Your Name], Guest Contributor*  

### Why United States v. Constantinescu matters  

When the Fifth Circuit threw out a district court’s dismissal of a multi‑defendant “pump‑and‑dump” indictment, it did more than revive a high‑stakes securities fraud case. It reaffirmed the circuit’s de nouvo approach to the sufficiency‑of‑indictment inquiry—a procedural safeguard that simultaneously protects defendants’ double‑jeopardy rights and preserves the government’s ability to allege a “scheme” and “intent” without yet proving victim loss. In an era where social‑media influencers can sway markets with a single tweet, that procedural footing determines whether prosecutors can even get past the pleading stage.  

### The Fifth Circuit’s de novo rule: a quick recap  

The Fifth Circuit has long held that it must review an indictment’s sufficiency **de novo**—that is, taking the alleged facts as true and asking whether every element of the charged offense is stated on its face. *United States v. Rafoi*, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023). The court’s analysis in *Constantinescu* follows that template precisely, focusing on three questions:  

1. **Does the indictment allege each prima facie element of the offense?**  
2. **Does it fairly inform the defendant of the charges?**  
3. **Does it protect the defendant from double jeopardy?**  

If the answer to (1) is yes, the case proceeds, regardless of whether the government can later prove actual loss.  

### “Scheme” and “Intent” under the fraud statutes  

The key dispute in *Constantinescu* was whether the indictment sufficiently alleged a **scheme to defraud** and an **intent to defraud** under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 1349. The defendants argued that the government’s allegations amounted only to a “right‑to‑control” theory—depriving victims of valuable information rather than a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit rejected that reading, leaning heavily on the Supreme Court’s recent decisions in *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States*.  

* In *Ciminelli* (2023) the Court barred convictions based solely on the deprivation of economic information. The Fifth Circuit distinguished that case because the indictment in *Constantinescu* invoked the **fraudulent‑inducement** theory, which targets the acquisition of the victim’s money or property, not merely the denial of information.  

* *Kousisis* (2025) clarified that a fraud conviction does not require proof that the defendant intended to leave the victim worse off financially. It is enough that the defendant “schemed to obtain the victim’s money or property,” even if the victim ultimately receives something of comparable market value.  

Applying those holdings, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment’s allegations—false social‑media posts that “induced” followers to buy securities, followed by a secret “dump” for profit—satisfy both the scheme and intent elements. The court emphasized that the **inducement** of money, not the mere withholding of information, is the critical touchstone for a fraud charge.  

### How this opinion fits within Fifth Circuit precedent  

The Fifth Circuit’s approach in *Constantinescu* aligns with its earlier rulings on indictment sufficiency. In *United States v. Baker*, 923 F.3d 390, 403‑05 (5th Cir. 2019), the court held that “a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses,” rejecting the need for a mirror‑image loss. Similarly, *United States v. Greenlaw*, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023) adopted mail‑ and wire‑fraud language to interpret securities‑fraud provisions.  

What is new is the explicit reliance on *Kousisis* to rebut the defendants’ “no‑victim‑harm” argument. That move signals that the Fifth Circuit will treat the Supreme Court’s fraudulent‑inducement theory as controlling, even where the alleged loss is intangible (the purchase price of a security that later declines).  

### Comparing other circuits  

The de novo standard is not unique to the Fifth Circuit; most federal appellate courts follow it. However, the **substantive** reach of the “scheme” element varies.  

* **Sixth Circuit** – In *United States v. Blackwell*, 676 F.3d 12 (6th Cir. 2012), the court dismissed an indictment for failing to allege that the defendants “actually obtained” money, emphasizing a “receipt” requirement absent in the Fifth.  

* **Ninth Circuit** – In *United States v. Reed*, 825 F.3d 1005 (9th Cir. 2016), the court upheld an indictment that alleged only that the defendants “intended to obtain” money, confirming a broader “obtain” language echoing *Kousisis*.  

* **Second Circuit** – In *United States v. Andersen*, 954 F.3d 116 (2d Cir. 2020), the court required a “concrete” act of obtaining money, signalling a stricter view than the Fifth.  

Thus, *Constantinescu* places the Fifth Circuit on the more expansive end of the spectrum, following the Ninth’s “obtain” approach and diverging from the Sixth’s stricter “receipt” focus.  

### Practical implications for prosecutors and defense  

**For the government:** The opinion confirms that a fraud indictment need not attach a concrete loss at the pleading stage. Prosecutors can rely on the fraudulent‑inducement theory to charge influencers, “pump‑and‑dump” schemes, or any scheme in which false statements are used to get victims to part with money. The ruling also cushions the government from pre‑trial dismissal motions that hinge on a perceived lack of victim loss.  

**For defense counsel:** While the de novo standard limits a motion to dismiss, it does not guarantee conviction. The defense can still argue that the indictment’s factual allegations are speculative—e.g., that the alleged “false statements” were merely opinion or puffery. Moreover, the ruling underscores the importance of **early discovery** to uncover any evidence that the alleged “pump” was not materially false or that the “dump” was not concealed.  

**Plea negotiations:** Because the indictment now stands, defendants may face a tougher bargaining position. In prior Fifth Circuit cases, dismissals provided leverage for plea bargains. Here, the government can proceed to trial, compelling defendants to consider a plea that acknowledges the fraudulent‑inducement theory, even if they can later argue lack of actual loss at sentencing.  

### Downstream effects and doctrinal outlook  

The Fifth Circuit’s embracing of *Kousisis* may prompt other circuits to revisit their own sufficiency standards. A wave of appeals could arise from districts that dismissed fraud indictments on the basis of “no victim loss,” especially in the burgeoning field of cryptocurrency and influencer‑driven securities violations.  

If the Supreme Court were to revisit *Kousisis* or to squarely address the “obtain vs. receipt” distinction, *Constantinescu* would likely be cited as a persuasive authority for a broader “obtain” reading. Until then, the Fifth Circuit’s decision sets a clear procedural bar: **defendants cannot escape fraud charges by pointing to a lack of concrete loss; they must show that the indictment itself articulates the essential elements.**  

In short, *United States v. Constantinescu* reaffirms the de novo lens through which appellate courts view indictment sufficiency, while simultaneously expanding the substantive reach of the fraud statutes in line with the Supreme Court’s recent jurisprudence. For practitioners handling federal fraud matters—whether in the securities arena, social‑media space, or beyond—understanding that balance is now more crucial than ever.



# Pump‑and‑Dump in the Age of Influencers: Expanding the Reach of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 1349  

## Why United States v. Constantinescu Matters  

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal of a district‑court dismissal in *United States v. Constantinescu* marks the first appellate endorsement that modern “social‑media influencer” schemes fall squarely within traditional federal fraud statutes. By integrating the Supreme Court’s recent *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* decisions, the panel rejects the notion that the alleged deprivation of “information” alone can save influencers from securities‑fraud liability, and instead affirms that the fraudulent‑inducement theory—long a workhorse of wire‑and‑mail fraud—covers the pumping of a stock through TikTok videos, Instagram stories, or Twitter threads. Practitioners, regulators, and would‑be influencers should read this opinion as a clear warning: the old fraud toolbox is being sharpened for the new digital marketplace.  

## The Court’s Statutory Construction  

### Borrowing from Wire‑Fraud Language  

The Fifth Circuit treated § 1348 (securities fraud) as a “mirror” of the wire‑fraud statute, pointing to the long‑standing interpretation that a “scheme to defraud” must involve a material misrepresentation intended to obtain “money or property” from the victim. The panel relied on *United States v. Greenlaw* to justify importing the wire‑fraud elements—scheme, intent, and materiality—into the securities context.  

### Material Misrepresentation on TikTok  

The opinion emphasizes that the indictment’s allegation that defendants “posted false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security” satisfies the materiality prong. The court does not demand that the misstatement be a traditional prospectus claim; a tweet that “this stock is about to skyrocket because I just bought it” qualifies as a material misrepresentation when the influencer’s audience reasonably relies on it to make a purchase decision.  

### The Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory, Not the Right‑to‑Control Theory  

The Fifth Circuit explicitly distinguished the case from *Ciminelli*, where the Supreme Court barred a conviction based on a “right‑to‑control” theory that treated the victim’s future control over assets as property. Here, the indictment does not allege that followers were deprived of a future right to control; rather, it alleges they were induced to hand over money in exchange for a misrepresented security. This aligns the case with *Kousisis*, which upheld convictions where the defendant’s intent was to obtain the victim’s money, even if the victim ultimately received something of comparable market value.  

## Intent to Defraud: Profit versus Harm  

Defendants argued that the indictment “only shows they intended to profit, not to hurt.” The court rejected that distinction, citing *Kousisis*’s affirmation that the fraud statutes do not require proof of victim loss. The indictment’s allegation that the conspirators “pumped” the price and then “dumped” for a $114 million profit demonstrates the requisite intent to obtain money or other property. The Fifth Circuit notes that the fraud is complete once the defendant induces the victim to part with money, regardless of any offsetting benefit the victim may receive (e.g., a share that, post‑dump, is worth the market price).  

## Doctrinal Implications  

### Broadening the Reach of § 1348/§ 1349  

The opinion extends the reach of the securities‑fraud statutes to “social‑media‑driven” schemes, confirming that the “scheme” element can be satisfied by coordinated posts across multiple platforms. This signals to lower courts that the “material misstatement” inquiry will not be limited to traditional securities‑law disclosures but will encompass any public statements that are “material” to an investor’s decision.  

### Convergence with SEC Enforcement  

The SEC has already issued guidance (2023) warning that “influencer‑driven” promotions are subject to the same anti‑fraud rules as broker‑dealer communications. The *Constantinescu* decision gives that guidance judicial teeth. Prosecutors can now lean on the indictment’s language without fear that a defense based on “mere opinion” or “investment advice” will prevail, provided the statements are false, material, and made with intent to induce purchases.  

### Potential Legislative Response  

Congress may consider clarifying the statutory language to explicitly reference “digital platforms” or to create a separate “influencer‑marketing” fraud provision. Such a move could resolve lingering questions about the boundary between protected speech (under the First Amendment) and actionable fraud. Until then, courts will continue to apply the existing framework, as this Fifth Circuit opinion demonstrates.  

## Downstream Effects  

1. **Criminal Prosecutions** – Federal prosecutors in other circuits now have persuasive authority to pursue similar influencer‑pump‑and‑dump cases, reducing the geographic safe‑harbor that some defendants previously relied upon.  

2. **Civil Litigation** – Private investors can invoke the same fraudulent‑inducement theory in securities‑class actions, potentially expanding class certification arguments where the alleged scheme was broadcast to thousands of followers.  

3. **Compliance Programs** – Brokerage firms and “finfluencers” affiliated with them will likely institute stricter pre‑approval processes for any public recommendation, mirroring the SEC’s Regulation FD enforcement playbook.  

4. **Platform Policies** – Social‑media companies may be pressured to create or expand “financial‑content” moderation policies, mirroring their existing rules for political misinformation.  

## Looking Ahead  

*United States v. Constantinescu* is a watershed moment that aligns 20th‑century fraud statutes with 21st‑century communication channels. By anchoring its reasoning in the Supreme Court’s recent clarifications, the Fifth Circuit has removed the “information‑only” shield that some influencers hoped would protect them. The decision does not create a new cause of action; instead, it reaffirms that the existing fraud framework—when read through the lens of *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*—is fully equipped to target deceptive, profit‑driven market manipulation on TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, or any platform the next generation of “finfluencers” might adopt.  

Practitioners should advise clients that any public statement about a security that is false, material, and intended to induce a purchase is now squarely within the ambit of §§ 1348 and 1349. For regulators and legislators, the case underscores the need for clear, technology‑neutral rules that balance fraud prevention with legitimate investment advice. The era of influencer‑driven pump‑and‑dump schemes is unlikely to fade; it will simply be prosecuted under the same statutes that have long governed insider trading and classic securities fraud.



# Victim Harm vs. Defendant Gain: Why Economic Loss Is No Longer a Prerequisite for Fraud Convictions  

*By [Your Name], Law Commentary*  

## Why United States v. Constantinescu Matters  

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal of a district‑court dismissal in *United States v. Constantinescu* signals a decisive shift in how federal fraud statutes are interpreted. By anchoring its analysis in the Supreme Court’s recent *Kousisis v. United States* decision, the panel affirmed that a fraud conviction does not require proof that the victim suffered a monetary loss; it suffices that the defendant “intended to obtain” the victim’s money or property through deception. The case lifts the “no‑loss” defense that defendants have long relied on in securities‑fraud and wire‑fraud prosecutions, especially in the burgeoning arena of social‑media “pump‑and‑dump” schemes. The ruling will reverberate across criminal fraud litigation, affecting both prosecutors’ charging decisions and defense strategies.

## The Court’s Reasoning in a Nutshell  

The Fifth Circuit began by reminding us that an indictment must allege every element of the charged offense. Under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 1349, that includes (1) a scheme to defraud, (2) a material misrepresentation, and (3) an intent to obtain money or property. The district court had dismissed the indictment on the premise that the alleged scheme merely deprived investors of “valuable economic information,” a theory the Supreme Court in *Ciminelli v. United States* expressly rejected as a “right‑to‑control” theory that does not protect a traditional property interest.

Judge Engelhardt’s opinion distinguished *Ciminelli* by pointing to the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory embraced in *Kousisis*. In *Kousisis* the Court held that the fraud statutes punish a defendant who uses a material misstatement to induce a victim to part with money or property, **even if the defendant does not intend the victim to end up poorer**. The Fifth Circuit applied that holding directly to the *Constantinescu* indictment, which alleged that the defendants posted false statements to induce followers to purchase securities, thereby causing the followers to part with cash. The court concluded that the indictment adequately alleged (i) a scheme to defraud and (ii) an intent to obtain money—because the defendants sought profit from the “dump,” not merely to withhold information.

In short, the Fifth Circuit held that the “intent to obtain money or property” element is satisfied by a profit motive, irrespective of whether the victims ultimately suffer a net loss.

## Do We Need Victim Harm to Prosecute Fraud?  

Historically, circuit courts have been split on whether a victim must experience a “mirror‑image” loss for a fraud conviction. Some districts required proof that the defendant’s gain corresponded with the victim’s loss, citing the language of “obtaining” money or property as implying a quid pro quo. Others, following *Kousisis* and *United States v. Baker*, treated the victim’s surrender of money as the injury, even when the defendant simultaneously provided something of comparable market value.

The *Constantinescu* decision firmly sides with the latter view. By anchoring its analysis in *Kousisis*, the Fifth Circuit emphasizes that the statutory text—“to obtain … money or property”—does not demand a net loss to the victim; the mere act of obtaining, irrespective of the victim’s eventual position, satisfies the element. The policy rationale is clear: fraud statutes are designed to protect the integrity of commercial transactions, not to guarantee that every victim ends up poorer.

## Assessing “Intent to Obtain” in Practice  

The judicial challenge now shifts to fact‑finding: how do judges and juries determine whether a defendant possessed the requisite intent to obtain money or property? The *Constantinescu* opinion suggests that prosecutors can rely on circumstantial evidence of profit motive—e.g., the defendants’ own gains, the timing of sales coincident with inflated prices, and communications indicating a plan to “cash out.”  

Defendants, however, may argue that their posts were opinions or investment advice, not deliberate misrepresentations, and that any purchases made by followers were voluntary. The line between “puffery” and “material falsehood” will be drawn based on the specificity of the statements, the defendants’ actual holdings, and whether they concealed the fact that they were selling.  

Future courts may look to the “totality‑of‑the‑circumstances” test used in *Kousisis*: 

1. **Materiality** – Is the misstatement about a fact that a reasonable investor would consider important?  
2. **Deception** – Did the defendant intend the victim to rely on the false statement?  
3. **Obtaining** – Did the defendant seek, or actually receive, money or property as a result?  

Proof of profit alone can satisfy (3), even if the victim’s purchase price reflects market value at the time of the transaction.

## Policy Arguments: Expansion vs. Restraint  

### In Favor of Expansion  

Proponents argue that the “no‑loss” rule creates a dangerous loophole. Sophisticated schemes can be structured so that victims receive assets of roughly equal value, allowing perpetrators to escape liability while still undermining market integrity. By focusing on the defendant’s intent to obtain, courts can target the *motive* behind deceptive conduct, preserving the deterrent effect of fraud statutes.

Moreover, the modern financial landscape—high‑frequency trading, algorithmic advice, and influencer‑driven markets—makes it increasingly difficult to track precise monetary loss. A victim‑loss requirement could impede prosecution of rapidly executed schemes where loss is diffused across thousands of small investors.

### Against Expansion  

Critics warn that eliminating the loss requirement may criminalize conduct that is more akin to reckless or negligent misrepresentation than deceit. They contend that the fraud statutes were intended to protect property rights, not to punish mere “bad advice” that happens to generate profit for the speaker. There is a risk of over‑charging ordinary social‑media personalities who, without intent to profit, share bullish opinions that later prove unfounded.

Furthermore, the expansion may burden juries with complex economic analyses, forcing them to infer intent from profit calculations rather than clear evidence of deception.

## Implications for Defense Strategies  

The “no‑loss” defense—asserting that the plaintiff suffered no pecuniary injury—has been a mainstay in securities‑fraud litigation. *Constantinescu* renders that defense largely ineffective in the Fifth Circuit, and likely elsewhere as *Kousisis* spreads.  

Defendants will now need to focus on:  

* **Challenging the materiality** of the alleged misstatements, arguing that the statements were opinion or forward‑looking predictions, not false facts.  
* **Proving lack of intent to obtain** by showing that any profit was incidental, that the defendants disclosed their positions, or that they genuinely believed in the investment thesis.  
* **Emphasizing the “receipt of equal‑value consideration”** to argue that the transaction was a fair exchange, not a fraudulent extraction of property.  

In practice, we may see more reliance on forensic accounting, email metadata, and social‑media analytics to demonstrate (or refute) a coordinated profit‑maximizing scheme. Plea negotiations may also shift, with prosecutors leveraging the broader theory of “fraudulent inducement” to secure agreements even when victim loss is minimal.

## Downstream Effects  

The Fifth Circuit’s opinion will likely be cited in other circuits confronting similar fact patterns—particularly those involving “influencer” securities promotions. As the Supreme Court’s *Kousisis* framework gains traction, we can anticipate a convergence toward a uniform “intent-to‑obtain” standard nationwide.

Legislatively, Congress may respond with clarifying amendments that either narrow the fraud statutes to require victim loss or, conversely, broaden them to explicitly encompass “no‑loss” fraudulent inducement. Until then, prosecutors have a potent tool to chase the ever‑evolving tactics of online market manipulators, while defendants must brace for a legal landscape where profit alone can be enough to sustain a fraud conviction.
