Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Does a securities‑fraud indictment require a traditional property interest, or can “money or property” be satisfied by inducing a purchase?
Question:
In United States v. Constantinescu the Fifth Circuit reversed a dismissal, saying the indictment alleged a scheme to defraud followers of “money or property” by fraudulently inducing them to buy securities. How does the court interpret the phrase “money or property” under 18 U.S.C. § 1348? Is the induced purchase of a security sufficient to satisfy the property‑interest element, even though the victim ultimately receives a market‑value security?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The Fifth Circuit holds that “money or property” under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 is satisfied whenever the fraud scheme causes a victim to part with cash or an asset‑like interest – even if the victim receives a security of roughly equal market value in return.  The statute does **not** require the victim to lose a “traditional” property right such as cash alone, nor does it require the victim to be left poorer after the transaction.  Because the indictment alleges that the defendants **fraudulently induced** their followers to purchase securities, the “money or property” element is met.

---

### Why the phrase “money or property” is satisfied by an induced purchase

1. **Statutory language and the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory**  
   § 1348 criminalizes a scheme “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses … any **money or property** in connection with the purchase or sale of any security.”  The Fifth Circuit reads this in line with the Supreme Court’s recent decisions in *Kousisis v. United States* and *Ciminelli v. United States*.  *Kousisis* expressly recognizes a **fraudulent‑inducement theory**: a defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property,” and the crime is complete when the defendant obtains that money or property, **regardless of whether the victim receives something of equal value**.  The Court in *Constantinescu* applies that same reasoning.

2. **Distinguishing the “right‑to‑control” theory rejected in *Ciminelli***  
   *Ciminelli* held that a fraud conviction cannot rest on the deprivation of merely “valuable economic information” – the “right‑to‑control” theory.  The Fifth Circuit emphasizes that the *Constantinescu* indictment does **not** rely on that theory; it alleges that the defendants caused followers to **hand over cash** (or an equivalent property interest) by convincing them to buy a stock.  The victims’ “property” is the money they spent; the security they receive is *not* the protected interest the statute requires the government to prove.

3. **The indictment’s allegations meet the element**  
   - ¶ 13 of the superseding indictment states that defendants “posted false … information … so that other investors were **induced to purchase the security**.”  
   - The purchase requires the followers to surrender **money** (or other property) in exchange for the security.  
   - The government therefore alleges that the defendants obtained **money or property** from the victims, satisfying the statutory element.

4. **No requirement that the victim suffer a net loss**  
   The Fifth Circuit cites *Kousisis* for the proposition that the fraud statutes do **not** demand a “mirror‑image” loss.  A defendant can be convicted even if the victim ends up with an asset of market value, because the crime is completed when the defendant obtains the victim’s money or property through deception.  The same logic underlies the Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that the indictment is sufficient.

---

### How the decision should be read for future cases

- **“Money or property”** is a broad term that includes any **exchange of value** caused by a fraudulent misrepresentation, not merely the loss of cash or a tangible asset.  
- An **induced purchase** of a security is enough because the victim’s **money** (or other property) is transferred to the defendant (or a related party) in exchange for the security.  
- The fact that the victim receives a security of comparable market value **does not negate** the “money or property” element; the Supreme Court has held that the fraud statute protects the **act of obtaining** that money/property, not the net economic outcome.  
- The prosecution does **not** need to allege that the victim is left poorer; it only needs to allege that the defendant **intended to obtain** the victim’s money or property through deception.

---

**Bottom line:** In *United States v. Constantinescu* the Fifth Circuit interprets “money or property” under § 1348 to include the victim’s money that is handed over in a fraud‑induced securities purchase.  The receipt of a market‑value security by the victim does not defeat the element; the indictment’s allegations of fraudulent inducement satisfy the statutory requirement.
