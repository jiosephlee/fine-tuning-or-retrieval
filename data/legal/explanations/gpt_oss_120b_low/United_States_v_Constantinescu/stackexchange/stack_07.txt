Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What is the “double jeopardy” purpose of an indictment, and how does the Fifth Circuit ensure it is met in fraud cases?
Question:
The opinion cites United States v. Gaytan, noting an indictment must provide a “double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.” In the context of securities‑fraud prosecutions, how does the court verify that the indictment’s allegations are sufficiently particular to protect the defendants from later re‑prosecution on the same conduct?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Fifth Circuit treats the “double‑jeopardy purpose” of an indictment as one of three statutory requisites for a sufficient charging document.  As Judge Engelhardt restates, an indictment must:

1. **Allege each element of the offense;**  
2. **Fairly inform the defendant of the charge;** and  
3. **Provide the defendant with a double‑jeopardy defense against future prosecutions**  
(United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996)).

The double‑jeopardy function is satisfied only when the **theory of the crime and the factual allegations are sufficiently particular to bind the government to the same “scheme” in any later proceeding**.  If the indictment is vague enough that the government could later bring a charge on a different set of facts or a different legal theory, the defendant could be subject to multiple prosecutions for the same conduct, violating the Fifth Amendment.

In *United States v. Constantinescu* the Fifth Circuit explained how it checks that the indictment meets this requirement in securities‑fraud cases:

1. **Take the indictment’s allegations as true.**  
   The court reviews the charging document de novo, assuming every factual claim is correct, and asks whether the alleged conduct *already* satisfies all elements of the offense.  (See *Rafoi*, 60 F.4th at 993.)

2. **Require a complete “scheme‑to‑defraud” allegation.**  
   The indictment must describe a **coherent scheme**—not just isolated misstatements—but a plan that “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived” (see *Evans*, 892 F.3d at 711‑12).  In *Constantinescu* the superseding indictment alleged that defendants **“posted false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security … and then secretly sold the security for a profit.”**  This allegation ties the deceptive conduct directly to the obtaining of money or property, satisfying the scheme element.

3. **Require an “intent to defraud” allegation.**  
   The charging document must show that the defendants **intended both to deceive and to obtain money or property**.  The opinion points out that the indictment’s statement that defendants “profited $114 million” from the pump‑and‑dump scheme is sufficient to demonstrate the required intent (see *Kousisis*, 145 S.Ct. at 1388‑89).

4. **Link the alleged conduct to the victims’ “money or property.”**  
   Because the indictment alleges that followers were induced to **purchase securities**—a transaction that requires the victims to part with money or other property—the government is bound to that particular mode of fraud.  The court notes that, under the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory approved in *Kousisis*, the fraud statutes protect the victim’s money or property even if the victim later receives securities of market value.  (See *Constantinescu*, ¶ 13.)

5. **Resulting limitation on future prosecutions.**  
   By demanding that the indictment specify the **misrepresentations, the induced purchases, and the defendants’ profit motive**, the Fifth Circuit ensures that any subsequent prosecution must be grounded in the same factual “scheme.”  The government cannot later pivot to a “right‑to‑control” theory (rejected in *Ciminelli*) or to a different set of misrepresentations without running afoul of the double‑jeopardy bar.

**In short**, the Fifth Circuit satisfies the double‑jeopardy purpose by requiring that an indictment in a securities‑fraud case **clearly outline a single, continuous scheme that involves the deception of victims into parting with money or property, and that the defendants intended to obtain that money or property.**  When the charging document meets these particularity requirements, the defendant is protected from any later prosecution that attempts to relitigate the same conduct under a different legal theory.
