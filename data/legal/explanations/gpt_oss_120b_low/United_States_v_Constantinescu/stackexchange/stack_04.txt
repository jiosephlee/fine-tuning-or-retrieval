Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What standard of review does the Fifth Circuit apply to a motion to dismiss for insufficiency of an indictment?
Question:
The opinion states the court reviews the sufficiency of an indictment de novo, citing United States v. Rafoi. How does that de novo standard differ from a more deferential review (e.g., for facial sufficiency), and what specific elements must the court look for when evaluating a fraud‐related indictment?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Fifth Circuit reviews a motion to dismiss an indictment for “insufficiency of the indictment” **de novo**.  That means the appellate court does **not** give any deference to the district court’s factual findings; it simply takes the allegations in the indictment as true and asks whether, on their face, the statutory elements of the charged offense are stated.  United States v. **Rafoi**, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023) (cited in *Constantinescu*).  

### How de novo review differs from a more deferential standard  

A deferential (facial‑sufficiency) standard—often phrased as “review for facial sufficiency” or “review under the **Rule 12(b)(3)** standard—asks only whether the indictment **fails to allege a crime at all**.  Under that approach a court may look to the *overall* adequacy of the charge and may give weight to the district court’s interpretation of the indictment’s language.  The focus is narrow: does the pleading omit an essential element such that the charge is legally insufficient?

By contrast, the Fifth Circuit’s de novo rule requires the appellate court to:

1. **Take the indictment’s factual allegations as true.**  
2. **Compare those allegations, element by element, with the statutory requirements** of the offense.  
3. **Conclude whether each material element is alleged**; if any element is missing, the indictment is insufficient and must be dismissed.

Thus, the de novo review is a *plain‑language* test of whether the charge, as written, states an offense, without regard to how the district court interpreted the pleadings.

### Elements the Fifth Circuit looks for in a securities‑fraud indictment  

In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the court spelled out the substantive elements that must appear in the indictment for a violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348/1349 (securities fraud and conspiracy to commit securities fraud).  The opinion treats the securities‑fraud statute as borrowing the classic “scheme‑to‑defraud” language of the mail‑ and wire‑fraud statutes and therefore requires the same two‑part analysis:

| Element | What the indictment must allege (per *Constantinescu*) |
|---------|--------------------------------------------------------|
| **(1) Scheme to defraud** | A *material misrepresentation* or *false pretenses* intended to deceive another party **and** an intent to obtain something of value (money or property) from the victim. *Id.* at 339 (quoting *Evans*, 892 F.3d at 711‑12). |
| **(2) Intent to defraud** | The defendant **intended** (a) to deceive the victim and (b) to cause some harm—or at least to obtain the victim’s money or property—by that deceit. *Id.* at 339‑340 (quoting *Evans*, 892 F.3d at 712). |

The opinion emphasizes two doctrinal points drawn from recent Supreme Court cases:

1. **The “fraudulent‑inducement” theory is sufficient.**  *Kousisis v. United States* (2025) held that a fraud conviction is proper when the defendant uses a material false statement to induce a victim to **hand over money or property**, even if the victim ultimately receives something of equal value.  The indictment therefore must allege that the defendants **induced followers to purchase securities**, i.e., to part with their money.

2. **Actual economic loss is not required.**  The Supreme Court in *Kousisis* rejected the notion that a victim must suffer a pecuniary loss; it is enough that the defendant **sought to obtain** the victim’s money or property.  Accordingly, the Fifth Circuit looks for allegations that the defendants intended to **profit** (the “dump” portion) rather than merely to deprive victims of information.

Applying those principles, the Fifth Circuit found that the superseding indictment in *Constantinescu*:

* Alleged that defendants **purchased** a security and then **posted false, misleading information** to cause others to buy it (fulfilling the “material misrepresentation” requirement).  
* Alleged that the scheme was designed to **inflate the price** so the defendants could **sell** at a profit (showing intent to obtain money/property).  
* Cited the profit figure ($114 million) to demonstrate the **object of the scheme** was monetary gain.

Because each of the two statutory elements is alleged, the indictment satisfies the de novo sufficiency test, and the appellate court reversed the district court’s dismissal.

### Take‑away for a motion to dismiss

When a defendant moves to dismiss an indictment on insufficiency grounds in the Fifth Circuit, the court will:

1. **Read the indictment’s allegations as true.**  
2. **Identify whether the indictment states each of the statutory elements** (scheme to defraud + intent to defraud).  
3. **Ignore any argument that the district court’s factual analysis was correct**; the appellate court does not defer to that analysis.  

If the indictment contains factual allegations that a defendant **misrepresented** material facts and **intended to obtain money or property** from the victim—whether or not the victim ultimately suffered a net loss—the Fifth Circuit will deem the indictment sufficient.
