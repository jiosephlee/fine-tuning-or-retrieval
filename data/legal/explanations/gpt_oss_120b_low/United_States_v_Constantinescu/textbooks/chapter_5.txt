Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 5: # Court’s Application of Law to the Facts  

In this chapter we dissect the Fifth Circuit’s reasoning that the superseding indictment in *United States v. Constantinescu* sufficiently alleged both a “scheme to defraud” and the requisite “intent to defraud.”  The court’s analysis pivots on the distinction between a mere deprivation of information and an actionable fraud‑inducement scheme, the application of the Supreme Court’s “fraudulent‑inducement” theory, and the rejection of the defendants’ arguments that the indictment failed to identify a protected property interest or a victim‑loss requirement.  By following the court’s step‑by‑step logic, law students can see how appellate courts translate statutory elements into a factual matrix and how precedent shapes that translation.

## Why the Indictment Goes Beyond Depriving Followers of Information  

The defendants contended that the indictment merely alleged they “deprived their followers of potentially valuable economic information,” a characterization that, under the Supreme Court’s decision in *Ciminelli v. United States* (2023), would not satisfy a fraud charge because it does not target a traditional property interest.  The Fifth Circuit rejected that view for two interconnected reasons.

First, the indictment does not limit its allegations to the withholding of information.  Paragraphs 13–14 of the superseding indictment describe a concrete conduct pattern: the defendants **purchased a security**, **posted false and misleading statements** about that security on social‑media platforms, **induced other investors to buy the security**, and then **secretly sold (dumped) the security for a profit**.  Each of those steps is a factual allegation that the defendants *caused* other people to part with their **money** (or, equivalently, their property interest in cash).  The key phrase “induced … to purchase the security” signals that the fraud was not about the mere loss of information but about the **transactional relinquishment of money** in exchange for a security whose price was artificially inflated.

Second, the indictment’s language mirrors the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory articulated by the Supreme Court in *Kousisis v. United States* (2025).  Under that theory, a fraud claim arises when a defendant **uses a material misrepresentation to trick a victim into a contract that requires the victim to hand over money or property**.  The indictment’s allegations satisfy each element of that theory:

* **Material misrepresentation:** “false and misleading information about the security” (¶ 13).  
* **Inducement:** Followers were “induced … to purchase the security” (¶ 13).  
* **Transfer of money/property:** The purchase requires the followers to pay cash, thereby transferring money to the defendants (or to the market, which the defendants then capture by dumping).  

Thus, the court concluded that the indictment does more than allege the deprivation of intangible information; it alleges a concrete scheme that coerces victims into surrendering money—clearly a protected property interest under the fraud statutes.

## Application of the Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory to the Social‑Media “Pump” Conduct  

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Kousisis* is central to its analysis.  *Kousisis* clarified that the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory is **not** a repackaging of the disfavored “right‑to‑control” theory rejected in *Ciminelli*.  Instead, it focuses on the defendant’s **goal of obtaining money or property** from the victim, regardless of whether the victim receives something of equal face value in return.

Applying that framework to the “pump” conduct, the court identified the following factual correspondences:

| Fraud Element (Kousisis) | Alleged Conduct in Constantinescu |
|--------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Material misrepresentation** | Defendants posted “false and misleading information” about the securities’ value and their own positions. |
| **Use of misrepresentation to induce a contract** | The false posts were intended to cause followers to **enter into purchase contracts** for the securities. |
| **Victim’s transfer of money/property** | Each purchase required the follower to **pay cash**, transferring money to the market (and ultimately to the defendants when they dumped). |
| **Defendant’s objective to obtain money/property** | The indictment states that defendants “pump … and dump … to profit” (¶ 13–14). |

Because the indictment contains factual predicates that map neatly onto each element of the fraudulent‑inducement theory, the court deemed the statutory “scheme to defraud” element satisfied.  The presence of social‑media platforms does not alter the analysis; the platforms are merely the medium through which the false statements were disseminated.  The essential inquiry remains whether the defendants **intended** to induce victims to part with money, and the indictment’s allegations answer that in the affirmative.

## Analysis of Intent: Profit Motive Satisfies “Intent to Obtain Money or Property”  

The defendants also argued that the indictment fails to allege an “intent to defraud” because it emphasizes the defendants’ own profit rather than any intended injury to victims.  The Fifth Circuit, guided by *Kousisis*, rejected this argument by emphasizing that the fraud statutes **do not require proof of a victim‑loss motive**.  The relevant Supreme Court language is that a defendant “schemes to obtain the victim’s money or property, regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.”

The indictment’s language provides at least two independent sources of the requisite intent:

1. **Profit motive:** Paragraph 13 states that the defendants’ scheme was designed to “pump … and dump … to profit.”  The phrase “to profit” plainly indicates an intention to **obtain money** for themselves.  Under the statutory language of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 1349, the “intent to obtain” is satisfied when the defendant seeks monetary gain, even if that gain is derived from the victims’ purchases.

2. **Deceptive act aimed at victims:** The indictment also alleges that the defendants “posted false … information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.”  The purpose of the false statements was **to cause the victims to send money**, not merely to mislead them for the defendants’ own satisfaction.  The combination of (a) a false statement, (b) the intention that the statement cause the victim to act, and (c) the expectation that the victim’s action results in a transfer of money, fulfills the “intent to defraud” requirement.

The court noted that the **profit motive itself is a sufficient manifestation of intent** because it demonstrates that the defendants consciously pursued an economic objective achieved through deceit.  There is no need to prove that the defendants *desired* the victims to be poorer; the law is satisfied once the defendants **purposefully sought the victims’ money** as the object of their scheme.

## Rejection of the Defendants’ “No Property Interest” and “No Victim Harm” Arguments  

Having established that the indictment alleges a fraudulent inducement of money, the Fifth Circuit turned to the defendants’ two remaining contentions:

### 1. No Property Interest  

The district court’s dismissal relied on the premise that the only alleged victim loss was the deprivation of “potentially valuable economic information,” which is not a property interest under *Ciminelli*.  The Fifth Circuit clarified that the indictment **does not rely on such a theory**.  By alleging that followers **purchased securities**—thereby exchanging cash for an overvalued asset—the indictment identifies a **clear property right**: the followers’ **right to their own money**.  The loss of that money, however temporarily, constitutes a deprivation of a traditional property interest that the fraud statutes protect.

### 2. No Victim Harm  

The defendants argued that because the victims ultimately received market‑value securities, there is no “economic loss,” and therefore no fraud.  The court again pointed to *Kousisis*, which rejected a “mirror‑image” requirement—that the victim’s loss must equal the defendant’s gain.  The Fifth Circuit emphasized that the fraud statutes focus on the **transfer of money or property** obtained through deceit, not on the net result of the transaction.  Even if the victims later realized a market‑value security, the **initial act of inducing them to part with cash based on false statements** is sufficient to constitute fraud.  This reasoning aligns with longstanding precedent that a fraud is complete at the moment the victim partakes of the defendant’s scheme and surrenders money, irrespective of subsequent restitution or lack of loss.

### Synthesis  

By methodically aligning the indictment’s factual allegations with the Supreme Court’s fraudulent‑inducement framework, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment **clearly states a fraud offense**.  The indictment enumerates:

1. A **scheme** (false statements, social‑media dissemination, induced purchases).  
2. An **intent** (profit motive, purposeful deception to obtain money).  
3. A **victim’s surrender of money** (purchase of securities).  

Consequently, the district court’s dismissal for failure to allege a property interest or victim harm was untenable, and the appellate court reversed and remanded.

---

**Takeaway for the Reader:**  
When evaluating the sufficiency of an indictment for fraud, focus on whether the charging document **explicitly ties deceptive conduct to a victim’s transfer of money or property**.  The presence of a profit motive and the use of false statements to induce purchases are sufficient to satisfy both the “scheme” and “intent” elements, even if the alleged victims do not ultimately suffer a net financial loss.  The Fifth Circuit’s reasoning in *Constantinescu* demonstrates how appellate courts apply the Supreme Court’s fraudulent‑inducement theory to modern, technology‑driven schemes.
