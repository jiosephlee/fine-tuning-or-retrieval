Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 2: # Legal Standards Governing Indictment Sufficiency  

The Fifth Circuit’s approach to determining whether a federal indictment “states an offense” is a cornerstone of criminal procedure.  In *United States v. Constantinescu* the court applied this framework to decide whether the government’s charging document met the constitutional and statutory requirements for a viable fraud prosecution.  This chapter unpacks the Fifth Circuit’s de novo review of indictment sufficiency, outlines the three statutory requirements that an indictment must satisfy, and shows how those requirements are applied through the circuit’s leading cases.  Finally, we connect the analysis to Rule 7(b) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, which codifies the elements that must appear in a charging document.  

---  

## De novo Review of Indictment Sufficiency  

When a defendant moves to dismiss an indictment on the ground that it fails to state an offense, the appellate court conducts a **de novo** review.  This means the court examines the indictment **as if it were the first fact‑finder**, taking every factual allegation in the charging document as true and determining whether, on that face, the required elements of the charged offense are alleged.  The Fifth Circuit articulated this standard in *United States v. Rafoi*, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023):  

> “The court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated.”  

De novo review is distinct from a more deferential standard that might be applied to a trial court’s discretionary rulings.  Because the adequacy of an indictment is a jurisdictional issue—​the government must first have a valid charging document before any trial can proceed—​the appellate court does not give weight to the district court’s credibility determinations or evidentiary findings.  The focus is purely on **whether the statutory elements are pleaded**.  

---  

## The Three Statutory Requirements for a Sufficient Indictment  

The Fifth Circuit derives its sufficiency test from the language of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) and the Supreme Court’s “strict pleading” rule in *Bell v. United States*, 432 U.S. 106 (1977).  The circuit has distilled the requirement into three core components.  

1. **Enumerating Each Prima Facie Element of the Charged Offense**  
   An indictment must set forth every element that the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt.  If any element is omitted, the indictment is facially deficient.  This includes both the actus reus (the prohibited conduct) and the mens rea (the required mental state).  In *United States v. Gaytan*, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996), the court explained that omission of an element “constitutes a failure to allege a material element of the offense.”  

2. **Providing Fair Notice to the Defendant**  
   The charging document must “fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him,” a principle rooted in the Sixth Amendment’s Confrontation Clause and the Due Process Clause.  The indictment need not be a narrative of every factual detail, but it must be clear enough that the defendant can understand the essential nature of the accusation and can prepare a defense.  *Gaytan* emphasizes that “fair notice” is satisfied when the defendant can reasonably infer what conduct is alleged.  

3. **Protecting Against Double Jeopardy**  
   The indictment must also give the defendant “a double‑jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.”  This means the charging document must be sufficiently specific that the government cannot later bring a new indictment for the same conduct under a different theory without violating the Fifth Amendment.  The Supreme Court articulated this double‑jeopardy protection in *Blockburger v. United States*, 284 U.S._⊥_ 299 (1932), and the Fifth Circuit has applied it repeatedly, requiring that each count allege a distinct element set.  

When an indictment satisfies all three prongs, the Fifth Circuit concludes that the government has properly stated an offense, and the indictment survives a motion to dismiss.  The analysis in *Constantinescu* follows this template: the court examined whether the indictment alleged the elements of securities fraud (scheme to defraud, intent to defraud, and obtaining money or property) and found it did.

## Fifth Circuit Cases Defining Indictment Adequacy  

The circuit’s jurisprudence provides concrete illustrations of how the three requirements operate in practice.  

### United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545 (5th Cir. 1996)  

*Gaytan* is the seminal Fifth Circuit authority on the three‑part test.  The court held that an indictment that merely recites the statutory language without linking it to specific conduct fails the “fair notice” requirement.  The decision underscores that **mere legal conclusions are insufficient**; the prosecution must articulate factual allegations that give life to each element.  

### United States v. Guzman‑Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233 (5th Cir. 2000)  

In *Guzman‑Ocampo*, the Fifth Circuit emphasized that an indictment must “enumerate each prima facie element” and that the failure to do so is fatal on a motion to dismiss.  The court rejected a government pleading that left the mental‑state element of “knowledge” undeveloped, holding that the omission amounted to a failure to allege a material element.  This case is frequently cited when evaluating whether a fraud indictment includes the requisite intent element.  

### United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982 (5th Cir. 2023)  

*Rafoi* clarified the **de novo** nature of the review.  The Fifth Circuit reiterated that the appellate court “takes the allegations of the indictment as true” and does not weigh the credibility of the evidence supporting those allegations.  The decision is useful for students because it illustrates the strict, mechanical approach courts take: if the indictment’s language, read literally, captures all statutory elements, the court will reverse a dismissal even if, in reality, the government’s evidence would be weak.

## Interaction with Rule 7(b) of the Federal Criminal Rules  

Rule 7(b) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure mirrors the statutory requirements and provides a procedural checklist for prosecutors:  

> “A pleading (information, indictment, or information‑and‑complaint) must contain the **essential elements** of the offense charged, **including** a short and plain statement of the nature of the charge, and **identification of the required elements** of the offense with particularity.”  

The rule’s language aligns closely with the three prongs described above:  

| Rule 7(b) Requirement | Corresponding Fifth Circuit Prong |
|----------------------|-----------------------------------|
| “Essential elements of the offense charged” | **Element enumeration** – every statutory element must be alleged. |
| “Short and plain statement … that the defendant will understand the charge” | **Fair notice** – the defendant must be able to prepare a defense. |
| “Particularity” (including “specific facts”) | **Double‑jeopardy protection** – prevents the government from later reprosecuting on a different theory. |

In practice, a Fifth Circuit court will read the indictment, compare its allegations to the statutory text, and ask whether Rule 7(b) is satisfied.  If the indictment lists the statutory elements but does not tie them to factual conduct (e.g., merely says “defendant committed securities fraud” without describing the “pump‑and‑dump” scheme), the court will find a **failure to provide fair notice**.  If the indictment omits the required mental‑state language—such as “intent to defraud” in a securities‑fraud count—the court will deem the charging document deficient for **not enumerating an element**.  Finally, if the indictment is so vague that the government could later tack on a new theory (for example, adding a “mail fraud” count based on the same conduct), the court will view this as a **double‑jeopardy risk**.

## Applying the Standards in *United States v. Constantinescu*  

In *Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit applied the de novo test, took the indictment’s allegations as true, and asked whether the three statutory requirements were met:  

1. **Element enumeration** – The indictment alleged (a) a scheme to “pump” securities by posting false statements, (b) the defendants’ deception (material misrepresentations), and (c) the objective of obtaining money/property (profit of $114 million).  Each of these maps onto the elements of 18 U.S.C. § 1348 (scheme to defraud, intent to defraud, obtaining money/property).  

2. **Fair notice** – By describing the “pump‑and‑dump” mechanism and the defendants’ social‑media conduct, the indictment gave the defendants clear notice of the conduct alleged—namely, using false statements to induce purchases.  The defendants could therefore prepare a defense concerning the truthfulness of their posts and the existence of a “scheme.”  

3. **Double‑jeopardy protection** – The indictment listed specific counts (conspiracy under § 1349 and multiple fraud counts under §§ 1348 & 2).  Each count pleaded a distinct statutory violation, preventing the government from later reprosecuting on a different fraud theory without new charges.  

Because the indictment satisfied all three prongs, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the district court erred in dismissing the case and reversed.  

---  

### Takeaways for the Law Student  

* **De novo review** means the appellate court does not weigh evidence; it only checks whether the charging document, on its face, contains all statutory elements.  
* An indictment must **enumerate every element**, give the defendant **fair notice**, and protect the defendant from **double jeopardy**.  
* The Fifth Circuit’s leading cases—*Gaytan*, *Guzman‑Ocampo*, and *Rafoi*—provide the doctrinal backbone for evaluating indictment sufficiency.  
* **Rule 7(b)** acts as a procedural embodiment of these principles; reading the rule in concert with the circuit’s case law ensures that an indictment is both legally and procedurally sound.  

Understanding these standards equips you to assess whether an indictment survives a motion to dismiss and to anticipate how appellate courts will handle challenges to charging documents in future cases.
