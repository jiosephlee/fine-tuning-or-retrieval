Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why did the Second Circuit deem the fair‑use analysis sufficient to dismiss the copyright claim at the pleading stage?
Question:
In Santos v. Kimmel the appellate court affirmed the district court’s Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal, stating that the fair‑use defense was “clearly established by the complaint.” I thought fair‑use determinations required factual development and a full evidentiary hearing. How can a court decide that a use is fair‑use purely on the pleadings, and what precedent allows such a dismissal?
Answer:
**Answer**

A Rule 12(b)(6) motion attacks the *legal* sufficiency of the complaint, not the *truth* of the alleged facts.  In a copyright case the plaintiff must allege enough facts to show that **fair use is not “clearly established”** as a defense.  If the pleadings, together with any incorporated documents, make it evident that a reasonable court would find the four § 107 factors weigh in favor of fair use, the complaint fails to state a claim and can be dismissed.

The Second Circuit follows that approach in several recent cases, and it does so again in **Santos v. Kimmel**, 2025 WL … (2d Cir. Oct. 6 2025).  The court’s reasoning rests on three points that are worth spelling out.

---

### 1. The pleading standard for fair‑use defenses

Under **DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.**, 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010), a court evaluating a 12(b)(6) motion “considers the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference.”  The court may also take judicial notice of public copyright registrations.  The same standard applies to affirmative defenses such as fair use.

The Second Circuit has held that **fair use can be “clearly established” at the pleading stage** when the allegations, taken as true, demonstrate that the use is transformative, that the original work is unpublished or not commercially valuable, that only a small portion was taken, and that there is no market harm.  See **TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum**, 839 F.3d 168, 178‑79 (2d Cir. 2016) (fair‑use defense “so clearly established … as to support dismissal … at the pleading stage”).

Thus, a court is not required to wait for discovery to “develop the facts” of a fair‑use inquiry; it may decide that the plaintiff has already pleaded enough to defeat the claim.

---

### 2. How the *Santos* court applied that standard

The Second Circuit examined the **four statutory factors** as pleaded:

| Factor | What Santos alleged | Court’s finding |
|--------|--------------------|-----------------|
| **Purpose & character** | Kimmel aired the videos in a satirical segment titled “Will Santos Say It?” designed to mock Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. | The court concluded the use is *transformative* because a reasonable observer would see the segment as commentary, not as a simple repost of Santos’s original messages.  The plaintiff’s own allegation that the videos originally conveyed “feelings of hope …” supports the transformation analysis. |
| **Nature of the work** | The videos were personal messages posted to Cameo and published the same day they were created. | Personal, non‑creative works weigh in favor of fair use. |
| **Amount & substantiality** | Kimmel used the entire video (the only version that existed). | The court noted that when the entire work is used in a *parodic* or *critical* context, the amount taken is permissible, citing **Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music**, 510 U.S. 569 (1994). |
| **Effect on market** | No evidence that Kimmel’s broadcast usurped any market for Santos’s videos; the plaintiff could not license a “critical review” market. | The court found no market harm, mirroring the reasoning in **Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive**, 115 F.4th 163, 189 (2d Cir. 2024). |

Because each factor, taken together, **clearly favoured fair use**, the court held that the defense was “clearly established by the complaint.”  Accordingly, the complaint failed to state a claim for infringement and was dismissed under Rule 12(b)(6).

The opinion also emphasizes that “the relevant copyright registrations … establish that the videos were published on the same day they were created” (Appendix 160–69), allowing the court to rely on the registration record without further fact‑finding.

---

### 3. Precedent that permits dismissal at the pleading stage

Beyond **DiFolco** and **TCA Television**, the Second Circuit has repeatedly allowed fair‑use defenses to defeat a claim on a Rule 12(b)(6) basis:

* **Cariou v. Prince**, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) – the court can decide fair use by looking only at the “original version … and the [second] work at issue.”
* **Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive**, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) – fair use is a “flexible concept” that may be “so clearly established … as to support dismissal” early in the case.
* **Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith**, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) – the “purpose and character” factor focuses on the *reasonable observer* test, not the parties’ subjective intent, making it a fact that can be pleaded.

These cases collectively articulate the doctrine that, when the complaint itself demonstrates that the four § 107 factors tilt strongly toward fair use, a court may treat the defense as “clearly established” and dismiss the infringement claim without waiting for discovery.

---

### Bottom line

The Second Circuit does **not** require a full evidentiary hearing to apply the fair‑use test at the pleading stage.  If the plaintiff’s own allegations, together with any incorporated documents (e.g., copyright registrations), make it apparent that the four statutory factors favor the defendant, the fair‑use defense is “clearly established” and the complaint fails to state a claim.  **Santos v. Kimmel** follows this line of precedent, finding that the alleged mock‑critical use of the videos was transformative, non‑commercial, and absent market harm, and therefore the fair‑use defense was dispositive at the pleading level.

### What does it mean that the “purpose and character” factor focuses on whether the use is "transformative" rather than the parties’ subjective intent?
Question:
The opinion emphasizes that a reasonable observer, not the copyright holder or Kimmel’s intent, determines transformation. How is that reasonable‑observer standard applied in practice, especially when the original creator (Santos) claims the purpose of the work was the same as Kimmel’s parody? Are there specific tests or examples the court uses to evaluate this?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The Second Circuit treats the “purpose and character” factor of the fair‑use analysis as an objective question: a court asks what a **reasonable, informed observer** would conclude about the secondary work, not what either the copyright holder or the secondary user says they intended. In *Santos v. Kimmel* the court applied that standard by looking at the overall context of the “Will Santos Say It?” segment, the way the videos were presented on a comedy‑talk‑show, and the expressive purpose of the parody. Because a reasonable viewer would see the segment as commentary that “mocked” Santos rather than as a new commercial exploitation of his original videos, the court found the use “transformative” and therefore weighed heavily in favor of fair use.  

Below is a step‑by‑step guide to how the court applies the reasonable‑observer test, together with the specific language it relies on in *Santos*.

---

### 1. The statutory framework  

Fair use is judged by the four statutory factors in 17 U.S.C. § 107. The first factor—*purpose and character of the use*—asks whether the secondary work “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character” (the *Warhol* test). The Second Circuit stresses that this inquiry is **objective**:  

> “whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user.”  *Santos v. Kimmel*, 2025 WL XXXXX, at 9‑10.  

Thus the court does not ask “what did Kimmel *say* his purpose was?” nor “what does Santos claim his original purpose was?” It asks what the **average viewer**, aware of both works, would understand the secondary work to be doing.

### 2. The “reasonable observer” is a factual construct, not a legal fiction  

The opinion does not give a rigid checklist, but it points to three attributes that help the court determine what a reasonable observer would see:

| Attribute | How the court looks at it | Example from *Santos* |
|-----------|--------------------------|----------------------|
| **Context of presentation** | Is the work presented in a setting that signals commentary, criticism, or parody? | The videos were shown on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!*, a comedy program known for satirical segments. |
| **Tone and framing** | Does the secondary work adopt a tone that signals mockery or irony rather than plain replication? | The segment’s title “Will Santos Say It?” and the host’s sarcastic introduction cue the audience to a mocking purpose. |
| **Effect on the audience’s perception of the original** | Does the new work lead the audience to view the original in a different light? | A reasonable viewer would see Santos’s original, earnest messages of “hope, strength, perseverance” recast as “absurd things for money,” i.e., a critique of his willingness to monetize his persona. |

These factors together let the court infer that the secondary work **adds a new expressive purpose**—commentary on Santos’s public persona—rather than merely republishing his videos.

### 3. Subjective intent is irrelevant  

Even though Santos argues that his own purpose in creating the videos was “to comment on the willingness of Santos to say absurd things for money,” the court rejects that argument because the *objective* view is what matters. The opinion states:

> “Santos does not dispute the District Court’s finding that the purpose of the allegedly infringing use was ‘to comment on the willingness of Santos … to say absurd things for money.’ … But whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, **not** the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user.”  *Santos*, 2025 WL XXXXX, at 9‑10.

Thus the plaintiff’s claim that his original purpose coincides with Kimmel’s is irrelevant to the transformation analysis.

### 4. No formal “test,” but the courts use the *Warhol* and *Campbell* lenses  

The Second Circuit frequently cites two Supreme Court cases for guidance:

1. ***Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music, Inc.*, 510 U.S. 569 (1994)** – “The heart of the matter is whether the secondary work merely supersedes the original or adds something new.”  
2. ***Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508 (2023)** – “The subjective intent of the user … does not determine the purpose of the use.”

In *Santos* the court applies these lenses by asking: *Does the JKL segment merely replace the original Cameo videos, or does it use them to make a distinct, critical comment?* The answer—based on what a reasonable viewer would perceive—is the latter, so the use is transformative.

### 5. Practical take‑aways for future cases  

* **Identify the audience’s expectations.** If the secondary work appears in a venue known for satire, that signals transformation.  
* **Focus on the overall impression, not on statements of intent.** Courts will look at titles, introductions, visual cues, and the surrounding program.  
* **If the plaintiff asserts the same purpose, that argument is “irrelevant”** unless it can be shown that the secondary work would lead a reasonable viewer to the same conclusion as the original—i.e., that the work is *not* a commentary but a straightforward reproduction.

---

**Bottom line:** In *Santos v. Kimmel* the Second Circuit applied the reasonable‑observer standard by examining the context, tone, and audience perception of Kimmel’s parody. Because a reasonable viewer would see the segment as a critique—not a duplicate of Santos’s original messages—the use was deemed transformative, satisfying the first fair‑use factor regardless of either party’s subjective intent.

### How does preemption analysis work for state‑law contract claims in a copyright case?
Question:
The court dismissed Santos’s breach‑of‑contract and breach‑of‑implied‑contract claims as preempted by the Copyright Act, but said it need not reach the merits because the claims were insufficient. What is the legal test for determining that a state‑law contract claim is preempted, and why did the court think Santos’s claims fell within that test?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Second Circuit applies the preemption analysis set out in 17 U.S.C. § 301 (a) and § 301 (b).  In a nutshell the test is:

1. **Subject‑matter preemption** – does the state‑law claim fall within the “general scope of copyright” (i.e., the exclusive rights that the Copyright Act grants)?  
2. **General preemption** – even if the claim is not within the statutory “exclusive rights” per se, is the state law imposing *a liability that is equivalent to* that created by the Act?  If so, the claim is pre‑empted.

The Second Circuit has repeatedly described the “equivalence” inquiry as asking whether the state cause of action “creates a cause of action that is essentially the same as a copyright infringement claim” (see *Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc.*, 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022); *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.*, 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010)).  If the plaintiff would have to prove the same elements that the Copyright Act already governs (ownership of a copy, copying of protected expression, etc.), the state claim is barred.  Conversely, a claim that is “qualitatively different” – for example, a negligence claim that does not turn on the exclusive rights – survives.

---

### How the court applied the test to Santos’s contract claims

**(a) What the contract suit sought**  
Santos alleged that Kimmel breached the Came Cameo Terms of Service (TOS) and that an implied contract existed between the parties.  The alleged breach required Kimmel to refrain from using the videos he had paid Santos to produce in a way that violated Santos’s copyright.  In other words, Santos’s state‑law claim was grounded in the same *right* the Copyright Act protects: the exclusive right to control reproductions and public performances of his videos.

**(b) Subject‑matter preemption**  
Because the breach‑of‑contract claim would enforce the same exclusive right—preventing an unauthorised “copy” of Santos’s videos from being publicly performed on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!*—the claim falls squarely within the subject matter that the Copyright Act pre‑empts.  The court therefore treated the contract claim as “within the general scope of copyright” and moved to the second prong of the analysis.

**(c) General preemption (equivalence)**  
Even assuming the TOS could be read as creating a contract, the relief Santos sought (an injunction or damages for the unauthorised use) would be identical to the remedy available under a copyright‑infringement action.  The plaintiff would have to show that Kimmel *copied* the protected work, used it without permission, and caused harm—exactly the elements the Act already governs.  Under the Second Circuit’s “equivalent liability” standard, that makes the state‑law claim pre‑empted.

**(d) Why the court did not need to decide the merits**  
The district court already concluded that the contract claims “failed to state a claim” under state law (e.g., Santos was not a party to the Cameo TOS, and he did not allege the essential terms of an implied contract).  Because the pleading already fell short of the pleading standard, the appellate court noted that it “need not reach the merits” of the preemption question.  Nevertheless, the court explained the preemption test to show that, even if Santos’s pleading were adequate, the claims would still have been barred.

---

### Take‑away for law students

- **Preemption is a two‑step inquiry:** first, does the state claim fall within the *subject matter* of copyright?  Second, does it impose *equivalent liability*?
- **A contract claim that enforces the copyright owner’s exclusive rights is typically pre‑empted** because it seeks the same remedy the Act provides.
- **Even if a claim is pre‑empted, a court may still dismiss it on pleading grounds**; the preemption analysis is only needed when the claim survives a Rule 12(b)(6) motion.

In *Santos v. Kimmel* the Second Circuit applied this framework and found that Santos’s breach‑of‑contract and breach‑of‑implied‑contract theories were essentially another way of asserting a copyright infringement claim, and therefore fell within the pre‑emptive sweep of the Copyright Act.  The court affirmed the district court’s dismissal, citing both the pre‑emption doctrine and the inadequacy of the pleadings.

### Why can a non‑signatory like Santos not enforce the Cameo Terms of Service against Kimmel?
Question:
Santos alleged that Kimmel breached the Cameo platform’s Terms of Service, yet the court said Santos was not a party to that contract and Illinois law presumes against third‑party benefits. How does the “strong presumption against conferring benefits to non‑contracting third parties” operate, and are there any circumstances where a third party could still enforce such terms?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Because Santos never agreed to the Came‑on Terms of Service, he cannot invoke them as a contract‑right holder. Illinois courts apply a “strong presumption against conferring benefits on non‑contracting third parties,” which means that a contract will be enforced only for a third party if the parties’ intent to create a third‑party‑beneficiary relationship is **express** or **so clear as to be equivalent to an express declaration**. The Second Circuit follows that rule in *Santos v. Kimmel* and finds no such intent in the Came‑on agreement, so Santos’s breach‑of‑contract claim fails.  

**How the presumption works (as applied in *Santos*)**

1. **Governing law** – The Terms of Service (ToS) designate Illinois law as controlling. Illinois case law (e.g., *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur*, 68 N.E.3d at 976) holds that, *absent a clear intent to benefit a non‑signatory, courts will not stretch a contract to cover that person*.  

2. **Express or “practically express” intent required** – The presumption is rebutted only if the contract contains language that “is so strong as to be practically an express declaration” that the third party may enforce the provision. The court emphasizes that *the burden is on the alleged third‑party beneficiary to point to that language*.

3. **Application to the Came‑on ToS** – The opinion notes that the ToS **does contain third‑party‑beneficiary clauses**, but they are limited to the parties the drafter explicitly named. Those clauses demonstrate that when the drafters *wanted* a third party to enforce a provision, they **did so expressly**. Because the ToS contains no comparable language extending any benefit to “the content creator” (i.e., Santos), the presumption remains intact and Santos cannot claim a contractual right.

4. **No implied‑in‑fact contract** – Even under New York law (which governs the alleged breach claim), a plaintiff must plead the essential terms of the contract and show a “meeting of the minds.” The court finds Santos’s complaint silent on those points, reinforcing that the presumption was not overcome.

**When a non‑signatory can still enforce a contract**

The *Santos* opinion, while focused on dismissing the claim, implicitly outlines the limited scenarios in which a third party may succeed:

| Situation | Why it defeats the presumption |
|-----------|--------------------------------|
| **Express third‑party‑beneficiary language** | If the contract says, e.g., “This agreement benefits *the creator of the video* and may be enforced by that creator,” the presumption is rebutted because the parties clearly intended to confer a benefit. |
| **“Practical” express declaration** | Even without the word “beneficiary,” language that unambiguously indicates the parties intended the contract to apply to a particular outsider (e.g., “Kimmel shall comply with all obligations set forth herein for the benefit of the video performer”) functions as a practical express statement. |
| **Statutory or regulatory schemes that incorporate third parties** | Some statutes (e.g., consumer‑protection laws) treat certain contracts as automatically benefiting a class of third parties. The presumption is displaced by the explicit statutory intent. |
| **Assignment or delegation** | If the contracting party expressly assigns its rights under the contract to the non‑signatory, the assignee can enforce those rights because the contract now includes the assignee as a party. |
| **Equitable doctrines (e.g., promissory estoppel) where reliance is shown** | While not a contract enforcement per se, a court may impose liability if the non‑signatory reasonably relied on a promise that the party making the promise intended to be binding. The *Santos* court did not reach this because Santos offered no such allegation. |

**Key take‑aways for a law student**

1. **Identify the governing law** – The presumption is a matter of state contract law; the opinion applies Illinois law because the ToS chose it.  
2. **Look for express third‑party language** – The mere existence of a contract does not create rights for outsiders; you must find a clear, unambiguous clause that names the outsider or otherwise makes the benefit “practically express.”  
3. **Assess pleading sufficiency** – Even if the contract could, in theory, benefit a third party, a plaintiff must still plead the essential terms and show a meeting of the minds; *Santos* failed on that front.  
4. **Remember the limited “exceptional” routes** – Assignments, statutory schemes, or equitable reliance may rescue a claim, but those are fact‑specific and not present here.

In sum, the “strong presumption against conferring benefits to non‑contracting third parties” operates as a default rule that keeps contracts *between* the signatories. Only an express, or effectively express, declaration by the parties can overcome it, and *Santos v. Kimmel* finds none in the Came‑on Terms of Service, so Santos has no enforceable contract claim against Kimmel.

### What are the pleading requirements for a fraud (fraudulent inducement) claim under New York law?
Question:
The opinion notes Santos’s fraud claim was dismissed because he failed to allege out‑of‑pocket loss. What specific elements must a plaintiff plead to survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion for fraudulent inducement in New York, and how does the out‑of‑pocket loss requirement differ from other fraud elements?
Answer:
**Answer**

Under New York law a claim for fraudulent inducement (sometimes called “fraudulent misrepresentation”) must allege, in non‑conclusory terms, each of the elements set out in *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, 88 N.Y.2d 413 (1996).  The Second Circuit’s opinion in **Santos v. Kimmel** restates those elements and explains why the plaintiff’s complaint was dismissed.

### The pleading elements

To survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion the complaint must plead the following **four** elements:

1. **A false representation of a material fact** – the plaintiff must allege that the defendant made a statement that was false and that the falsity was not merely a matter of opinion.

2. **Knowledge of its falsity (or reckless disregard for the truth)** – the plaintiff must show that the defendant knew the statement was false or acted with reckless indifference to its truth.

3. **Intent to induce the plaintiff to act** – the false statement must have been made with the purpose of persuading the plaintiff to enter into a transaction or take a particular action.

4. **Actual reliance and resulting **out‑of‑pocket loss*** – the plaintiff must allege that he relied on the false statement and that such reliance caused him a concrete, monetary loss.  New York courts require that the loss be “out‑of‑pocket” (i.e., a direct financial detriment), not merely a loss of a non‑pecuniary interest or a speculative future loss.  The district court, and the appellate court in *Santos*, relied on *Lama* and the more recent *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.*, 29 N.Y.3d 137 (2017) for this requirement.

The opinion phrases the last requirement as “the out‑of‑pocket loss as required under New York law” and holds that Santos “failed to allege that he suffered any actual out‑of‑pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud.”  Because the complaint did not include any factual allegations describing a dollar amount or the chain of causation from the misrepresentation to a monetary loss, the court concluded the pleading was insufficient.

### How the out‑of‑pocket loss requirement differs from the other elements

- **False representation, knowledge, and intent** are *qualitative* elements.  They describe the defendant’s conduct and mental state and can be pleaded with relatively general factual assertions (e.g., “Defendant told Plaintiff that X was true, knowing it was false, in order to induce Plaintiff to sign a contract”).

- **Out‑of‑pocket loss** is a *quantitative* element.  New York courts have consistently required the plaintiff to allege a **specific monetary injury** that is the direct result of reliance.  Mere speculation that the fraud “hurt the plaintiff’s reputation” or “caused a loss of opportunity” is insufficient.  The loss must be an actual spend or forfeit of money (or a clearly cognizable monetary equivalent) that the plaintiff can trace to the fraudulent inducement.

Thus, while the first three elements speak to *what* the defendant did and *why*, the fourth element demands a concrete statement of *how much* the plaintiff was harmed in dollars (or a comparable measure).  Without that allegation the complaint is facially deficient, as the *Santos* court affirmed.

### Practical take‑away for pleading

When drafting a fraud‑inducement complaint in New York:

1. **State the false statement and the factual basis for its falsity**, citing any documentary or testimonial evidence you plan to rely on.
2. **Explain that the defendant knew the statement was false or acted recklessly**.
3. **Show that the defendant’s purpose was to induce the plaintiff’s specific conduct** (e.g., signing a contract, making a payment).
4. **Identify the exact monetary loss** – e.g., “Plaintiff paid $25,000 to Defendant based on Defendant’s false representation that …, resulting in a loss of $25,000.”  If there are additional consequential losses, they must also be linked to the misrepresentation and expressed in monetary terms.

Only after these factual vows are made will a fraud‑inducement claim survive a 12(b)(6) motion in New York courts. The *Santos* opinion illustrates that failure to allege the out‑of‑pocket loss alone is fatal, even if the other three elements are alleged.

### Can the market‑harm factor of fair‑use be satisfied when the secondary work is a parody that may actually increase interest in the original?
Question:
The court said Santos did not suffer market harm because Kimmel’s segments were not a substitute for the original videos. In cases like Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose, courts sometimes find no market harm for parodies. How do courts assess the “effect on the potential market” when the secondary work could boost the original’s visibility or value?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Second Circuit in *Santos v. Kimmel* follows the Supreme Court’s approach in *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose* that the fourth fair‑use factor looks at **whether the secondary work usurps the market for the original by offering a competing substitute**, not whether it merely devalues the original or “spoils” its reputation.

The opinion states:

> “We ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it **usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute**.”  *Hachette Book Grp.*, 115 F.4th at 189; see *Campbell*, 510 U.S. at 592.  

Thus, a parody that might increase the original’s visibility is not a market‑harm problem so long as it does **not** provide a product that a typical buyer of the original would purchase instead of the original.

### How courts apply this in practice

1. **Identify the “original market.”**  
   In *Santos*, the original market was the Cameo videos that Santos created for pay‑per‑view requests. The defendants’ use was a televised sketch on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* that showed excerpts of those videos within a commentary segment.

2. **Ask whether the secondary work is a substitute.**  
   The court found that a television audience looking for a Cameo video “to watch the unedited, personalized performance” would not be satisfied by a five‑minute comedy sketch that merely excerpted the video for satirical commentary. There is no “substitutable product” in the market for “parody or criticism” of the original, just as *Campbell* observed that “creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons… [which] removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”

3. **Consider the possibility of a “secondary market” created by the parody.**  
   The opinion does not require a plaintiff to prove that the parody *reduces* the value of the original; it only requires proof that the parody *replaces* the original in its own market. Even if the parody generates publicity that could boost sales of the original, that is **irrelevant** to the fourth factor unless the parody actually *captures* that market. The court’s reasoning makes clear that increased visibility is not “market harm” within the meaning of § 107.

### Implication for your question

When a parody **might increase interest in the original work**, courts still ask the same substitution question:

*Is there a market for the original that the parody is now serving instead of the original?*  

If the answer is no—because the parody is a different medium, a different audience, or serves a commentary purpose rather than a commercial one—then the “effect on the potential market” factor is satisfied, even if the parody incidentally promotes the original.

In *Santos*, the court concluded that **no market harm existed** because the Kimmel segment was not a substitute for the Cameo videos. The same logic would apply to any parody that, although it may boost the original’s visibility, does not compete with the original for the same buyers or licensing opportunities.

### Does the fact that the videos were published on the same day they were created affect the fair‑use analysis?
Question:
The opinion mentions the copyright registrations showing the videos were published the same day they were created. How does the timing of publication influence the fair‑use factors, particularly the “nature of the copyrighted work” and the “amount and substantiality” factors?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The fact that the videos were “published” on the same day they were created does not, by itself, tip any of the four § 107 fair‑use factors against the defendant. In *Santos v. Kimmel* the Second Circuit noted the contemporaneous publication only to show that there was no pre‑existing market for a “pre‑published” work that the defendants could be said to supplant. Consequently, the timing is largely irrelevant to the “nature of the work” and “amount and substantiality” factors; those factors are evaluated on the content of the works, not on when the copyright holder first offered them to the public.

---

### How the court treated the timing

1. **Nature of the copyrighted work (Factor 2)**  
   The Second Circuit explained that this factor asks whether the work is “published or unpublished, factual or fictional, highly creative or purely informational” (citing *Harper & Row*). The court’s discussion of the registration records (¶ …“the videos were published on the same day they were created”) was presented merely as a factual background. It was not used to argue that the videos were “unpublished” or “raw” creations that would deserve stronger protection. Because the videos were already publicly available the moment they were made, the court treated them as *published* works, which ordinarily carries less weight against a fair‑use claim. The timing therefore reinforced the view that the works were not unpublished, but it did not add any additional prejudice to the defendants.

2. **Amount and substantiality (Factor 3)**  
   This factor looks at how much of the original work was taken and whether what was taken is the “heart” of the work. The court said that to decide the factor we need only compare “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue” (citing *Cariou*). The fact that the original videos were released the same day they were created does not change the analysis of how much was copied. The court’s focus was on whether the *JKL* segment reproduced a substantial portion of the video, not on when the original was first made available. The timing therefore has no bearing on the “amount” inquiry.

3. **Why the timing was mentioned**  
   The opinion (¶ …“the relevant copyright registrations establish that the videos were published on the same day they were created”) is cited to support the proposition that there was **no separate, pre‑existing market** that the defendants could have displaced. The Fourth factor (market effect) is satisfied when the secondary use does not serve as a market substitute for the original. Because Santos’ own videos were already publicly released at the moment of creation, there was no “potential market” for a later, competing version; the defendants’ use was a parody/commentary, not a market replacement. The timing thus shores up the fourth factor, not the second or third.

### Takeaway for fair‑use analysis

- **Timing of publication is only relevant to the market‑effect factor** when it shows that the original work had already been exploited (or not) and therefore there is no market that the secondary use could usurp.
- **It does not alter the inherent nature of the work** (creative vs. factual) nor **the quantitative/qualitative amount taken**; those remain grounded in the substantive content of the two works.
- In *Santos*, the court’s mention of “same‑day publication” was a factual shorthand to demonstrate that no separate licensing market existed, reinforcing the conclusion that the fourth factor weighed in favor of fair use. The second and third factors were assessed independently of that timing.

Hence, the same‑day publication fact does not affect the “nature of the copyrighted work” or “amount and substantiality” analyses; it simply underpins the finding that the defendants’ use did not harm a pre‑existing market.

### What is the significance of the court’s citation to Harper & Row’s “good‑faith” comment in modern fair‑use doctrine?
Question:
The opinion discusses that the Supreme Court’s statement that fair use presumes good faith has been “distanced from” in later cases. How have courts treated the good‑faith requirement in recent fair‑use jurisprudence, and does bad‑faith conduct ever defeat a fair‑use defense?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Second Circuit in *Santos v. Kimmel*, 2025 WL 5551234 (2d Cir. Oct. 6, 2025), makes clear that the Supreme Court’s off‑hand remark in *Harper & Row* that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing” is no longer a controlling element of the fair‑use analysis.  

### How the court treats the “good‑faith” comment today  

1. **Citation as historical background, not a required factor** – Judge Lohier acknowledges that *Harper & Row* “stated … that fair use presupposes good faith,” but then notes that “the Court has since distanced itself from this statement.” The opinion does not treat good‑faith as a separate prong of the § 107 inquiry; instead it relies on the ordinary four‑factor test.  

2. **Focus on the statutory factors** – The court’s discussion of the first factor (“purpose and character of the use”) centres on *transformativity* and the perspective of a “reasonable observer,” citing *Andy Warhol Found.* and *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose*. Nothing in the analysis ties the outcome to the parties’ subjective good‑faith or lack thereof.  

3. **No “bad‑faith” bar to fair use** – Santos argued that Kimmel’s “false representations” showed bad faith and should defeat the defense. The court expressly rejects that contention:  

   > “We disagree. … It is true that the Supreme Court stated … ‘fair use presupposes good faith…’ But the Court has since distanced itself from this statement.”  

   The opinion then proceeds to affirm the fair‑use finding on the basis of the four statutory factors, not because Kimmel acted in good faith.  

### Does bad‑faith conduct ever defeat a fair‑use defense?

*Santos* indicates that, at least in the Second Circuit, **bad‑faith conduct alone is insufficient to defeat a fair‑use defense**. The court treats *Harper & Row*’s good‑faith remark as dicta that has been “distanced from” by later precedent, and it requires no showing of good‑faith to satisfy any of the four § 107 factors.  

Other recent Second Circuit cases echo this approach:

* ***Authors Guild v. Google* (2015)** – the court held that Google’s digitization was fair use despite the fact that the publisher may have objected in bad‑faith, focusing instead on transformative purpose and market effect.  
* ***Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive* (2024)** – the court again emphasized the four‑factor analysis and did not treat the plaintiff’s alleged “bad‑faith” claim of “systematic piracy” as a separate defeat of fair use.

Thus, modern fair‑use jurisprudence treats *Harper & Row*’s good‑faith language as an historical footnote. A party’s bad‑faith motives may be considered **only insofar as they affect the four statutory factors** (e.g., by showing a commercial motive that weighs against transformation or market harm), but they do not create an independent “good‑faith” requirement that, if violated, automatically bars the defense.
