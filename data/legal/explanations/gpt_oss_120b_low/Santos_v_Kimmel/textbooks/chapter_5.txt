Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 5: # Significance and Takeaways

The Second Circuit’s decision in **Santos v. Kimmel** is a concise illustration of how appellate courts apply the fair‑use doctrine and the preemptive reach of the Copyright Act.  While the case turned on the dismissal of both the federal copyright claims and the state‑law contract and fraud allegations, the opinion furnishes several clear lessons for practitioners who draft and litigate copyright‑related suits.  The following discussion distills those lessons and explains how they fit within the court’s reasoning.

## Parody and Commentary Are Presumptively Fair Use

The court’s analysis reaffirms a well‑established principle: a work that comments on, criticizes, or parodies the original is “presumptively” a fair use.  The Fourth factor—market effect—is often the most contested, but the First factor—purpose and character—carries great weight when the secondary work is transformative.  In **Santos**, the Second Circuit emphasized that the relevant inquiry is the perception of a “reasonable observer,” not the subjective intent of either the copyright holder or the secondary user.  The court noted that a reasonable viewer would see Kimmel’s televised segments as a satirical critique of Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money, not as an extension of Santos’s original message of hope and encouragement.

Because the secondary work adds a new purpose (public ridicule) and does not merely supersede the original videos, the court treated the transformation as strong.  This mirrors earlier decisions such as **Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music**, **Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts v. Goldsmith**, and **Cariou v. Prince**, which all identified commentary or parody as classic fair‑use contexts.  Practitioners should therefore recognize that when a defendant’s use is directed at commenting on the original, the fair‑use defense enjoys a presumption that must be overcome by the plaintiff—an evidentiary burden that is difficult to meet at the pleading stage.

## Bad‑Faith Motives Do Not By‑Default Defeat Fair Use

Santos argued that Kimmel’s “false representations” and alleged bad faith should bar the fair‑use defense.  The court rejected this theory, pointing to the Supreme Court’s evolution from **Harper & Row**—which suggested that fair use “presupposes good faith”—to a more nuanced modern view that separates the fairness doctrine from the user’s motive.  In later cases, including **Swatch Group Management Services v. Bloomberg**, the Second Circuit has treated bad‑faith allegations as one factor among many, not a decisive shortcut to defeat a fair‑use claim.

The takeaway is clear: while a court may consider the user’s motive as part of the “purpose and character” analysis, a plaintiff cannot rely solely on evidence of bad faith to overcome the presumption of fairness in a parody or commentary context.  Evidence of bad faith must be paired with a showing that the secondary work fails the other fair‑use factors—particularly that it is not transformative or that it appropriates the market for the original.

## Digital Platform Contracts Do Not Bind Non‑Signatories

Santos’s state‑law breach‑of‑contract claim hinged on the argument that Kimmel’s violation of the **Cameo** Terms of Service (ToS) should benefit Santos, even though Santos never agreed to those terms.  The court applied the well‑settled “presumption against third‑party benefits” in Illinois contract law, requiring an explicit declaration that the contract is intended to create enforceable rights for non‑contracting parties.  The absence of such language in the Cameo ToS led the court to dismiss the claim.

This analysis underscores a practical point for litigants: when alleging a breach of a digital platform’s user agreement, the plaintiff must either be a party to the agreement or demonstrate that the agreement expressly creates third‑party beneficiary rights.  Merely pointing to a platform’s policies is insufficient.  Attorneys should scrutinize the ToS for beneficiary language and, if absent, consider alternative theories (e.g., tortious interference) rather than attempting to stretch contract claims.

## Federal Copyright Preempts State Contract Claims

Even if Santos had successfully pleaded the essential terms of an implied contract, the court indicated that the breach‑of‑contract and breach‑of‑implied‑contract claims would be preempted by the Copyright Act.  The Supreme Court’s preemption doctrine, articulated in **Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios**, holds that when a state law claim “attempts to regulate the same conduct” that Congress has occupied—namely, the exclusive rights of copyright holders—federal law displaces the state claim.

In **Santos**, the court found that the alleged contractual obligations derived from the ToS were essentially a regulation of copying and public performance—activities already governed by the Copyright Act.  Consequently, the state claims could not survive.  Litigants must therefore evaluate whether their state‑law theories are genuinely independent of the copyright exclusive rights.  If the claim seeks to enforce a contractual prohibition on copying, display, or distribution, preemption is likely.

## Strategic Pleading Considerations

The decision highlights two procedural lessons for practitioners drafting complaints that involve both federal copyright and state‑law causes of action:

1. **Plead Fair‑Use Defenses Early** – Although defendants typically raise fair use as an affirmative defense, plaintiffs can preemptively address the fair‑use factors in the complaint to show that the use is not protected.  In **Santos**, the plaintiff’s reliance on ambiguous language about his own purpose left the court to conclude that the transformative nature of the secondary work was evident.  A well‑pleaded complaint should detail why each fair‑use factor weighs against the defense, especially the market‑harm analysis, which can be the most contested factor.

2. **Exacting Contractual Pleading Requirements** – When asserting breach of a digital platform’s ToS, the plaintiff must allege (a) that they are a party to the agreement or that the agreement expressly creates a third‑party beneficiary right, and (b) the essential terms of the contract, including the specific provision allegedly breached.  The court in **Santos** dismissed the claim for failure to allege these essentials.  Plaintiffs should therefore attach the ToS as an exhibit, quote the precise clause, and explain how the defendant’s conduct violates that clause and benefits the plaintiff.

Finally, the court’s reliance on the *pleading* standard—Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal when a complaint “fails to state a claim upon which relief can be granted”—demonstrates that appellate courts will not re‑evaluate the merits of the underlying factual disputes at this stage.  Plaintiffs must ensure that their allegations plausibly allege each element of the federal and state claims, and that they anticipate the fair‑use presumption that leans heavily in favor of parody or commentary uses.

---

**In sum**, **Santos v. Kimmel** reinforces the Second Circuit’s strong protection for parody and commentary, clarifies that bad‑faith motives are not a shortcut to defeat fair use, limits the reach of digital platform contracts to non‑signatories, and confirms that state contract claims that regulate copying are preempted by federal copyright law.  For litigants, the decision serves as a practical roadmap: craft pleadings that anticipate the fair‑use presumption, ground any contract theories in explicit contractual language, and be mindful of preemption when coupling federal copyright claims with state‑law causes of action.
