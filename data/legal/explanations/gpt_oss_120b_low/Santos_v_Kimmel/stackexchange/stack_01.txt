Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why did the Second Circuit deem the fair‑use analysis sufficient to dismiss the copyright claim at the pleading stage?
Question:
In Santos v. Kimmel the appellate court affirmed the district court’s Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal, stating that the fair‑use defense was “clearly established by the complaint.” I thought fair‑use determinations required factual development and a full evidentiary hearing. How can a court decide that a use is fair‑use purely on the pleadings, and what precedent allows such a dismissal?
Answer:
**Answer**

A Rule 12(b)(6) motion attacks the *legal* sufficiency of the complaint, not the *truth* of the alleged facts.  In a copyright case the plaintiff must allege enough facts to show that **fair use is not “clearly established”** as a defense.  If the pleadings, together with any incorporated documents, make it evident that a reasonable court would find the four § 107 factors weigh in favor of fair use, the complaint fails to state a claim and can be dismissed.

The Second Circuit follows that approach in several recent cases, and it does so again in **Santos v. Kimmel**, 2025 WL … (2d Cir. Oct. 6 2025).  The court’s reasoning rests on three points that are worth spelling out.

---

### 1. The pleading standard for fair‑use defenses

Under **DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.**, 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010), a court evaluating a 12(b)(6) motion “considers the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference.”  The court may also take judicial notice of public copyright registrations.  The same standard applies to affirmative defenses such as fair use.

The Second Circuit has held that **fair use can be “clearly established” at the pleading stage** when the allegations, taken as true, demonstrate that the use is transformative, that the original work is unpublished or not commercially valuable, that only a small portion was taken, and that there is no market harm.  See **TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum**, 839 F.3d 168, 178‑79 (2d Cir. 2016) (fair‑use defense “so clearly established … as to support dismissal … at the pleading stage”).

Thus, a court is not required to wait for discovery to “develop the facts” of a fair‑use inquiry; it may decide that the plaintiff has already pleaded enough to defeat the claim.

---

### 2. How the *Santos* court applied that standard

The Second Circuit examined the **four statutory factors** as pleaded:

| Factor | What Santos alleged | Court’s finding |
|--------|--------------------|-----------------|
| **Purpose & character** | Kimmel aired the videos in a satirical segment titled “Will Santos Say It?” designed to mock Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. | The court concluded the use is *transformative* because a reasonable observer would see the segment as commentary, not as a simple repost of Santos’s original messages.  The plaintiff’s own allegation that the videos originally conveyed “feelings of hope …” supports the transformation analysis. |
| **Nature of the work** | The videos were personal messages posted to Cameo and published the same day they were created. | Personal, non‑creative works weigh in favor of fair use. |
| **Amount & substantiality** | Kimmel used the entire video (the only version that existed). | The court noted that when the entire work is used in a *parodic* or *critical* context, the amount taken is permissible, citing **Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music**, 510 U.S. 569 (1994). |
| **Effect on market** | No evidence that Kimmel’s broadcast usurped any market for Santos’s videos; the plaintiff could not license a “critical review” market. | The court found no market harm, mirroring the reasoning in **Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive**, 115 F.4th 163, 189 (2d Cir. 2024). |

Because each factor, taken together, **clearly favoured fair use**, the court held that the defense was “clearly established by the complaint.”  Accordingly, the complaint failed to state a claim for infringement and was dismissed under Rule 12(b)(6).

The opinion also emphasizes that “the relevant copyright registrations … establish that the videos were published on the same day they were created” (Appendix 160–69), allowing the court to rely on the registration record without further fact‑finding.

---

### 3. Precedent that permits dismissal at the pleading stage

Beyond **DiFolco** and **TCA Television**, the Second Circuit has repeatedly allowed fair‑use defenses to defeat a claim on a Rule 12(b)(6) basis:

* **Cariou v. Prince**, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) – the court can decide fair use by looking only at the “original version … and the [second] work at issue.”
* **Hachette Book Group v. Internet Archive**, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) – fair use is a “flexible concept” that may be “so clearly established … as to support dismissal” early in the case.
* **Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith**, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) – the “purpose and character” factor focuses on the *reasonable observer* test, not the parties’ subjective intent, making it a fact that can be pleaded.

These cases collectively articulate the doctrine that, when the complaint itself demonstrates that the four § 107 factors tilt strongly toward fair use, a court may treat the defense as “clearly established” and dismiss the infringement claim without waiting for discovery.

---

### Bottom line

The Second Circuit does **not** require a full evidentiary hearing to apply the fair‑use test at the pleading stage.  If the plaintiff’s own allegations, together with any incorporated documents (e.g., copyright registrations), make it apparent that the four statutory factors favor the defendant, the fair‑use defense is “clearly established” and the complaint fails to state a claim.  **Santos v. Kimmel** follows this line of precedent, finding that the alleged mock‑critical use of the videos was transformative, non‑commercial, and absent market harm, and therefore the fair‑use defense was dispositive at the pleading level.
