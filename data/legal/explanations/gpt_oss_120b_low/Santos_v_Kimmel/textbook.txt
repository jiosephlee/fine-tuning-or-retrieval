Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Procedural History and Parties  

## Facts: Santos’s Personalized Video Requests and Kimmel’s Parody Segments  

The dispute in *Santos v. Kimmel* originates on the online marketplace Cameo, a platform that permits users to commission short, personalized video messages from celebrities. In early 2024, former Congressman George Santos placed a series of requests on Cameo for “personalized motivational videos.” Each request specified that the video should convey “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.” Santos complied with every request, producing a distinct video for each order and delivering the finished files to the requesting users through the platform’s standard delivery mechanism.  

During the same period, Jimmy Kimmel, host of *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* (JKL), approached the Cameo platform under a series of fictitious user names. Kimmel’s requests were identical in wording to Santos’s, but Kimmel’s intent, as later alleged by Santos, was to obtain the videos for a satirical segment on JKL titled “Will Santos Say It?”  In each segment, Kimmel edited the raw Cameo video to insert sarcastic commentary, intercut the footage with comedic graphics, and aired the resulting clip on the broadcast show. The broadcast material presented the videos as evidence of Santos’s alleged willingness to say “absurd things for money,” thereby transforming Santos’s original motivational content into political satire.  

The factual matrix therefore comprises two distinct layers of activity: (1) the creation and delivery of personalized videos by Santos in response to bona‑fide Cameo orders, and (2) the subsequent appropriation, editing, and public broadcast of those videos by Kimmel and his employer, ABC, for a comedic purpose. Understanding this dichotomy is essential because the court’s analysis of copyright infringement turns on whether the broadcast use is “transformative” of the original works and whether it supersedes the market for the videos Santos created.  

## The Complaint: Copyright Infringement and State‑Law Claims  

On June 15, 2024, Santos filed a complaint in the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York. The pleading articulated four primary causes of action:  

1. **Copyright infringement** – Santos asserted that Kimmel, ABC, and Disney reproduced, prepared derivative works of, and publicly displayed his copyrighted videos without a license, thereby violating 17 U.S.C. §§ 106 and 106A.  

2. **Breach of contract** – Santos alleged that Kimmel violated the Cameo Terms of Service, which he characterized as a contract governing the permissible uses of the videos.  

3. **Breach of implied contract** – In addition to the express Terms of Service, Santos claimed an implied agreement existed whereby Kimmel would not exploit the videos for commercial broadcast without his consent.  

4. **Fraudulent inducement** – Santos contended that Kimmel misrepresented his purpose in requesting the videos, thereby inducing Santos to create them under false pretenses.  

The complaint was accompanied by copies of the videos, screenshots of the Cameo request forms, and an exhibit of the broadcast segments. Santos framed his state‑law claims as “qualitatively different” from a copyright infringement action, arguing that they rested on contract theory and tort principles rather than exclusive rights under federal law.  

## District Court Ruling and Dismissal Under Rule 12(b)(6)  

Judge Cote, presiding in the Southern District, granted Kimmel’s motion to dismiss pursuant to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6). The court’s order, issued on September 2, 2024, held that Santos “fails to state a claim upon which relief can be granted” for two reasons:  

* **Copyright claims barred by fair use.** After a brief fair‑use analysis, the court concluded that the broadcast segments were “highly transformative,” serving a purpose of commentary and parody. The court applied the four statutory fair‑use factors, finding in favor of the defendants on each. Accordingly, the copyright claims could not survive a pleading‑stage motion.  

* **State‑law claims preempted or insufficient.** The court applied the doctrine of federal preemption, reasoning that Santos’s breach‑of‑contract and breach‑of‑implied‑contract theories were “inextricably intertwined” with the exclusive rights granted by the Copyright Act, and thus could not proceed. Regarding fraudulent inducement, the court found that Santos’s complaint omitted any allegation of actual monetary loss, a required element under New York law.  

Because the complaint was deemed legally insufficient on all counts, the district court entered a final judgment dismissing the action without prejudice.  

## Appeal to the Second Circuit: Issues Presented  

Dissatisfied with the dismissal, Santos filed a notice of appeal on September 12, 2024, and the case was docketed as No. 24‑2196‑cv before the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit. The appeal presented two discrete issues for appellate review:  

1. **Whether the district court erred in applying the fair‑use doctrine at the pleading stage, thereby improperly barring the copyright claims.** Santos argued that the alleged transformation was insufficient and that the district court’s reliance on “subjective intent” misapplied the *Warhol* standard.  

2. **Whether the district court correctly concluded that the state‑law claims were preempted or failed to state a claim.** Santos contended that the contract and fraud theories were separate from copyright infringement and that the court’s preemption analysis was misplaced. He also argued that the pleading was adequate to allege the requisite out‑of‑pocket loss for fraudulent inducement.  

Judge Lohier, writing for a three‑judge panel, framed the appeal as a “Rule 12(b)(6) case”—the court would review the complaint de novo, examining the factual allegations, attached exhibits, and incorporated documents to determine whether the plaintiff’s pleadings meet the threshold of “fair notice” of a plausible claim.  

## Relevant Jurisdictional and Choice‑of‑Law Considerations  

The procedural posture of *Santos v. Kimmel* raises several jurisdictional and choice‑of‑law issues that are germane to the appellate analysis:  

* **Federal jurisdiction.** The plaintiff’s copyright claims invoke the exclusive federal question jurisdiction of the district court under 28 U.S.C. § 1331. The state‑law claims, although grounded in New York and Illinois law, are properly joined under supplemental jurisdiction because they arise from the same nucleus of operative facts as the copyright claim (28 U.S.C. § 1367).  

* **Choice of law for contract claims.** The complaint identifies the Cameo Terms of Service as the governing agreement, which the platform designates as being governed by Illinois law. Consequently, the district court applied Illinois principles, such as the “presumption against third‑party benefits” (see *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp.*). The fraudulent inducement claim, however, is pleaded under New York law, and the court applied the New York requirement that a plaintiff allege actual out‑of‑pocket loss.  

* **Preemption analysis.** The Supremacy Clause (U.S. Const. Art. VI, cl. 2) mandates that any state law that “stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress” is preempted. The district court’s preemption determination hinged on whether the state‑law claims “attempted to regulate the same rights” that the Copyright Act exclusively reserves to Congress.  

* **Standard of review on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion.** The Second Circuit follows *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.*, 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010), reviewing the complaint “de novo” but giving the plaintiff the benefit of any reasonable inferences that can be drawn from the pleadings and accompanying exhibits. The appellate court may also take judicial notice of publicly available copyright registrations, as noted in *Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp.*, 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005).  

These jurisdictional and choice‑of‑law scaffolds frame the Second Circuit’s analysis. By understanding which substantive law governs each claim and the standards applied at the pleading stage, a law student can follow how the appellate court arrives at its ultimate affirmation of the district court’s dismissal.

# Copyright Infringement Claim and the Fair‑Use Analysis  

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit affirmed the district court’s dismissal of George Santos’s copyright‑infringement claims on the ground that the defendants’ use of the videos he created for the Came Cameo platform was protected by the fair‑use defense.  The opinion walks the reader through each of the four statutory factors of 17 U.S.C. § 107, shows how the court applied them, and explains why a fair‑use defense can be decisive even at the pleading stage.  The discussion below breaks down the court’s analysis and the legal principles that underlie it, keeping the focus on the facts and reasoning in *Santos*.

## The Fair‑Use Defense under 17 U.S.C. § 107  

Section 107 codifies a flexible, equitable defense that permits “otherwise infringing” uses when they serve socially valuable purposes such as criticism, commentary, news reporting, teaching, or parody.  The statute enumerates four non‑exclusive factors that a court must balance:

1. **Purpose and character of the use** (including whether the use is transformative).  
2. **Nature of the copyrighted work**.  
3. **Amount and substantiality of the portion used**.  
4. **Effect of the use on the market for the original work**.  

The Second Circuit repeatedly stresses that the factors are not a checklist; they are considered holistically, and the outcome can hinge on the “totality of the circumstances” (see *Hachette Book Group, Inc. v. Internet Archive*, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024)).  Moreover, the court may determine that fair use “is so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright‑infringement claim” at the pleading stage (see *TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum*, 839 F.3d 168, 178 (2d Cir. 2016)).  This de novo standard means the appellate court reviews the district court’s 12(b)(6) dismissal without deference to the lower court’s factual findings, focusing instead on the pleadings, attached exhibits, and any incorporated documents.

## Purpose and Character of the Use – Transformation, Parody, and Commentary  

The first factor asks whether the second work merely supersedes the original or adds something new—i.e., whether it is **transformative**.  Transformation is assessed by asking what a reasonable observer would perceive, not by looking at the subjective intent of either party (see *Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023)).  

In *Santos*, the defendants’ videos were aired on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* as part of a recurring segment titled “Will Santos Say It?” The segment repeatedly showed Santos fulfilling absurd requests (e.g., shouting a nonsensical phrase for money) and then mocked him on‑air.  The district court—and ultimately the circuit—found that the purpose of the defendants’ use was **commentary and criticism** of Santos’s willingness to say “absurd things for money.”  The court rejected Santos’s argument that the same purpose—promoting “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity”—was also his original intent.  The Second Circuit emphasized that the transformation inquiry looks to the **reasonable observer**: a viewer of the *JKL* segment would see a satirical, critical take on Santos rather than a straightforward promotional message.

Parody is a subset of commentary that often satisfies the transformation requirement because it invokes the original work for the purpose of ridicule.  The court cited *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music, Inc.*, 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994), noting that a parody’s commentary on the original “removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”  Although the *Santos* videos were not a parody of a specific copyrighted work (they were original recordings made for Cameo), the *JKL* segments functioned as a **parodic commentary** on the content of those recordings.  Consequently, the first factor “strongly supports a finding of fair use.”

The court also addressed Santos’s allegation that Kimmel’s “false representations” demonstrated bad‑faith intent, which Santos claimed should defeat the fair‑use defense.  While *Harper & Row* once suggested that fair use “presupposes good faith,” later Second Circuit precedent has moved away from treating good‑faith as a decisive element (see *Swatch Group Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P.*, 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014)).  The court concluded that the alleged bad faith did not undermine the transformative, critical nature of the use.

## Nature of the Copyrighted Work  

The second factor considers whether the work is **published** or **unpublished**, and whether it is highly creative versus factual.  Works that are unpublished or highly creative receive stronger protection.  In *Santos*, the videos were created expressly for the Cameo platform and were **published** on the same day they were recorded, as shown by the registration exhibits (Appendix 160‑169).  Because the works were already in the public domain (in the sense that they were disseminated to customers), the factor weighed in favor of fair use.  Moreover, the videos were largely **creative**—they contained Santos’s performance—but the fact of prior publication reduces the weight of this factor, consistent with the Second Circuit’s approach in *TCA Television* (the court stressed that publication “lessens” the protection afforded by this factor).

## Amount and Substantiality of the Portion Used  

The third factor asks how much of the original work was taken and whether the portion used was the “heart” of the work.  The pleadings in *Santos* identified that the *JKL* segments incorporated the **entire** Cameo video into each broadcast.  At first glance, copying the whole work might weigh against fair use.  However, the court noted that in parody and commentary cases, taking the whole work can be justified when it is necessary to “convey its expressive qualities” (see *Campbell*, 510 U.S. at 592).  

Because the *JKL* segments sought to mock the very content of the videos, the complete duplication was deemed **necessary** to achieve the critical purpose.  The Second Circuit therefore found that the amount and substantiality factor did **not** outweigh the transformation found in the first factor, echoing the reasoning in *Cariou v. Prince*, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013), which held that the “necessity” of copying for a critical purpose can tip this factor in favor of fair use.

## Effect on the Market – Market Substitution Test and Parody Exception  

The fourth factor focuses on whether the secondary use usurps the market for the original work or harms its potential value.  The court distinguished two sub‑analyses:

1. **Direct market substitution** – does the secondary work serve as a competing product?  
2. **Potential licensing market** – would the copyright holder have been willing to license the secondary use for commentary or parody?

In *Santos*, the *JKL* broadcast did not offer a **substitute** for the Cameo videos; rather, it presented a satirical critique that likely **decreased** the market value of the original videos for a prospective buyer.  The court echoed *Campbell*’s observation that “creators of imaginative works will not license critical reviews or lampoons,” thereby signaling that there is **no viable licensing market** for the defendants’ use.  Accordingly, any alleged market harm was minimal and, if anything, the use could be viewed as a *market‑creating* activity for the defendants’ own show.

The court also pointed out that the plaintiff had **no evidence** of actual market damage or of a market for a non‑parodic, non‑critical version of the videos.  The absence of a demonstrable market for a “competing substitute” meant the fourth factor “does not bar” the fair‑use defense.  This aligns with the Second Circuit’s approach in *TCA Television* and *Cariou*, where the court found that speculative market harm is insufficient at the pleading stage.

## Reliance on Precedent and De Novo Review Standards  

*Sant*os* relied heavily on a line of Second Circuit cases that have shaped fair‑use analysis:

* **Warhol** – stressed that the *objective* view of a reasonable observer controls the transformation inquiry.  
* **Campbell** – articulated the parody exception and the “no licensing market” rationale.  
* **Cariou** – clarified that a court need only compare the *original* works with the *secondary* works, not conduct a full factual inquiry, at the pleading stage.  
* **TCA Television** – confirmed that fair use can be “clearly established” by the complaint, justifying a 12(b)(6) dismissal.  

Because the appellate court reviews a 12(b)(6) motion **de novo**, it looks at the pleadings, attached evidence, and any incorporated documents, applying the law afresh.  The Second Circuit’s standard requires that the plaintiff’s complaint contain **sufficient factual allegations** to make the claim plausible; if the asserted fair‑use factors, when taken together, point unmistakably toward a protected use, the claim may be dismissed without further fact‑finding.  In *Santos*, the court concluded that the plaintiff’s own allegations—particularly the commentary motive and the lack of market substitution—already established that fair use was “clearly” applicable.

## Why Fair Use Can Defeat a Claim at the Pleading Stage  

A plaintiff’s 12(b)(6) motion challenges the **legal sufficiency** of the complaint, not the merits of the case.  The fair‑use defense is a **complete defense**: if it is shown to apply, the plaintiff cannot recover even if infringement occurred.  The Second Circuit recognizes that the fair‑use inquiry is **often dispositive** on the pleadings because the plaintiff must allege facts that overcome the defense’s presumptive strengths.  When the plaintiff’s allegations, read in the light most favorable to the defendant, satisfy the statutory factors in the defendant’s favor, the court may conclude that the complaint “fails to state a claim” (see *TCA Television*, 839 F.3d at 178).

In *Santos*, the combination of (1) a transformative, critical purpose, (2) a published work, (3) a necessary full‑copy for parody, and (4) an absence of market substitution, collectively painted a picture that a **reasonable court** would view as classic fair use.  The court therefore held that even assuming the facts alleged were true, the fair‑use defense would likely defeat the copyright claim.  Under the *TCA* standard, that suffices to grant summary dismissal.

---  

By dissecting each statutory factor as applied in *Santos v. Kimmel*, this chapter illustrates how the Second Circuit balances the interests of copyright owners against the public’s interest in free expression, and why a strong fair‑use defense can extinguish a copyright claim before any trial‑level fact‑finding occurs.

# State‑Law Claims: Contractual and Fraud Grounds  

In *Santos v. Kimmel* the Second Circuit affirmed the district court’s dismissal of all of former Congressman George Santos’s state‑law claims.  Although the appeal was framed as a challenge to the district court’s rulings, the appellate court’s analysis provides a clear illustration of three doctrinal areas that law students must master when evaluating state‑law causes of action that arise in the context of copyrighted works: (1) the preemption doctrine, (2) the third‑party‑beneficiary and implied‑in‑fact contract analyses, and (3) the pleading standards required to survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion on a fraudulent‑inducement claim.  This chapter breaks down the court’s reasoning, explains the legal standards it applied, and shows how the cited precedent fits into each step of the analysis.  

---

## 1. Preemption of State Contract Claims by the Copyright Act  

### 1.1 The Preemption Rule  

Section 301 of the Copyright Act provides that *“all rights… that are equivalent to any of the exclusive rights within the general scope of copyright”* are preempted when they are covered by federal law.  The Supreme Court, in *Petrofina Corp. v. United States*, explained that preemption is triggered when a state claim “covers the same subject matter as a copyright claim and is “equivalently” regulated by the Act.”  The Second Circuit follows this approach, looking first to whether the state claim is “a claim that could be litigated in a federal copyright action.”  When the answer is yes, the state claim is barred, even if the plaintiff frames the dispute in contract terms.

### 1.2 Application to Santos’s Claims  

Santos asserted two contract theories arising from the Cameo platform’s Terms of Service (TOS):  

1. **Breach of contract** – alleging that Jimmy Kimmel, ABC, and Disney violated provisions of the TOS that govern how a creator may use the videos he supplied.  
2. **Breach of implied contract** – contending that, although not a signatory, he was entitled to contractual protections because the parties had “an intention” to treat him as a beneficiary of the TOS.  

The district court dismissed both claims on the ground of preemption.  The Second Circuit declined to revisit that ground because, even assuming the claims were not preempted, the pleadings failed to state a viable claim (see below).  Nonetheless, the court noted that the *California*‑style “contract‑based” claim would, in effect, be a claim that *“the defendants used the plaintiff’s copyrighted videos without permission”*—the very subject of a copyright infringement action.  Because the Copyright Act already governs the exclusive rights to reproduce, distribute, and publicly display the videos, the state‑law contract claims were “functional equivalents” of a copyright claim and therefore preempted.  

The court’s brief discussion of preemption reflects the well‑established principle that a claimant cannot sidestep the exclusive federal scheme by couching the dispute in contract language.  See *Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc.*, 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (contract claim “wholly subsumed” by copyright).  In *Santos*, the appellate panel treated the preemption analysis as a threshold issue that, even if decided otherwise, would not rescue the state claims because of additional procedural deficiencies.

---

## 2. Third‑Party‑Beneficiary Analysis Under Illinois Law  

### 2.1 The General Presumption  

Illinois courts apply a strong presumption against the creation of enforceable rights for non‑signatories of a contract.  The presumption can be overcome only when the contract contains “express language” or “clear, unequivocal intent” that the parties intended to confer benefits on a third party.  *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur*, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016).  The presumption reflects the policy that contracts should bind only those who have bargained for the obligations.

### 2.2 How the Circuit Applied the Presumption  

Santos’s breach‑of‑contract claim rested on the Cameo TOS, which Kimmel agreed to when he created his “Will Santos Say It?” videos.  Santos argued that, because he was the creator of the underlying videos, the TOS’s provisions that protect “creators” should also protect him, even though he never signed the agreement.  

The Second Circuit rejected that inference.  First, the opinion emphasized that the TOS contains explicit third‑party‑beneficiary language for certain provisions, illustrating that the parties **did** use clear language when they intended to create enforceable rights for outsiders.  The absence of comparable language for the provisions Santos relied upon means the parties **did not** intend to extend those benefits to him.  Second, the court pointed to *Barry* and noted that a pleading must allege “an express declaration” that the contract applies to the non‑signatory.  Santos’s complaint merely alleged a vague “relationship” to the TOS, without any factual foundation that the parties intended to create enforceable rights for him.  

Because the presumption was not rebutted, the claim could not proceed under Illinois law.  The court therefore affirmed the district court’s dismissal of the breach‑of‑contract claim on the basis that Santos lacked standing to enforce the TOS as a third‑party beneficiary.

---

## 3. Implied‑in‑Fact Contracts Under New York Law  

### 3.1 Elements of an Implied‑In‑Fact Contract  

New York recognizes implied‑in‑fact contracts when the parties’ conduct demonstrates a “meeting of the minds” and the essential terms of the agreement can be inferred.  The standard requires:  

1. **Mutual assent** (a meeting of the minds).  
2. **Essential terms** that are sufficiently definite (e.g., price, subject matter, performance obligations).  
3. **A basis for liability**—the plaintiff must show that the defendant’s conduct was “reasonable to infer” the existence of a contract.  

*Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear‑Brown Grp., Inc.*, 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) sets out this framework.  Moreover, pleading rules demand that a complaint **allege the essential terms in non‑conclusory language**; speculative or conclusory statements are insufficient.  *Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc.*, 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994) emphasizes the need for detailed factual allegations supporting each element of the contract claim.  

### 3.2 The Court’s Reasoning on Santos’s Implied‑Contract Claim  

Santos argued that, although he never signed the Cameo TOS, the parties’ interaction—Kimmel requesting a video, Santos delivering it, and Kimmel subsequently broadcasting it—created an implied contract obligating Kimmel to compensate Santos or to refrain from using the video beyond the original purpose.  

The Second Circuit found the pleading deficient on three fronts:  

1. **Lack of a meeting of the minds.**  The complaint did not allege any specific communications indicating that Kimmel promised to respect Santos’s rights beyond the initial transaction.  The court noted that an implied contract cannot be inferred from a simple purchase‑like exchange; there must be an indication that the parties contemplated additional obligations.  

2. **Failure to state essential terms.**  Santos’s complaint merely alleged that “defendants breached the contract” without identifying the price, the scope of the license, duration, or any other term that would define the parties’ obligations.  As *Caniglia* teaches, “the complaint must set out the essential terms of the alleged contract in non‑conclusory language”—something the Santos pleading omitted.  

3. **Absence of factual support for liability.**  The pleading did not explain why a reasonable observer would infer that Kimmel intended to be bound by an implied agreement to limit the use of the videos.  The Second Circuit stressed that “the implication of a contract must be grounded in the parties’ conduct,” and the conduct at issue (Kimmel’s request and use of the videos) was insufficient to establish such an implication.  

Because the complaint failed to satisfy the pleading requirements for an implied‑in‑fact contract, the district court’s dismissal was affirmed.  The appellate court stressed that even if some factual basis existed, Santos would still need to amend the complaint to plausibly allege the essential terms and a clear meeting of the minds, which he did not provide.

---

## 4. Fraudulent Inducement – New York Elements and the Out‑of‑Pocket Loss Requirement  

### 4.1 Elements of Fraudulent Inducement in New York  

Under New York law, a plaintiff asserting fraudulent inducement must plead, with particularity, the following elements:  

1. **A false representation of a material fact** (or omission of a material fact).  
2. **Knowledge of its falsity** or reckless disregard for the truth.  
3. **Intent to induce reliance** by the plaintiff.  
4. **Justifiable reliance** by the plaintiff on the misrepresentation.  
5. **Actual damages**—specifically, **out‑of‑pocket loss** directly caused by the reliance.  

*Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996).  The damages element is not a speculative “harm to reputation” or “emotional distress”; the plaintiff must demonstrate a concrete monetary loss that resulted from the fraud.  *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.*, 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142‑43 (2017) clarified that a plaintiff’s claim fails if it does not allege any actual out‑of‑pocket loss.

### 4.2 How the Court Evaluated Santos’s Allegations  

Santos claimed that Kimmel, through “false representations” about the purpose of the videos, fraudulently induced him to create content that he would not otherwise have produced.  The district court dismissed the claim for lack of a pleading on out‑of‑pocket loss; the Second Circuit affirmed.  

The appellate panel pointed out that Santos’s complaint did **not** allege any specific monetary loss:

* He did not assert that he incurred expenses (e.g., production costs) that he would not have otherwise incurred.  
* He failed to show that the broadcast of the videos caused a loss of future revenue, licensing opportunities, or any other identifiable financial detriment.  

Because the complaint omitted a concrete description of damages, it did not satisfy the “out‑of‑pocket loss” requirement.  The court therefore applied the *Lama* and *Connaughton* standards and concluded that the fraudulent‑inducement claim could not survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion.  

---

## 5. Synthesis: Why Each State Claim Failed  

Putting the four analyses together, the Second Circuit’s reasoning can be summarized as follows:

| Claim | Key Legal Standard | How the Standard Was Applied |
|------|--------------------|------------------------------|
| **Breach of contract (Cameo TOS)** | Illinois third‑party‑beneficiary presumption; requires express language extending benefits to non‑signatory. | No express declaration; TOS expressly limited benefits to signatories; presumption remained intact. |
| **Breach of implied contract** | New York implied‑in‑fact contract: meeting of the minds, essential terms, factual basis; pleading must allege terms in non‑conclusive language. | No alleged meeting of the minds; no essential terms (price, scope, duration) pleaded; lack of factual support. |
| **Fraudulent inducement** | New York fraud: false statement, knowledge, intent, reliance, **out‑of‑pocket loss**. | No allegation of concrete monetary loss; damages element absent. |
| **Preemption** (underlying bar to both contract claims) | Copyright Act § 301 preempts state claims that are “equivalently” regulated by federal law. | Claims essentially sought to prohibit an unauthorized copy/derivative use, which is precisely the subject of copyright law. |

Because each claim either ran afoul of a substantive doctrinal bar (preemption) or failed to meet the pleading standards required for a viable state‑law cause of action, the district court’s dismissal was upheld.

## 7. Take‑aways for Students  

1. **Preemption is a substantive bar**: Even if a plaintiff can articulate a state‑law claim, the court will first ask whether the Copyright Act occupies the field.  When the claim is “functionally equivalent” to a copyright infringement action, it is preempted.  
2. **Third‑party‑beneficiary rights are narrow**: Under Illinois law, the default rule is that non‑signatories cannot enforce a contract absent clear, express language.  Plaintiffs must point to that language; vague references to “benefit” are insufficient.  
3. **Implied contracts demand precise pleading**: A New York implied‑in‑fact contract claim must identify the parties’ meeting of the minds and the essential terms—price, scope, duration, etc.—in a non‑conclusory manner.  Assertions of “an agreement” without factual detail will be struck.  
4. **Fraud claims require concrete loss**: New York courts will not allow a fraudulent‑inducement claim to proceed if the plaintiff cannot plausibly allege an out‑of‑pocket monetary loss caused by reliance.  Mere reputational or intangible harms do not satisfy the damages element.  
5. **Rule 12(b)(6) is a high hurdle at the pleading stage**: The court’s de novo review of the complaint focuses on whether the allegations, taken as true, state a claim that is plausible on its face.  If any of the doctrinal elements—preemption, third‑party status, essential terms, or loss—are missing, the claim is dismissed without reaching the merits.  

By dissecting *Santos v. Kimmel* through these lenses, law students can see how appellate courts blend substantive doctrine with procedural pleading rules to prune untenable state‑law claims that arise alongside federal copyright disputes.

# Legal Reasoning Framework: From Pleadings to Disposition  

The Second Circuit’s opinion in **Santos v. Kimmel** illustrates the disciplined, step‑by‑step analysis a federal appellate court uses when it reviews a district court’s Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal.  The opinion walks the reader through the standards of review, the sources of factual material, the application of the “reasonable observer” test to the fair‑use analysis, the balancing of the four statutory factors at the pleading stage, the treatment of preemption and third‑party‑beneficiary defenses, and finally the disposition of the case.  Understanding each of these steps helps a law student see how appellate courts move from the bare allegations in a complaint to a final affirmation (or reversal) of a dismissal.

## Standard of Review for Rule 12(b)(6) Motions – De Novo, Sufficiency of the Complaint  

A Rule 12(b)(6) motion challenges the legal sufficiency of a complaint.  The appellate court reviews the district court’s decision **de novo**—that is, it considers the matter anew, without deference to the trial court’s conclusions.  The Supreme Court articulated the modern standard in *Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly* and *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*: the complaint must contain “plausible” factual allegations that, when taken as true, entitle the plaintiff to relief.  The Second Circuit follows this guidance, as noted in *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.*, 622 F.3d at 110, emphasizing that the court must look at “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached … and documents incorporated by reference.”  The appellate judge therefore asks two questions:

1. **Did the plaintiff plead facts sufficient to state a claim?**  
2. **If the pleadings are taken as true, does the law support a claim?**  

If the answer to either question is no, the complaint may be dismissed outright, even before discovery.

## Use of the Complaint, Exhibits, and Incorporated Documents as the Factual Foundation  

Because the court cannot conduct discovery at the pleading stage, it must rely on the plaintiff’s own allegations and any accompanying documents.  In *Santos*, the panel expressly adopted the “complaint‑centric” approach: the factual record is limited to the narrative in the pleading, the exhibits (the videos posted on Cameo, the copyright registrations), and any documents the plaintiff incorporated by reference (e.g., the Cameo Terms of Service).  The court may also take **judicial notice** of publicly available registrations in the Copyright Office’s record, as the Second Circuit has done in *Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp.*, 413 F.3d at 261.  No extrinsic evidence is permitted; the analysis must be confined to what the plaintiff has already put forward.

## Application of the “Reasonable Observer” Test to the Purpose‑and‑Character Factor  

The first fair‑use factor asks whether the secondary use is “transformative.”  The Second Circuit has clarified that transformation is measured by the perspective of a **reasonable observer**, not by the subjective intent of either the copyright holder or the secondary user.  This principle appears in *Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. at 544.  In *Santos*, the court rejected the plaintiff’s argument that Kimmel’s parody “served the same purpose” as the original videos because the plaintiff himself had intended the videos to demonstrate “hope… and perseverance.”  The court held that a reasonable viewer of the broadcast segment would perceive it primarily as **satirical commentary** on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money—different from the inspirational tone of the original works.  Consequently, the purpose‑and‑character factor “strongly supports a finding of fair use.”

### Why Subjective Intent Is Irrelevant  

Even though Kimmel allegedly acted in “bad faith” by misrepresenting his identity on Cameo, the Supreme Court’s *Harper & Row* language that “fair use presupposes good faith” has been narrowed in subsequent cases.  The Second Circuit noted that later decisions, such as *Swatch Group Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P.*, treat good‑faith considerations as peripheral when the core “transformative” inquiry is satisfied.  Thus, the court focused on the **objective effect** of the use on the audience, not on Kimmel’s internal motivations.

## Balancing the Four Fair‑Use Factors at the Pleading Stage  

Although the statutory fair‑use analysis is traditionally a “four‑factor” test, the Second Circuit recognizes that at the pleading stage the court may decide the issue based solely on the plaintiff’s allegations.  The opinion cites *TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum*, which permits dismissal when the fair‑use defense is “clearly established” by the complaint.  The panel applied each factor as follows:

1. **Purpose and Character** – Transformative parody; strong support for fair use.  
2. **Nature of the Copyrighted Work** – The original videos are creative, which ordinarily leans against fair use, but this factor is weighted less when the secondary use is highly transformative.  
3. **Amount and Substantiality** – Kimmel used the entire video, but the court noted that parody may appropriate the whole work if necessary to convey its critique, following *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music, Inc.*.  
4. **Effect on the Market** – The complaint showed no evidence that the parody served as a market substitute for the original inspirational videos.  The panel emphasized that potential licensing markets for parody are “nonexistent,” a point reinforced by *Campbell* and *Authors Guild v. Google*.

Because the first factor alone tipped the balance heavily in favor of fair use, and the remaining three did not counteract that effect, the court concluded that the fair‑use defense was “clearly established” and therefore the copyright claims could be dismissed on a Rule 12(b)(6) basis.

## Assessment of Preemption and Third‑Party‑Beneficiary Defenses as Matters of Law  

The plaintiff also asserted state‑law claims for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement.  The Second Circuit treated the preemption analysis as **purely legal**: if a state claim is “virtually identical” to a copyright claim, the Copyright Act preempts it under 17 U.S.C. § 301.  However, the court declared that it need not resolve the preemption question because the plaintiff’s contract claims **failed to state a claim** under state law.

### Third‑Party‑Beneficiary Analysis  

Santos relied on the Cameo Terms of Service, arguing that the defendants breached obligations owed to him as a third‑party beneficiary.  The court applied two jurisdiction‑specific principles:

* **Illinois law** (governing the Terms of Service) creates a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to non‑contracting third parties,” requiring an “express declaration” that the contract was intended to benefit the plaintiff.  ( *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp.* ).  
* **New York law** (governing the implied‑contract claim) demands that the plaintiff allege the essential terms of the contract and show a “meeting of the minds.”  The complaint’s allegations were conclusory and omitted the requisite specificity ( *Caniglia*; *DG & A Mgmt. Servs.* ).

Because the plaintiff did not satisfy either jurisdiction’s threshold for establishing a third‑party beneficiary relationship, the court dismissed the contract claims as a matter of law, irrespective of any preemption analysis.

## Disposition: Affirmation of Dismissal and Implications for Future Litigation  

Having found that (1) the copyright claims were barred by the fair‑use defense at the pleading stage, and (2) the state‑law claims either were preempted or failed to state a claim, the Second Circuit affirmed the district court’s **final judgment of dismissal**.  The affirmation is a **summary judgment** on the merits of the claims, not merely a procedural dismissal; the appellate court’s analysis resolves the substantive issues.

### Practical Implications  

1. **Pleading Adequacy** – Plaintiffs must articulate why a use is *not* transformative and must identify a concrete market harm.  Vague assertions that “the purpose was the same” are insufficient.  
2. **Document Reliance** – At the pleading stage, a plaintiff may rely only on the complaint and attached documents.  Incorporating external facts requires either a pleading allegation or a properly attached exhibit.  
3. **Preemption Defense** – Even if a plaintiff frames a claim in state‑law terminology, the court will strip away any “virtually identical” elements to the copyright claim and may preempt the claim outright.  
4. **Third‑Party Beneficiary Claims** – To succeed, a plaintiff must point to an express third‑party beneficiary clause or, under New York law, allege the essential contract terms and a clear meeting of the minds.  General assertions that a contract “applies” to non‑signatories will not survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion.  

In sum, **Santos v. Kimmel** offers a clear template: the appellate court begins with a de novo review of the pleading’s sufficiency, constructs the factual record from the complaint and its attachments, applies the reasonable‑observer standard to the fair‑use analysis, balances the statutory factors without venturing beyond the pleadings, treats preemption and third‑party‑beneficiary issues as pure questions of law, and then either affirms or reverses the district court’s dismissal.  Mastery of this logical framework equips a law student to navigate Rule 12(b)(6) practice and to anticipate the evidentiary and legal hurdles that lie ahead in copyright and contract litigation.

# Significance and Takeaways

The Second Circuit’s decision in **Santos v. Kimmel** is a concise illustration of how appellate courts apply the fair‑use doctrine and the preemptive reach of the Copyright Act.  While the case turned on the dismissal of both the federal copyright claims and the state‑law contract and fraud allegations, the opinion furnishes several clear lessons for practitioners who draft and litigate copyright‑related suits.  The following discussion distills those lessons and explains how they fit within the court’s reasoning.

## Parody and Commentary Are Presumptively Fair Use

The court’s analysis reaffirms a well‑established principle: a work that comments on, criticizes, or parodies the original is “presumptively” a fair use.  The Fourth factor—market effect—is often the most contested, but the First factor—purpose and character—carries great weight when the secondary work is transformative.  In **Santos**, the Second Circuit emphasized that the relevant inquiry is the perception of a “reasonable observer,” not the subjective intent of either the copyright holder or the secondary user.  The court noted that a reasonable viewer would see Kimmel’s televised segments as a satirical critique of Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money, not as an extension of Santos’s original message of hope and encouragement.

Because the secondary work adds a new purpose (public ridicule) and does not merely supersede the original videos, the court treated the transformation as strong.  This mirrors earlier decisions such as **Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music**, **Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts v. Goldsmith**, and **Cariou v. Prince**, which all identified commentary or parody as classic fair‑use contexts.  Practitioners should therefore recognize that when a defendant’s use is directed at commenting on the original, the fair‑use defense enjoys a presumption that must be overcome by the plaintiff—an evidentiary burden that is difficult to meet at the pleading stage.

## Bad‑Faith Motives Do Not By‑Default Defeat Fair Use

Santos argued that Kimmel’s “false representations” and alleged bad faith should bar the fair‑use defense.  The court rejected this theory, pointing to the Supreme Court’s evolution from **Harper & Row**—which suggested that fair use “presupposes good faith”—to a more nuanced modern view that separates the fairness doctrine from the user’s motive.  In later cases, including **Swatch Group Management Services v. Bloomberg**, the Second Circuit has treated bad‑faith allegations as one factor among many, not a decisive shortcut to defeat a fair‑use claim.

The takeaway is clear: while a court may consider the user’s motive as part of the “purpose and character” analysis, a plaintiff cannot rely solely on evidence of bad faith to overcome the presumption of fairness in a parody or commentary context.  Evidence of bad faith must be paired with a showing that the secondary work fails the other fair‑use factors—particularly that it is not transformative or that it appropriates the market for the original.

## Digital Platform Contracts Do Not Bind Non‑Signatories

Santos’s state‑law breach‑of‑contract claim hinged on the argument that Kimmel’s violation of the **Cameo** Terms of Service (ToS) should benefit Santos, even though Santos never agreed to those terms.  The court applied the well‑settled “presumption against third‑party benefits” in Illinois contract law, requiring an explicit declaration that the contract is intended to create enforceable rights for non‑contracting parties.  The absence of such language in the Cameo ToS led the court to dismiss the claim.

This analysis underscores a practical point for litigants: when alleging a breach of a digital platform’s user agreement, the plaintiff must either be a party to the agreement or demonstrate that the agreement expressly creates third‑party beneficiary rights.  Merely pointing to a platform’s policies is insufficient.  Attorneys should scrutinize the ToS for beneficiary language and, if absent, consider alternative theories (e.g., tortious interference) rather than attempting to stretch contract claims.

## Federal Copyright Preempts State Contract Claims

Even if Santos had successfully pleaded the essential terms of an implied contract, the court indicated that the breach‑of‑contract and breach‑of‑implied‑contract claims would be preempted by the Copyright Act.  The Supreme Court’s preemption doctrine, articulated in **Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios**, holds that when a state law claim “attempts to regulate the same conduct” that Congress has occupied—namely, the exclusive rights of copyright holders—federal law displaces the state claim.

In **Santos**, the court found that the alleged contractual obligations derived from the ToS were essentially a regulation of copying and public performance—activities already governed by the Copyright Act.  Consequently, the state claims could not survive.  Litigants must therefore evaluate whether their state‑law theories are genuinely independent of the copyright exclusive rights.  If the claim seeks to enforce a contractual prohibition on copying, display, or distribution, preemption is likely.

## Strategic Pleading Considerations

The decision highlights two procedural lessons for practitioners drafting complaints that involve both federal copyright and state‑law causes of action:

1. **Plead Fair‑Use Defenses Early** – Although defendants typically raise fair use as an affirmative defense, plaintiffs can preemptively address the fair‑use factors in the complaint to show that the use is not protected.  In **Santos**, the plaintiff’s reliance on ambiguous language about his own purpose left the court to conclude that the transformative nature of the secondary work was evident.  A well‑pleaded complaint should detail why each fair‑use factor weighs against the defense, especially the market‑harm analysis, which can be the most contested factor.

2. **Exacting Contractual Pleading Requirements** – When asserting breach of a digital platform’s ToS, the plaintiff must allege (a) that they are a party to the agreement or that the agreement expressly creates a third‑party beneficiary right, and (b) the essential terms of the contract, including the specific provision allegedly breached.  The court in **Santos** dismissed the claim for failure to allege these essentials.  Plaintiffs should therefore attach the ToS as an exhibit, quote the precise clause, and explain how the defendant’s conduct violates that clause and benefits the plaintiff.

Finally, the court’s reliance on the *pleading* standard—Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal when a complaint “fails to state a claim upon which relief can be granted”—demonstrates that appellate courts will not re‑evaluate the merits of the underlying factual disputes at this stage.  Plaintiffs must ensure that their allegations plausibly allege each element of the federal and state claims, and that they anticipate the fair‑use presumption that leans heavily in favor of parody or commentary uses.

---

**In sum**, **Santos v. Kimmel** reinforces the Second Circuit’s strong protection for parody and commentary, clarifies that bad‑faith motives are not a shortcut to defeat fair use, limits the reach of digital platform contracts to non‑signatories, and confirms that state contract claims that regulate copying are preempted by federal copyright law.  For litigants, the decision serves as a practical roadmap: craft pleadings that anticipate the fair‑use presumption, ground any contract theories in explicit contractual language, and be mindful of preemption when coupling federal copyright claims with state‑law causes of action.
