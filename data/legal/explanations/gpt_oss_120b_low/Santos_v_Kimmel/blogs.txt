# Parody, Commentary, and Fair Use: Why *Santos v. Kimmel* Reinforces the Transformative Standard

*By [Your Name], Law & Technology Analyst*  

---

## Why the Case Matters

When a late‑night host commandeers a fan‑made video and repurposes it for a comedy sketch, the dispute seems frivolous—until it lands in federal court. Former Congressman George Santos’s suit against Jimmy Kimmel and ABC raised a more consequential question: **How far must a secondary work be “transformative” to qualify for the fair‑use defense when the work in question is a parody or commentary?** The Second Circuit’s decision, issued on October 6, 2025, squarely addresses that question, confirming a trajectory that began with *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose Music* (1994) and was sharpened by the Supreme Court’s recent rulings in *Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith* (2023). For creators who remix, parody, or critique existing media on broadcast or streaming platforms, *Santos* offers a clear, if unsurprising, endorsement of a **transformative‑purpose‑over‑subjective‑intent** approach.

---

## The Court’s Reasoning in a Nutshell

### The Four‑Factor Fair‑Use Test

Judge Lohier’s opinion follows the classic § 107 analysis, but the crux of the decision hinges on the **first factor—purpose and character of the use**. The court emphasizes that the “reasonable observer” test, not the copyright holder’s internal motivation, determines whether a secondary use adds something new.

* **Purpose and Character** – The Second Circuit held that Kimmel’s “Will Santos Say It?” segment was a commentary on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. Although the underlying videos themselves were upbeat messages of “hope, strength, perseverance,” the broadcast context reframed them as public criticism. The court rejected Santos’s argument that because he *intended* the videos to be flattering, the secondary use could not be transformative. The Supreme Court’s language in *Goldsmith*—that transformation is measured by the “new expression, meaning, or message” for a “reasonable observer”—was applied verbatim.

* **Nature of the Work** – The videos are copyrighted expressive works, but the court noted that they were published the same day they were created, a factor that carries little weight for parody cases where the original work is already in the public domain of discourse.

* **Amount and Substantiality** – Kimmel aired the entire video, but the court considered the entirety permissible because parody often requires the “heart” of the original to conjure the target’s identity. This mirrors the Second Circuit’s earlier decision in *Cariou* and aligns with *Campbell*, where the Supreme Court held that even wholesale copying can be fair if the purpose is commentary.

* **Market Effect** – The opinion underscores that no “substitutable market” exists for a comedic critique of a political figure’s willingness to say outrageous things. The market‑harm analysis therefore favored fair use, consistent with *Campbell*’s observation that “the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons … removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”

### Rejecting the Bad‑Faith Argument

Santos tried to leverage Harper & Row’s “good‑faith” language, alleging that Kimmel’s false representations taint the fair‑use defense. The court noted that later cases, including *Swatch Group* and *Authors Guild v. Google*, have moved away from treating good‑faith as a prerequisite. The decision makes clear that **subjective bad faith does not defeat fair use** if the objective transformation analysis is satisfied.

### State‑Law Claims: A Side Note

While the opinion also dismisses Santos’s breach‑of‑contract and fraudulent‑inducement claims as either pre‑empted or insufficiently pleaded, the fair‑use analysis alone is the headline. The state‑law discussion reiterates a familiar theme: **copyright preemption** can sweep away contractual theories that merely replicate the exclusive rights granted by the Act.

---

## How *Santos* Fits Into Recent Jurisprudence

### From *Campbell* to *Goldsmith* to *Santos*

*Campbell* laid the groundwork for a flexible transformation test, especially in parody. The Supreme Court emphasized that a parodist need not “hide in the shadows” but may openly copy to “illuminate the target.” The Second Circuit’s *Santos* opinion extends that logic to broadcast television, confirming that the medium does not diminish the transformation inquiry.

Two years later, *Goldsmith* clarified that the subjective intent of the secondary user is irrelevant; the court must ask what a **reasonable observer** perceives. *Santos* applies that lens flawlessly, dismissing Santos’s contention that his own purpose of spreading positivity should narrow the analysis. In doing so, the Second Circuit aligns itself with the Supreme Court’s modern, audience‑centric approach.

### The “All‑or‑Nothing” Misconception

Critics sometimes argue that the fair‑use doctrine forces a binary choice: either a work is wholly transformative or not. *Santos* demonstrates that the courts can recognize transformation even when the secondary work **incorporates the entirety** of the original, as long as the new context adds a distinct, critical message. This nuance is crucial for content creators who rely on **full‑clip excerpts** to make a point—think news segments, documentary interludes, or late‑night sketches.

---

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

### A Safe Harbor for Broadcast Parody

The decision reinforces a de‑facto “broadcast safe harbor” for parodic commentary. Networks can now point to *Santos* when defending segments that recycle fan‑generated content, provided the segment’s purpose is clearly critical or satirical. This could embolden shows like *Saturday Night Live*, *The Daily Show*, or even YouTube creators to push the boundaries of remix without fearing pre‑emptive copyright suits.

### Potential Limits

Although *Santos* affirms the transformative standard, it does **not** expand fair use to cover non‑critical, purely commercial repurposing. The court’s analysis remains tethered to the **“new purpose or different character”** threshold. Future plaintiffs will still need to demonstrate that the secondary work’s “message” diverges from the original’s. Moreover, the decision does not resolve the murkier question of **whether a plaintiff can assert a state‑law contract claim when the alleged infringer is merely a third‑party user of the platform**—the court sidestepped that issue by focusing on pleading deficiencies.

### Ripple Effects for Platform Terms of Service

Santos’s dismissal of contract claims also signals that platforms’ terms of service will **not automatically generate third‑party beneficiary rights** for non‑users. Plaintiffs seeking to hold a platform accountable for third‑party misuse must craft a more direct contractual relationship or rely on statutory avenues.

---

## Bottom Line

*United States v. Santos* may have originated as a publicity stunt turned litigious quibble, but the Second Circuit’s opinion crystallizes a pivotal principle: **fair use hinges on how a reasonable observer perceives the secondary work’s purpose, not on the original creator’s subjective intent**. By reaffirming the transformative standard articulated in *Campbell* and *Goldsmith*, the court provides a robust shield for parody and commentary—especially in the high‑visibility world of broadcast television.

For creators, lawyers, and media companies alike, the ruling is a reminder that the **public‑interest lens**—the “new message” test—remains the decisive factor. As long as the repurposed content is deployed to critique, comment, or satirize, courts will likely view it through the same fair‑use prism that has protected American cultural criticism for decades.

# Preemption Perplexities: When Do State Contract Claims Run Afoul of the Copyright Act?

## Why Santos v. Kimmel Matters

The Second Circuit’s decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* may look at first glance like a routine affirmation of a fair‑use defense, but the opinion contains a more consequential discussion about the reach of the Copyright Act’s preemption doctrine over state‑law contract claims. Former Congressman George Santos tried to leverage the Cameo platform’s terms of service to sue Jimmy Kimmel, ABC, and Disney for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement after Kimmel turned a series of paid, personalized videos into a televised parody. The appellate court affirmed the district court’s dismissal, holding that the contract claims were preempted—or, where preemption did not attach, that the pleadings were insufficient.

For litigants who hope to protect their copyrighted works through state‑law doctrines—particularly contract theories—*Santos* is a cautionary tale. It underscores how tightly the Second Circuit (and, by extension, many other circuits) reads the “exclusive rights” language of § 106 and the preemption provision of § 301, often barring any parallel state‑law claim that “relates to” the same rights. At the same time, the decision illustrates the court’s willingness to dispose of state‑law claims on pleading grounds when the plaintiff cannot demonstrate a contractual relationship or an actual loss. Understanding the nuanced line drawn in *Santos* is essential for any practitioner considering a state‑law route to protect—or enforce—a copyrighted work.

## The Court’s Preemption Analysis in a Nutshell

### The Statutory Framework

Section 301 of the Copyright Act provides that “all rights … that are equivalent to any of the exclusive rights within the general scope of copyright” are preempted when a work is covered by federal copyright. The Supreme Court has long warned that this preemption is “broad,” designed to preserve the uniformity of copyright law and to discourage a patchwork of state regimes that could undermine the federal scheme.

### The Second Circuit’s Approach

The Second Circuit has consistently applied a two‑step test for preemption, rooted in *Miller v. Universal City Studios* and refined in *Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio* (2022). First, the court asks whether the state law claim “asserts a right that is within the bundle of exclusive rights protected by the Copyright Act.” If the answer is yes, the second inquiry asks whether the claim “necessitates a proof of copying” or “relates to a restrictive covenant that is exclusively within the statutory scheme.” Only if the claim survives both prongs can it proceed.

In *Santos*, the panel framed the breach‑of‑contract and breach‑of‑implied‑contract claims as attempts to enforce the Cameo Terms of Service—an agreement between the platform (and, by extension, its users) and the purchaser of a personalized video. Santos argued that Kimmel’s use of the videos on “Jimmy Kimmel Live!” breached those terms, which allegedly prohibited commercial exploitation without the creator’s consent.

The court, however, declined to reach a full preemption analysis on the merits. Citing *Melendez*, the panel noted that the alleged contractual violations “relate directly to the exclusive rights to reproduce, distribute, and publicly display the videos,” which are squarely within the copyright monopoly. Consequently, the claims were deemed preempted, and the district court’s dismissal was affirmed.

### Why the Court Skipped the Full Test

The opinion emphasizes that even if a claim might survive preemption, it must still satisfy state‑law pleading standards. Santos’s complaint failed to:

1. **Identify himself as a contracting party** to the Cameo Terms of Service. The court highlighted the “strong presumption against conferring benefits to non‑contracting third parties” under Illinois law, noting the absence of any express third‑party beneficiary language in the agreement.
2. **Allege the essential terms of an implied contract** with the defendants. Under New York law, the plaintiff must plead the existence of a meeting of the minds and the specific contractual provisions that were breached. Santos’s allegations were conclusory and lacked the requisite detail.
3. **Show an out‑of‑pocket loss** for the fraudulent inducement claim, a requirement under New York precedent.

Because these deficiencies were fatal on a Rule 12(b)(6) basis, the court never needed to grapple with the more doctrinal question of whether the state‑law claims truly “relate to” the exclusive rights.

## Analytical Perspective: Is the Preemption Bar Too Rigid?

The *Santos* decision reflects a trend toward a broadly protective view of federal preemption, but it also raises concerns about the ability of creators to enforce contractual rights that sit alongside, but are not identical to, copyright. A few points merit closer scrutiny:

1. **Distinguishing Contractual Obligations from Copyright Infringement** – The court treated any contract claim that “touches” the exclusive rights as preempted, even though contract law can impose obligations that go beyond simple copying (e.g., promises not to exploit a work in a particular context). In *Melendez*, the Second Circuit allowed a contract claim to survive where the contract governed a “non‑exclusive license” that did not intrude on the statutory rights. *Santos* could have examined whether the Cameo terms imposed a usage restriction that was *contractual* rather than *copyright* in nature, potentially preserving the claim.

2. **The “Related to” Test’s Vagueness** – The phrase “relates to” is deliberately elastic, but it also makes outcomes unpredictable. Some circuits (e.g., the Ninth) have been more willing to carve out space for state law claims that revolve around breach of a licensing agreement, especially where the agreement addresses *how* a work is used rather than *whether* it can be used. A more granular analysis—distinguishing between “right to copy” and “right to exploit under a specific licensing regime”—might yield a different result.

3. **Policy Considerations** – The Court’s emphasis on preemption preserves the uniformity of copyright law, but it can also incentivize plaintiffs to “wrap” their expectations into a copyright claim rather than a contract claim, potentially crowding the federal docket and limiting the nuanced remedies that contract law can provide (e.g., specific performance, injunctive relief tailored to contractual breaches).

Overall, while the *Santos* court was correct that the complaint fell short of pleading a viable contract claim, its quick move to preemption may signal an increasingly expansive reading of § 301 that could foreclose legitimate state‑law avenues.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

### 1. Heightened Pleading Standards for Contract Claims

Litigants will need to ensure that any state‑law claim is tightly framed around *contractual* obligations that are *independent* of the exclusive rights. This means:

- Explicitly pleading the existence of a contractual relationship, including the parties, consideration, and essential terms.
- Demonstrating that the alleged breach concerns a duty *outside* the scope of the exclusive rights (e.g., a promise not to license the work to a competitor, a non‑competition clause, or a moral‑rights limitation that states recognize separately).

### 2. Strategic Use of “License” Language

Future plaintiffs may consider drafting platform terms that incorporate *express third‑party beneficiary* language, thereby sidestepping the presumption against third‑party enforcement. By expressly stating that the creator is a third‑party beneficiary of the user’s agreement not to use the work beyond the platform’s intended purpose, a plaintiff could forge a clearer contractual nexus.

### 3. Potential for Circuit Splits

The Second Circuit’s approach, if followed by other appellate courts, could solidify a nationwide bar on contract claims that involve any element of copying or distribution. However, other circuits—especially the Ninth and Eleventh—have demonstrated more willingness to allow contract claims to proceed where the contractual provision is *ancillary* to the exclusive rights. A split may soon emerge, inviting the Supreme Court to clarify the preemption boundary.

### 4. Policy Debate Over “Contractual” vs. “Copyright” Remedies

Scholars and policymakers may revisit the balance between a uniform federal scheme and the flexibility of state contract law. Some argue that a narrow preemption doctrine encourages parties to negotiate detailed licensing agreements, while others warn that it unduly limits the ability of creators to enforce promises that fall outside the narrow copyright monopoly.

## Practical Takeaways for Practitioners

1. **Scrutinize Platform Terms** – Before filing a state‑law claim based on a platform’s terms of service, confirm that the plaintiff is a party to the agreement or that the terms expressly create a third‑party beneficiary right. Without this, the claim will be dismissed on the same footing as Santos’s breach‑of‑contract suit.

2. **Separate Copyright and Contract Theories** – Draft pleadings that clearly differentiate a *copyright* claim (e.g., infringement, fair use) from a *contract* claim (e.g., breach of a licensing agreement). If the contract claim can stand on its own—without invoking the exclusive rights—it stands a better chance of surviving preemption.

3. **Anticipate the Fair‑Use Defense** – Even if the contract claim survives, the defendant will likely raise a fair‑use defense (as Kimmel did). Plaintiffs should be prepared to argue that the “purpose and character” factor is *not* transformative, perhaps by emphasizing the creator’s intent and the commercial context of the original work.

4. **Document Actual Harm** – For fraudulent inducement and related tort claims, ensure that the complaint alleges a concrete out‑of‑pocket loss. Courts are unlikely to infer damages where the plaintiff merely alleges reputational harm or “lost opportunities” without a quantifiable monetary figure.

## Concluding Thoughts

*Santos v. Kimmel* serves as a timely reminder that the Copyright Act’s preemption doctrine can be a formidable barrier to state‑law contract claims, especially when those claims intersect with the exclusive rights of reproduction, distribution, or public performance. While the Second Circuit’s decision may appear procedural—essentially a 12(b)(6) dismissal—it solidifies a doctrinal posture that could limit the ability of creators to rely on contract law to police the post‑creation uses of their works.

For plaintiffs, the lesson is clear: if you want to enforce a contractual promise concerning a copyrighted work, you must *prove* a contractual relationship that imposes obligations *outside* the scope of copyright, and you must plead those obligations with the same level of specificity required for any contract claim. Otherwise, the twin swords of preemption and pleading standards will cut your case down before it can even reach a substantive hearing.

The tension between federal uniformity and state‑law flexibility is unlikely to dissolve soon. As more creators turn to platforms like Cameo, TikTok, and OnlyFans, where user‑generated content is routinely repurposed, courts will increasingly be asked to draw the line. Whether that line will be sharpened by the Supreme Court or remain a patchwork of circuit opinions remains an open—and fascinating—question for copyright scholars and litigators alike.



## Third‑Party Beneficiary Doctrine Meets Platform Terms of Service: Lessons from *Santos v. Kimmel*

**Why this case matters**  
When a famous figure contracts with a platform, we often assume that the platform’s user agreement protects not just the parties who signed it but also anyone who might be “caught in the middle” of that relationship. The Second Circuit’s decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* shatters that comforting assumption. By holding that former congressman George Santos—who never signed Cameo’s Terms of Service—cannot enforce those terms against Jimmy Kimmel (and ABC/Disney), the court sent a clear signal that third‑party beneficiaries must be **expressly** identified in the contract, and that the ordinary presumption against imposing contractual obligations on non‑signatories will dominate even in the high‑profile world of online content creation.  

The ruling is an important checkpoint for creators, influencers, and the platforms that host them. As the line between “user” and “subject of user‑generated content” blurs, courts will increasingly be asked to decide whether a non‑signatory can invoke platform contracts to protect their interests. *Santos* offers a roadmap—rooted in Illinois law for the terms themselves and New York law for the alleged implied contract—that will guide litigants for years to come.

---

### The court’s reasoning in a nutshell

The Second Circuit applied a two‑pronged analysis. First, it asked whether Illinois law—chosen to govern the Cameo Terms of Service—permits a non‑signing third party to claim a benefit under the agreement. Illinois follows a **strong presumption against third‑party benefits**, requiring an “express declaration” that the contract was intended to create enforceable rights for outsiders. The court noted that the Cameo Terms contain a limited third‑party beneficiary clause that *does* confer rights to third parties, but only when the language is unambiguous. In Santos’s complaint, there was no indication that the parties meant to extend any of the contractual protections to Santos; the agreement was silent on that point. Consequently, the presumption stood, and Santos could not rely on the contract.

Second, the court turned to New York law to evaluate Santos’s alleged **implied‑in‑fact contract** claim. Under New York principles, a contract can be inferred only where the complaint alleges an “meeting of the minds” and sets out the essential terms. The opinion found that Santos’s pleading was deficient on both counts. He failed to allege the specific provisions of the Cameo Terms that Kimmel allegedly breached, and he offered no factual basis for a mutual understanding that Kimmel would honor those provisions toward Santos. Without those foundational elements, the claim could not survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion.

The court wrapped up its analysis by noting that even if Santos could sidestep the third‑party presumption, his alleged **fraudulent inducement** claim also fell flat because he never demonstrated any out‑of‑pocket loss—an essential element under New York law.

---

### Do we agree with the court?

*The decision is sound, but not without nuance.*  

The Illinois presumption against third‑party benefits is a well‑settled principle, and the court applied it faithfully. However, the reasoning glosses over an emerging reality: many platform agreements are drafted with the expectation that the platform’s content will be **repurposed, remixed, and broadcast** by third parties who are not signatories. By insisting on an express third‑party beneficiary clause whenever a non‑signer seeks enforcement, courts may be forcing platforms to anticipate every conceivable downstream use—a practically impossible task.

The New York implied‑contract analysis is equally textbook. Yet the pleading standards for implied contracts are notoriously high, and the Second Circuit’s de novo review of a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal gives little leeway for defendants to “prove” the existence of a contract at the pleading stage. Santos’s complaint could have been salvaged with minimal amendment—perhaps by attaching the specific clause of the Cameo Terms that purports to protect “any person who purchases a video” or by furnishing emails that suggest Kimmel understood he was dealing with a third‑party performer. The court’s refusal to allow that modest clarification signals a **strict pleading regime** that may discourage legitimate claims where the contractual language is ambiguous but not overtly hostile to third‑party rights.

---

### Doctrinal implications

#### 1. **Reaffirmation of the expressive third‑party beneficiary doctrine**

The opinion reiterates that **expressness is king**. Any platform that wishes to give non‑signing creators enforceable rights must draft a clear, unambiguous beneficiary provision. A vague “may be used for promotional purposes” clause will not survive a suit by a third party seeking to enforce the agreement. This creates a clear drafting imperative for platforms that host user‑generated content that will later be broadcast or otherwise commercialized.

#### 2. **Heightened pleading burden for implied‑contract claims**

By emphasizing the need for “essential terms” and a “meeting of the minds,” the Second Circuit raises the bar for plaintiffs who rely on implied contracts to sidestep a lack of direct contractual privity. This aligns with the broader trend in federal courts to **tighten pleading standards** post‑*Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly* and *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*. Practitioners must now anticipate these deficiencies and either secure direct agreements with platforms or craft pleadings that explicitly echo the contract’s language.

#### 3. **Preemption remains a moving target**

Although preemption was not resolved because the state‑law claims failed on other grounds, the court’s analysis hints that **copyright preemption** will continue to choke off many creative‑rights claims against platforms. The implication is that plaintiffs should focus on **contract-based remedies**—and those, as we see, must be meticulously pleaded.

---

### Potential downstream effects

1. **Platform drafting wars**  
   Expect a wave of **“third‑party beneficiary addendums”** across the tech industry. Companies like Cameo, Patreon, and TikTok may begin to insert boilerplate clauses that explicitly enumerate who can enforce the agreement (e.g., “any individual who purchases a custom video may enforce the anti‑misuse provisions”) to avoid being blindsided by lawsuits from non‑signers.

2. **Strategic litigation by creators**  
   Influencers and content creators will likely **bundle “right‑of‑first‑refusal” clauses** into their own agreements with platforms, ensuring they have a contractual foothold if the platform later licenses the work to a third party. This could give birth to a new class of “platform‑creator contracts” that function as quasi‑joint ventures.

3. **Judicial spill‑over into other circuits**  
   While *Santos* is a Second Circuit decision, its reasoning is grounded in well‑established Illinois and New York law. Courts in the Ninth, Fifth, and Eleventh Circuits that confront similar third‑party beneficiary disputes—especially involving nationwide platforms—are likely to follow suit, reinforcing a **national standard** that favors strict expressness over implied benefits.

4. **Potential legislative response**  
   Lawmakers concerned about the power imbalance between individual creators and massive platforms may consider **statutory “fair‑use” protections** that bypass contract doctrines altogether. A federal “Creator Protection Act” could carve out a statutory cause of action for non‑signers whose works are used in a transformative, commentary‑driven context, similar to the fair‑use defense but with a private right of action.

---

### Bottom line

*Santos v. Kimmel* may have involved a relatively low‑stakes dispute over a satirical TV segment, but the opinion is a **landmark on the intersection of contract law and digital platforms**. It underscores that, absent an explicit third‑party beneficiary clause, non‑signers cannot turn a platform’s Terms of Service into a shield against alleged misuse. Simultaneously, the decision sharpens the pleading requirements for implied contracts, making it harder for creators to rely on vague contractual expectations.

For content creators, the takeaway is simple: **don’t assume a platform’s user agreement protects you**. If you want enforceable rights, you must either negotiate a direct contract with the platform or ensure the platform’s agreement expressly names you as a beneficiary. For platforms, the lesson is equally clear: if you intend to protect third‑party creators, **write that protection in bold, unambiguous language**—or be prepared to watch your users go to court and lose.



## Fraudulent Inducement and Out‑of‑Pocket Loss: Why Santos’s Claim Fell Flat  

**By [Your Name],** *Legal Analyst*  

### Why the Decision Matters  

The Second Circuit’s dismissal of former Congressman George Santos’s fraudulent‑inducement claim against Jimmy Kimmel, ABC, and Disney may look like a footnote in a bizarre copyright fight, but it underscores a fundamental hurdle for any plaintiff who tries to bring a fraud suit in the entertainment arena: New York law demands a concrete out‑of‑pocket loss. The court’s refusal to “read‑in” speculative harms sends a clear message that plaintiffs must do more than allege deception—they must show that the deception actually cost them money. In a sector where “creative” contracts and “viral” collaborations are routine, the ruling narrows the path for “gotcha” lawsuits that hinge on vague promises of exposure or goodwill.

### The Court’s Reasoning in a Nutshell  

Santos sued on several theories, including fraudulent inducement, alleging that Kimmel misrepresented the purpose of the videos he ordered via Cameo and then used them for ridicule on “Jimmy Kimmel Live!” The Second Circuit affirmed the district court’s dismissal, holding that Santos failed to plead the elemental damage requirement for a fraud claim under New York law.  

New York courts have long required a plaintiff to prove **(1)** a false representation, **(2)** reliance on that representation, **(3)** intent to induce reliance, and **(4)** **actual loss**—typically measured as out‑of‑pocket expenses incurred because of the reliance. *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, 88 N.Y.2d 413 (1996). The court emphasized that a plaintiff cannot recover for intangible harms such as “damage to reputation” or “loss of opportunity” unless they can be tied to a quantifiable dollar loss. Santos’s complaint alleged that he was “deceived” and that the videos were used in a mocking context, but it offered no facts showing that he spent money (or forewent a paying opportunity) as a direct result of Kimmel’s alleged misrepresentation.  

The Second Circuit therefore found the fraud claim “procedurally defective” and dismissed it under Rule 12(b)(6). The court declined to reach any preemption analysis because the pleading defect was fatal.

### Our Take: Is the Out‑of‑Pocket Requirement Too Rigid?  

The out‑of‑pocket threshold is not a novel invention; it is a well‑settled doctrinal line meant to prevent limitless liability for intangible harms. Yet the entertainment industry increasingly relies on “exposure” deals, “cross‑promotion” arrangements, and platform‑based collaborations where the promised benefit is not a direct cash payment but a boost in visibility. In that context, the requirement for a concrete monetary loss can feel out‑of‑step with the realities of content creation.  

The court, however, was correct in applying the traditional rule. Allowing plaintiffs to recover for “lost exposure” would open the floodgates to speculative damages, making it nearly impossible for defendants to anticipate liability. Moreover, the damage requirement is not a form‑letter bottleneck; it is a factual inquiry. If Santos had attached invoices for the time he spent filming, the cost of his Cameo account, or a contractual payment he forfeited because the videos were repurposed, the claim might have survived. The decision therefore serves as a reminder that **plaintiffs must anchor their fraud allegations in the financial ledger, not just the narrative of deception.**

### Doctrinal Implications  

1. **Reaffirmation of New York Fraud Elements** – The opinion reinforces the four‑element test and, most importantly, the requirement that loss be “actual” and “quantifiable.” Lower courts in the Second Circuit will likely cite this opinion when facing similarly under‑pleaded fraud complaints in entertainment cases.  

2. **Limited Scope for Preemption Arguments** – By disposing of the claim on pleading grounds, the court avoided the more complex question of whether state‑law fraud claims are preempted by the Copyright Act. Future plaintiffs cannot rely on preemption defenses to salvage a claim; they must first clear the pleading hurdle.  

3. **Impact on Third‑Party Beneficiary Theory** – The decision also dovetails with the court’s earlier analysis of Santos’s contract claims, emphasizing that a non‑signatory cannot borrow the rights of a contract’s parties without clear, express language. This “third‑party” limitation will shape how content‑creators structure platform terms of service and how they attempt to bring third‑party claims.  

### What This Means for Future Plaintiffs  

If you’re a creator courting a celebrity or a network for a cameo, cross‑promotion, or any “in‑kind” deal, keep these drafting tips in mind:

* **Document the Transaction** – Save invoices, payment receipts, and any written acknowledgment of the fee or exchange. Even a modest payment for a cameo can serve as the out‑of‑pocket loss the court expects.  

* **Explicitly Define the Deliverables** – A contract that spells out “The video will be used solely on Platform X for promotional purposes” gives you a clear benchmark for breach and, by extension, for calculating loss when the promise is broken.  

* **Tie the Damages to a Monetary Figure** – If you claim that a misuse of your content cost you a sponsorship, attach the sponsorship contract and show the dollar value of the lost deal. Speculative “loss of reputation” won’t cut it.  

* **Consider a Separate Claim for Unfair Competition or Right of Publicity** – Those doctrines sometimes permit recovery for non‑monetary harms, but they also have their own pleading standards.  

### Downstream Effects on Entertainment Litigation  

The Second Circuit’s ruling will likely discourage a wave of “gotcha” fraud suits that hinge on ambiguous promises of exposure. Plaintiffs will need to do the legwork of quantifying damages before filing, which could shift more disputes to pre‑litigation negotiation or mediation—where parties can more readily agree on a settlement that reflects intangible losses without the rigor of a court‑approved damage calculation.

Moreover, the decision may prompt platforms like Cameo to tighten their terms, perhaps inserting clearer language about third‑party rights and the scope of permissible uses. Content creators, in turn, may seek more robust indemnity provisions when they agree to produce videos for public figures, ensuring that any misuse triggers a pre‑agreed monetary penalty.

### Bottom Line  

Santos’s fraudulent‑inducement claim fell flat not because the court sympathized with his view of the “mockery” inflicted by Kimmel, but because he failed to plead the concrete, out‑of‑pocket loss that New York law demands. The decision reinforces the timeless principle that fraud is a pecuniary harm doctrine, even in an age where “value” is often measured in likes, shares, and viral reach. For anyone navigating the murky waters of entertainment collaborations, the lesson is clear: **If you want to sue for fraud, bring the receipts.**
