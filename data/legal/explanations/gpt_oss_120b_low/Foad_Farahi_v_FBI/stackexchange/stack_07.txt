Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### How does the D.C. Circuit’s de novo review of FOIA summary‑judgment decisions affect the appellate analysis?
Question:
The opinion states that FOIA summary‑judgment grants are reviewed de novo. I’m trying to understand what this means in practice. Does the appellate court disregard the district court’s factual findings entirely, or does it give any deference to the lower court’s assessment of the agency’s declarations? How does de novo review interact with the agency’s statutory burden under § 552(a)(4)(B)?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the D.C. Circuit, a grant of summary judgment in a FOIA case is reviewed **de novo** – that is, the appellate court looks at the record anew and makes its own determination whether the agency has satisfied the statutory burden that a FOIA exemption applies.  See *Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI*, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021) (citing the opinion you provided, § III).

### What “de novo” means here

1. **No deference to the district court’s factual findings.**  
   The appellate court does **not** simply accept the district court’s factual conclusions about whether, for example, the FBI’s investigation is “pending or reasonably anticipated.”  Instead, it evaluates the same evidence – the agency’s declarations and any other record in the docket – and decides for itself whether the statutory elements of Exemption 7(A) are met.  The opinion states that “we review a grant of summary judgment **de novo**” and then proceeds to apply the burden‑shifting framework to the FBI’s declarations rather than to the district court’s summary.

2. **Limited deference to the agency’s own assertions.**  
   While the court conducts a fresh analysis, it still accords the agency a **fair measure of deference** on matters that are “entitled to deference” because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency.  Cf. *Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998).  This deference is not a blanket grant of judgment‑proof authority; it simply means the court will accept the FBI’s **nexus** between the records and a law‑enforcement function when the agency “explains a rational connection” (see § III‑A).  The court therefore relies heavily on the Hardy and Bender declarations, but it still checks that those declarations provide the requisite specificity.

3. **Interaction with the agency’s § 552(a)(4)(B) burden.**  
   Under § 552(a)(4)(B) the government bears the burden of showing that an exemption applies.  The appellate court, in its de novo review, asks whether the agency‑provided evidence satisfies **both** statutory prongs of Exemption 7(A):

   * **Compiled for law‑enforcement purposes** – the court looks at the Hardy declaration (public and ex‑parte) to see that the FBI identified a “law‑enforcement” nexus.  The opinion says the district court’s earlier “seemingly” finding is supplanted by the appellate court’s own assessment that the FBI “carried its burden” because the declarations establish that nexus.  

   * **Disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings** – the appellate court again examines the same declarations (Hardy’s detailed ex‑parte statement and Bender’s update) to determine whether they show a **concrete, prospective law‑enforcement proceeding**.  The opinion notes that an agency may establish pendency merely by “attesting to their existence” (citing *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d at 59).  Accordingly, the court does not rely on the district court’s “seemingly” language but makes its own conclusion that the FBI’s statements satisfy this element.

### Practical effect

- **The district court’s factual conclusions are not binding.**  The appellate court re‑evaluates the factual record (the agency’s declarations) and may reach a different result if the declarations are insufficient.  In *Farahi*, the appellate court affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment **because**, after its own de novo review, it found the agency’s declarations met the statutory burden—not because it accepted the lower court’s summary of the facts.

- **Agency burden remains unchanged.**  The government must still prove the two elements of Exemption 7(A) **on the record before the appellate court**.  De novo review does not lift or shift that burden; it merely changes who makes the ultimate factual determination.

- **Deference is limited to the agency’s expertise.**  The court may give “fair measure of deference” to the FBI’s expertise in law‑enforcement matters, but that deference is applied **within** the de novo analysis; the court still scrutinizes the adequacy of the agency’s affidavits and may reject them if they are overly conclusory.

### Bottom line

In the D.C. Circuit, a FOIA summary‑judgment decision is reviewed *de novo*: the appellate court ignores the district court’s factual findings and decides afresh whether the agency has satisfied the statutory burden under § 552(a)(4)(B) and the elements of the invoked exemption.  The agency’s own declarations receive a modest, expertise‑based deference, but the court’s ultimate judgment rests on its independent assessment of those declarations.  Consequently, the appellate analysis is a fresh, independent evaluation of the agency’s evidence, not a rubber‑stamp of the lower court’s conclusions.
