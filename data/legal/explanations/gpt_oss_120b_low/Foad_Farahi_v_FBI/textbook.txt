Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Chapter 1: Facts and Procedural Posture  

In order to understand the appellate court’s decision in **Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation**, a law student must first grasp the factual and procedural background that gave rise to the dispute.  This chapter presents that background in a organized, narrative form, following the sub‑topics outlined in the assignment.  The discussion stays strictly within the facts and procedural history set out in the opinion; it does not venture into broader legal doctrine except where a minimal explanation is needed to make sense of the court’s reasoning.

## 1.1 Farahi’s Immigration Status and Removal Proceedings  

Foad Farahi is an Iranian national who entered the United States in 1993 and has remained here for more than three decades.  In 2002 the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) placed Farahi in **removal proceedings** because he had overstayed his visa.  Removal proceedings are civil proceedings before an immigration judge that can result in the respondent being ordered to leave the United States.  

During those proceedings Farahi repeatedly sought protection from removal.  He filed applications for **asylum**, for **withholding of removal**, for protection under the **Convention Against Torture**, and for **voluntary departure**.  Each of these relief avenues requires the respondent to demonstrate, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he faces persecution, torture, or other serious harm if returned to his home country.  

The government, represented by DHS, opposed Farahi’s relief by presenting documentary evidence and live testimony from an FBI agent.  That testimony asserted that Farahi had “connections to high‑level terrorists.”  The agency’s claims formed the factual foundation of DHS’s removal request and were later invoked by the FBI to justify withholding records under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA).

## 1.2 Allegations of Terrorist Connections in DHS/Immigration Filings  

The immigration filings contain specific allegations that Farahi was involved with extremist groups.  According to the record cited by the court, the government alleged that Farahi:  

* Was a **recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood**,  
* Had **connections to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed** and other known Al Qaeda members,  
* Was **linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists**, including **Jose Padilla**, and  
* Provided support to charities that the government identified as financing terrorism, such as the **Holy Land Foundation** and the **Global Relief Foundation**.  

These allegations were presented to the immigration judge as part of DHS’s effort to demonstrate that Farahi posed a national‑security risk and therefore should be removed.  The same factual matrix underlies the FBI’s claim that its file on Farahi was compiled for law‑enforcement purposes.

## 1.3 Scope of the FOIA Request and the Volume of Responsive Material  

In 2015 Farahi filed a **FOIA request** seeking “his FBI file.”  Under FOIA, an agency must produce all non‑exempt records responsive to a request unless an enumerated exemption applies.  The FBI’s search turned up a substantial body of material: **approximately 10,750 pages of documents and 80 compact discs** that potentially contained information about Farahi.  The sheer size of the responsive set underscores the practical challenges that FOIA litigants and agencies face when the request implicates extensive investigative files.

## 1.4 Agency’s Initial Response, Missed Deadline, and Subsequent Disclosures  

FOIA imposes a statutory deadline—generally **20 business days**—by which an agency must either (1) produce the records, (2) provide a lawful withholding determination, or (3) request an extension with proper justification.  The FBI **failed to meet this deadline**.  Consequently, Farahi filed a suit in the United States District Court for the District of Columbia seeking judicial enforcement of his FOIA rights.

After the suit was filed, the FBI embarked on a multi‑year review of the 10,750 pages and 80 CDs.  Over time the agency **disclosed a modest portion of the material** to Farahi, while **withholding the majority** on the ground that the records fell within **Exemption 7(A)**—the exemption that protects records “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” when disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with **pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings**.

## 1.5 Summary‑Judgment Motions, Hardy and Bender Declarations, and District‑Court Rulings  

### Initial Summary‑Judgment Motion (October 2019)  

Having completed its review, the FBI moved for **summary judgment** in October 2019, asserting that the withheld records were properly exempt.  To support the motion the agency submitted a **declaration from David Hardy**, then Section Chief of the FBI’s Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS).  Hardy’s declaration stated, in general terms, that the records were compiled for investigations into **national‑security and international‑terrorism violations** and that their release could interfere with **pending or reasonably anticipated** law‑enforcement proceedings.

The FBI sought to file a **supplemental Hardy declaration** **under seal and ex parte** (i.e., without Farahi’s participation).  Farahi offered no opposition, and the district court **granted** the motion to file the supplemental declaration in October 2020.

### District Court’s First Ruling on Summary Judgment (November 2022)  

When the district court considered the FBI’s summary‑judgment motion in November 2022, it concluded that:  

* The records were indeed **compiled for law‑enforcement purposes**, and  
* The FBI had **“seemingly” met its burden** of showing that disclosure could interfere with pending or anticipated enforcement proceedings.  

However, the court expressed **two concerns**:  

1. **Staleness** – considerable time had elapsed since the original declarations, raising doubt about whether the investigations were still ongoing.  
2. **Segregability** – the court needed more information to determine whether any non‑exempt portions of the records could be separated and released.  

Because of those concerns, the court **denied the FBI’s summary‑judgment motion without prejudice**, ordering the agency to **update its assessment** of the status of the investigations and to provide a more detailed segregability analysis.

### Updated Declarations and Renewed Motion (2023)  

In response, the FBI submitted **new declarations** from **Joseph Bender**, who was then Acting Section Chief of RIDS.  Bender’s **public declaration** explained that RIDS had consulted the FBI agent overseeing Farahi’s file, who confirmed that the investigations **remained ongoing**.  Bender also filed an **ex parte declaration** giving additional detail on the nature of the investigations and expanding on the earlier segregation analysis.

The district court, after reviewing Bender’s submissions, **granted** the renewed summary‑judgment motion.  The court accepted Bender’s confirmation that the investigations were still active and held that the FBI had shown why the exempt material was **not reasonably segregable** from the non‑exempt material.

### Appeal  

Farahi appealed the district‑court’s grant of summary judgment to the **United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit**.  His appeal focused on (1) whether the FBI had sufficiently demonstrated that the investigations were “pending or reasonably anticipated” despite the passage of time, and (2) whether the agency had met its statutory duty to **segregate** any non‑exempt portions of the records.

## 1.6 Appeal to the D.C. Circuit and Issues Presented  

The **appellate docket** (No. 23‑5243) presented the following **core issues** for the D.C. Circuit’s review:  

1. **Whether Exemption 7(A) applies** to the FBI’s records on Farahi, specifically:  
   * Did the FBI satisfy its burden of showing the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes?  
   * Did the FBI demonstrate that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with **pending or reasonably anticipated** enforcement proceedings?  

2. **Whether the district court properly evaluated the “pending or reasonably anticipated” requirement** given the elapsed time between the original declarations and the final ruling.  

3. **Whether the FBI fulfilled its duty under § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) to take reasonable steps to segregate and release any non‑exempt portions** of the records.  

4. **Whether the district court erred in relying on ex parte declarations** (Hardy’s supplemental declaration and Bender’s ex parte filing) when assessing both the exemption and the segregability analysis.  

The appellate court’s answer to these questions—affirming the district court’s grant of summary judgment—rested on a factual foundation that the foregoing sections have set out.  Understanding the timeline, the nature of the allegations, the magnitude of the responsive material, and the procedural moves by both parties is essential before delving into the court’s legal analysis of Exemption 7(A) and the segregation requirement.

# Chapter 2: The FOIA Statutory Scheme and Exemption 7(A)

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) balances two competing interests: the public’s right to know what the federal government is doing and the government’s need to withhold certain information.  Understanding this balance is essential for reading any FOIA opinion, including *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*.  This chapter walks you through the statutory framework that underlies the Court’s analysis and then concentrates on Exemption 7(A)—the provision the Court relied on to uphold the FBI’s withholdings.

## 2.1 FOIA’s “right to know” provision and statutory text (552(a)(3), (b))

FOIA’s core command is found at **28 U.S.C. § 552(a)(3)**, which states that “any person” may *“inspect and copy any agency record … unless the record (or any portion thereof) is ... specifically exempted from disclosure by law.”*  The statute therefore creates a **presumption of openness**: agencies must disclose all records that are responsive to a request, and the burden is on the agency to show that an exemption applies.

The definition of “record” is set out in **§ 552(b)**, which includes any *“any item, collection, or grouping of information, regardless of physical form or characteristics, that is either created or obtained by an agency and that is under agency control.”*  This broad definition captures paper documents, electronic files, and even audio or video recordings—precisely the types of material the FBI located in response to Farahi’s request (over 10,000 pages and 80 CDs).

Because the statute is written in a “right‑to‑know” language, any exception to disclosure must be expressly enumerated.  The Court in *Farahi* therefore began its analysis by confirming that the FBI’s withholdings could not be justified by any ground other than a statutory exemption.

## 2.2 Enumerated exemptions and the agency’s burden to invoke them (552(a)(4)(B))

FOIA lists nine categorical exemptions in **§ 552(b)**, each addressing a distinct class of sensitive information (e.g., national security, internal personnel rules, trade secrets).  The **agency’s burden** for every exemption is set out in **§ 552(a)(4)(B)**: the agency *“shall bear the burden of proving that the record is exempt.”*  This is a **strict burden of proof**—the agency must present competent evidence, often in the form of sworn declarations, that satisfies the statutory elements of the exemption it invokes.

In *Farahi*, the FBI invoked **Exemption 7(A)**.  The district court therefore required the FBI to produce declarations that established (1) the records were *compiled for law‑enforcement purposes* and (2) disclosure *could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings* that were *pending or reasonably anticipated*.  The Court’s ultimate affirmation of the district court’s grant of summary judgment underscores that the agency met its burden.

## 2.3 Text of Exemption 7(A) – compiled for law‑enforcement purposes & interference with proceedings

Exemption 7(A) reads:

> “Records or portions of records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes … if the release of such records *could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings*.”  28 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A).

Two elements must be satisfied:

1. **Compiled for law‑enforcement purposes.**  The record must have been created *“in furtherance of a law‑enforcement investigation or prosecution.”*  The Supreme Court has emphasized a “nexus” between the record and the agency’s law‑enforcement function.  In *Farahi*, the FBI’s declarations described the file as assembled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism” and linked the material to ongoing investigations of Farahi’s alleged terrorist affiliations.  The Court accepted that nexus, noting that the FBI’s status as a law‑enforcement agency grants its determinations a “fair measure of deference.”

2. **Reasonable expectation of interference with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.**  The statute does not require the agency to prove that disclosure *will* interfere, only that it *could* reasonably be expected to do so.  The Court in *Farahi* found the FBI’s explanation—that releasing the material would tip off targets and jeopardize an ongoing terrorism investigation—sufficient to satisfy this element.

## 2.4 Judicial interpretation of “pending or reasonably anticipated” proceedings (CREW precedent)

The phrase *“pending or reasonably anticipated”* has been clarified by precedent.  In **Citizens for Responsible & Ethical Government v. DOJ**, 746 F.3d 1082 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (the *CREW* decision), the Court held that for Exemption 7(A) to apply, the agency must show that the underlying enforcement action is either already under way (*pending*) or that the agency has a *reasonable basis* to anticipate that a proceeding will occur in the future.  The *CREW* opinion emphasized that a bare assertion that “investigations are ongoing” may be insufficient; the agency must provide *some specificity* about the nature of the anticipated proceeding.

In *Farahi* the district court initially flagged the FBI’s Hardy declaration as “seemingly” satisfying the *pending or reasonably anticipated* requirement but ordered an update because of the passage of time.  The FBI responded with fresh declarations from Bender, confirming that the investigation remained active as of March 2023.  The appellate Court accepted this update, reasoning that the new declaration functioned as an *update* to the earlier, more detailed Hardy statements rather than a stand‑alone justification.  The Court therefore reiterated that an agency may satisfy the *pending or anticipated* prong by *updating* an earlier sufficient showing, provided the update does not merely repeat a conclusory assertion.

## 2.5 Relationship of Exemption 7(A) to national‑security investigations

Exemption 7(A) is distinct from the national‑security exemptions (e.g., Exemptions 1 and 3), but courts have consistently accorded **heightened deference** to law‑enforcement agencies when the records pertain to national‑security or counter‑terrorism investigations.  The D.C. Circuit has stated that “the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national‑security concerns are implicated… is especially strong” (see *Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ*, 331 F.3d 918, 927‑28 (D.C. Cir. 2003)).  This deference does not waive the agency’s burden; rather, it means that the court will accept the agency’s sworn statements as credible unless there is a reason to doubt them.

In *Farahi*, the Court noted the FBI’s specialized expertise in terrorism investigations and the sensitivity of the alleged connections to foreign terrorist organizations.  Accordingly, the Court gave “fair measure of deference” to the FBI’s determinations that the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that disclosure would jeopardize the investigation.

## 2.6 The “reasonable steps” segregation requirement (552(a)(8)(A)(ii))

Even when an exemption applies to an entire document, FOIA obligates agencies to make **reasonable efforts** to separate (or “segregate”) any non‑exempt portions and release them.  The statute says that an agency must *“take reasonable steps to segregate and release nonexempt information”* and that *“any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided … after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”*  28 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii).

The segregation analysis proceeds in two stages:

1. **Identify the exempt and non‑exempt material.**  The agency must provide a clear categorical or line‑by‑line explanation of why particular portions fall under the exemption.  In *Farahi*, the district court required the FBI to explain its categorical withholding scheme, but Farahi failed to demonstrate that the categories were overly broad, leading the Court to deem the FBI’s categorization satisfactory.

2. **Assess reasonableness of segregation.**  The agency must show that the non‑exempt material is **reasonably segregable**—i.e., it can be isolated without rendering the exempt material “inextricably intertwined.”  The D.C. Circuit has articulated that a “tiny amount of non‑exempt text interspersed line‑by‑line” does not satisfy the segregation requirement (see *Mead Data Center, Inc. v. Dep’t of the Air Force*, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)).  In *Farahi*, the district court concluded, based on the FBI’s detailed ex parte declaration, that the exempt material was not reasonably segregable because the non‑exempt portions were scattered throughout the massive files and could not be released without disclosing the core investigative content.

The appellate Court affirmed this finding, emphasizing that the FOIA’s purpose is to disclose *information*, not merely isolated words that would compromise the integrity of a law‑enforcement investigation.

---

**Bottom line for students:** The *Farahi* opinion illustrates the practical application of FOIA’s statutory scheme.  The agency bears the burden of proving each exemption element; the Court gives deference to the agency’s expertise, especially in national‑security contexts; and even when an exemption applies, agencies must still attempt to segregate any releasable material.  Mastery of these concepts will enable you to follow the Court’s reasoning in any FOIA case.

# Chapter 3: Agency Burden of Proof and the Use of Declarations  

In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, 23‑5243 (D.C. Cir. 2025), the appellate court examined how an agency satisfies the statutory burden imposed by Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) Exemption 7(A).  The decision offers a clear illustration of (1) what the government must prove—both that a record was “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” and that disclosure would “reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings”; (2) the types of factual evidence agencies may rely upon, including public and ex‑parte declarations; (3) the level of deference courts give to law‑enforcement agencies; (4) the “nexus” requirement that ties the material to the agency’s investigative mission; and (5) how a district court’s analysis of those declarations is reviewed de novo on appeal.  This chapter unpacks each of those elements, using the *Farahi* opinion as a concrete guide.

---

## 3.1 Burden of Proof: “Compiled for Law‑Enforcement Purposes” and “Interference”

FOIA places the initial burden on the agency to demonstrate that a claimed exemption applies.  5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B).  For Exemption 7(A) the burden is two‑fold:

1. **Compiled for law‑enforcement purposes.**  The record must have been created “in furtherance of a law‑enforcement activity.”  *Campbell v. Department of Justice*, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998).  The agency must show a rational connection between the subject matter of the record and a legitimate investigative or enforcement function of the agency.

2. **Reasonably expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.**  The statute adds, “if the disclosure … could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.”  The courts have interpreted “proceedings” to mean any pending or reasonably anticipated criminal, civil, or administrative action.  *Citizens for Responsible & Ethical Government v. Department of Justice*, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (quoting *Mapother v. Department of Justice*, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).  The government must therefore establish that (a) a proceeding exists or is imminently expected, and (b) disclosure would likely impair that proceeding—typically by alerting targets, jeopardizing evidence, or undermining investigative tactics.

In *Farahi*, the appellate court reiterated that these two prongs are *juridical*—the agency must *prove* each element, not merely *assert* it.

---

## 3.2 Types of Evidence the Agency May Rely On – Public and Ex‑Parte Declarations

Because the very records the agency seeks to protect are often classified or otherwise unreleasable, the government is permitted to submit **affidavits or declarations** as evidence of the exemption.  The FOIA statute itself does not distinguish among forms of evidence, but case law mandates that the declarations be sufficiently detailed to enable the court to assess the exemption.

- **Public declarations** are filed in the open docket.  They are subject to scrutiny by the requesting party and must contain a factual basis that can be challenged.  In *Farahi*, the FBI first supplied a public declaration by David Hardy, Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS).  Hardy outlined, in broad terms, that the records were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism” and that the investigation was ongoing.

- **Ex‑parte declarations** are filed under seal and disclosed only to the court.  They are used when the agency asserts that the very content of the records is too sensitive to be placed on the public record.  The *Farahi* court accepted ex‑parte declarations from Hardy (originally) and later from Joseph Bender, Acting Section Chief of RIDS, as permissible because they provided the factual detail necessary to satisfy the exemption without compromising security.

The key limitation is that **the government cannot rely solely on conclusory statements**.  The *CREW* decision warned that a vague assertion that “investigations remain ongoing” may be insufficient.  However, the *Farahi* court held that when a declaration is **supplemented** by earlier, more detailed affidavits, a brief update can be adequate to confirm that nothing has changed.  The appellate court emphasized that the ex‑parte Bender declaration functioned as an *update* to the Hardy declarations, not as the sole foundation for the exemption.

---

## 3.3 The Deference Doctrine for Law‑Enforcement Agencies

Courts give **substantial deference** to an agency’s judgment that a record falls within Exemption 7(A).  The rationale is twofold: (1) law‑enforcement agencies possess specialized expertise about ongoing investigations; and (2) the statute itself foresees the need to protect investigative techniques.

- **Campbell** set the baseline: “Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference.”  The deference is *fair* rather than *absolute*; the agency must still meet the statutory burden.

- **National‑security contexts** receive heightened deference.  In *Center for National Security Studies v. Department of Justice*, 331 F.3d 918, 927‑28 (D.C. Cir. 2003), the court noted the “principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated,” particularly for counter‑terrorism investigations.

In *Farahi*, the appellate court applied this deference doctrine, noting that the FBI’s determinations about the national‑security nature of the investigation were entitled to a “fair measure of deference.”  Nevertheless, the court still required the agency to satisfy the two statutory prongs described in § 552(b)(7)(A).

---

## 4.4 Required Nexus Between the Investigation and Agency Duties (Pratt Standard)

Deference does not excuse the agency from proving a **logical nexus** between the record and a law‑enforcement function.  *Pratt v. Webster*, 673 F.2d 408, 420‑21 (D.C. Cir. 1982) articulated the two‑part inquiry:

1. **Investigation‑Agency Nexus:** The record must be linked to an official investigative or enforcement duty of the agency.
2. **Individual‑or‑Incident Nexus:** The record must relate to a specific individual, incident, or alleged violation.

The *Farahi* court applied this standard by pointing to Hardy’s declaration that the FBI’s file “documented Farahi’s connections to high‑level terrorists” and was compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security.”  Those statements satisfy the *Pratt* nexus: the FBI’s statutory duty to investigate terrorism is the investigative purpose, and Farahi’s alleged ties constitute the individual nexus.

---

## 3.5 The District Court’s Analysis of Hardy’s Public and Ex‑Parte Declarations

At the district‑court level, the judge first evaluated the **public Hardy declaration**.  Hardy described the overarching investigative framework and asserted that the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes.  Although the declaration was relatively concise, the judge found it provided a **reasonable factual basis** for the exemption’s first prong.

The **ex‑parte Hardy declaration** supplied the granular details that could not be disclosed publicly—e.g., the specific investigative steps, sources, and evidentiary materials.  The district court treated this sealed affidavit as satisfying the *interference* prong: it explained how the release of even a fragment of the file could alert subjects, thereby jeopardizing the investigation.

Because the district court had earlier noted the “significant passage of time,” it required an **update** on the status of the investigation before granting summary judgment.  This led to the subsequent submission of Bender’s declarations.

---

## 3.6 The Role of Bender’s Supplemental Declaration as an Update, Not a Standalone Basis

When the FBI returned to the court in 2023, it filed a **public declaration** and an **ex‑parte declaration** by Joseph Bender.  The public declaration basically repeated that RIDS had confirmed with the supervising agent that “investigations remained ongoing.”  The ex‑parte declaration gave additional detail on why the investigation continued and reiterated the lack of segregability.

The appellate court stressed that **Bender’s declaration was not intended to re‑prove the exemption**; rather, it functioned as an **affirmative update** confirming that the factual situation identified in Hardy’s earlier declarations had not changed.  The court reasoned that because the district court had already found Hardy’s affidavits sufficient to meet the burden, a brief confirmation that “nothing has changed” was adequate.

This approach aligns with the *CREW* caution: a lone, terse statement may be insufficient, but when it **supplements** a previously adequate factual foundation, it can satisfy the court’s need for an “up‑to‑date” status report.  The appellate court therefore upheld the district court’s reliance on Bender’s supplemental declaration, finding no error in treating it as an update rather than a standalone justification.

---

## 3.7 Summary‑Judgment Review: De Novo Application of the Burden

FOIA summary‑judgment rulings are reviewed **de novo**—the appellate court re‑examines the evidence and the legal standards without deference to the district court’s factual findings.  *Representatives Committee for Freedom of the Press v. FBI*, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021).  However, the appellate court still respects **the agency’s procedural obligations** (e.g., providing sufficient factual detail) and the **deference doctrine** (i.e., giving a fair measure of weight to the agency’s expertise).

In *Farahi*, the appellate court applied de novo review to confirm that:

1. The FBI met the statutory burden on both prongs of Exemption 7(A).
2. The agency’s declarations—taken together—provided the requisite factual specificity.
3. The district court correctly concluded that the records were not reasonably segregable.

Because the appellate court found no error in the district court’s factual assessment of the declarations, it affirmed the summary‑judgment grant.

---

### Take‑aways for Students

- **Burden Placement:** The agency always carries the initial burden to demonstrate that a FOIA exemption applies.  In Exemption 7(A) cases, this entails a two‑prong showing (compiled for law‑enforcement purposes; interference with pending or anticipated proceedings).
- **Evidence Types:** Agencies may rely on public affidavits and sealed (ex‑parte) declarations.  The latter are permissible when disclosure of the supporting facts would defeat the purpose of the exemption.
- **Deference:** Courts give a “fair measure of deference” to law‑enforcement agencies, especially in national‑security contexts, but the agency must still satisfy the statutory elements.
- **Nexus Requirement:** The *Pratt* test demands a logical connection between the record, the agency’s investigative duty, and the particular individual or incident.
- **Updating Declarations:** An agency’s later, brief declaration can serve as an update to earlier, detailed affidavits; it need not re‑establish the entire factual basis.
- **Appellate Review:** FOIA summary‑judgment decisions are reviewed de novo, yet the appellate court will respect a district court’s proper application of the burden and deference doctrines.

Understanding how *Farahi* navigates these principles gives you a practical roadmap for analyzing FOIA exemption disputes, especially those involving Exemption 7(A) and law‑enforcement agencies.

# Chapter 4: Segregability — When Non‑Exempt Information Must Be Released  

In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* the D.C. Circuit was asked to decide whether the FBI had satisfied its statutory duty to “segregate and release” any non‑exempt material that lay hidden in a massive file of roughly 10,750 pages and 80 CDs.  The court’s discussion provides a clear illustration of how segregation operates in practice, what the law requires of an agency, and how a court evaluates the agency’s justification.  This chapter walks you through those points, grounding each rule in the opinion and showing how the judges applied them.

## 4.1 Statutory segregation requirement and its policy rationale  

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) imposes a two‑step duty on agencies once they determine that a record contains exempt material.  First, the agency must identify the exemption that applies.  Second, **U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)** commands the agency to take “reasonable steps” to “segregate and release any reasonably segregable portion of a record” that is *not* covered by the exemption.  The statutory language mirrors the FOIA’s overall policy: the public’s right of access is paramount, but it must be balanced against legitimate government interests in secrecy.  The segregation requirement is the mechanism that preserves that balance.  Instead of a blanket denial, the agency must carve out the non‑exempt “information” and give it to the requester, thereby ensuring that the public does not lose more than is necessary to protect the interest protected by the exemption.

The *Farahi* opinion repeatedly cites § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) and the parallel provision in § 552(b) that “any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided… after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”  The court treats the duty as non‑negotiable: if the agency cannot show that the non‑exempt material is reasonably separable, it must withhold the entire record under the applicable exemption—in this case Exemption 7(A).

## 4.2 What constitutes a “reasonably segregable” portion  

The D.C. Circuit has fashioned a practical definition of “reasonably segregable” through a line of cases that the *Farahi* court relies upon.  Two seminal decisions are instructive:

* **Mead Data Central, Inc. v. Department of the Air Force**, 566 F.2d 242 (D.C. Cir. 1977).  The court held that when exempt material is “inextricably intertwined” with non‑exempt material—so that the two are interleaved line‑by‑line—segregation is not reasonable.  The focus is on *information* rather than the mere presence of any non‑exempt words.  The court said, “the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down” (Mead at 261 n.55).  The implication is that the agency must be able to excise whole sections, pages, or documents that contain only exempt material; isolated sentences or phrases embedded in a larger exempt narrative do not satisfy the requirement.

* **Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. Department of Veterans Affairs**, 57 F.4th 1061 (D.C. Cir. 2023).  This later case reaffirmed the Mead standard and clarified that “reasonable segregation” is judged on a *case‑by‑case* basis, looking at the specific record structure.  The court emphasized that an agency’s claim that a few words can be redacted is insufficient; there must be a “meaningful” portion of the record that can be released without compromising the exempt interest.

In *Farahi*, the district court applied these principles when it concluded that the FBI’s justification—found in Bender’s supplemental ex‑parte filing—demonstrated that exempt material could not be reasonably separated from non‑exempt material.  The court noted that the “protected records were not reasonably segregable” because the exempt content was “interspersed line‑by‑line throughout the document,” echoing Mead and Perioperative.

## 4.3 Agency‑level category‑by‑category analysis  

Before the court evaluates whether the material can be segregated, the agency must “identify distinct categories” of records and explain how each category is treated.  The requirement comes from **Citizens for Responsible & Ethics in Washington v. Department of Justice**, 746 F.3d at 1088 (the “CREW” decision).  CREW held that categories must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each.

The purpose of the rule is two‑fold:

1. **Transparency to the court** – By breaking the file into discrete, labeled groups (e.g., “interrogation transcripts,” “electronic surveillance logs,” “intelligence assessments”), the agency gives the judge a roadmap for evaluating each exemption claim.

2. **Avoiding over‑broad withholding** – If the agency lumps together disparate documents under a single, amorphous category, it can hide non‑exempt material behind the exemption claim.

In *Farahi*, the FBI’s initial FOIA response used categories, but Farahi challenged the adequacy of those categorizations.  The appellate court found that Farahi “forfeited his arguments” because he offered no specific basis to prove the categories were overbroad (citing *Khine v. Department of Homeland Security*, 943 F.3d at 967).  Consequently, the court accepted the FBI’s categorical breakdown as sufficient for the segregation analysis.

## 4.4 The district court’s assessment of the FBI’s segregation justification via Bender’s ex‑parte filing  

When the district court revisited the summary‑judgment motion, it faced two unanswered questions: (1) whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated, and (2) whether any non‑exempt material could be disclosed.  The FBI responded with a *public* declaration from Joseph Bender (Acting Section Chief, RIDS) and a *sealed* ex‑parte declaration that elaborated on the factual basis for both points.

**Key elements of the court’s assessment:**

1. **Reliance on the updated declaration** – The court accepted Bender’s statements that the investigations “remain ongoing” as an *update* to the earlier Hardy declarations, not as the sole foundation for the pending‑proceedings analysis.  Because the district court had already found the Hardy declarations “seemingly” sufficient, Bender’s brief comment was deemed adequate to show that nothing had changed.

2. **Segregability analysis** – The district court turned to Bender’s ex‑parte filing for a “detailed justification” of why the exempt portions could not be separated.  Although the public opinion does not reproduce the sealed content, the court’s order references that the filing explained the *intertwined* nature of the records.  The court found this explanation met the “reasonable specificity” standard required for an agency affidavit (see *Juarez v. Department of Justice*, 518 F.3d at 61).

3. **Application of Mead/Perioperative** – By concluding that the exempt material was “interspersed line‑by‑line,” the court directly applied the two‑step test: (i) identify the exempt content, (ii) determine whether that content can be isolated without destroying the informational value of the remaining material.  The court determined the answer was negative, and therefore the FBI satisfied its statutory duty.

The appellate court affirmed this reasoning, emphasizing that the district court had correctly applied the segregation standard and that the FBI’s filings contained the requisite level of detail.

## 4.5 Limits of segregation when exempt material is interspersed line‑by‑line  

The “line‑by‑line” limitation is not a technical requirement that the exemption be found on a single line of text; rather, it is a conceptual shortcut for asking whether the non‑exempt information can be **meaningfully** extracted.  Courts look for:

* **Structural separability** – Are there whole pages, sections, or documents that contain only non‑exempt facts?  If yes, the agency must release those.
* **Functional separability** – Even if a document mixes exempt and non‑exempt material, can the agency redact the exempt paragraphs while preserving the rest?  If the redactions would produce a document that is “gibberish” or devoid of context, segregation is unreasonable.
* **Practical feasibility** – The agency must consider the burden of segregation.  A request that would require piecemeal redaction of thousands of pages, each with a handful of exempt sentences, is unlikely to meet the “reasonable steps” standard.

In *Farahi*, the district court’s finding that the FBI’s records were “inextricably intertwined” indicates that the agency could not point to any discrete, self‑contained portion that would convey useful information once the exempt material was removed.  The court’s reliance on the sealed Bender declaration suggests that the FBI detailed the technical impossibility of extracting meaningful non‑exempt content—perhaps because the records consist of investigative notebooks, analyst memos, and raw intelligence that are all interdependent.

## 4.6 Consequences of failing to segregate: full withholding under Exemption 7(A)  

When an agency cannot demonstrate reasonable segregation, the FOIA mandates that the entire record be withheld under the applicable exemption.  The statutory text is explicit: “Any reasonably segregable portion… shall be provided… after deletion of the portions which are exempt.”  If nothing is “reasonably segregable,” the deletion step does not produce any material to turn over, and the agency may lawfully retain the whole record.

The *Farahi* court’s judgment illustrates the practical impact:

1. **No partial disclosure** – Even though the FBI’s file spanned 10,750 pages, the court concluded that no “meaningful information” could be released.  Consequently, Farahi received none of the requested documents.

2. **Reaffirmation of exemption scope** – By allowing the FBI to withhold the entire file, the court reinforced the principle that Exemption 7(A) does not merely protect the *existence* of an investigation but also protects any *information* that, if disclosed, would jeopardize that investigation.  The segregation requirement cannot be used to “carve out” non‑exempt trivia if the overall document is so entangled that the exempt core would be compromised by any release.

3. **Judicial deference to agency expertise** – The appellate court highlighted that the FBI’s expertise and the nature of national‑security investigations merit “deference” (citing *Campbell v. Department of Justice*).  This deference is especially salient when the agency must assess the practical feasibility of segregation.

4. **Strategic implication for FOIA litigants** – Plaintiffs must be prepared to challenge the agency’s claim of inseparability.  That means requesting the agency produce detailed explanations—ideally with redacted exemplars—that show precisely why each purportedly non‑exempt portion cannot be isolated.  Without such specificity, the court may find the agency’s assertion insufficient and order partial release.

In sum, the *Farahi* decision demonstrates that the segregation requirement is a substantive hurdle for agencies: they must not only invoke an exemption but also prove that the remaining material can be meaningfully disclosed.  When the record is so interwoven that only a thin layer of exempt text separates the rest, the law permits the agency to withhold the whole file, and the court will uphold that decision so long as the agency’s affidavit meets the “reasonable specificity” standard articulated in *Juarez* and *Mead*.  

---  

**Take‑away points for you, the law student:**  

* Segregation is a *mandatory* step, not a discretion left to the agency.  
* “Reasonably segregable” requires more than a cursory redaction; the non‑exempt portion must retain informational value on its own.  
* Agencies must break the request into distinct categories and explain, category by category, how each is treated.  
* Courts will scrutinize the agency’s affidavit (public and ex‑parte) for sufficient detail; a terse statement is rarely enough unless it builds on earlier, more detailed declarations.  
* If the record fails the segregation test, the entire document may be lawfully withheld under the applicable exemption, as the *Farahi* court affirmed.  

Understanding these principles will equip you to evaluate future FOIA segregation disputes and to craft persuasive arguments—whether you are representing a requesting party or defending an agency’s position.

# Chapter 5: Court’s Reasoning, Holding, and Significance  

In *Farahi v. FBI*, the D.C. Circuit affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the FBI, holding that the agency properly relied on FOIA Exemption 7(A) to withhold the requested records.  The opinion illustrates how a court conducts de novo review of a summary‑judgment ruling, how it applies the two prongs of Exemption 7(A), and why the agency’s segregation analysis was deemed sufficient.  The chapter walks through each of these steps and draws out the lessons for future FOIA litigation, especially where national‑security investigations are at issue.  

## 5.1 De novo standard of review for summary‑judgment rulings in FOIA cases  

FOIA cases are not exempt from the ordinary rules of civil procedure.  When a district court decides a motion for summary judgment, the appellate court “reviews… de novo.”  *Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI*, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021).  De novo review means the appellate court does not give any deference to the lower court’s factual findings; it independently determines whether the moving party has met its burdens under the statute.  

In a FOIA context the moving party is the agency.  Under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B) the agency bears the burden of proving that an exemption applies.  The appellate court therefore asks: (1) did the agency establish that the records fall within the statutory class of exemption?  and (2) did the agency satisfy the procedural obligations attached to that exemption (e.g., segregation, reasonable specificity)?  The D.C. Circuit’s opinion follows this template, re‑examining the FBI’s evidence on each element of Exemption 7(A).  

## 5.2 Application of Exemption 7(A): confirming the “compiled for law‑enforcement purpose” element  

Exemption 7(A) shields “records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” when disclosure could interfere with enforcement proceedings.  The first prong requires a **nexus** between the record and a law‑enforcement function.  The court reiterated that, because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, its own determination that a file was created “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism” is entitled to a “fair measure of deference.”  *Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998).  

The appellate court examined the agency’s public Hardy declaration, which expressly stated that the 10,750 pages and 80 CDs were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism… including pending law‑enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.”  The court found this declaration provided the requisite “rational nexus” between Farahi’s file and the FBI’s law‑enforcement duties.  The decision emphasized that the deference afforded to the FBI does not excuse a bare conclusion; the agency must articulate a factual connection, which it did through Hardy’s description of the investigation’s focus on alleged terrorist recruitment, financing, and connections to known Al Qaeda operatives.  

## 5.3 Holding that enforcement proceedings were pending/anticipated despite the passage of time  

The second prong of Exemption 7(A) asks whether disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.”  The D.C. Circuit has interpreted this requirement to mean that the proceedings must be **pending or reasonably anticipated**.  *Citizens for Responsible & Ethics in Washington v. DOJ*, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014).  

Farahi argued that several years had elapsed since the FBI’s original declaration, making the claim of “ongoing” investigations stale.  The appellate court rejected that argument for three reasons:

1. **Initial showing was sufficient** – The district court had already found that the FBI “seemingly” met its burden to prove pending proceedings.  The later Bender declaration was simply an update, not a new basis for the exemption.  

2. **Ex parte updates are permissible** – The court noted that the government may satisfy the “pending” requirement by “attesting to the existence” of investigations, even in an ex‑parte declaration, so long as the assertion is not a conclusory bare statement.  The Hardy declarations provided the substantive factual detail, while Bender’s brief update confirmed that nothing had changed.  

3. **Breadth of the investigation** – The court pointed to the FBI’s public allegations (recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, links to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed, support for charities linked to terrorism) to infer that the investigation was extensive and likely to continue gathering evidence.  Under *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 59 (D.C. Cir. 2008), an investigation that is still gathering evidence for a possible future criminal case qualifies as a “pending” proceeding.  

Accordingly, the appellate court held that the agency met the “pending or reasonably anticipated” requirement despite the intervening years.  

## 5.4 Acceptance of the agency’s segregation analysis and rejection of plaintiff’s arguments  

Even when an exemption applies, FOIA obligates an agency to “segregate and release any non‑exempt portions” of a record.  5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii).  The district court, and thus the appellate court, examined the FBI’s second‑round submission, which included a supplemental ex‑parte declaration from Acting Section Chief Bender.  The declaration explained why the exempt material was “inextricably intertwined” with the non‑exempt material, satisfying the “reasonable specificity” standard articulated in *Juarez* and *Mead Data Center*.  

Farahi contended that the sheer volume of the file guaranteed the presence of some releasable information.  The court rejected this, citing *Mead*, which holds that scattered, isolated words or phrases are not “reasonably segregable” when they are interspersed throughout a larger, protected document.  The focus of FOIA is on “information,” not on the mere existence of non‑confidential language.  Because the FBI’s filings demonstrated that any non‑exempt content could not be isolated without compromising the protected portions, the court upheld the agency’s segregation analysis.  

## 5.5 The final affirmation and its impact on future FOIA requests involving ongoing counter‑terrorism investigations  

The appellate court affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment, concluding that the FBI had satisfied both prongs of Exemption 7(A) and had met its procedural obligations to attempt segregation.  The decision underscores several important points for future FOIA litigation:

* **De novo review does not abandon deference** – While the appellate court conducts its own analysis, it still accords the agency “fair measure of deference” where the agency’s expertise in law‑enforcement matters is at issue.  

* **Temporal decay is not fatal** – An agency may rely on earlier, detailed declarations to establish pending proceedings, with later, brief updates sufficing to show that the status has not changed.  

* **Segregation can be denied where the documents are “inextricably intertwined.”**  The burden shifts to the agency to demonstrate with reasonable specificity why separation is impracticable; a detailed affidavit can satisfy that burden.  

* **National‑security investigations receive heightened deference** – The court emphasized that “the principle of deference to the executive… is particularly appropriate for records implicating national security.”  This reinforces the high hurdle plaintiffs face when seeking disclosure of counter‑terrorism files.  

## 5.6 Practical takeaways for agencies: importance of detailed, updated declarations and clear category definitions  

From an agency perspective, *Farahi* offers a roadmap for defending FOIA withholdings in sensitive investigations:

1. **Begin with a thorough, fact‑rich declaration** – The Hardy declarations supplied a narrative linking the records to a specific law‑enforcement investigation, satisfying the “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” prong.  

2. **Provide periodic updates** – When a case drags on, a brief ex‑parte declaration confirming that the investigation remains ongoing can preserve the exemption without re‑loving the entire factual record.  

3. **Address segregability head‑on** – An affidavit that explains why non‑exempt material is “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material meets the statutory segregation requirement.  The affidavit should cite specific passages, document structures, or investigative sensitivities that make separation impracticable.  

4. **Define categories distinctly** – Although Farahi waived his objection to the FBI’s categorization scheme, the court reminded that categories must be “sufficiently distinct” to allow judicial review.  Agencies should therefore craft narrow, logical categories and provide a brief rationale for each.  

5. **Document the existence of pending or anticipated proceedings** – Even a concise statement that “investigations remain ongoing” is adequate if it is backed by earlier, detailed declarations establishing the nature and scope of the investigation.  

By following these practices, agencies can better withstand appellate scrutiny and reduce the likelihood of protracted FOIA disputes.  

---  

In sum, the D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Farahi* demonstrates that while FOIA’s “presumption of openness” remains a cornerstone of government transparency, courts will rigorously apply Exemption 7(A) when national‑security investigations are at stake.  The de novo standard obliges reviewers to re‑evaluate the agency’s evidence, but the deference afforded to law‑enforcement agencies, especially in the counter‑terrorism realm, means that a well‑drafted, fact‑supported declaration can successfully shield an entire voluminous record from disclosure.
