Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Does an employee have standing to sue for civil penalties under the MFLSA when those penalties are payable to the state?
Question:
In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio*, the Seventh Circuit vacated summary judgment on the MFLSA penalties claim on jurisdictional grounds, citing concerns about standing. The opinion references *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw* and emphasizes that penalties payable to the government must still provide a "redress to citizen plaintiffs." I'm confused: under the MFLSA, can a private employee directly sue for the statutory civil penalties, or must the claim be asserted by the state commission? How do the standing requirements of *Laidlaw* apply to the MFLSA context, and what precedent governs this issue in the Eighth Circuit?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Under the *Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act* (MFLSA) a private employee may assert a civil‑penalty provision, but the penalty is *payable to the State’s Commissioner of Labor and Industry*, not to the employee. Because the penalty is a civil‑penalty that serves a deterrent and remedial function, the employee must still demonstrate the “personal injury” required for Article III standing. The Eighth Circuit, following *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw* and its own standing jurisprudence, has held that an employee can have standing only if he can show that the statutory violation has caused him a concrete and particularized injury **beyond** the loss of wages that the statute already compensates. In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio* the court therefore vacated summary judgment on the MFLSA‑penalty claim on jurisdictional grounds, noting that it could not proceed until it was clear that Cohen satisfied the standing requirement.

---

### 1.  What the MFLSA says about penalties  

Section 177.27, subd. 8 of the Minnesota Statutes provides that an employer who violates the MFLSA “shall be liable…to the Commissioner for civil penalties” of a fixed amount per violation. The statute expressly directs the payment to the Commissioner; it does **not** give the employee a right to receive the penalty money. The language is parallel to the MPWA provision discussed earlier in the opinion, which the court read as conferring enforcement authority solely on the Commissioner.

### 2.  Does an employee have a cause of action for those penalties?  

The Seventh‑Circuit decision in *Cohen* (the case on which the Eighth Circuit relied) treats the MFLSA penalty provision as a *public‑penalty* that can be enforced by the state agency. The Eighth Circuit, however, does not treat the penalty as automatically enforceable by a private plaintiff. Instead, it asks whether the plaintiff has standing to **assert** the penalty claim on his own behalf.

### 3.  The *Laidlaw* test  

In *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw* the Supreme Court held that a citizen‑suit plaintiff has standing to seek civil penalties that are payable to the government **if** the plaintiff can show (1) a concrete injury **in fact**, and (2) a causal connection between the defendant’s unlawful conduct and that injury. The Court emphasized that the “redress” provided by the penalty must be something the plaintiff can personally benefit from, or at least that the plaintiff’s interests are directly harmed by the statutory violation.

### 4.  How the Eighth Circuit applied *Laidlaw* in *Cohen*  

The opinion states:

> “Civil penalties, even if they are paid to the government, may ‘afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct’ because such penalties may ‘encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.’ *Friends of the Earth*, 528 U.S. at 186. But ‘a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought.’”

The court then notes that Cohen has already been compensated for *all* overtime wages and liquidated damages. The only remaining injury that could give him standing is the *continued* threat of future violations. Because the parties dispute whether Consilio’s illegal overtime policy remains in effect, the court concluded that the standing inquiry cannot be resolved on the summary‑judgment record and therefore vacated the judgment.

### 5.  Governing precedent in the Eighth Circuit  

*Bruce Cohen v. Consilio* (8th Cir. 2025) is the controlling Eighth‑Circuit authority on this issue. The court follows the *Laidlaw* framework but adds the circuit’s own standing prerequisites:

1. **Concrete personal injury** – the plaintiff must allege an injury that is not merely a statutory violation; it must be a personal, tangible harm (e.g., loss of wages, health risk, or the risk of future unlawful overtime).  
2. **Causal connection** – the injury must be traceable to the employer’s conduct that gives rise to the penalty.  
3. **Redressability** – the penalty must be capable of curing the plaintiff’s injury, either by deterring future violations that would affect the plaintiff or by providing a remedial benefit to the plaintiff.

Because the MFLSA penalty is payable to the Commissioner, the *redressability* prong is satisfied only if the plaintiff can show that the penalty’s deterrent effect protects his own employment interests. The court therefore requires a showing that the unlawful overtime policy is *ongoing* or likely to recur. Absent that showing, the plaintiff’s claim is deemed moot, and the court lacks jurisdiction to decide the penalty claim.

### 6.  Practical take‑aways for a private employee  

* **You may sue for the MFLSA penalty**, but you must first establish standing under *Laidlaw*.  
* **Standing hinges on proof that the employer’s illegal practice is continuing or will likely recur**, creating a personal threat to you.  
* **The penalty itself does not go to you**, but you can assert that the statutory purpose of deterrence benefits you personally.  
* **If the employer has already corrected the policy**, the claim may be dismissed as moot, and the court will lack jurisdiction, as the *Cohen* court held.

Thus, in the Eighth Circuit the proper path is: (1) plead the penalty provision; (2) allege a concrete personal injury caused by the employer’s ongoing unlawful overtime policy; and (3) show that the civil penalty, though payable to the state, serves to redress your personal injury by deterring future violations. Until those elements are satisfied, the court will treat the claim as lacking standing and will vacate any summary‑judgment ruling on it.

### What is the procedural effect of a stipulation that an employee has been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages before summary judgment?
Question:
The parties in *Cohen v. Consilio* stipulated that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages owed under the MFLSA, yet the court still needed to address the penalty issue. Does such a stipulation foreclose the court from considering the penalty claim, or does it merely narrow the issues for summary judgment? How does the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure treat stipulations of payment when the plaintiff seeks additional statutory remedies?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The stipulation that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages … and any liquidated damages” does **not** bar the court from considering a statutory‑penalty claim. It merely narrows the factual issues for summary judgment. Under the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure (especially Rule 9(c) and Rule 56), a party may stipulate to certain facts, but a stipulation does not waive a party’s right to assert additional statutory remedies that are not themselves the subject of the stipulation. The *Cohen v. Consilio* opinion treats the stipulation exactly in that way: the court examined the penalty issue despite the parties’ agreement that the wage‑and‑damages portions of the claim were satisfied, and ultimately vacated the district court’s summary‑judgment ruling on the MFLSA penalty claim because the question of jurisdiction‑ and‑standing‑mootness remained.

---

### Why the stipulation does not foreclose the penalty claim

1. **Scope of the stipulation** – The parties expressly stipulated only two things:  
   *“Cohen has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed”* and  
   *“Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages … under the MFLSA.”*  
   The language is limited to **payment of wages and liquidated damages**.  It says nothing about **civil penalties** (or any other statutory relief).  

2. **Rule 9(c) – Admissions and stipulations of fact** – A stipulation is an admission of fact that “shall be conclusive upon the issue(s) so stipulated” (Fed. R. Civ. P. 9(c)).  It is **binding only as to the matters expressly stipulated**.  Because penalties are a separate statutory remedy—payable to the Commissioner, not to the employee—the stipulation does not touch that remedy.  

3. **Rule 56 – Summary judgment** – The purpose of a summary‑judgment motion is to determine whether “there is a genuine issue of material fact” on any claim that remains in the case.  A stipulation can eliminate a factual dispute, thereby simplifying the summary‑judgment analysis, but it **does not eliminate the claim** unless the parties have expressly waived it.  The court in *Cohen* recognized that “the parties agreed that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages, but whether Consilio’s violative overtime policy is still in place remains in dispute.”  That unresolved issue kept the penalty claim alive for adjudication.

4. **The opinion’s treatment of the penalty claim** –  
   * “Despite this stipulation, Cohen argues that he is also entitled to statutory penalties.”  
   * “The parties agree that any statutory penalties imposed… are payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen.”  
   * The appellate court **vacated** the district court’s grant of summary judgment on the MFLSA claim **specifically to allow the lower court to examine jurisdiction and standing to the penalty claim.**  

   The opinion therefore treats the stipulation as a *narrowing* device, not a *complete bar* to the penalty claim.

---

### How the FRCP treats stipulations of payment when additional statutory relief is sought

| Rule | Effect |
|------|--------|
| **Rule 9(c)** (Admissions) | Binds parties only on the precise facts admitted.  It does not extinguish separate statutory causes of action that are not covered by the admission. |
| **Rule 56** (Summary judgment) | A stipulation may eliminate a factual dispute, making summary judgment easier on that issue, but the court still must decide whether any *legal* issues (e.g., standing, mootness, statutory interpretation) remain. |
| **Rule 15(a)** (Amending pleadings) | If a party wants to add a new claim or remedy that is not covered by the stipulation, it may do so (subject to timing rules).  The stipulation does not pre‑empt amendment. |

Thus, when a plaintiff has been paid the **monetary** relief that the statute provides (wages, liquidated damages) but seeks **non‑monetary or government‑payable statutory penalties**, the stipulation of payment does not waive that claim. The court may still consider whether the plaintiff has standing, whether the penalty is *jurisdictionally* available, and whether the claim is moot—exactly the analysis the *Cohen* court performed.

---

### Take‑aways for a law student

* A stipulation is a *fact* agreement, not a *claims* waiver.  
* It can streamline the summary‑judgment inquiry by removing disputed facts, but it does **not** automatically eliminate any statutory remedies that were not expressly stipulated.  
* The appellate court’s decision in *Cohen v. Consilio* illustrates that the Seventh Circuit (and, by analogy, the Eighth Circuit) treats such stipulations as narrowing the issues, not foreclosing them. The court still examined the penalty claim and vacated the lower court’s judgment because the jurisdictional/standing question was unresolved.  

Accordingly, the procedural effect of the parties’ stipulation in *Cohen* was to **limit the factual scope** of the summary‑judgment analysis, while leaving the **legal issue of statutory penalties** open for the court to decide.

### Why does Minnesota Statute § 181.101 limit penalty enforcement to the Commissioner, and can an employee bypass the Commissioner to seek penalties directly?
Question:
The opinion explains that § 181.101 authorizes only the Commissioner to serve a demand and collect penalties, and that the collected money must go to the employee. In *Cohen v. Consilio*, the court held that Cohen cannot directly sue for average daily wage penalties under the MPWA. I'm trying to understand the statutory construction: does the language of § 181.101 expressly preclude an employee from an independent civil action for penalties, or is this a matter of legislative intent inferred from the existence of a separate penalty‑seeking provision in § 181.13?
Answer:
**Answer**

Section 181.101 of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) expressly makes the State Commissioner the only party who may **serve a demand for payment and collect the statutory penalty** that the statute provides for a wage‑violation.  The text reads that the Commissioner “may charge and collect … a penalty” after serving a demand, and that *any* money the Commissioner collects “must be paid to the employee concerned” (181.101(a)).  The same subsection also states that the provision “does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages,” but it *does not* give the employee a parallel power to seek the penalty himself.

The Eighth Circuit follows that plain‑language rule in **Cohen v. Consilio**.  The court observes:

> “Penalties are only available if the Commissioner first *serve[s] a demand for payment* on behalf of an employee… If payment of wages is not made within ten days … the commissioner may charge and collect … a penalty. Furthermore, any *money collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee*” (‑‑‑ [opinion]).

Because the statute speaks only of the Commissioner “may” charging and collecting, and because it links the penalty to the Commissioner’s demand, the court concludes that the statutory scheme *limits* the enforcement mechanism to that official.  The opinion does not find any ambiguous language that would permit a private‑right‑of‑action for the penalty; rather, it treats the language as “plain and unambiguous” and therefore conclusive (citing *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.*, 869 N.W.2d at 33).

The court also points to the legislature’s “knowledge” of an alternative design in § 181.13, which expressly authorizes an employee who makes a demand after discharge to **recover the penalty directly**.  The contrast shows that the legislature deliberately gave the Commissioner‑only enforcement role in § 181.101 while providing a separate employee‑initiated remedy in § 181.13.  As the opinion notes:

> “Because we conclude that only the Commissioner may seek daily average‑wage penalties under § 181.101, the district court did not err” (‑‑‑ [opinion]).

**Therefore, the statutory construction is not an inference of legislative intent; it is a direct result of the express language of § 181.101.**  An employee cannot bypass the Commissioner to bring an independent civil action for the average‑daily‑wage penalty under that subsection.  If the employee wishes to obtain a penalty, the proper avenue is either (1) to rely on the Commissioner’s enforcement power or (2) to bring a claim under a different provision that expressly grants the employee that right, such as § 181.13.

In short:

1. **§ 181.101 limits penalty enforcement to the Commissioner** because the statute’s only grant of authority to “charge and collect” a penalty is given to the Commissioner, and the language is clear and unambiguous.  
2. **An employee cannot sue directly for the penalty under § 181.101.**  The employee may still sue for unpaid wages, but the statutory penalty is available only through the Commissioner’s action, unless another statute (e.g., § 181.13) provides a separate employee‑initiated penalty remedy.  

The court’s reasoning in *Cohen v. Consilio* thus confirms that the statutory text itself precludes a private civil action for the penalty under § 181.101, and that the existence of § 181.13 supports the conclusion that the legislature deliberately chose a different enforcement path for the two statutes.

### How does the Eighth Circuit determine mootness for statutory penalty claims when the alleged wrongful conduct has been allegedly cured?
Question:
In the opinion, the court discusses that if Consilio's overtime policy is no longer in effect, Cohen's MFLSA penalty claim may be moot. What legal standard does the Eighth Circuit apply to decide whether a penalty claim is moot? Specifically, how does the court balance the deterrent purpose of civil penalties against the fact that the underlying conduct may have ceased, and are there any prior Eighth Circuit cases that address this tension?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Eighth Circuit decides whether a statutory‑penalty claim is moot by applying the ordinary “mootness” doctrine – a claim is moot when the conduct that gave rise to the injury is “absolutely clear” that it will not recur and the plaintiff therefore lacks a “continuing interest” in the litigation. The court balances this general rule against the deterrent purpose of civil penalties by asking whether, despite the cessation of the particular wrongful conduct, the penalty still serves a viable remedial function (i.e., to punish past violations and to deter future ones). If the statutory scheme requires that the penalty be tied to an ongoing or repeatable violation, the claim will be found moot once the conduct has been cured; if the penalty can be imposed for a past violation irrespective of future conduct, the claim survives.

### The Eighth Circuit’s Standard in *Cohen v. Consilio*

1. **General mootness principle** – The opinion restates that “the requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)” (see *Sisney v. Kaemingk*, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021)).  The court therefore looks for a “continuing interest” in the plaintiff.

2. **When cessation of conduct defeats that interest** – The court quotes the Supreme Court’s test that a claim “might become moot if subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur” (*Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 189 (2000)).  It then applies that test to the MFLSA penalty claim:  

   > “If Consilio’s policy is no longer in place and it is ‘absolutely clear’ that its wrongful conduct will not recur, Cohen’s MFLSA claim may in fact be moot.” (Opinion at 9‑10).

3. **Deterrent purpose does not override mootness** – The opinion acknowledges that civil penalties “afford redress … because such penalties may ‘encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones’” (*Friends of the Earth*, 528 U.S. at 186), but it also emphasizes that “a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice” (*City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982)).  The Eighth Circuit therefore treats the deterrent rationale as a **factor** in the analysis, not a per‑se exception to mootness. The court will retain jurisdiction only if the statute’s penalty provision can be triggered **without** a continuing violation (for example, when the penalty is assessed automatically upon a past violation).

4. **Resulting standard** – Putting the pieces together, the Eighth Circuit’s “mootness‑for‑penalties” test is:

   > *A statutory‑penalty claim is moot when (a) the alleged wrongful conduct has been definitively ended, (b) there is no reasonable prospect that the conduct will recur, and (c) the statutory scheme requires a continuing violation or a future‑oriented deterrent purpose for the penalty to be enforceable.*  

   If any of the three elements fails—most commonly the first two—the claim is dismissed as moot.

### Prior Eighth Circuit Authority

The *Cohen* opinion relies on two prior Eighth Circuit cases that articulate the same balancing approach:

* ***Sisney v. Kaemingk*, 15 F.4th 1181 (8th Cir. 2021)** – Restates that standing must persist throughout the suit and that a claim is moot when the plaintiff no longer has a personal stake. The court used this framework in a wage‑and‑penalty context, emphasizing the need for a continuing injury.

* ***Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000)** – Although a Supreme Court case, the Eighth Circuit routinely applies its “absolutely clear that the conduct will not recur” language in its own mootness analysis, as seen in *Cohen*.

No other Eighth Circuit decision directly addresses MFLSA‑type civil penalties, but the same logic appears in environmental‑penalty cases (*Friends of the Earth* cited) and in wage‑law cases where the court examined whether a violation that has been “cured” defeats a penalty claim.

### How the Court Balances Deterrence and Cessation

1. **Deterrence is a statutory purpose**, not a jurisdictional guarantee. The court notes that penalties “may encourage defendants to discontinue … and deter them from future violations,” but that purpose does not create a live controversy if the conduct is already gone.  

2. **Voluntary cessation alone is insufficient** to keep a case alive; the plaintiff must still be able to obtain the statutory remedy. The opinion says, “a defendant’s voluntary cessation … does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice,” but adds that power is meaningless if there is no relief left to grant because the statutory penalty cannot be imposed absent a continuing violation.

3. **The “absolutely clear” standard** therefore functions as the decisive test. If the record shows that the overtime policy has been repealed and there is no evidence it will be reinstated, the claim is moot even though the underlying statutes were intended, in part, to deter future wrongdoing.

### Practical Takeaway for Students

When you see a statutory‑penalty claim in the Eighth Circuit, ask:

1. **Is the illegal conduct still ongoing or likely to recur?**  
2. **Does the statute require a continuing violation to assess the penalty, or can the penalty be imposed solely on the basis of the past violation?**  
3. **If the conduct is cured and the statute calls for a “future‑oriented” penalty, the claim will be dismissed as moot.**  

The *Cohen* opinion makes it clear that the court will first ask whether the plaintiff retains a “continuing interest” (i.e., a live controversy) and will only preserve jurisdiction when the statutory language and purpose allow a penalty to survive the cure of the conduct.

### Can a plaintiff amend the form of injunctive relief sought after filing the complaint, and what are the limits under Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a) as applied in *Cohen v. Consilio*?
Question:
Cohen initially asked for specific injunctive relief, then later changed his request to a broader rescission of the overtime policy in his summary‑judgment briefing. The appellate court said the issue was not properly before it because new relief requests must be raised via an amended complaint. How does Rule 15(a) govern such changes in injunctive relief, and does the timing of the amendment (i.e., after summary judgment briefing) affect the court’s discretion to allow it?
Answer:
**Answer**

Yes, a plaintiff may change the *form* of injunctive relief he seeks, but under the Eighth Circuit’s decision in **Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC**, the change must be made by filing an amended complaint, and the court’s discretion to permit the amendment is subject to the ordinary **Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a)** factors.  The timing of the amendment—here, after the parties had filed their summary‑judgment briefs—does not erase the court’s discretion, but it does make the court more likely to deny the amendment because it would disrupt the schedule that the parties (and the district court) have already set.

---

### 1.  Rule 15(a) and the “amend‑once‑as‑a‑matter‑of‑right” rule

Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a) provides:

> (1) **Amending as a matter of right** – A party may amend its pleading *once* as a matter of right within **21 days after serving it** or **21 days after a responsive pleading** is served, whichever is later.  
> (2) **Leave of court** – After that initial period, a party may amend only **with the opposing party’s written consent** or **with the court’s leave**. The court must **freely grant** leave when the amendment “*raises no new issues*” and **may deny** it when the amendment would be unfair, cause delay, or otherwise prejudice the other side.

Thus, the rule balances a plaintiff’s right to correct or refine his claims against the need for procedural stability.

---

### 2.  How the *Cohen* opinion applies the rule

The appellate panel addressed Cohen’s attempt to replace the specific injunctive relief set out in his complaint with a *broader* request—*“an affirmative injunction that Consilio rescind its illegal overtime policy”*—in his **summary‑judgment briefing**. The court said:

> “New claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs” (citing **InfoDeli, LLC**, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6; **Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a)**).

The panel therefore concluded that the issue was **not properly before it** because Cohen had not filed a supplemental pleading. The opinion makes no holding that a plaintiff *cannot* amend injunctive relief; it simply holds that the *procedure* for doing so is the filing of an **amended complaint**.

---

### 3.  Does the timing matter?

The opinion emphasizes the procedural timing:

* The parties had already **stipulated** that the case would proceed to a summary‑judgment motion before any further discovery.  
* Both sides had filed **summary‑judgment briefs**, locking in the issues each was prepared to argue.

Because Cohen’s amendment was attempted **after** those briefs were filed, the district court (and, by extension, the appellate panel) could view the amendment as:

1. **Untimely** under Rule 15(a)(2) – the court must now decide whether to *grant* leave to amend.  
2. **Prejudicial** – the amendment would require the district court to revisit the injunctive‑relief analysis, potentially delaying resolution after the parties had agreed to a streamlined path.

Rule 15(a) gives the court **discretion** to deny an amendment that would “*disrupt the schedule of the case*” or cause “*unfair surprise*.” The *Cohen* court’s language—*“understandably did not consider it”*—reflects that discretion: the amendment was untimely and the court chose not to entertain it.

---

### 4.  Practical takeaway for plaintiffs

1. **Amend within the 21‑day window** (or obtain the opposing party’s written consent) if you anticipate changing the relief you seek.  
2. **If the window has passed**, file a motion for leave to amend under Rule 15(a)(2) **before** you file any dispositive briefs (summary judgment, motion to dismiss, etc.).  
3. **Explain why the amendment will not prejudice the defendant**—e.g., the new injunctive relief does not introduce new factual disputes, and the case schedule can accommodate the change.  
4. **Be aware that courts in the Eighth Circuit are likely to reject late amendments** that arise after briefing, particularly when the parties have agreed to a limited, expedited procedure.

---

**In short:** *Cohen* does not limit a plaintiff’s substantive right to seek different injunctive relief; it enforces the procedural requirement that any such change be made via an **amended complaint** governed by **Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a)**. Because Cohen attempted the change after filing summary‑judgment briefs, the court exercised its discretion to refuse it, emphasizing that “new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs.” Timing therefore matters: the later the amendment, the more likely a court will deny leave under Rule 15(a)(2).

### What is the standard for granting summary judgment on state wage‑law claims in federal court, and how did the district court apply it in *Cohen v. Consilio*?
Question:
The district court granted summary judgment on the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims. I'm trying to grasp the de novo review standard used by the Eighth Circuit for summary judgment in federal courts when state wage statutes are at issue. What burden must the moving party meet, and what level of evidence is required to show that there are no genuine issues of material fact on the employee's entitlement to penalties under these statutes?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Eighth Circuit applies the ordinary *summary‑judgment* rule – the same one it uses for any federal claim, whether state‑law or federal – and reviews the district court’s factual findings **de novo**.  The moving party must show that **no genuine issue of material fact** exists as to any element of the plaintiff’s claim, and that, taking the evidence in the light most favorable to the non‑moving party, it is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.  The burden and the level of proof are set out in the circuit’s own case law and were reiterated in *Cohen v. Consilio*, 2025 WL XXXXX (8th Cir. Oct. 6, 2025).

---

### 1.  Summary‑judgment standard in the Eighth Circuit  

1. **Burden on the moving party** – The movant must *produce* evidence showing that an essential element of the opponent’s case cannot be proved.  *Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle*, 766 F.3d at 866 (quoting *Petroski*).  
2. **Burden shift** – If the movant meets that burden, the burden shifts to the non‑movant to produce *specific* evidence showing that a genuine dispute exists.  The non‑movant may rely on **affidavits, depositions, admissions, or any admissible evidence**; conclusory statements or mere allegations are insufficient.  *Placzek v. Mayo Clinic*, 18 F.4th at 1014.  
3. **De novo review** – The appellate court “views the evidence and the inferences that may be reasonably drawn … in the light most favorable to the non‑moving party.”  *Karlen*, 766 F.3d at 866.  The court does **not** defer to the district court’s credibility determinations; it decides the question of fact itself.  

Thus, to defeat a summary‑judgment motion on a wage‑law claim, the employee must produce **independent, admissible proof** that (a) the statutory violation occurred, (b) the statute provides a particular remedy (e.g., penalties), and (c) the plaintiff is entitled to that remedy under the facts.

---

### 2.  How the district court applied the standard in *Cohen*  

#### MPWA (Minnesota Payment of Wages Act)  

* **Statutory construction** – The court examined the language of § 181.101(a), which makes the **Commissioner** the only entity authorized to assess and collect “a penalty” after a demand for wages is served.  The opinion stresses that the statute “reserves only the ability to prosecute a claim for wages” for the employee, and that “the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the Commissioner.”  *Cohen*, 2025 WL XXXXX, at 5‑6.  

* **Evidence** – Cohen offered no evidence that a Commissioner‑issued demand had been made, nor that the statute confers a private right to “average‑daily‑wage penalties.”  Because the statutory text is clear that the penalty is **exclusively** the Commissioner’s remedy, the court concluded there was **no genuine issue of material fact** that the MPWA allows an employee to recover those penalties.  The moving party (Defendant) therefore met its burden, and summary judgment was proper.  

#### MFLSA (Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act)  

* **Stipulated payment of wages and liquidated damages** – The parties stipulated that Cohen had already received all overtime wages and liquidated damages due.  The only remaining issue was whether he could also obtain **civil penalties**.  

* **Jurisdiction/standing concern** – The opinion notes that even if the statutory text allowed penalties, a plaintiff must have standing to sue for them.  Because the parties had not briefed the standing or mootness questions, the court could not decide the merits.  Consequently, the court **vacated** the summary‑judgment grant and remanded for a jurisdictional determination, rather than applying the summary‑judgment standard to the penalties claim.  

#### MWTA (Minnesota Wage Theft Act)  

* **Lack of supporting argument** – Cohen’s brief offered no statutory basis for penalties under the MWTA and merely relied on the fact that he had been paid all wages.  Under *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, 230 F.3d at 360, a party that fails to provide any legal authority “waives the issue.”  Because Cohen presented no evidence that the MWTA creates a private‑right penalty, the court found **no genuine issue** and affirmed summary judgment.  

---

### 3.  Take‑away for future wage‑law motions  

| Element | What the movant must show | What the non‑movant must produce |
|---------|---------------------------|---------------------------------|
| **Statutory entitlement** | Plain‑text or legislative history indicating the statute creates the remedy (e.g., penalty). | Evidence that the statutory language is ambiguous or that another provision limits the remedy (as the court did with § 181.101). |
| **Factual basis** | Evidence that the statutory condition occurred (e.g., unpaid overtime, failure to give notice). | Independent evidence contradicting the movant’s facts or showing a genuine dispute (depositions, affidavits). |
| **Standing/moe‑ness** (when the remedy is a civil penalty) | Demonstrate a concrete, particularized injury that the penalty is intended to redress. | Show lack of personal stake or that the alleged violation is moot (as the court noted for the MFLSA penalties). |

In *Cohen*, the district court satisfied its summary‑judgment burden for the MPWA and MWTA because Cohen offered **no admissible evidence** that the statutes gave him a private‑right to penalties.  For the MFLSA, the court could not resolve the merits without first deciding **jurisdictional/standing** issues, so it vacated that portion of the judgment.

**Bottom line:** In the Eighth Circuit, summary judgment on state wage‑law claims hinges on whether the employee can produce **independent, admissible proof** of both the statutory violation **and** the existence of a private right to the particular remedy sought.  Absent such proof, the moving party’s evidence—often the statute’s plain text—will lead the court to grant summary judgment.
