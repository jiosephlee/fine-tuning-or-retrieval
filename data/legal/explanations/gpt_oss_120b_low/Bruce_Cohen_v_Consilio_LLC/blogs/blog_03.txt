Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

## The Perils of Stipulating “All Claims Resolved” Before Summary Judgment: Lessons from *Cohen v. Consilio*

**Why this case matters**  
Wage‑and‑hour disputes are fertile ground for hefty statutory penalties, liquidated damages, and injunctive relief. In the Eighth Circuit’s October 2025 decision in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the parties—by mutual stipulation—agreed that Cohen had already been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages he claimed. The district court, taking that stipulation at face value, sailed through the summary‑judgment (SJ) phase on the overtime‑wage claims and left the more speculative penalty and injunctive issues for later. On appeal, the circuit affirmed the SJ on the wage‑wage claims but vacated and remanded the judgment on the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) penalties, citing unresolved jurisdiction and standing concerns.  

The outcome underscores a strategic hazard: stipulating that “all claims are resolved” can truncate the factual record on issues that remain genuinely in dispute—particularly statutory penalties and equitable relief. Litigators who rely on such stipulations without adequate safeguards may find themselves facing unexpected remands, wasted resources, and, in some cases, exposure to liability that could have been avoided with clearer drafting.

Below, I walk through the court’s reasoning, flag the doctrinal pitfalls, and offer practical guidance for drafting stipulations in wage‑and‑hour cases (and beyond).

---

## 1. The court’s anatomy of the stipulation

### 1.1. A “clean‑sheet” for overtime wages  

The parties’ stipulation that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages was uncontroversial. Both sides even submitted a payment schedule documenting the $256,010.01 Consilio paid to Cohen and his co‑workers. Because the dispute centered on whether any *additional* overtime compensation existed, the district court felt no need to probe further and granted SJ on the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA wage‑wage claims.

### 1.2. Penalties left in limbo  

Crucially, the stipulation said nothing about *penalties*—the statutory damages that, under Minnesota law, flow to the state commissioner (MPWA) or the Commonwealth (MFLSA). Cohen argued that he was entitled to average‑daily‑wage penalties under the MPWA and to the MFLSA’s $42,000 penalty provision. Consilio countered that the statutes did not create a private right to those penalties. The district court, perhaps buoyed by the “all claims resolved” language, dismissed the penalty issues without detailed analysis, reasoning that the parties had agreed no further relief remained.

### 1.3. Injunctive relief never reached the table  

Cohen’s injunctive request—to rescind the illegal overtime policy—was likewise sidelined. He shifted his sought relief between the complaint and the SJ brief, and the district court, noting the procedural irregularity, declined to address it. The appellate court agreed the issue was not properly before it.

---

## 2. The Eighth Circuit’s appellate reversal

### 2.1. Standing and jurisdiction for penalties  

Judge Kelly’s opinion treats the MFLSA penalty claim as a classic standing problem. Even though penalties are payable to the state, the plaintiff must still demonstrate a personal stake. The court leaned on *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw* to show that civil penalties can serve a remedial purpose, but it also emphasized that standing is *facts‑driven*. Because the parties had not resolved whether Consilio’s policy remained in effect, the court could not determine whether Cohen’s injury was ongoing—an essential element of standing. Consequently, the district court’s SJ on the penalty claim was vacated, and the case sent back for a jurisdictional determination.

### 2.2. The MPWA penalty analysis survived  

On the MPWA side, the court applied a straightforward textualist approach: § 181.101 authorizes the commissioner—not the employee—to seek daily‑average‑wage penalties. The court noted the Legislature’s ability to grant private penalties in other statutes (e.g., §§ 181.13, 181.14) and concluded that the absence of such language in § 181.101 indicates a clear intent to limit enforcement to the commissioner. The district court’s SJ therefore stood.

### 2.3. The injunctive claim left untouched  

Because Cohen never properly amended his complaint to articulate a concrete injunctive request, the appellate court declined to rescue the claim. This illustrates the procedural trap: stipulations cannot conjure a claim that was never properly before the court.

---

## 3. Doctrinal implications

### 3.1. Stipulations are not a “blanket‑kill” for all relief  

The *Cohen* decision reaffirms that a stipulation that “all claims are resolved” is only as strong as the language it contains. Courts will not assume that parties intended to foreclose *all* potential relief, especially when statutory penalties or equitable remedies are on the table. The Eighth Circuit’s willingness to vacate the SJ on the MFLSA penalties shows that appellate courts will scrutinize whether a stipulation truly eliminates the *jurisdictional* basis for a claim.

### 3.2. The “private‑penalty” distinction  

Minnesota’s wage‑law framework creates a bifurcation: some statutes empower employees to sue for penalties (e.g., the MWTA’s § 181.032), while others vest enforcement exclusively in the commissioner (e.g., § 181.101). Courts will look to the *plain language* and *legislative history* to determine the intended private right. Practitioners must therefore tailor their stipulations to the specific statutory scheme—blanket language can backfire when the statute’s penalty mechanism is ambiguous.

### 3.3. Standing remains fact‑intensive even for “government‑paid” penalties  

The appellate analysis underscores that a plaintiff’s personal stake is not automatically satisfied because the penalty is payable to the state. The plaintiff must show a concrete injury—typically the continued risk of illegal conduct or the loss of a statutory right. This aligns with the Supreme Court’s standing doctrine post‑*Lujan* and *Friends of the Earth*. Litigants should be prepared to prove ongoing exposure, or else risk a mootness or jurisdictional dismissal.

---

## 4. Practical takeaways for litigators

1. **Draft stipulations with surgical precision**  
   - Specify exactly which heads of relief are *settled* (e.g., “all overtime wages and liquidated damages”).  
   - Explicitly *exclude* penalties, injunctive claims, or any other statutory remedies you intend to keep alive.  
   - Include a clause stating that the stipulation does not waive any right to seek statutory penalties unless the parties expressly agree to that term.

2. **Anticipate statutory nuance**  
   - When negotiating a settlement in a wage‑and‑hour case, identify which statutes generate private penalties and which do not.  
   - If the employer wishes to avoid penalties, consider a separate “penalty waiver” provision, acknowledging the statutory source and the parties’ intent.

3. **Preserve the factual record for penalties**  
   - Even if you stipulate that wages are paid, retain discovery on the *policy* itself—whether the illegal overtime scheme remains in effect.  
   - Courts can deem a penalty claim moot only when the offending conduct is *clearly* ceased; vague or future‑policy language leaves the door open for further litigation.

4. **Handle injunctive relief early**  
   - If you seek an injunction, file an amended complaint *before* moving for SJ.  
   - Avoid “flip‑flopping” on the requested form of relief; consistency signals to the court that the injunction is an integral part of the claim, not an afterthought.

5. **Plan for appellate review**  
   - Expect that appellate courts will scrutinize any “all claims resolved” stipulation for *overbreadth*.  
   - Keep a contemporaneous docket of the parties’ negotiations, especially any side‑letter agreements, to demonstrate the parties’ actual intent if the matter reaches an appellate bench.

---

## 5. Looking ahead

The *Cohen* decision may prompt both employers and employee‑representing firms to revisit their settlement templates. In jurisdictions with similar statutory structures—Illinois, New York, California—practitioners should expect parallel disputes over the scope of private penalties and the adequacy of stipulations. Moreover, as courts continue to enforce rigorous standing requirements for civil penalties, plaintiff counsel will need to build a stronger factual foundation demonstrating ongoing risk.

In the end, *Cohen v. Consilio* offers a cautionary tale: a well‑intentioned stipulation that aims to streamline litigation can inadvertently preserve the very issues litigants hoped to extinguish. By drafting stipulations that are both precise and comprehensive, wage‑and‑hour practitioners can avoid costly remands, preserve the efficiencies of summary judgment, and, most importantly, safeguard their clients’ substantive rights.
