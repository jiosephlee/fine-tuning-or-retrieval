Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

## Injunctions in Wage‑and‑Hour Disputes: Why the Eighth Circuit Refused to Grant Relief in Cohen’s Case  

The Eighth Circuit’s recent opinion in **Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC**, 8th Cir. 2025, offers a vivid reminder that, even when a plaintiff has a strong substantive claim, procedural missteps can shut the door on the most potent remedy—an injunction. Cohen’s attempt to force Consilio to rescind its “premium overtime” policy was foiled not because the court doubted the policy’s legality, but because the claim was never properly presented at the right stage of the litigation. Below we unpack the procedural dynamics that barred injunctive relief, situate the decision within the broader landscape of injunctive standards in wage‑and‑hour law, and outline the steps plaintiffs should take to preserve an injunction when a case pivots toward statutory penalties or damages.

### Why This Case Matters  

In wage‑and‑hour litigation, injunctions serve a dual purpose: they instantly correct ongoing unlawful conduct and they deter future violations. Courts have routinely ordered employers to reinstate overtime pay, to cease misclassification, or to destroy unlawful policies. Consequently, a decision that bars an injunction—especially on purely procedural grounds—has ripple effects for every employee who seeks immediate relief from a pervasive pay practice. Cohen’s case underscores how the timing of claim amendments and the requirements of Rule 12(b)(6) and Rule 15(a) can be as decisive as the merits.

### The Procedural Roadblock: Claim Amendment After Summary Judgment  

Cohen’s original complaint asked for both monetary relief (unpaid overtime, liquidated damages, penalties) **and** an affirmative injunction requiring Consilio to withdraw its August 2019 overtime policy. By the time the case reached the summary‑judgment phase, the parties had stipulated that all overtime wages and liquidated damages had been paid. Cohen then shifted focus, seeking “the rescission of the illegal policy” as his sole injunctive request. He raised this request not in a formally amended complaint, but in his **summary‑judgment briefing**, essentially attempting to “add” a new remedy at the appellate stage.

The Eighth Circuit, following well‑established procedural doctrine, refused to consider the injunction. The court invoked **Fed. R. Civ. P. § 15(a)**, which requires that a plaintiff amend the pleading **before** a responsive pleading is served, or later with the court’s leave. More importantly, the court stressed that **new claims**—or new **relief sought**—cannot be introduced through an opposition brief. As Judge Kobes wrote, “new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs.” The appellate court therefore concluded that the injunction request was procedurally barred and not before the court for review.

### The Injunctive Standard in Wage‑and‑Hour Cases  

Even if the procedural hurdle had been cleared, Cohen would still have needed to satisfy the classic four‑prong test for a preliminary injunction (as articulated in *Winter v. NRDC*, 555 U.S. 420 (2009) and applied in the Eighth Circuit in *United States v. C.D. Savile Corp.*, 704 F.3d 987 (8th Cir. 2013)):

1. **Likelihood of success on the merits** – In wage‑and‑hour cases this often hinges on whether the employer’s policy runs afoul of the FLSA or, in Cohen’s case, the Minnesota statutes (MPWA, MFLSA, MWTA).  
2. **Irreparable harm** – The plaintiff must show that monetary damages are inadequate. Courts have recognized that continued wage violations cause ongoing injury to employees’ livelihoods, satisfying this prong.  
3. **Balance of equities** – Courts weigh the employer’s interest in maintaining its payroll practices against the employee’s lost wages.  
4. **Public interest** – Enforcing wage and hour laws is considered a strong public policy.

Cohen’s case would likely have cleared the first two prongs—he could point to the employer’s unilateral policy change and the statutory prohibition on eliminating overtime premiums. The third and fourth prongs are usually favorable to employees, especially where the employer’s conduct is clearly contrary to state wage law. Nonetheless, the procedural misstep meant the court never reached this substantive analysis.

### The Strategic Pitfall of “Pivoting” to Penalties  

Cohen’s case also illustrates a strategic danger for plaintiffs: **pivoting** from an injunction to a damages‑only claim can unintentionally waive injunctive relief. After the parties stipulated that Cohen had been paid all overtime and liquidated damages, Cohen asserted that his remaining remedy was statutory penalties under the MFLSA. By focusing the opposition brief on the MFLSA penalty issue and omitting any discussion of the injunction, Cohen effectively signaled to the court that the injunctive claim was no longer contested—a classic “waiver by omission.”  

Courts have warned that defendants can move to dismiss an unasserted claim for lack of a timely pleading (see *Taylor v. Dearborn Real Estate Co.*, 942 F.2d 1155 (8th Cir. 1991)). While Cohen could have filed a **motion to amend** his complaint before the district court’s summary‑judgment deadline, the parties’ stipulation that “the most expedient and efficient path forward would be … to decide the summary‑judgment motion” created a tacit understanding that the case would close once the monetary claims were resolved. In that context, the appellate court was correct to treat the injunction request as procedurally untimely.

### Doctrinal Implications  

The Cohen decision reinforces two doctrinal points that will shape future wage‑and‑hour litigation in the Eighth Circuit:

1. **Injunctive relief is not an automatic fallback** – Plaintiffs must **preserve** the injunction claim from the outset, either by stating it in the complaint or by amending the pleading **before** any dispositive motion is filed.  
2. **Stipulations and settlement‑style agreements can waive procedural rights** – Even informal stipulations, like the parties’ agreement to resolve the case via summary judgment, can be interpreted as a waiver of unasserted claims. Plaintiffs must explicitly carve out any relief they wish to retain, or ask the court to enter a **protective order** preserving the claim for later determination.

### Practical Takeaways for Practitioners  

- **Draft the complaint with a clear injunctive prayer**. If you anticipate needing an injunction, articulate the specific conduct to be enjoined and the statutory basis.  
- **File a motion to amend before summary judgment** if you need to add or clarify injunctive relief. The Eighth Circuit is willing to grant leave to amend when there is good cause and no undue prejudice.  
- **Avoid blanket stipulations** that “resolve all claims” without expressly excluding injunctive relief. A precise stipulation can preserve the injunction while still allowing for damages.  
- **Consider early motion practice**. If your case hinges on an injunction, file a **pre‑motion for preliminary injunction** contemporaneously with any motion for summary judgment, or at least before the court rules on the merits of monetary claims.  
- **Maintain the “injunctive” thread in all briefs**. Even if the main focus is penalties, a brief should reference the pending injunctive claim to avoid waiver through omission.

### Potential Downstream Effects  

The decision is likely to prompt more cautious pleading strategies in the Midwest. Employers may feel emboldened to rely on settlement stipulations that sweep away injunctive claims, knowing that plaintiffs must be meticulous to preserve them. Conversely, employee advocates will likely push for **court‑issued scheduling orders** that force parties to address injunctive claims early, preventing a “wait‑and‑see” approach that could allow unlawful policies to remain in effect while damages are litigated.

In the long run, the Cohen opinion may nudge the Eighth Circuit toward a more **procedurally strict** stance on injunctive relief in wage‑and‑hour cases, aligning with the circuit’s broader emphasis on finality and efficiency. Plaintiffs who overlook the procedural gatekeepers risk ending up with money in the bank but no guarantee that the offending policy will ever be dismantled.

### Bottom Line  

Cohen’s case is a textbook example of how **procedural precision** can trump substantive merit when seeking injunctions in wage‑and‑hour disputes. The Eighth Circuit did not rule on the legality of Consilio’s “premium overtime” policy; it simply refused to entertain a claim that was never properly before the court. For practitioners, the takeaway is clear: **file, amend, and argue your injunction early**, and never assume that a settlement of back‑pay automatically preserves the right to a court‑ordered policy change. The injunction remains one of the most powerful tools in the employee’s arsenal—provided it survives the procedural gauntlet.
