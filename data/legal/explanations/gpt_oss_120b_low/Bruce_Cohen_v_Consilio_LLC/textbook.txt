Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Chapter 1: Factual and Procedural Background  

In order to read and understand the appellate opinion in **Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC**, a law student must first master the factual matrix and procedural history that framed the Court’s analysis.  The following sections lay out, in plain‑language narrative, who the parties are, what employment practices were at issue, how the lawsuit began, what agreements the parties entered into before trial, and why the case reached the Eighth Circuit.  By keeping the discussion tethered to the record as presented in the opinion, we avoid extraneous doctrinal digressions and provide a solid foundation for the later legal analysis.

## 1.1. Parties and Employment Relationship  

**Plaintiff:**  Bruce Cohen, a licensed attorney who was hired in 2018 as an *hourly* document reviewer for Consilio, LLC.  Cohen performed the same work as other Minnesota‑based attorney reviewers—reviewing legal documents for client law firms and corporate counsel.  His compensation was expressed as a “base pay rate” per hour, and he was classified as a non‑exempt employee for purposes of Minnesota wage law.  

**Defendant:**  Consilio, LLC, a nationwide provider of legal document‑review services with a regional office in Minnesota.  Consilio employed Cohen and a cohort of similarly situated attorneys on an hourly basis.  As the employer, Consilio was subject to the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), each of which imposes duties on employers regarding overtime pay, penalties, and notice requirements.  

The relationship at issue is therefore an employer‑employee one governed by state wage‑and‑hour statutes, not an independent‑contractor or freelance arrangement.  The classification of Cohen as an hourly employee is critical because the statutes at issue protect “employees” who perform work for compensation under the direction of an employer.

## 1.2. The 2019 Premium Overtime Policy and Its Effect  

In **July 2019**, Consilio circulated an internal email to Cohen and all other Minnesota‑based hourly attorney reviewers.  The email announced a forthcoming **“premium overtime policy”** that would become effective in **August 2019**.  Under the new policy, Consilio *eliminated* the customary overtime premium (the additional ½‑time pay for hours worked over 40 in a workweek) and instead promised that overtime hours would be compensated **“at [their] base pay rate.”**  

The policy therefore altered the contractual and statutory calculation of overtime compensation.  While Minnesota law generally requires non‑exempt employees to receive a premium (time‑and‑a‑half) for overtime, the policy sought to replace that premium with straight‑time pay.  The policy’s language was unambiguous: it did not merely adjust the definition of “hours worked”; it expressly removed the premium.  Whether Consilio could lawfully implement such a change became the central factual dispute.

## 1.3. Initiation of the Lawsuit and Class‑Action Allegations  

In **August 2020**, Cohen filed a lawsuit in the United States District Court for the District of Minnesota on his own behalf and on behalf of a putative class of similarly situated employees.  The complaint alleged that Consilio’s premium overtime policy violated three Minnesota statutes:

1. **Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA)** – §§ 181.101, 181.171, subd. 1.  
2. **Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA)** – §§ 177.25, 177.27, subd. 8.  
3. **Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA)** – §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, 177.27.  

Cohen’s pleaded remedies included **back overtime pay**, **liquidated damages**, **statutory penalties** (e.g., “average daily wage” penalties under the MPWA, civil penalties under the MFLSA and MWTA), and **injunctive relief** to force Consilio to rescind the illegal policy.  The complaint framed the claim as a collective‑action suit, seeking to enforce statutory rights for all employees subject to the same policy.

## 1.4. Summary Judgment Stipulations and Payments Made  

After the complaint was filed, the parties engaged in extensive settlement discussions.  The resulting **stipulations** are crucial because they shaped the issues that survived to summary judgment:

* **Payments to Cohen and other employees** – Consilio paid a total of **$256,010.01** to satisfy Cohen’s MFLSA overtime and liquidated‑damages claims.  Cohen’s personal receipt consisted of **$3,225.21 in overtime** and **$462 in liquidated damages**.  The stipulation expressly stated that Cohen **“has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit”** and **“does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the MFLSA.”**  

* **Unsettled penalties** – The parties could not agree on the amount of statutory penalties.  Cohen asserted entitlement to:  
  * **$172,080** in average‑daily‑wage penalties under the MPWA,  
  * **$42,000** in MFLSA penalties, and  
  * **$42,000** in MWTA penalties.  
  Consilio contended that the statutes did not authorize such penalties at all.  

* **Procedural stipulation** – Both sides agreed that the most efficient course was for the district court to decide Consilio’s **summary‑judgment motion** on the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims **before any further discovery**.  They jointly acknowledged that **if the court granted summary judgment in full, “no claims would remain and the case would be fully resolved.”**  

Thus, the parties narrowed the dispute to whether the statutes themselves permitted the claimed penalties, not whether the underlying wage amounts were owed (those were already paid).

## 1.5. District Court’s Summary Judgment and the Appeal  

The district court **granted summary judgment** in Consilio’s favor on **all three statutory claims**, concluding that the statutes did not provide Cohen with a right to the asserted penalties.  The court’s order therefore left Cohen with no remaining relief; the case was, according to the stipulation, “fully resolved.”

Cohen filed an **appeal** to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit, arguing that the district court erred in its statutory construction and in its jurisdictional analysis, particularly with respect to the MFLSA penalties.  The appellate record therefore consists of the district court’s opinion, the parties’ briefs, and the stipulations noted above.  

The Eighth Circuit’s opinion, authored by Judge Kelly, proceeds to **review the summary‑judgment rulings de novo**—i.e., with fresh eyes, applying the appropriate standard of review for each claim.  The appellate court ultimately **affirmed** the district court on the MPWA and MWTA claims, **vacated and remanded** the MFLSA penalty issue for a jurisdictional determination, and left undisputed other matters (e.g., injunctive relief, attorney’s fees).  

Understanding this factual and procedural background equips the student to follow the court’s step‑by‑step reasoning: why the court treated each statute differently, how it applied the de novo standard, and what standing and mootness considerations arose on the MFLSA claim.  The next chapters will unpack those legal concepts in the context of the opinion’s analysis.

# Chapter 2: Legal Framework – Minnesota Wage Statutes and the MFLSA  

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* the Court wrestled with three distinct Minnesota statutes that regulate overtime compensation and the penalties that may be imposed when an employer violates those rules.  Understanding the opinion requires a clear picture of each statutory scheme, the remedies it provides, and how the statutes allocate those remedies between the employee and the state.  This chapter walks you through the relevant provisions, the mechanics of the penalties, and the policy arguments that the Court relied on when it affirmed most of the district court’s judgment and remanded the MFLSA penalty issue.

## 2.1. Overview of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) §§ 181.101, 181.171 and Penalty Mechanics  

The MPWA is Minnesota’s core wage‑payment enforcement statute.  Two sections are pivotal in *Cohen*:  

* **§ 181.101(a)** – This subsection creates a **civil enforcement mechanism** in which the State Commissioner of Labor and Industry may intervene on an employee’s behalf.  The process is:  

  1. The employee (or the Commissioner on the employee’s behalf) **serves a demand for payment** on the employer.  
  2. If the employer fails to pay the demanded wages **within ten days**, the Commissioner may **collect the wages and a statutory penalty**.  The penalty is calculated as an “average daily wage” multiplied by the number of days the employer remained in non‑compliance.  
  3. **All money collected, including the penalty, is paid to the employee**.  

* **§ 181.171, subd. 1** – This provision gives an employee a **right of action** to sue for violations of the MPWA (including violations of § 181.101).  The statute expressly states that when a court finds an employer liable, the employer is “liable for **compensatory damages and other appropriate relief**” and may also be held liable for “the **civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated**.”  

The *Cohen* Court emphasized that the **penalty language in § 181.101 is limited to the Commissioner**.  The statute does **not** authorize an employee to **directly** sue for the average‑daily‑wage penalty; rather, the penalty is a **government‑enforced sanction** that ultimately benefits the employee because the Commissioner must remit the collected funds to the employee.  This distinction is crucial because it determines who has standing to pursue the penalty and whether an employee can seek it in a private civil action.

## 2.2. Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) §§ 177.25, 177.27 – Overtime Entitlement, Liquidated Damages, and Statutory Penalties  

The MFLSA mirrors the federal Fair Labor Standards Act but is tailored to Minnesota workers.  The opinion focuses on three provisions:

* **§ 177.25** – Defines the **overtime entitlement**.  Non‑exempt employees who work more than 40 hours in a workweek must be paid at **one and a half times** their regular rate for each overtime hour.  This is the substantive wage right that Cohen alleged Consilio violated by paying only the base rate under its “premium overtime” policy.

* **§ 177.27, subd. 8** – Provides for **liquidated damages** equal to the amount of unpaid overtime wages.  The statute treats liquidated damages as a **mandatory double‑damage** measure: the employee receives both the back‑wage amount and an equal amount as liquidated damages.  In *Cohen*, Consilio paid Cohen the back‑wage amount ($3,225.21) and the liquidated damages ($462) as required by the MFLSA.

* **Statutory penalties** – The MFLSA also authorizes a **civil penalty** that the employer must pay to the **Commissioner**, not directly to the employee.  The penalty is intended to deter future violations and to fund enforcement.  The Court noted that, although the penalty is **payable to the state**, it is still a **remedial tool** that can be sought by a private plaintiff only if the plaintiff has standing to assert a sovereign‑interest claim (see discussion in § 2.4).

Because the parties stipulated that Cohen had already received all overtime wages and liquidated damages, the only remaining relief under the MFLSA was the **statutory penalty**.  The district court granted summary judgment in favor of Consilio on that ground, but the appellate court vacated that judgment, citing jurisdictional concerns about Cohen’s standing and the possible mootness of the penalty claim.

## 2.3. Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4) – Notice Requirements and Penalties  

The MWTA is a newer enforcement statute aimed at curbing “wage theft” by imposing **notice and penalty obligations** on employers.  The relevant sections are:

* **§ 181.032(f)** – Requires an employer **to provide written notice** to affected employees when it changes any term that affects the employee’s wages or benefits, including overtime policies.  Failure to give the required notice is a **violation** of the MWTA.

* **§ 181.032(d)(4)** – Enumerates the **civil penalties** for a violation of § 181.032(f).  The penalty is a fixed monetary sum per employee, payable to the **Commissioner** (and ultimately to the employee as the Commissioner disburses it).  

In *Cohen*, the plaintiff argued that Consilio’s August 2019 email announcing the “premium overtime” policy did not satisfy the statutory notice requirement.  However, the district court found that Cohen offered **no legal authority** showing that the MWTA creates a penalty **beyond** the statutory scheme, and Cohen’s claim was therefore dismissed.  The appellate court affirmed that dismissal because Cohen **did not raise a viable argument** for a penalty under the MWTA.

## 2.4. Distinction Between Employee‑Direct Remedies and Penalties Payable to the Commissioner/Government  

A central theme in the opinion is the **separation of remedial streams**:

1. **Employee‑direct remedies** – These are the **wage back‑pay, liquidated damages, and injunctive relief** that flow directly to the employee.  Under the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA, an employee can sue for the amount of wages owed and, where the statute commands it, liquidated damages (as in § 177.27).  These remedies are **personal** to the employee and do not require the involvement of the state.

2. **Penalties payable to the Commissioner** – Both the MPWA and MFLSA provide **civil penalties** that are **collected by the Commissioner** after the employer fails to comply with a demand.  The statutes expressly limit the **right to enforce the penalty** to the Commissioner; the employee may **receive the money** once the Commissioner disburses it, but the **standing to sue for the penalty** resides with the **state**.  Consequently, an employee’s private lawsuit cannot directly recover the penalty unless the statute expressly permits a private right of action for that penalty.

The *Cohen* Court relied on this distinction to reject Cohen’s attempt to obtain the MPWA average‑daily‑wage penalty in a private action.  The Court read the statutory text and legislative history to conclude that the legislature **“knew how”** to grant penalties—by vesting that authority in the Commissioner—so the employee cannot bypass the commissioner and sue directly for the same penalty.

## 2.5. Legislative Intent and the “Knows‑How” Argument  

When a statute provides a remedy in one place but is silent elsewhere, courts often look to **legislative intent** to determine whether the omission is intentional.  The *Cohen* opinion invoked the **“knows‑how” argument**:  

* Minnesota statutes that **grant penalties**—such as § 181.13(a) (allowing a penalty for unpaid wages) and § 181.14 (same for resigning employees)—show that the legislature **knew how** to give employees a penalty by **authorizing the Commissioner** to collect it.  

* By contrast, the MPWA’s § 181.101 **does not** give employees a private right to sue for the average‑daily‑wage penalty; that authority is **exclusively** vested in the Commissioner.  The Court read this silence as **intentional**—the legislature chose the Commissioner route for this particular penalty, presumably to centralize enforcement and preserve uniformity.

The “knows‑how” reasoning underscores a broader principle: **courts should not rewrite statutes** to create private penalties where the legislature deliberately placed the enforcement in the hands of an administrative agency.  This principle guided the Court’s conclusion that the district court was correct in granting summary judgment on Cohen’s MPWA penalty claim.

---

### Take‑aways for the Law Student  

* **Read the statutory text**.  The MPWA and MFLSA each contain specific language assigning penalty‑collection authority to the Commissioner.  The plain language controls unless ambiguous.  

* **Distinguish between wage‑recovery remedies (employee‑direct) and civil penalties (government‑collected)**.  The former can be pursued by any aggrieved employee; the latter generally require a state‑initiated action.  

* **Consider legislative purpose**.  When the legislature provides a penalty mechanism in one provision but not another, the omission is usually deliberate, indicating the legislature “knows how” to structure enforcement.  

* **Standing matters**.  Even when a penalty exists, an employee must have a **personal stake** in that penalty—often a “suit‑by‑the‑Commissioner” model—otherwise the claim may be dismissed for lack of standing, as the Court suggested on the MFLSA penalty issue.  

Understanding these nuances equips you to follow the appellate court’s analysis in *Cohen* and to anticipate how similar wage‑law disputes will be resolved under Minnesota’s statutory framework.

# Chapter 3: Summary Judgment Standard and Its Application  

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit was called upon to review a district‑court grant of summary judgment on three wage‑and‑hour claims.  The appellate court’s analysis provides a clear illustration of how the Eighth Circuit applies the de novo standard of review, the test for “genuine issues of material fact,” and the burden‑shifting framework that governs summary‑judgment motions.  This chapter unpacks those doctrinal pillars, explains the key precedents that shape them, and shows step‑by‑step how the court evaluated the evidentiary record in Cohen’s case.

---

## 3.1. Eighth Circuit Precedent on Summary Judgment  

The Eighth Circuit’s approach to summary judgment is articulated in a line of decisions that together form a three‑part scaffold:

1. **Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863 (8th Cir. 2014).**  
   Karlen emphasizes that appellate courts review a district court’s summary‑judgment ruling *de novo*—that is, as if the motion were being considered for the first time.  The review is conducted “viewing the evidence and the inferences that may be reasonably drawn from the evidence in the light most favorable to the non‑moving party.”  This language, borrowed from *Petroski v. H & R Block Enterprises, LLC*, underscores that the appellate court must not merely rubber‑stamp the trial court’s conclusions; it must reconstruct the evidentiary picture and ask whether any fact‑finder could reasonably find a genuine dispute.

2. **Placzek v. Mayo Clinic, 18 F.4th 1010 (8th Cir. 2021).**  
   Placzek restates the classic summary‑judgment test: “Summary judgment is proper if there are no genuine issues of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  The decision clarifies two concepts embedded in that sentence:  
   *A genuine issue of material fact* exists when the evidence is such that a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the non‑moving party.  *Material* means the fact could affect the outcome under the applicable legal rule.  The court warns against treating credibility determinations, which are reserved for a jury, as matters of law.

3. **Green Plains Otter Tail, LLC v. Pro‑Env’t, Inc., 953 F.3d 541 (8th Cir. 2020).**  
   Building on Karlen and Placzek, Green Plains discusses the practical mechanics of the burden shift.  First, the moving party must produce “genuine evidence”—typically affidavits, depositions, or admissible documents—that creates a genuine issue.  If the moving party meets that burden, the burden then shifts to the non‑moving party to “show that there is a genuine issue for trial” by pointing to specific evidence in the record that contradicts or undermines the movant’s evidence.  Failure to do so entitles the movant to judgment as a matter of law.

These three cases together form the bedrock of the Eighth Circuit’s summary‑judgment jurisprudence and are directly invoked by the panel in *Cohen*.

---

## 3.2. Burden Shifting: Moving Party’s Evidentiary Showing vs. Non‑Moving Party’s Response  

The summary‑judgment framework proceeds in two distinct phases:

### Phase 1 – Moving Party’s Evidentiary Showing  

The party seeking summary judgment (the “movant”) bears the initial burden of demonstrating that there is no genuine dispute.  In practice, this means providing admissible evidence that, if believed, would entitle the movant to judgment as a matter of law.  The movant may rely on:

- **Affidavits or declarations** that are based on personal knowledge and are under oath.  
- **Depositions** and **interrogatory answers** that are fully responsive.  
- **Uncontroverted documents** (e.g., payroll records, settlement agreements).  

Crucially, the movant must *affirmatively* produce evidence; a mere assertion of lack of liability does not satisfy the burden.

### Phase 2 – Non‑Moving Party’s Response  

If the movant satisfies its initial burden, the onus shifts to the non‑moving party (the “respondent”) to point to “specific facts” in the record that create a genuine issue.  The respondent may do this by:

- Identifying portions of the movant’s evidence that are ambiguous, incomplete, or contradictory.  
- Introducing its own admissible evidence (e.g., affidavits, declarations, documentary exhibits) that directly conflicts with the movant’s proof.  
- Demonstrating that a reasonable juror could infer a different conclusion from the same set of facts.

The appellate court, in its de novo review, will consider the entire record, give the respondent the benefit of the doubt, and resolve any ambiguities in the respondent’s favor.

In *Cohen*, Consilio was the movant on all three statutory claims.  Consilio produced the payment receipt showing that Cohen had been paid the overtime wages and liquidated damages he claimed under the MFLSA, and it offered the parties’ stipulation that “Cohen has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed.”  The district court treated those stipulations as conclusive proof that no factual dispute existed regarding wages, and it granted summary judgment.  On appeal, the Eighth Circuit revisited whether Consilio had met its burden on each claim and whether Cohen, as the respondent, had offered sufficient evidence to create a genuine issue—particularly on the penalty‑and‑mootness questions that were not fully briefed.

---

## 3.3. The “Light Most Favorable to the Non‑Moving Party” Standard  

When the appellate court conducts its de novo review, it must view the evidence “in the light most favorable to the non‑moving party.”  This standard operates on two levels:

1. **Adopt the Respondent’s Narrative** – The court reads the evidentiary materials as if the respondent’s version of the facts were true.  For example, if an affidavit contains a conclusory statement, the court assumes the underlying factual premise is correct, unless the affidavit is inherently unreliable.

2. **Avoid Credibility Determinations** – Since credibility is a fact‑finding function reserved for a jury, the appellate court cannot discount an affidavit or deposition simply because the movant disputes its truthfulness.  Instead, the court looks to see whether the respondent’s evidence, if believed, would create a genuine issue.

In *Cohen*, the panel applied this lens to the MPWA claim.  Cohen argued that the plain language of § 181.101 authorized “average daily wage penalties.”  The court, however, found that the statutory text, read in its entire context, expressly limited the authority to assess penalties to the Commissioner.  Even assuming Cohen’s construction, the court noted that the statute provides no mechanism for an employee to recover those penalties directly.  Consequently, there was no genuine dispute about the statutory entitlement, and summary judgment was proper.

Conversely, on the MFLSA claim, the court recognized that the parties had stipulated payment of overtime wages and liquidated damages, but the issue of *penalties*—which are payable to the state—remained unresolved.  Because the statutory scheme for MFLSA penalties is distinct from wage recovery, the court determined that the record did not conclusively show that Cohen could not obtain such penalties.  Moreover, the court raised a separate jurisdictional concern (standing and mootness) that required further factual development.  Here, the “light most favorable” analysis forced the appellate court to treat Cohen’s unresolved penalty claim as potentially viable, leading to the vacatur and remand.

---

## 3.4. How the District Court’s Evidentiary Record Was Evaluated  

The district court’s summary‑judgment analysis in *Cohen* hinged on three statutory claims.  The Eighth Circuit’s de novo review examined each claim through the lenses described above.

### 3.4.1. MPWA Claim  

- **Movant’s Evidence:** Consilio submitted the statutory language of §§ 181.101 and 181.171, highlighting the provision that penalties are imposed only after the Commissioner serves a demand and the employer fails to pay within ten days.  
- **Respondent’s Evidence:** Cohen relied on a plain‑language reading of § 181.101, asserting that the statute’s reference to “civil penalties” automatically created a private right to recover average daily wage penalties.  
- **Court’s Evaluation:** Applying the “light most favorable” standard, the appellate panel examined the statutory scheme holistically.  It noted that Minnesota legislature had expressly authorized penalties in other wage statutes (e.g., §§ 181.13, 181.14) and that § 181.101’s language limited penalty enforcement to the Commissioner.  Because the statute unambiguously reserved the penalty function to the agency, the court concluded that no genuine issue existed as to whether Cohen could recover the penalties.  Accordingly, summary judgment was affirmed.

### 3.4.2. MFLSA Claim  

- **Movant’s Evidence:** Consilio produced the payment receipt and the parties’ stipulation that Cohen had received all overtime wages and liquidated damages owed under the MFLSA.  
- **Respondent’s Evidence:** Cohen stipulated the same payment but argued that statutory penalties—separate from wages and liquidated damages—were still available and that the district court lacked jurisdiction to decide the penalty issue because of possible mootness.  
- **Court’s Evaluation:** The appellate court recognized that the parties had agreed on wage and liquidated‑damage payments but had *not* addressed the penalty provision, which the MFLSA channels to the state.  The record contained no evidence that the penalty was unavailable, nor that the statutory scheme barred an employee from seeking the penalty indirectly (e.g., by forcing the state to collect and then distribute).  Moreover, the court flagged a standing and mootness problem: if Consilio’s offending policy was no longer in effect, Cohen’s penalty claim could be moot; if the policy persisted, the claim remained live.  Because the record was insufficient to resolve these factual questions, the district court’s grant of summary judgment was improper.  The Eighth Circuit therefore vacated that portion of the judgment and remanded for a jurisdictional determination.

### 3.4.3. MWTA Claim  

- **Movant’s Evidence:** Consilio cited the statutory text of §§ 181.032(f) and (d)(4), arguing that the statute provides no civil‑penalty remedy for failure to give notice.  
- **Respondent’s Evidence:** Cohen offered no independent evidence of a statutory penalty provision; his argument rested on the fact that he had already been paid.  
- **Court’s Evaluation:** The appellate panel, following *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, found that Cohen’s brief contained no legal authority supporting a penalty claim under the MWTA.  With no genuine issue raised, summary judgment was affirmed.

### 3.4.4. Injunctive Relief  

The district court declined to consider an injunction because Cohen altered the requested relief after the complaint was filed and failed to amend his pleading.  The appellate court applied the procedural rule that new claims or relief must be raised through an amended complaint, not through briefing, and therefore upheld the district court’s discretion.

---

### Synthesis  

The *Cohen* opinion demonstrates the Eighth Circuit’s disciplined application of the summary‑judgment standard:

1. **De novo review** forces the appellate court to reconstruct the evidentiary record without deference to the trial court’s factual findings.  
2. **Genuine issues of material fact** are identified by asking whether a reasonable juror could find for the non‑moving party based on the record.  
3. **Burden shifting** requires the movant first to produce uncontroverted evidence; the respondent then must point to specific facts that create a dispute.  
4. **The “light most favorable” rule** ensures that any ambiguity is resolved in the respondent’s favor, preventing the movant from winning on the basis of conclusory statements.  

In *Cohen*, the district court’s summary‑judgment grant was upheld where the record showed no factual dispute (MPWA, MWTA) and reversed where the record left open factual questions essential to the claim’s viability (MFLSA penalties and standing).  Understanding these steps equips a law student to dissect future appellate summary‑judgment opinions with precision.

# Chapter 4: The MPWA Penalty Issue – Who May Seek Daily‑Average‑Wage Penalties?

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit confronted a seemingly simple question: does Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (“MPWA”) permit an aggrieved employee, rather than the state Commissioner of Labor and Industry, to pursue the statutory “average‑daily‑wage” penalties that the Act authorizes?  The court’s answer—no—rests on a close reading of the statutory language, well‑established rules of statutory construction in Minnesota, and a comparison with other state wage statutes that explicitly empower employees to obtain penalties.  This chapter walks law‑students through each component of the court’s analysis, showing how the judges moved from the text of § 181.101(a) to the ultimate holding that the district court correctly granted summary judgment on Cohen’s MPWA claim.

---

## 4.1. Text of § 181.101(a) and Its Limitation to Commissioner‑Initiated Demand

Section 181.101(a) is the operative provision governing the assessment and collection of penalties under the MPWA.  Its text can be broken into three sequential steps:

1. **Employee Demand** – “When an employer fails to pay wages … the employee may file a demand for payment with the Commissioner.”  
2. **Commissioner Action** – “If the employer does not pay the wages within ten (10) days after service of the demand, the Commissioner may charge and collect … the wages earned … and a penalty.”  
3. **Disposition of Collected Money** – “Any money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.”

Two features of this language are dispositive.  First, the statutory **right to impose a penalty** is expressly **conditioned on the Commissioner’s action**—the phrase “the Commissioner may charge and collect … a penalty” makes clear that the authority to assess the penalty resides solely with the Commissioner.  Second, the **only party who may receive the money** is the employee, but the **only party who may impose the penalty** is the Commissioner.  The statute therefore creates a **two‑step enforcement scheme**: an employee initiates a demand; the Commissioner, not the employee, decides whether a penalty attaches and then forwards the penalty proceeds to the employee.

Cohen’s argument rested on the broader purpose clause of the MPWA and the ordinary language of “civil action seeking redress” in § 181.171, but the court emphasized that **the plain text of § 181.101(a) overrides any broader inference**.  When a statute’s language is “plain and unambiguous,” Minnesota courts apply the text as the legislature’s ultimate expression of intent (see **Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31, 33 (Minn. 2015)**).  The Eighth Circuit, borrowing that principle, concluded that the statutory scheme left no room for an employee to independently sue for the daily‑average‑wage penalty.

---

## 4.2. Interpretation of “Civil Action Seeking Redress” Under § 181.171

Section 181.171 creates a **private right of action** for employees: “A person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of certain MPWA provisions, including § 181.101.  The provision also states that an employer “is liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief” and that “an employee may also recover civil penalties or damages, so long as they are provided for in the section of the MPWA that the employer allegedly violates.”

The appellate court parsed this language carefully:

* The **right to bring a civil action** is limited to **seeking redress for the substantive violation**—typically unpaid wages or commissions.  
* The **reference to “civil penalties or damages”** is **contingent on whether the underlying violated section itself furnishes a penalty provision**.  In other words, the private right of action can only incorporate penalties that the **statute itself authorizes**.

Because § 181.101(a) **does not itself authorize the employee to assess a penalty**—it reserves that authority for the Commissioner—the “civil penalties” language in § 181.171 cannot be read to create a new, employee‑driven penalty right.  The court therefore held that § 181.171 **does not expand** the enforcement mechanism laid out in § 181.101(a); it merely provides a procedural avenue for the employee to **recover what the statute already provides**, namely the unpaid wages (and any penalty the Commissioner may impose).

---

## 4.3. Precedent on Statutory Construction in Minnesota (Allan, Burkstrand)

The court’s reliance on **statutory construction principles** is grounded in a line of Minnesota precedent that treats clear statutory language as controlling:

* **Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d at 33** – When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, “it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.”  
* **Burkstrand v. Burkstrand, 632 N.W.2d 206, 210 (Minn. 2001)** – The “plain meaning rule” dictates that courts first look to the ordinary meaning of the words, and only if ambiguity exists should they turn to extrinsic aids.

Applying these rules, the Eighth Circuit found **no ambiguity** in § 181.101(a).  The phrase “the Commissioner may … charge and collect … a penalty” is unqualified and exclusive; it does not contain language such as “or the employee may” that would suggest a dual enforcement pathway.  Accordingly, the court declined to engage in any extensive legislative history analysis because the text itself offered a **clear directive**.

---

## 4.4. Legislative Comparison: Statutes That Explicitly Grant Employee‑Penalty Rights vs. MPWA’s Silence

To underscore that the MPWA’s silence on employee‑initiated penalties is intentional, the court examined **parallel Minnesota wage statutes** that **do** empower employees to seek statutory penalties directly:

1. **Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.13(a)** – Provides that a **discharged employee** may recover **unpaid wages or commissions** *and* “may charge and collect a penalty” after a demand is made.  
2. **Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.14, subd. 2** – Extends the same employee‑penalty right to employees who resign or quit.  

These provisions are **explicit**: they contain the phrase “may charge and collect a penalty” attached to the employee’s own demand, without reference to the Commissioner.  The court cited **Lagasse v. Horton, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022)**, which notes that the legislature “knows how” to empower employees when it wishes to do so.  By contrast, the MPWA **omits** any such employee‑penalty language, delegating the penalty function solely to the Commissioner.

The juxtaposition serves two purposes:

* It **demonstrates legislative intent**—the drafters deliberately chose a different enforcement model for the MPWA.  
* It **reinforces the textual analysis**—the absence of employee‑penalty language in § 181.101(a) is not an oversight but a deliberate design choice, affirmed by the presence of employee‑penalty language elsewhere in the wage code.

---

## 4.5. Court’s Conclusion and Affirmation of the District Court’s MPWA Ruling

Having established that **(1) § 181.101(a) gives the Commissioner exclusive authority to assess penalties, (2) § 181.171 does not create a new employee‑penalty cause of action, and (3) the legislature has expressly provided employee‑penalty rights in other statutes when it intended to**, the Eighth Circuit concluded that **Cohen cannot recover the average‑daily‑wage penalties under the MPWA**.

The court therefore affirmed the district court’s summary‑judgment grant on the MPWA claim, holding that no genuine issue of material fact existed as to whether Cohen possessed a statutory right to the penalties.  The decision underscores a broader lesson for practitioners: **when a statute delineates a specific enforcement mechanism, that mechanism controls**, and employees must look to the statutory text (and any express employee‑penalty provisions) before asserting a private right to penalties.

--- 

### Takeaways for the Reader

1. **Read the operative provision first.**  § 181.101(a)’s explicit grant of penalty authority to the Commissioner is dispositive.  
2. **Apply the plain‑meaning rule.**  Minnesota courts give effect to unambiguous language without resorting to policy arguments.  
3. **Use legislative comparison wisely.**  The presence of employee‑penalty language in other statutes signals that its omission in the MPWA is intentional.  
4. **Recognize the limits of § 181.171.**  The private right of action there does not expand the statutory penalties; it merely provides a procedural route to enforce what the statute already allows.

By following these analytical steps, a law student can reliably navigate similar penalty‑authority questions in wage‑law litigation and appreciate how appellate courts balance statutory text, construction principles, and legislative context.

# Chapter 5: MFLSA Penalties – Standing, Mootness, and Jurisdiction  

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit was called upon to decide whether an employee who had already been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (“MFLSA”) could also recover the civil penalties that the statute provides to the state commissioner.  The court’s analysis turned on three doctrinal pillars: the nature of the MFLSA penalty provision, the citizen‑plaintiff standing doctrine, and the mootness rules that apply when the alleged unlawful conduct may have ceased.  This chapter walks you through each of those pillars, shows how the appellate court applied them to the facts, and explains why the summary‑judgment ruling was vacated and the case remanded for a jurisdictional determination.

---

## 5.1. MFLSA Penalty Provision and Its Payment to the Commissioner  

The MFLSA, Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25 and 177.27, mirrors the federal Fair Labor Standards Act but tailors certain enforcement mechanisms to Minnesota law.  Section 177.27 authorizes a civil penalty of **$1,000 per each affirmative violation** (or $2,000 for willful violations).  Crucially, the statute **specifies that the penalty is “paid to the Commissioner”** of the Minnesota Department of Labor and Workplace Standards, not to the employee who brings the action.  The text reads in relevant part:

> “When an employer is found to have violated any provision of this chapter, the employer shall be liable … for a civil penalty … which shall be collected by the Commissioner and shall be payable to the Commissioner.”

The purpose of directing the penalty to the commissioner is two‑fold: (1) it provides a deterrent that is independent of the employee’s individual loss, and (2) it funds the state’s enforcement efforts.  Because the penalty is statutorily earmarked for the commissioner, the employee’s private right to recover it is limited; the employee may **assert a claim for the penalty**, but the **benefit flows to the state**.

In *Cohen*, the parties stipulated that Cohen had already been paid the overtime wages and liquidated damages due under the MFLSA.  The dispute therefore centered on whether Cohen could survive a summary‑judgment motion on the **penalty** claim, and whether the court even possessed jurisdiction to decide that claim given the procedural posture of the case.

---

## 5.2. Citizens’ Standing Doctrine  

### 5.2.1. The General Rule  

Standing requires that a plaintiff demonstrate a **personal stake** in the outcome of the dispute.  The Supreme Court’s classic formulation—**injury‑in‑fact, causation, and redressability**—remains the baseline.  For **citizen‑plaintiff** actions, the Court has recognized a narrow category of plaintiffs who can seek civil penalties on behalf of the government, even though the penalty benefits the state rather than the individual.  The leading case is **Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.**, 528 U.S. 167 (2000).  There, the Court held that a plaintiff who suffers **environmental injury** may sue for civil penalties because the statutory scheme is intended to encourage enforcement and deter future violations, thereby **affording a remedial interest** to the citizen.

Friend of the Earth identified three factors that justify citizen‑plaintiff standing for penalties:

1. **Statutory Intent:** The legislature designed the penalty to serve a deterrent function, not merely to compensate the plaintiff.  
2. **Personal Harm:** The plaintiff must show an injury that is “fairly traceable” to the statutory violation.  
3. **Redressability:** The penalty must be a legally cognizable means of alleviating the injury or preventing its recurrence.

### 5.2.2. Application to the MFLSA  

The Eighth Circuit treated the MFLSA penalty as analogous to the environmental penalties in *Laidlaw*.  However, the court noted two important distinctions:

* **Payment Destination:** The MFLSA expressly directs the penalty to the state commissioner.  Unlike *Laidlaw*, where the penalty could be used to fund remedial programs that directly benefitted the citizen plaintiff, the MFLSA’s penalty is a **government‑to‑government** transfer.  
* **Statutory Language on Private Enforcement:** Section 177.27 does not grant a private right of action for the penalty; it merely **authorizes** the commissioner to **collect** the penalty **when** a violation is found.  The plaintiff must therefore rely on the **“citizen‑plaintiff” exception** rather than a direct statutory right.

The appellate court concluded that **Cohen possessed a potential citizen‑plaintiff interest** because the penalty is meant to deter future wage‑law violations—a public interest that Cohen, as an employee who may be subject to further unlawful overtime policies, could claim.  Nonetheless, the court cautioned that **standing is a jurisdictional threshold**; it must be resolved before any merits discussion.  Because the parties had **not briefed** the standing issue, the court could not affirm the district court’s grant of summary judgment without first addressing whether Cohen satisfied the *Laidlaw* criteria.

---

## 5.3. Mootness Considerations When the Alleged Wrongful Practice May Have Ceased  

### 5.3.1. General Mootness Principles  

A case becomes moot when **“the issues presented are no longer live or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.”**  The Eighth Circuit follows the Supreme Court’s doctrine that a claim is moot if the underlying conduct has **absolutely ceased** and there is **no reasonable expectation** that it will recur.  The *Friends of the Earth* decision emphasized that a **voluntary cessation** by the defendant does **not** automatically render a case moot; the court may still adjudicate the legality of the practice.

### 5.3.2. The Voluntary‑Cessation Question in *Cohen*  

In *Cohen*, the record showed that Consilio had **repaid** the overtime wages and liquidated damages, and the parties stipulated that the disputed **2019 overtime policy** had been **replaced** with a new policy.  However, the court could not find a **binding factual finding** that the offending policy was permanently eliminated.  The appellate panel therefore identified two possible scenarios:

1. **Policy Still in Effect:** If Consilio continues to pay overtime at the base rate, the **risk of future violations remains**, and the civil penalty serves its deterrent purpose.  In this scenario, the claim is **not moot**.  
2. **Policy Discontinued and No Reason to Expect Recurrence:** If the policy has been permanently abandoned, Cohen’s claim for a penalty that is **paid to the state** may become **moot**, because there is no longer any ongoing or threatened injury.

Because the parties had not produced evidence to resolve which scenario applied, the appellate court found that the **district court lacked authority** to adjudicate the merits of the MFLSA penalty claim without first determining whether the claim was **still live**.  Consequently, the court vacated the summary‑judgment order and remanded for a **jurisdictional inquiry** (standing and mootness) before addressing the merits.

---

## 5.4. The District Court’s Summary‑Judgment Grant and Why the Appellate Court Vacated It  

The district court applied the standard for summary judgment: **no genuine dispute of material fact** and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.  Relying on the stipulation that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages, the district court concluded that there was nothing left for the court to decide and entered summary judgment for Consilio on **all three statutory claims**.

The Eighth Circuit, however, identified two fatal defects in that analysis:

1. **Failure to Address Standing:** The district court treated the penalty claim as a **plain‑law question**, assuming Cohen could recover the penalty.  Because standing is a **jurisdictional prerequisite**, the court should have **first** examined whether Cohen’s citizen‑plaintiff interest satisfied *Laidlaw*’s three‑part test.  By skipping this step, the district court acted **without jurisdiction** over the penalty claim.  

2. **Ignoring Potential Mootness:** The district court also erred by **not** evaluating whether the alleged wrongful practice—Consilio’s “base‑pay‑only” overtime policy—was **still in effect**.  Without resolving that factual dispute, the court could not determine whether the penalty claim was **moot**.  The appellate panel highlighted that **summary judgment is inappropriate** when a **material factual issue** (the continued existence of the offending policy) remains unresolved.

Given these shortcomings, the appellate court **vacated** the district court’s summary‑judgment grant **as to the MFLSA penalty claim** and instructed the lower court to undertake a proper jurisdictional analysis.

---

## 5.5. Remand Instructions – Jurisdictional Analysis Before Deciding Merits  

The appellate court’s remand order is narrowly tailored:

1. **Conduct a Standing Inquiry.**  
   * Apply the *Friends of the Earth* framework:  
     - Determine whether the MFLSA penalty reflects a **legislative intent** to deter future wage‑law violations.  
     - Assess whether Cohen has suffered a **personal injury** sufficient to give him a stake in the penalty’s imposition (e.g., a continuing risk of being subjected to unlawful overtime policies).  
     - Evaluate whether the **penalty would redress** that injury by deterring Consilio’s future conduct.  

2. **Resolve the Mootness Question.**  
   * Require the parties to **produce evidence**—such as internal memos, policy documents, or witness testimony—showing whether Consilio’s “base‑pay‑only” policy **remains** or has been **permanently abandoned**.  
   * If the policy **continues**, the claim is **live**; if it has **ceased** with **no reasonable expectation of recurrence**, the claim is **moot** and should be dismissed for lack of jurisdiction.  

3. **Determine Whether the Penalty is Recoverable.**  
   * Assuming standing and non‑mootness are established, the district court must decide whether the **statutory scheme permits a private citizen‑plaintiff** to **enforce the penalty** on behalf of the commissioner.  This may involve interpreting the **plain language** of § 177.27 and any relevant legislative history, as well as applying the **citizen‑plaintiff** doctrine.  

4. **Preserve the Summary‑Judgment Standard for Merits.**  
   * Once jurisdictional hurdles are cleared, the district court may return to the **summary‑judgment analysis**.  At that point, the court will consider whether any **material facts** remain in dispute (e.g., the amount of the penalty, whether the violation was willful) and whether the parties are entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

By compelling the district court to resolve these **jurisdictional** prerequisites first, the appellate panel ensures that any subsequent summary‑judgment decision will rest on a **properly vested** claim.  This procedural safeguard reflects the principle that **courts may not render judgment on a claim they lack authority to hear**.

---  

**Take‑away points for the law student:**  

* The MFLSA’s penalty provision is **statutory** and **paid to the state**, creating a unique citizen‑plaintiff scenario.  
* **Standing** is not automatic; it requires a showing of personal injury and a redressable interest consistent with the statute’s deterrent purpose.  
* **Mootness** hinges on whether the alleged unlawful conduct has truly ceased; a voluntary policy change does not automatically moot a claim.  
* A district court must **first** resolve standing and mootness before proceeding to a summary‑judgment analysis; failure to do so is a jurisdictional error that warrants vacatur and remand.  

Understanding these concepts equips you to dissect similar wage‑law cases where the plaintiff seeks statutory penalties that benefit the government rather than the individual.

# Chapter 6: The MWTA Claim, Injunctive Relief, and Final Disposition  

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit’s opinion resolves three distinct strands of the plaintiff’s case: the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) claim, the request for injunctive relief, and the court’s ultimate disposition of the appeal.  This chapter explains why the court left the MWTA claim and the injunctive‑relief request untouched, how procedural facts shaped those decisions, and what the final order—affirmation in part, vacatur and remand in part—means for employers who adopt overtime policies in Minnesota.

---

## 6.1. MWTA Notice Requirement and Absence of Statutory Penalty Authority  

The MWTA, Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f) and (d)(4), is a “wage‑theft” statute designed to protect employees from unlawful wage‑deduction or non‑payment practices.  Two provisions are relevant to Cohen’s claim:

1. **Notice Requirement.**  Section 181.032(f) obligates an employer to give employees written notice of any change in the method or rate of compensation.  Failure to provide such notice gives the employee a cause of action for “any damages” the statute authorizes.

2. **Penalty Provision.**  Unlike the MPWA, the MWTA does **not** contain a separate civil‑penalty scheme.  The statute’s remedial language is limited to “damages” and the recovery of unpaid wages; it does not empower the Commissioner—or any private party—to assess a daily‑average‑wage penalty.

The Eighth Circuit therefore examined the text of the MWTA and found no basis for Cohen’s request for penalties.  The court noted that Cohen had “stipulated that he received all pay Consilio owes him,” and that his only remaining argument was that the employer had failed to give proper notice.  Because the MWTA provides no statutory penalty for a notice violation, the plaintiff could not recover the $172,080 in average‑daily‑wage penalties he sought under the MPWA, nor any analogous amount under the MWTA.

The court’s reasoning follows a straightforward textual‑interpretation rule: when a statute is silent on a particular remedy, that remedy is unavailable.  Minnesota courts have repeatedly held that the absence of a penalty provision means the legislature intentionally limited relief to wage restitution.  Accordingly, the Eighth Circuit concluded there was no legal foundation for the MWTA penalty claim and left the district court’s dismissal undisturbed.

---

## 6.2. Procedural Posture – Stipulation of Full Payment and Lack of Substantive Argument  

A key procedural fact shaping the MWTA analysis is the parties’ **stipulation** that Cohen had been paid “for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed” and “any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled.”  This stipulation removed any genuine dispute over the **amount** of back‑pay, narrowing the dispute to whether an additional statutory penalty existed.

Because the MWTA claim hinged entirely on a plaintiff‑asserted penalty—something the statute does not grant—the court deemed there was no genuine issue of material fact.  Moreover, Cohen’s brief offered no authority or case law supporting a penalty theory under the MWTA.  In the absence of a substantive legal argument, the appellate court applied the principle articulated in *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000): a party that fails to raise a legal issue in the record “waives” that issue, and the court is not bound to fashion a discussion where none was offered.

Thus, the procedural posture—full payment stipulation coupled with a lack of argument—rendered the MWTA claim uncontroversial and legally untenable.  The Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s dismissal as a matter of law.

---

## 6.3. Injunctive Relief Standards (Rule 65, Standing, Pleading Requirements)  

Cohen also sought **injunctive relief**: an order compelling Consilio to rescind its 2019 overtime policy.  Federal courts evaluate injunctions under Rule 65 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, which requires:

1. **A clear showing of irreparable injury** that cannot be remedied by money damages.  
2. **A likelihood of success on the merits.**  
3. **Balancing of equities** and consideration of the public interest.  

In addition, **standing** is required; the plaintiff must demonstrate a concrete, personal injury that is actual or imminent.

Cohen’s procedural record failed on two counts:

- **Pleading Deficiency.**  At the onset of the case, Cohen’s complaint named specific injunctive relief.  However, when the case progressed to summary judgment, he **changed the relief sought**, asking the court to “withdraw the 2019 policy.”  This shift was not accompanied by an amended complaint or a supplemental pleading meeting Rule 12(b)(6) standards.  The Eighth Circuit cited *InfoDeli, LLC* and Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a) to emphasize that new claims or materially altered relief must be pleaded in an amended complaint, not simply in a briefing.

- **Lack of Demonstrated Irreparable Harm.**  Because Cohen had already been paid all wages and liquidated damages, the record offered no evidence that the continued existence of the policy caused a non‑monetary injury (e.g., loss of future employment opportunities or statutory rights).  Without such proof, the “irreparable injury” prong of the injunction test could not be satisfied.

Consequently, the appellate court concluded that the injunctive‑relief request was **not properly before it**.  The court declined to address the merits, noting that the issue was procedurally premature and did not survive the pleading standards required for equitable relief.

---

## 6.4. The Appellate Court’s Final Order: affirmation, vacatur, and remand  

The Eighth Circuit’s **final order** contains three distinct actions:

1. **Affirmation of the District Court’s Summary‑Judgment Decisions** on the MPWA and MWTA claims, and on the dismissal of the injunctive‑relief request.  The court found no genuine issues of material fact and no statutory authority for the penalties Cohen pursued.

2. **Vacatur of the Summary‑Judgment Grant on the MFLSA Claim** and **remand** for the district court to determine whether it has subject‑matter jurisdiction.  The majority opinion held that, even though Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages, the question of whether the underlying illegal overtime policy continues—hence whether statutory penalties are still viable—raised a standing and mootness issue that the district court had not properly resolved.

3. **Separate Concurring Opinion** (by Circuit Judge Kobes) reaffirmed the vacatur and remand regarding the MFLSA claim and expressed skepticism about Cohen’s standing for penalties under *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw*.

The net effect is that the appeal is **partially successful for Consilio** (the MPWA, MWTA, and injunctive‑relief claims remain dismissed) but **remains open for further litigation** on the MFLSA penalties issue.

---

## 6.5. Practical Implications for Employers’ Overtime Policies and Employee Penalties in Minnesota  

The decision yields several practical lessons for Minnesota employers who implement overtime or compensation policies:

1. **Statutory Penalties Must Be Explicitly Authorized.**  Employers can rely on the text of the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA to determine whether penalties are available.  The MWTA, for instance, does **not** provide a daily‑average‑wage penalty; therefore, an employer’s failure to give notice will not trigger such a penalty.

2. **Commissioner‑Only Enforcement of Certain Penalties.**  Under the MPWA’s § 181.101, only the state Commissioner may impose and collect daily‑average‑wage penalties.  Private employees cannot sue for those penalties directly, a point reinforced by the court’s reliance on *Hull v. ConvergeOne*.

3. **Full Payment Stipulations Can Limit Litigation Scope.**  When parties stipulate that wages and liquidated damages have been paid, any remaining claims must be grounded in separate statutory remedies (e.g., penalties).  Without a statutory basis, courts will dismiss those claims outright.

4. **Injunctions Require Proper Pleading and Evidentiary Support.**  Employers should be prepared for the possibility of injunctive relief only if a plaintiff can demonstrate irreparable harm and satisfy Rule 65.  Changing the relief sought without filing an amended complaint will likely result in the court refusing to consider the request.

5. **Policy Changes Do Not Immunize Employers from Future Penalties.**  Even if an employer later abandons an illegal overtime policy, courts may still entertain penalty claims if the plaintiff can show that the offending conduct could reasonably be expected to recur.  The *MFLSA* portion of this case underscores the importance of assessing ongoing liability after a policy change.

In sum, *Cohen v. Consilio* clarifies that **Minnesota wage‑and‑hour statutes are narrowly confined** to the remedies they expressly provide.  Employers should carefully draft overtime policies to comply with statutory notice requirements and avoid practices that would trigger penalties enforceable only by the state Commissioner.  Employees, on the other hand, must anchor any claim for penalties or injunctive relief in the specific language of the governing statutes and satisfy the procedural thresholds for standing, pleading, and irreparable injury.
