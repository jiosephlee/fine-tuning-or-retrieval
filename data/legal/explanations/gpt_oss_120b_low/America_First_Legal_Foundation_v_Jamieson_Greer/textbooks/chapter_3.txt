Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 3: # Chapter 3: AFL’s Two Standing Theories and the Court’s Analysis  

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* the D.C. Circuit was asked to decide whether the plaintiff, America First Legal Foundation (“AFL”), could sue the Office of Special Counsel (“OSC”) for refusing to investigate a alleged Freedom of Information Act (“FOIA”) violation by the Department of Justice (“DOJ”).  The district court had dismissed AFL’s claims on the merits, but the appellate court first asked whether the district court possessed subject‑matter jurisdiction at all.  The key gate‑keeping question was whether AFL satisfied Article III standing.  The opinion breaks AFL’s standing argument into two “theories” and evaluates each under the Supreme Court’s and the Circuit’s standing framework.  This chapter dissects those theories, the Court’s causation and redressability analysis, and the ultimate conclusion that AFL lacked a concrete injury.

---

## First Theory: Injury from DOJ’s Alleged Failure to Produce FOIA Records  

### The Claim  

AFL alleged that OSC’s refusal to investigate DOJ’s FOIA policy—specifically, the policy of not searching employee emails without the employee’s consent—prevented AFL from receiving the records it had requested under FOIA.  In other words, AFL contended that the injury was *informational*: the records remained in DOJ’s possession because OSC did not exercise its enforcement powers.

### Causation Analysis  

To survive the standing inquiry, a plaintiff must show that the defendant’s conduct is **the cause in fact** of the injury.  The Supreme Court has long required a “but‑for” causal connection that is not speculative (see *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.*, 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024)).  The D.C. Circuit applied this principle by tracing the alleged causal chain:

1. **OSC’s alleged duty** – Section 1216(a)(3) of the Civil Service Reform Act directs OSC to “conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.”  
2. **Potential OSC action** – If OSC investigated, it could have invoked the corrective‑action provisions of § 1214 or the disciplinary provisions of § 1215.  
3. **MSPB involvement** – Those provisions would have allowed OSC to bring the matter before the Merit Systems Protection Board (“MSPB”), which could sanction DOJ employees.  
4. **DOJ’s compliance** – A sanction or corrective order might have compelled DOJ to produce the requested emails.

The Court rejected this chain as **speculative** for three reasons:

* **Independent agency discretion.** Both OSC and the MSPB enjoy broad discretion in deciding whether to pursue enforcement.  As the Court noted, “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” (*FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.*, 602 U.S. at 383).  
* **Attenuated link to DOJ.** The injury AFL suffered (the absence of records) is directly traceable to DOJ’s refusal to produce, not to OSC’s decision not to investigate.  The Court emphasized that “any informational injury from DOJ’s refusal to disclose agency records is plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC.”  
* **No requirement that OSC’s investigation produce a remedy.** Even if OSC investigated, the statute does not obligate OSC to obtain the records; it merely permits corrective or disciplinary action.  Hence, there is no necessary causal pathway from OSC’s inaction to DOJ’s continued withholding.

Because the alleged cause was indirect, conjectural, and contingent on the discretionary actions of two bodies not parties to the suit, the Court concluded that AFL failed the causation prong of standing.

### Redressability Analysis  

Even assuming causation, a plaintiff must show that a favorable judicial decision would **likely redress** the injury.  The Supreme Court defines redressability as the need for a judgment to “affect the parties in a manner that will, in all probability, cure the plaintiff’s injury” (*Lujan*, 504 U.S. at 560).  The D.C. Circuit’s redressability analysis focused on two points:

1. **Binding effect limited to parties.** A judgment ordering OSC to investigate would bind only OSC; it would not compel DOJ, the MSPB, or any other agency to produce the records.  The Court reiterated that “redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might persuade actors who are not before the court” (*Haaland v. Brackeen*, 599 U.S. at 293).  
2. **Speculative downstream effects.** The Court stressed that “a favorable judgment here would not very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” AFL’s informational injury.  The chain from OSC investigation to MSPB sanction to DOJ compliance is too uncertain to satisfy the “more likely than not” standard required for redressability.

Given these weaknesses, the Court held that the first standing theory collapses on both causation and redressability grounds.

---

## Second Theory: Informational Injury from the Lack of OSC Investigation and Notices  

### The Claim  

AFL’s alternative theory asserted that OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived AFL of a **procedural right**—the right to receive the notices and information that the statute requires when OSC investigates a “prohibited personnel practice” under § 1214.  AFL argued that the statutory language of § 1216(c) makes the investigation **mandatory**, and that the absence of any notice constitutes a concrete, informational injury.

### Entitlement Analysis  

The standing inquiry asks whether the plaintiff possesses an **entitlement** to the information he seeks.  The Court examined the statutory text:

* **§ 1216(a)(3)** obliges OSC to “conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.”  The provision uses the verb “shall,” indicating a duty to investigate.  
* **§ 1214** sets out procedural safeguards—notice to the complaining party, updates on progress, etc.—but **only** in the context of investigations of “prohibited personnel practices.”  

The Court found that while § 1216 imposes a duty to investigate, it **does not** import the procedural rights of § 1214 into a § 1216 investigation.  In other words, the statutory duty to investigate does not create a **statutory entitlement** to the procedural information that § 1214 provides.  As the opinion notes, “A plaintiff claiming informational standing suffers a concrete injury if he is denied information that a ‘statute entitled him to receive.’”

Because § 1216 does not expressly grant a right to receive investigative notices or updates, AFL could not demonstrate an entitlement to that information.

### Discretionary Nature of OSC’s Investigative Decision‑Making  

Even assuming a broad reading of § 1216, the Court reminded us that OSC’s decision **whether** to follow the § 1214 procedural scheme is **discretionary**.  The Court cited *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*, 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011), which held that OSC may elect whether to apply § 1214’s notice requirements in a § 1216 investigation.  Moreover, the Court invoked the classic precedent *Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. 821, 831–32 (1985), which established that an agency’s refusal to exercise enforcement discretion is virtually unreviewable unless the agency’s action is arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise unlawful.  Because OSC’s refusal to investigate falls squarely within its discretionary enforcement authority, the judiciary lacks the power to compel a particular investigative approach or to award the procedural information AFL demands.

### Failure to Show a Concrete Injury  

Standing demands an **injury in fact** that is concrete and particularized.  The Court stressed that “the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient” (*Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009)).  AFL’s alleged injury—lack of notice—does not affect a protected, concrete interest because:

* No statutory entitlement exists to that notice; and  
* Even if the notice were provided, it would not alter the underlying FOIA dispute or grant AFL any substantive benefit.

Consequently, AFL’s second theory also fails to establish the requisite concrete injury.

---

## Court’s Synthesis and Conclusion  

After dissecting both theories, the D.C. Circuit concluded that AFL **did not satisfy any element of Article III standing**:

* The first theory faltered on causation (the injury could not be linked to OSC’s inaction) and redressability (a judgment would not bind the non‑parties whose conduct actually caused the injury).  
* The second theory failed because AFL lacked a statutory entitlement to the investigative information and because OSC’s discretion insulated its decision from judicial compulsion, leaving AFL without a concrete injury.

Because standing is a jurisdictional threshold, the Court vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss the case for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction.  The decision underscores the high bar that plaintiffs must meet when they seek to enforce procedural duties of federal agencies: they must show a concrete, particularized injury that is fairly traceable to the defendant’s conduct and that a favorable court order is likely to remedy.  In the absence of those elements, the courts must refrain from adjudicating the merits.
