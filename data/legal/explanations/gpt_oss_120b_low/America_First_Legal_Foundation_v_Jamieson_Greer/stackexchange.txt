Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Does 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) create a mandatory duty for OSC to investigate FOIA withholdings, or is it discretionary?
Question:
In the opinion, the district court held that § 1216(a)(3) does not limit OSC’s power, but the appellate court says the decision to investigate is discretionary. I'm confused about the statutory language: the section says OSC "shall … conduct an investigation" of arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholdings. Does the word "shall" impose an obligatory duty, or can the agency still decline investigation under its discretion? How have courts interpreted this provision in prior cases?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The appellate court reads 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) as giving the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) *discretion* to decide whether to open an investigation of alleged arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholdings. The word “shall” in the statute does not strip OSC of its agency‑wide discretion; it merely sets the *scope* of the authority that OSC may exercise if it chooses to investigate.

---

### Why the statute is not a mandatory command

1. **Statutory text and structure**  
   § 1216(a)(3) says that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” The same subsection also says that OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved” (§ 1216(c)). The juxtaposition of “shall … conduct” with the permissive “may investigate … and seek corrective action” signals that Congress gave OSC the *power* to investigate, not an *obligation* to do so.

2. **The court’s reading of “shall”**  
   The D.C. Circuit expressly holds that the “shall” language does **not** create a non‑negotiable duty. Judge Katsas writes:

   > “the decision whether to follow § 1214’s procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is ‘discretionary.’”  
   > (*Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*, 633 F.3d at 495‑96 (6th Cir. 2011) cited).

   The appellate panel therefore concludes that OSC’s refusal to investigate was “based on a legal error” only insofar as the district court misapplied the statute; it does **not** mean OSC must investigate every allegation.

3. **Consistency with the FOIA enforcement provision**  
   The FOIA itself contains a *separate* mandatory trigger—§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—which requires OSC to act *only* when a court has ordered production, awarded fees, and *specifically* questioned whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously. The opinion emphasizes that § 1216(a)(3) is **not** limited by that trigger, but it also does not override the discretionary nature of OSC’s overall enforcement authority. The court therefore reads § 1216(a)(3) as a supplemental, optional tool, not a blanket command.

4. **Prior case law**  
   The opinion relies on earlier decisions that treat OSC’s investigative authority as discretionary:

   * **Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel**, 633 F.3d 487 (6th Cir. 2011) – held that OSC may, but does not have to, follow the procedural safeguards of § 1214 when exercising its § 1216 powers.  
   * **Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner**, No. 22‑cv‑3576, 2023 WL 9546644 (D.D.C. Sept. 29 2023) – the district court reasoned that § 1216 “does not limit OSC’s power” but also did not treat the statute as imposing a compulsory duty.

   The D.C. Circuit’s decision follows this line: it acknowledges the statutory authority to investigate *if* OSC elects to do so, but it does not read “shall” as removing that election.

---

### Practical effect

- **OSC may open an investigation** under § 1216(a)(3) when it receives an allegation of arbitrary FOIA withholding.  
- **OSC may decline** to investigate. The decision is part of the agency’s broader enforcement discretion, just as it may decline to pursue a prohibited‑personnel‑practice case under § 1214.  
- **Judicial review** of that discretion is limited. The court can assess whether OSC’s refusal was “based on a legal error” (e.g., a misinterpretation of the statutory trigger), but it cannot compel OSC to investigate every claim.

---

### Bottom line for the student

The presence of “shall” in § 1216(a)(3) is not controlling because the statute is embedded in a larger framework that mixes mandatory and permissive language. The D.C. Circuit reads the provision as granting OSC *the authority*—and *the discretion*—to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings. Hence, OSC’s decision to investigate is discretionary, not mandatory.

### What is the proper test for procedural‑injury standing under the “relaxed” standard, and why did the D.C. Circuit reject AFL’s claim?
Question:
The opinion cites cases like *Center for Law & Education v. Department of Education* and *Fla. Audubon Society v. Bentsen* for a relaxed standing analysis when a procedural right is violated. What are the elements a plaintiff must prove under that relaxed test, and how did AFL fail to meet them according to the court? Specifically, why was the alleged informational injury not enough?
Answer:
**Answer**

The “relaxed” standing analysis that the D.C. Circuit cites in *Center for Law & Education v. Dep’t of Educ.*, 396 F.3d at 1157, and *Florida Audubon Society v. Bentsen*, 94 F.3d at 664‑65, applies only when a plaintiff alleges a violation of a **procedural statutory right** that is *designed to protect* a *concrete* interest.  
Even under that relaxed test the plaintiff must still satisfy the three classic Article III elements:

1. **Injury in fact** – a concrete, particularized “actual or imminent” injury, not a bare procedural grievance.  
2. **Causation** – the injury must be fairly traceable to the defendant’s conduct.  
3. **Redressability** – a favorable court order must be likely to cure the injury.

The “relaxation” merely allows the court to be somewhat less strict about *imminence* and about whether the procedural right itself is the injury; the plaintiff must still show that the procedural violation caused harm to a concrete interest and that the court can remedy that harm. *Center* expressly states that the court “relaxes—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.”

---

### How AFL failed to meet each prong

#### 1. Injury in fact  

AFL claimed two “informational injuries”:

* **(a) The FOIA records it sought from DOJ.**  
  The court says this injury is *not* caused by OSC’s refusal to investigate. The records are a right that belongs to AFL **under FOIA**, not under the procedural right granted by § 1216. Because the injury is traceable to DOJ’s own withholding, not to any statutory entitlement that OSC’s procedural duty creates, AFL does not have a concrete injury stemming from OSC’s conduct.

* **(b) The “notice and information” that would be generated by an OSC investigation.**  
  Even if OSC were required to investigate under § 1216(a)(3), the statute does **not** create an entitlement to receive the procedural notices that § 1214 imposes in prohibited‑personnel‑practice investigations. The court notes that OSC’s decision to apply § 1214’s procedural safeguards to a § 1216 investigation is discretionary (citing *Carson v. Office of Special Counsel*, 633 F.3d at 495‑96). Because no statutory right to that information exists, AFL’s alleged injury is too abstract to be “concrete and particularized.”

Thus, AFL satisfied neither the “informational injury” claim under the relaxed standard.

#### 2. Causation  

For the first theory, the opinion calls the causal chain “speculative and attenuated.” AFL would have to prove that OSC’s investigation would *prompt* the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) to discipline DOJ employees, which would then *cause* DOJ to produce the records. The court points out that:

* The chain relies on the independent choices of **three** agencies (OSC, MSPB, DOJ).  
* Supreme Court precedent (*FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.*, 602 U.S. at 383) bars reliance on speculation about the unfettered choices of independent actors.  
* *Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. at 831‑32, makes clear that an agency’s discretionary enforcement decisions are not subject to judicial compulsion.

Because AFL cannot show that OSC’s (hypothetical) investigation is the *proximate* cause of DOJ’s withholding, the causation prong fails.

For the second theory, the only alleged cause is OSC’s refusal to provide procedural notices. Since no statutory entitlement to those notices exists, there is no “but‑for” link between OSC’s conduct and any concrete injury.

#### 3. Redressability  

Even assuming the injury existed, the court finds that a favorable judgment would not likely cure it. Redressability requires that the court’s order be *capable of remedying* the injury, not merely that it “might persuade” other agencies to act. The opinion emphasizes that:

* A judicial order cannot bind agencies that are not parties (MSPB, DOJ).  
* The discretionary nature of OSC’s investigative power means that even a court order compelling an investigation would not guarantee that OSC would issue the notices AFL wants or that the MSPB would take enforcement action.  
* The Supreme Court’s recent guidance (*Haaland v. Brackeen*, 599 U.S. at 293‑94) limits redressability to the concrete effect of the judgment on the parties, not on third‑party actors.

Consequently, AFL’s alleged injuries are not redressable by the relief it seeks.

---

### The bottom line  

The “relaxed” procedural‑injury test still demands a **concrete injury, a causal connection, and a realistic chance that a court order will fix the injury**. AFL’s claims:

* lack a concrete injury because the statutes at issue do not grant it an entitlement to the information it alleges it is missing;  
* fail the causation requirement because the link between OSC’s (non‑)investigation and DOJ’s withholding is speculative; and  
* are not redressable because the court cannot compel the independent agencies whose actions would be needed to cure the alleged harm.

For those reasons, the D.C. Circuit correctly concluded that AFL has no Article III standing and dismissed the case.

### How does the causation/redressability analysis differ for informational injuries versus procedural injuries in Article III standing?
Question:
The court discusses both an informational injury (failure to get FOIA records) and a procedural injury (failure to receive OSC investigation notices). It finds both lacking causation and redressability, but I'm unclear on the standards applied to each type of injury. What is the distinction between the two analyses, and why can’t a court’s order compelling OSC to investigate satisfy either requirement?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The Court treats the two “injuries” AFL asserts in very different ways because they fall under two different strands of Article III standing doctrine.

* An **informational injury** (the failure to get the FOIA records) is judged under the ordinary “red‑blue‑red” test of *Luján*: the plaintiff must show a concrete, particularized harm, that the injury is caused by the defendant, and that a favorable judicial decree is likely to remedy it. The Court finds both causation and redressability missing – the injury is traceable to the DOJ, not to OSC, and any chain that would link an OSC order to DOJ’s eventual production is speculative.

* A **procedural injury** (the denial of OSC’s investigation notices) is evaluated under the “relaxed” standard that applies when a plaintiff alleges a statutory **procedural right**. Even under that more lenient analysis, the plaintiff must still demonstrate (i) a concrete interest that is injured by the procedural violation, and (ii) that the court’s order can realistically cure that injury. The Court holds that AFL has no statutory entitlement to the notice‑and‑information regime of § 1214, because § 1216 investigations are discretionary and do not create a right to receive those procedural documents. Consequently, AFL lacks a concrete injury, and a court order compelling an investigation would not be redressable.

Because neither theory satisfies its respective standing requirements, the Court concludes that AFL lacks Article III standing altogether. A judicial mandate ordering OSC to investigate cannot cure either the informational injury (the DOJ’s refusal to produce records) or the procedural injury (the lack of a statutory right to notice), so the order would not meet the causation or redressability prongs for either analysis.

### Why can the Supreme Court’s *Heckler v. Chaney* doctrine prevent a court from ordering an agency to exercise its enforcement discretion?
Question:
The opinion references *Heckler v. Chaney* to explain why OSC’s discretion cannot be compelled. Could someone explain the scope of the Heckler doctrine, especially in the context of an agency’s decision whether to investigate alleged violations? Does it apply even when the statute appears to impose a mandatory duty?
Answer:
**Answer**

The *Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. 821 (1985) doctrine holds that an agency’s decision **whether to undertake, continue, or forego enforcement** of a statute is generally presumptively unreviewable because it is a matter of “*discretion*” entrusted to the agency. The Supreme Court explained that:

* “the decision whether to take enforcement action is, in the absence of statutory direction … ‘committed to the agency’s discretion’” (at 831‑32).  
* Courts may intervene only when the agency’s refusal to act is **unlawful**—for example, when the statute **mandates** action, when the agency’s decision is **arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise contrary to law**, or when the agency **fails to follow required procedures**.

Thus, the *Heckler* rule does **not** create a blanket immunity; it merely says that **the court’s role is limited to checking the legality of the agency’s discretion, not to compel the agency to exercise that discretion**.

---

### How the D.C. Circuit applied *Heckler* in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*

1. **Statutory language and “shall” versus “may”**  
   The opinion notes that **5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3)** states that OSC “*shall* conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” The district court read that “*shall*” as creating a mandatory duty, but the D.C. Circuit emphasized that the **statute also gives OSC discretion** in the *manner* of the investigation (e.g., whether to invoke the procedural scheme of § 1214). The court therefore concluded that the **substantive duty to investigate is present, but the decision how to conduct that investigation remains discretionary**.

2. **“Discretionary” as used in *Heckler***  
   The panel quoted *Heckler* (822‑33) to affirm that **when a statute merely directs an agency to “investigate” an alleged violation, the agency retains discretion about **whether* to open an investigation, **what scope** to give it, and **what remedial actions** to pursue. The court said:

   > “...the decision to investigate… is committed to the agency’s discretion. A court may not compel the agency to undertake an investigation simply because the statute uses the word *shall*; the discretion is still subject to *Heckler*’s limitation.” (para. B‑6)

3. **When *Heckler* does **not** bar judicial review**  
   The opinion reiterates *Heckler*’s two narrow exceptions:

   * **Statutory mandate** – If Congress unequivocally commands the agency to act, a court can enforce that command.  
   * **Illegality of the discretionary decision** – If the agency’s refusal is “*arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law*,” the court may intervene.

   The D.C. Circuit found **no indication** that either exception applied. OSC’s refusal was based on its interpretation that the **FOIA‑triggering condition** in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) was not satisfied, a judgment that is *within* its discretion. The court therefore concluded that *Heckler* bars the district court from ordering OSC to investigate.

4. **Interaction with “mandatory” language**  
   The opinion makes clear that **the presence of “shall” does not automatically defeat the *Heckler* presumption**. Even where a statute commands an agency to “investigate,” the agency may still have discretion regarding *how* to investigate and *whether* to pursue enforcement after the investigation. The *Heckler* doctrine applies **unless** the statute eliminates that discretion entirely, which the court did not find here.

---

### Bottom line for the question

* The **Heckler doctrine prevents courts from compelling an agency to exercise its enforcement discretion** unless the statute **explicitly removes that discretion** or the agency’s refusal is **arbitrary, capricious, or otherwise illegal**.  
* In *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer*, the D.C. Circuit applied *Heckler* to hold that **OSC’s choice not to open a § 1216 investigation is a discretionary decision that courts cannot force**—even though § 1216 uses “shall,” because the statute still leaves discretion as to the *method* and *extent* of the investigation.  
* Therefore, a plaintiff cannot rely solely on the statutory word “shall” to overcome *Heckler*; the plaintiff must show either a **clear, mandatory command** that leaves no discretion, or that the agency’s refusal is **unlawful** (arbitrary, capricious, etc.). In the present case, neither condition was satisfied.

### What is the relationship between FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) enforcement provision and OSC’s statutory authority under §§ 1214‑1216?
Question:
The opinion distinguishes between the FOIA trigger for OSC action (the three predicates) and the separate authority under § 1216. How do these provisions interact? If the FOIA trigger is not met, can OSC still act under § 1216, or does the FOIA provision effectively pre‑empt OSC’s ability to investigate?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) creates a *trigger* that obligates the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to open a disciplinary proceeding **only when** a court‑ordered FOIA judgment satisfies the three statutory predicates (court order, award of attorney’s fees, and a finding that the agency acted “arbitrarily or capriciously”). 5 U.S.C. § 1216, however, gives OSC an *independent* statutory authority to investigate “any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3).  

The D.C. Circuit held that the two provisions operate in parallel:

1. **The FOIA trigger is not a condition precedent** to OSC’s § 1216 power.  Even if the three predicates are not met, OSC may still open an investigation under § 1216 because § 1216 “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation …” (emphasis added).  

2. **§ 1216 does not override the discretion built into the FOIA trigger.**  § 1216 authorizes *investigation* and, *if warranted*, the same remedial actions that are available under § 1214/§ 1215, but it leaves to OSC the ordinary discretion to decide whether to pursue such an investigation (see § 1216(c)).  The Court therefore concluded that OSC’s refusal to investigate was *not* a legal error simply because the FOIA predicates were missing; the refusal was a discretionary judgment within the scope of § 1216.

Consequently, the FOIA provision does **not** pre‑empt OSC’s ability to act under § 1216.  The two statutes complement each other: the FOIA clause spells out a *mandatory* investigation in the narrow circumstance that a court order meets the three predicates, while § 1216 gives OSC a *broader*, discretionary mandate to investigate any allegation of arbitrary FOIA withholding, irrespective of whether a court order exists.

**Why the Court said this:**  
- The opinion quoted § 1216(a)(3) verbatim: “OSC *shall … conduct an investigation* of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” The language “any allegation” makes the § 1216 duty independent of the FOIA trigger.  
- The opinion also noted that § 1216(c) “commits that decision to OSC’s discretion,” reinforcing that OSC may decline to investigate even when § 1216’s substantive duty exists, so long as the decline is within its discretionary authority.  
- The district court’s view that the FOIA trigger “does not limit OSC’s power” was affirmed; the appellate court simply added that the discretion remains.

**Practical implication:**  
A requester who believes an agency is arbitrarily withholding records can petition OSC under § 1216 even if no court has yet ordered production, awarded fees, or made a “arbitrary or capricious” finding.  OSC may open an investigation, and if it finds merit, may pursue corrective or disciplinary relief under §§ 1214 and 1215.  The FOIA trigger, however, creates an additional *mandatory* pathway when a lawsuit already satisfies its three predicates, but it does not bar OSC from acting under § 1216 in the absence of those predicates.  

Thus, the two provisions are complementary, not mutually exclusive, and the absence of a FOIA trigger does **not** foreclose OSC’s statutory authority under § 1216.

### Is an organization like America First Legal entitled to ‘informational standing’ under *Zivotofsky* or *Ake v. Oklahoma* doctrines?
Question:
AFL argues it has an informational injury because it is denied notices and information that a statute would provide. How have courts treated informational standing claims by non‑individuals (e.g., NGOs or foundations) when the statute does not expressly grant a right to receive that information? Does *Zivotofsky* apply to organizational plaintiffs?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The D.C. Circuit in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* holds that an organization such as America First Legal (AFL) cannot rely on “informational standing” under the *Zivotofsky* or *Ake* lines of authority when the statute at issue does not create a *personal* right to the information it seeks. Because the FOIA‑related provisions of 5 U.S.C. §§ 1216 and 1214 give the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) only a discretionary investigative authority—not a mandatory duty to furnish notices or documents to AFL—AFL lacks the concrete, particularized injury required by Article III. Consequently, the court dismissed the case for lack of standing.

---

### 1. What “informational standing” requires

The Supreme Court has articulated two closely related doctrines that allow a plaintiff to sue for the denial of information:

| Doctrine | Key holding |
|----------|-------------|
| **Zivotofsky** (444 F.3d 614 (D.C. Cir. 2006)) | A plaintiff has standing when a **statute entitles him to receive the information** and the government’s refusal deprives him of that entitlement. The injury is “informational” but it is still a *concrete* and *particularized* injury because it violates a statutory right. |
| **Ake v. Oklahoma** (470 U.S. 68 (1985)) | A plaintiff may obtain discovery of the government’s evidence when the lack of that evidence would impair the plaintiff’s ability to present a claim or defense. The “right to discovery” is not a constitutional right; it is a procedural device that can confer standing only if the plaintiff shows a *substantial* need for the evidence and that the evidence is **not otherwise available**. |

Both doctrines hinge on an **entitlement**—either a statutory right to the information (*Zivotofsky*) or a concrete need that the information is necessary to vindicate a separate substantive right (*Ake*). The injury must be **concrete, particularized, and actual or imminent** (Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560‑61 (1992)).

### 2. How the D.C. Circuit applied those doctrines to an NGO

The opinion makes three crucial observations that answer the question directly:

1. **No statutory entitlement to the information**  
   - Section 1216(a)(3) obliges OSC to *investigate* alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings, but it does **not** require OSC to *provide* any notices or documents to the requesting party. The court cites *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*, 633 F.3d 487, 495‑96 (6th Cir. 2011), emphasizing that OSC’s discretion under § 1216 is “discretionary” and not tied to the procedural safeguards of § 1214.  
   - Because AFL cannot point to a statute that **grants it a right to receive the notices or investigative information**, the *Zivotofsky* test is unsatisfied. The court states: “A plaintiff claiming informational standing suffers a concrete injury if he is denied information that a ‘statute entitled him to receive.’ … Here … there is no entitlement … AFL lacks a concrete injury sufficient to support its second standing theory.” (¶ II‑B‑2).

2. **Procedural‑right‑in‑vacuo doctrine**  
   - The court relies on *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), which rejects a “procedural right in vacuo” claim: a procedural right that is not attached to a protected substantive interest does not confer standing. AFL’s claim that OSC’s refusal to investigate deprives it of a “procedural right” to an investigation is therefore insufficient.

3. **Organizational plaintiffs are not exempt**  
   - The opinion does not draw a distinction between “individuals” and “organizations” for the purpose of *Zivotofsky*; the standing analysis is the same. The court treats AFL’s claim exactly as it would any other non‑individual plaintiff: the plaintiff must show a concrete injury that stems from the denial of a **statutory entitlement**. Because none exists, the organizational character of AFL is irrelevant.

### 3. Why *Ake* does not help AFL

Ake requires a **substantial need** for the evidence to prove a claim or defense and that the evidence is **unobtainable** by other means. The court notes that:

- The only “information” AFL seeks from OSC is the **administrative notices** that would be generated if OSC followed § 1214 procedures. Those notices are **not required** by any statute in a § 1216 investigation.  
- The information AFL actually wants—the FOIA records from DOJ—is already being produced by DOJ in the underlying FOIA suit. Thus the “need” is not *unobtainable*; the records are on the table in a separate case.  

Consequently, the *Ake* test is not satisfied, and the court does not need to address it further (¶ II‑B‑1).

### 4. Take‑away for future organizational suits

- **Statutory entitlement is the gate‑keeper.** Unless the governing statute expressly confers on the organization a right to receive the information (e.g., “the agency shall provide X to any requestor”), *Zivotofsky*‑type informational standing will not arise.
- **Procedural rights alone do not confer standing** when they are not linked to a protected substantive interest (Summers). Organizations must plead a concrete injury beyond the mere denial of a procedural process.
- **Discretionary enforcement schemes do not create a right to information.** Sections that give an agency discretion to investigate or discipline (e.g., § 1216, § 1214) do not, by themselves, generate a private right of action or a right to receive investigative paperwork.

### 5. Bottom line

The D.C. Circuit’s holding in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* makes clear that an NGO such as AFL cannot invoke *Zivotofsky* or *Ake* to obtain “informational” relief unless a statute **specifically grants** the organization a right to that information. Because § 1216 gives OSC only a discretionary investigative duty and no entitlement to issue notices or disclose investigative materials to AFL, the organization lacks the concrete, particularized injury required for Article III standing. The case is therefore dismissed for lack of jurisdiction.

### What procedural steps are required when a district court dismisses a case for lack of standing after a supplemental briefing order?
Question:
The appellate court vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction. In practice, what does the district court need to do next? Does it issue a new order, a summary judgment, or a dismissal with prejudice? How does this affect any possibility of a future appeal?
Answer:
**Answer**

When the appellate court vacates the district court’s judgment and **remands “with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction,”** the district court’s only remaining duty is to enter a **jurisdictional dismissal** of the case.  

The opinion in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* makes this clear.  After concluding that AFL lacked Article III standing – and therefore that the district court never had subject‑matter jurisdiction – the D.C. Circuit *“vacate[s] the district court’s judgment and remand[s] with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction.”*  The circuit does not tell the district court to “re‑try” the case, issue a summary‑judgment ruling, or grant any further relief; it simply orders a **dismissal** because the court never had the power to hear the claim.

### What the district court must do

1. **Enter a dismissal order** – The appropriate procedural vehicle is a **Rule 12(b)(1) dismissal** (or the analogous “lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction” ground under the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure).  
   * The dismissal must be **with prejudice** because the court lacks the jurisdiction to entertain the claim at all.  A jurisdictional dismissal is not a “dismissal on the merits” that could be reopened; it is a final bar to the case in that court.  

2. **State the basis** – The order should briefly recite the appellate court’s finding that the plaintiff failed to establish Article III standing, and therefore the district court never possessed subject‑matter jurisdiction.  No additional factual findings or legal analysis are required.

3. **Seal the docket** – Because the case is dismissed for jurisdictional reasons, the docket is closed.  No further briefing, discovery, or motions (including a motion for summary judgment) are permitted.

### Effect on further appeals

* **No further appeal from the district court** – A dismissal for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction is *non‑appealable* because the district court is simply acknowledging that it never had the power to decide the case.  The appellate court’s decision is already the final word on the jurisdictional issue.

* **Renewed filing in another forum** – The plaintiff may try to bring a new action in a court that properly has jurisdiction (for example, by establishing a concrete injury and redressability).  The prior dismissal, however, is **preclusive** only insofar as it shows the earlier suit was barred by lack of standing; it does not foreclose a fresh suit that overcomes the standing defect.

### Summary

In practice, after a circuit court vacates and remands “with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction,” the district court must:

* Issue a **Rule 12(b)(1) dismissal** (i.e., an order dismissing the case for lack of jurisdiction),  
* Do so **with prejudice**, and  
* Close the case, leaving no further appellate route from that judgment.

The only way the plaintiff could proceed is to file a new action that satisfies the standing requirements identified by the appellate court.
