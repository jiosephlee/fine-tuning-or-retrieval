Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Why can the Supreme Court’s *Heckler v. Chaney* doctrine prevent a court from ordering an agency to exercise its enforcement discretion?
Question:
The opinion references *Heckler v. Chaney* to explain why OSC’s discretion cannot be compelled. Could someone explain the scope of the Heckler doctrine, especially in the context of an agency’s decision whether to investigate alleged violations? Does it apply even when the statute appears to impose a mandatory duty?
Answer:
**Answer**

The *Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. 821 (1985) doctrine holds that an agency’s decision **whether to undertake, continue, or forego enforcement** of a statute is generally presumptively unreviewable because it is a matter of “*discretion*” entrusted to the agency. The Supreme Court explained that:

* “the decision whether to take enforcement action is, in the absence of statutory direction … ‘committed to the agency’s discretion’” (at 831‑32).  
* Courts may intervene only when the agency’s refusal to act is **unlawful**—for example, when the statute **mandates** action, when the agency’s decision is **arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise contrary to law**, or when the agency **fails to follow required procedures**.

Thus, the *Heckler* rule does **not** create a blanket immunity; it merely says that **the court’s role is limited to checking the legality of the agency’s discretion, not to compel the agency to exercise that discretion**.

---

### How the D.C. Circuit applied *Heckler* in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*

1. **Statutory language and “shall” versus “may”**  
   The opinion notes that **5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3)** states that OSC “*shall* conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” The district court read that “*shall*” as creating a mandatory duty, but the D.C. Circuit emphasized that the **statute also gives OSC discretion** in the *manner* of the investigation (e.g., whether to invoke the procedural scheme of § 1214). The court therefore concluded that the **substantive duty to investigate is present, but the decision how to conduct that investigation remains discretionary**.

2. **“Discretionary” as used in *Heckler***  
   The panel quoted *Heckler* (822‑33) to affirm that **when a statute merely directs an agency to “investigate” an alleged violation, the agency retains discretion about **whether* to open an investigation, **what scope** to give it, and **what remedial actions** to pursue. The court said:

   > “...the decision to investigate… is committed to the agency’s discretion. A court may not compel the agency to undertake an investigation simply because the statute uses the word *shall*; the discretion is still subject to *Heckler*’s limitation.” (para. B‑6)

3. **When *Heckler* does **not** bar judicial review**  
   The opinion reiterates *Heckler*’s two narrow exceptions:

   * **Statutory mandate** – If Congress unequivocally commands the agency to act, a court can enforce that command.  
   * **Illegality of the discretionary decision** – If the agency’s refusal is “*arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law*,” the court may intervene.

   The D.C. Circuit found **no indication** that either exception applied. OSC’s refusal was based on its interpretation that the **FOIA‑triggering condition** in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) was not satisfied, a judgment that is *within* its discretion. The court therefore concluded that *Heckler* bars the district court from ordering OSC to investigate.

4. **Interaction with “mandatory” language**  
   The opinion makes clear that **the presence of “shall” does not automatically defeat the *Heckler* presumption**. Even where a statute commands an agency to “investigate,” the agency may still have discretion regarding *how* to investigate and *whether* to pursue enforcement after the investigation. The *Heckler* doctrine applies **unless** the statute eliminates that discretion entirely, which the court did not find here.

---

### Bottom line for the question

* The **Heckler doctrine prevents courts from compelling an agency to exercise its enforcement discretion** unless the statute **explicitly removes that discretion** or the agency’s refusal is **arbitrary, capricious, or otherwise illegal**.  
* In *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer*, the D.C. Circuit applied *Heckler* to hold that **OSC’s choice not to open a § 1216 investigation is a discretionary decision that courts cannot force**—even though § 1216 uses “shall,” because the statute still leaves discretion as to the *method* and *extent* of the investigation.  
* Therefore, a plaintiff cannot rely solely on the statutory word “shall” to overcome *Heckler*; the plaintiff must show either a **clear, mandatory command** that leaves no discretion, or that the agency’s refusal is **unlawful** (arbitrary, capricious, etc.). In the present case, neither condition was satisfied.
