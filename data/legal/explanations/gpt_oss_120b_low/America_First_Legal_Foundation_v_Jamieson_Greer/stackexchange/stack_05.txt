Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### What is the relationship between FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) enforcement provision and OSC’s statutory authority under §§ 1214‑1216?
Question:
The opinion distinguishes between the FOIA trigger for OSC action (the three predicates) and the separate authority under § 1216. How do these provisions interact? If the FOIA trigger is not met, can OSC still act under § 1216, or does the FOIA provision effectively pre‑empt OSC’s ability to investigate?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) creates a *trigger* that obligates the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to open a disciplinary proceeding **only when** a court‑ordered FOIA judgment satisfies the three statutory predicates (court order, award of attorney’s fees, and a finding that the agency acted “arbitrarily or capriciously”). 5 U.S.C. § 1216, however, gives OSC an *independent* statutory authority to investigate “any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3).  

The D.C. Circuit held that the two provisions operate in parallel:

1. **The FOIA trigger is not a condition precedent** to OSC’s § 1216 power.  Even if the three predicates are not met, OSC may still open an investigation under § 1216 because § 1216 “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation …” (emphasis added).  

2. **§ 1216 does not override the discretion built into the FOIA trigger.**  § 1216 authorizes *investigation* and, *if warranted*, the same remedial actions that are available under § 1214/§ 1215, but it leaves to OSC the ordinary discretion to decide whether to pursue such an investigation (see § 1216(c)).  The Court therefore concluded that OSC’s refusal to investigate was *not* a legal error simply because the FOIA predicates were missing; the refusal was a discretionary judgment within the scope of § 1216.

Consequently, the FOIA provision does **not** pre‑empt OSC’s ability to act under § 1216.  The two statutes complement each other: the FOIA clause spells out a *mandatory* investigation in the narrow circumstance that a court order meets the three predicates, while § 1216 gives OSC a *broader*, discretionary mandate to investigate any allegation of arbitrary FOIA withholding, irrespective of whether a court order exists.

**Why the Court said this:**  
- The opinion quoted § 1216(a)(3) verbatim: “OSC *shall … conduct an investigation* of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” The language “any allegation” makes the § 1216 duty independent of the FOIA trigger.  
- The opinion also noted that § 1216(c) “commits that decision to OSC’s discretion,” reinforcing that OSC may decline to investigate even when § 1216’s substantive duty exists, so long as the decline is within its discretionary authority.  
- The district court’s view that the FOIA trigger “does not limit OSC’s power” was affirmed; the appellate court simply added that the discretion remains.

**Practical implication:**  
A requester who believes an agency is arbitrarily withholding records can petition OSC under § 1216 even if no court has yet ordered production, awarded fees, or made a “arbitrary or capricious” finding.  OSC may open an investigation, and if it finds merit, may pursue corrective or disciplinary relief under §§ 1214 and 1215.  The FOIA trigger, however, creates an additional *mandatory* pathway when a lawsuit already satisfies its three predicates, but it does not bar OSC from acting under § 1216 in the absence of those predicates.  

Thus, the two provisions are complementary, not mutually exclusive, and the absence of a FOIA trigger does **not** foreclose OSC’s statutory authority under § 1216.
