Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Does 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) create a mandatory duty for OSC to investigate FOIA withholdings, or is it discretionary?
Question:
In the opinion, the district court held that § 1216(a)(3) does not limit OSC’s power, but the appellate court says the decision to investigate is discretionary. I'm confused about the statutory language: the section says OSC "shall … conduct an investigation" of arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholdings. Does the word "shall" impose an obligatory duty, or can the agency still decline investigation under its discretion? How have courts interpreted this provision in prior cases?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The appellate court reads 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) as giving the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) *discretion* to decide whether to open an investigation of alleged arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholdings. The word “shall” in the statute does not strip OSC of its agency‑wide discretion; it merely sets the *scope* of the authority that OSC may exercise if it chooses to investigate.

---

### Why the statute is not a mandatory command

1. **Statutory text and structure**  
   § 1216(a)(3) says that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” The same subsection also says that OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved” (§ 1216(c)). The juxtaposition of “shall … conduct” with the permissive “may investigate … and seek corrective action” signals that Congress gave OSC the *power* to investigate, not an *obligation* to do so.

2. **The court’s reading of “shall”**  
   The D.C. Circuit expressly holds that the “shall” language does **not** create a non‑negotiable duty. Judge Katsas writes:

   > “the decision whether to follow § 1214’s procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is ‘discretionary.’”  
   > (*Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*, 633 F.3d at 495‑96 (6th Cir. 2011) cited).

   The appellate panel therefore concludes that OSC’s refusal to investigate was “based on a legal error” only insofar as the district court misapplied the statute; it does **not** mean OSC must investigate every allegation.

3. **Consistency with the FOIA enforcement provision**  
   The FOIA itself contains a *separate* mandatory trigger—§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—which requires OSC to act *only* when a court has ordered production, awarded fees, and *specifically* questioned whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously. The opinion emphasizes that § 1216(a)(3) is **not** limited by that trigger, but it also does not override the discretionary nature of OSC’s overall enforcement authority. The court therefore reads § 1216(a)(3) as a supplemental, optional tool, not a blanket command.

4. **Prior case law**  
   The opinion relies on earlier decisions that treat OSC’s investigative authority as discretionary:

   * **Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel**, 633 F.3d 487 (6th Cir. 2011) – held that OSC may, but does not have to, follow the procedural safeguards of § 1214 when exercising its § 1216 powers.  
   * **Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner**, No. 22‑cv‑3576, 2023 WL 9546644 (D.D.C. Sept. 29 2023) – the district court reasoned that § 1216 “does not limit OSC’s power” but also did not treat the statute as imposing a compulsory duty.

   The D.C. Circuit’s decision follows this line: it acknowledges the statutory authority to investigate *if* OSC elects to do so, but it does not read “shall” as removing that election.

---

### Practical effect

- **OSC may open an investigation** under § 1216(a)(3) when it receives an allegation of arbitrary FOIA withholding.  
- **OSC may decline** to investigate. The decision is part of the agency’s broader enforcement discretion, just as it may decline to pursue a prohibited‑personnel‑practice case under § 1214.  
- **Judicial review** of that discretion is limited. The court can assess whether OSC’s refusal was “based on a legal error” (e.g., a misinterpretation of the statutory trigger), but it cannot compel OSC to investigate every claim.

---

### Bottom line for the student

The presence of “shall” in § 1216(a)(3) is not controlling because the statute is embedded in a larger framework that mixes mandatory and permissive language. The D.C. Circuit reads the provision as granting OSC *the authority*—and *the discretion*—to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings. Hence, OSC’s decision to investigate is discretionary, not mandatory.
