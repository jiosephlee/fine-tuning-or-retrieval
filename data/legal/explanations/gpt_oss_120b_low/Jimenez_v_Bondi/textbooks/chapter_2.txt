Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Governing Law and Standard of Review  

In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the Tenth Circuit was asked to review a Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) denial of asylum and withholding of removal.  Understanding the court’s analysis requires a solid grasp of the statutory scheme that creates the two forms of protection, the evidentiary burdens placed on the alien, and the standards of review that the appellate court applies to the BIA’s factual and legal determinations.  This chapter breaks those components down and explains how they operate in the context of the opinion.

## 2.1. Definition of “refugee” and the elements of asylum under the INA  

The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) defines a **refugee** in § 1101(a)(42)(A) as any person who is **outside** his or her country of nationality **and** is unable or unwilling to return because of **persecution** or a **well‑founded fear of persecution** on account of one of five protected grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.  The term “asylum” refers to the discretionary relief the Attorney General may grant to an alien who meets the refugee definition while already present in the United States.

To obtain asylum, the applicant must **prove by a preponderance of the evidence** that he or she is a refugee.  The *refugee* inquiry breaks into three elements, each of which the petitioner must establish:

1. **Past persecution** – an incident or series of incidents that rise to the level of persecution.  The harm must be more than mere harassment, threats, or restrictions; it must be serious enough to be “offensive” and cause substantial suffering.  Courts treat the incidents **cumulatively**, looking at the total impact on the claimant.  (*Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d at 646.)

2. **Nexus** – the persecution must be "on account of" a protected ground.  The protected ground must be **a central reason** for the harm; it cannot be merely incidental or peripheral.  (*Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.3d at 646; *Dallakoti v. Holder*, 619 F.3d at 1268.)

3. **Perpetrator** – the persecutor must be the government itself or a force the government is **unable or unwilling** to control.  If a private actor harms the alien, the alien must show that the government cannot or will not protect him or her.  (*Niang v. Gonzales*, 422 F.3d at 1194.)

If the applicant establishes past persecution, the INA creates a **rebuttable presumption** that the alien has a well‑founded fear of future persecution.  The government then bears the burden to show, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the alien can relocate safely or that protection is otherwise available.  If the applicant fails to show past persecution, the alien must demonstrate directly a well‑founded fear of future persecution meeting the same three prongs.

## 2.2. Elements required for withholding of removal  

**Withholding of removal** is a mandatory (non‑discretionary) form of relief that bars the government from deporting the alien to a country where he or she would face persecution.  The statutory language is found in § 241(b)(2) of the INA.  The elements mirror those of asylum but differ in two important ways:

1. **Higher evidentiary standard** – the applicant must prove each element by **clear and convincing evidence**, a higher threshold than the preponderance required for asylum.  (*Carias‑Mejia v. Garland*, 860 F. App’x at 124.)

2. **No discretionary grant** – because withholding is mandatory, the government cannot refuse it on policy grounds; it can only succeed by defeating one of the three elements.

Thus, an alien who cannot meet the asylum burden will also fail the withholding burden unless he or she can satisfy the stricter standard of proof.

## 2.3. Burden of proof  

- **Asylum** – the alien carries the burden of **preponderance of the evidence** on all three elements (past persecution, nexus, and perpetrator).  The government may meet its burden of proof only when it wishes to rebut the presumption of future persecution (e.g., by showing safe internal relocation).  

- **Withholding of removal** – the alien still bears the burden of proof, but the standard is **clear and convincing evidence** on each element.  This heightened standard reflects the mandatory nature of the relief and the serious consequences of deportation to a country where the alien faces persecution.  

In *Jimenez*, the Tenth Circuit noted that the petitioners “failed to demonstrate” the required elements, implicitly applying the relevant burden and standard to the record before the court.

## 2.4. Standard of review for BIA findings  

When a circuit court reviews a BIA decision, the **statutory framework of 8 U.S.C. § 1252** governs the scope and depth of review.

1. **Factual findings** – Reviewed under the **substantial‑evidence** standard.  This means the court asks whether **any reasonable adjudicator** could have reached the same conclusion based on the **administrative record**.  If the record contains sufficient evidence that would support the BIA’s fact finding, the appellate court must affirm, even if the court might have decided differently.  (*Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d at 645.)

2. **Legal conclusions** – Subject to **de novo** review.  The court does not give deference to the BIA’s interpretation of statutory language, regulatory definitions, or legal standards.  The appellate court may accept, modify, or reject the BIA’s legal analysis as it deems appropriate.  

3. **Mixed‑motivation cases** – The court may consult the immigration judge’s (IJ’s) “more complete explanation” of the same factual and legal issues, even though the BIA’s order is the final agency decision.  This approach comes from *Uanreroro v. Gonzales*, 443 F.3d at 1204, and allows the court to ensure that the BIA’s reasoning was not arbitrary, capricious, or contrary to law.

In *Jimenez*, the panel emphasized that they were “not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation” while still applying the substantial‑evidence standard to the BIA’s factual determinations.

## 2.5. Key precedent governing review  

The Tenth Circuit’s approach in *Jimenez* rests on three leading cases:

| Citation | Core Holding | Relevance to *Jimenez* |
|---|---|---|
| **Singh v. Bondi**, 130 F.4th 848 (10th Cir. 2025) | Confirms that the appellate court has jurisdiction to review final removal orders, including consolidated asylum claims, and restates the limits of review to the administrative record. | Provides the statutory basis (8 U.S.C. § 1252) for the court’s authority to hear the petition and frames the “record‑based” nature of the review. |
| **Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder**, 666 F.3d 641 (10th Cir. 2012) | Establishes the substantial‑evidence standard for factual findings and clarifies the three‑prong asylum analysis (past persecution, nexus, perpetrator). | Guides the court’s assessment of whether the BIA’s factual findings on past persecution, nexus, and government protection are supported by substantial evidence. |
| **Uanreroro v. Gonzales**, 443 F.3d 1197 (10th Cir. 2006) | Allows the appellate court to consider the IJ’s more detailed explanation when reviewing a BIA decision, without substituting the IJ’s analysis for the BIA’s. | Explains why the Tenth Circuit in *Jimenez* could look to the IJ’s reasoning while still applying the proper standard of review. |

Together, these decisions create a three‑step roadmap for reviewing asylum and withholding cases:

1. **Confirm jurisdiction and scope** (Singh).  
2. **Apply the substantive asylum/withholding framework** (elements and burdens).  
3. **Review the BIA’s factual findings under substantial‑evidence** and **legal conclusions de novo**, consulting the IJ’s record when helpful (Rivera‑Barrientos; Uanreroro).

Understanding this framework equips a law student to follow the court’s reasoning in *Jimenez v. Bondi* and to anticipate how future appellate courts will evaluate similar challenges to BIA asylum determinations.
