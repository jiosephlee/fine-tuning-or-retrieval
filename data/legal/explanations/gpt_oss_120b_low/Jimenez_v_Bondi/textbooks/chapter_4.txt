Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 4: # Chapter 4: Nexus to a Protected Ground – Political Opinion  

In an asylum claim the applicant must not only show that she or he suffered past persecution, but also that the persecution was **on account of**—that is, **because of**—a statutorily protected ground.  For the petitioners in *Jimenez v. Bondi* the protected ground asserted was **political opinion**.  This chapter explains how the Tenth Circuit frames the nexus requirement, what “central reason” means in mixed‑motive cases, how the court evaluated the petitioners’ evidence, and why it concluded that the political motive was merely tangential.  

---  

## 4.1. Nexus Requirement under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)  

Section 1158(b)(1)(B)(i) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) provides that an asylum applicant must establish that the persecution was “on account of” one of the five protected grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or **political opinion**.  The phrase “on account of” creates a *nexus* between the persecutor’s motive and the protected characteristic.  The Supreme Court has described the clause as a *“causation*” requirement: the protected ground must be a **reason** for the persecution, not simply a coincidental or peripheral fact (see *Matter of Acosta*, 19 I&N Dec. 417, 422 (BIA 1985)).  

In practice, the nexus inquiry asks the fact‑finder to determine whether the persecutor’s **primary** motivation for the harmful act was the applicant’s political stance.  When the persecutor has multiple motives, the protected ground must be at least **one central reason** for the conduct.  If the political opinion is merely incidental, the statutory nexus is not satisfied.  

---  

## 4.2. “Central Reason” Test and Its Application in Mixed‑Motive Cases  

The Tenth Circuit has articulated a two‑part “central reason” test:  

1. **Identify the persecutor’s motive(s).** This is done by examining the factual record—statements by the persecutor, the context of the threat or attack, and any corroborating evidence.  

2. **Determine whether the protected ground is a central, rather than peripheral, motive.** The protected ground cannot be “incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate” to another reason for the harm.  *Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d at 646 (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)).  

When a case involves **mixed motives**, the court does not require the applicant to prove that the protected ground was the *sole* cause of the persecution, but it must be a *substantial* factor that drove the persecutor’s decision.  *O.C.V. v. Bondi*, No. 23‑9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025).  Conversely, if the evidence shows another motive—such as personal retaliation, economic interest, or a private dispute—that overwhelmingly dominates, the nexus fails.  

---  

## 4.3. Evidence of Motive: Threats Tied to Jimenez’s Investigation of Don Rafa’s Property versus Political Campaigning  

In *Jimenez*, the petitioners presented two strands of alleged motive:  

* **Political Campaign Activity.**  The family had been active Liberal Party members since 2012 and had canvassed for a candidate in the 2022 presidential election.  Beginning in May 2022, the petitioners received threatening phone calls that referenced their Liberal Party affiliation.  A brochure delivered to their factory read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.”  

* **Personal/Professional Conflict with Don Rafa.**  In June 2022, Jimenez, acting as a provisional lawyer, intervened in a property dispute involving a tenant who turned out to be “Don Rafa,” a guerrilla‑linked narco‑trafficker aligned with the Colombian president.  During his investigation Jimenez discovered that the property was a hub for illegal activity and that law enforcement had unsuccessfully attempted raids.  After this contact, the petitioners received a series of escalating threats: two gunmen held Jimenez at gunpoint, later returned to assault a factory employee and demand the family’s whereabouts, and a man physically restrained the petitioners’ younger son at school.  The gunmen explicitly linked the threats to “Don Rafa” and accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office.  

The record shows that the **most explicit statements of motive** came from the perpetrators themselves.  The assailants identified Don Rafa as the source of the threats and framed Jimenez’s actions as retaliation for interfering with a criminal enterprise, not as punishment for Liberal Party activism.  Moreover, the original phone calls that referenced the Liberal Party occurred **before** Jimenez’s investigation of the property and did not culminate in violence.  The violent incidents—gunpoint confrontations, the attempted kidnapping of the family, and the school assault—were directly tied to the property dispute and the perpetrators’ claim that Jimenez had exposed or threatened their illegal operations.  

Thus, the factual narrative presents a **mixed‑motive scenario** in which the political affiliation is present but is not the dominant driver of the violent acts.  The court’s task was to decide whether the political opinion rose to the level of a central reason.  

---  

## 4.4. Comparison to Tenth Circuit Precedent on Nexus  

The Tenth Circuit’s precedent clarifies how the “central reason” test is applied:  

* **Dallakoti v. Holder**, 619 F.3d at 1268, held that an applicant’s political belief must be a “central” motive; mere political participation that is merely “tangential” to the persecutor’s grievance does not satisfy the nexus.  The court emphasized that the persecutor’s statements must demonstrate that the protected ground was a “principal factor” in the decision to harm.  

* **Yuk v. Ashcroft**, 355 F.3d at 1236, reiterated that the reviewing court’s role is limited to determining whether **substantial evidence** supports the Board’s finding that the protected ground was a central motive; the court does not re‑weigh evidence but assesses whether any reasonable adjudicator could have reached the same conclusion.  

Applying these standards, the Tenth Circuit in *Jimenez* examined whether the Board’s finding—that the primary motive was personal retaliation—was supported by substantial evidence.  The explicit attribution of the threats to “Don Rafa” and the timing of the escalated violence—following Jimenez’s legal intervention—mirrored the reasoning in *Dallakoti*: the political activity was present but not the decisive factor.  The court therefore concluded that the Board’s assessment was consistent with the controlling precedent.  

---  

## 4.5. Court’s Conclusion: Primary Motive Was Personal/Retaliatory, Not Political  

After weighing the evidence, the panel held that the petitioners **failed** to satisfy the nexus requirement for political opinion.  The key points of the court’s reasoning were:  

1. **Substantial Evidence of a Non‑Political Motive.**  The perpetrators’ own declarations linked the threats to Jimenez’s investigation of a criminal property and to his perceived role as a “snitch” for law enforcement.  This directly ties the violence to a personal, retaliatory motive rather than to political expression.  

2. **Temporal Relationship.**  The most serious threats occurred **after** Jimenez’s involvement with the property dispute, whereas the earlier, less severe threats that mentioned the Liberal Party were not followed by violence.  The shift in the persecutors’ language demonstrates a change in motive from political to personal.  

3. **Lack of Direct Evidence Connecting the Persecutors to the Petitioners’ Political Activity.**  The record contains no testimony, police report, or documentary evidence indicating that the gunmen or Don Rafa acted because the petitioners supported a rival political candidate.  The only statements that reference politics are the pre‑investigation phone calls, which the court treated as insufficient to establish a causal link to the later assaults.  

4. **Application of the “Central Reason” Test.**  Because the political opinion was at most a **secondary** factor—if at all—it did not rise to the level of a central reason for the persecution.  Accordingly, the statutory nexus was not satisfied.  

Given these findings, the court affirmed the Board’s determination that the petitioners’ alleged persecution was **not** on account of political opinion.  Without a valid nexus, the petitioners could not meet the statutory definition of a refugee, and the asylum claim failed.  

---  

### Take‑aways for the Reader  

*When reading an asylum opinion, focus on the nexus analysis as a distinct element that sits between the factual showing of persecution and the legal conclusion of refugee status.*  

1. **Identify the protected ground asserted.**  Here, political opinion.  
2. **Extract the persecutor’s motive from the record.**  Look for explicit statements, timing, and context.  
3. **Apply the “central reason” test.**  Ask whether the protected ground is the *key* driver of the harm, not just an incidental backdrop.  
4. **Check the circuit’s precedent.**  The Tenth Circuit requires a substantial‑evidence showing that the protected ground is a central motive; it will not re‑weigh the evidence for you.  
5. **Conclude on the nexus.**  If the protected ground is merely tangential, the claim fails even if past persecution is otherwise established.  

Understanding this analytical framework equips you to dissect future opinions that grapple with the nexus requirement, and to anticipate the evidentiary thresholds that asylum claimants must meet to survive the political‑opinion prong.
