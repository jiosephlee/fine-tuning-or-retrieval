Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Factual and Procedural Background  

Understanding any immigration‑court opinion begins with a clear picture of the parties, the events that gave rise to the claim, and the procedural posture of the case.  In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the petitioners are Colombian nationals who sought asylum and withholding of removal after fleeing a series of threats and assaults that they alleged were tied to their political activity.  The Ninth (sic) Tenth Circuit’s analysis of each claim rests on the factual matrix set out below and on the procedural record that led the case to appellate review.  This chapter walks the student through the essential facts and the procedural steps, highlighting the material details that the court considered when evaluating the petitioners’ claims.

## 1.1. Petitioners’ Biographical Facts and Political Activities in Colombia  

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jiménez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons were long‑time residents of Bogotá, Colombia.  The family owned and operated a small furniture‑factory business in the capital.  Since 2012 both Jiménez and Duarte had been active members of the Colombian Liberal Party, a mainstream political organization that opposed the left‑leaning coalition that brought President Gustavo Petro to office in 2022.  During the 2022 presidential campaign the Jiménez family engaged in door‑to‑door canvassing on behalf of the Liberal Party’s candidate, Federico Gutierrez.  Their political activity placed them squarely in the public eye of local party operatives and, as the record later shows, exposed them to hostile actors who opposed the Liberal Party’s platform.

## 1.2. The Property Dispute and Encounters with “Don Rafa”  

In June 2022 Jiménez, then a provisional law graduate and intern with Colombia’s National Prosecutor’s Office, was approached by a client—a woman from San Bernardo—who sought assistance in regaining possession of a rental property.  The tenant, who refused to vacate, turned out to be the client’s ex‑husband and, more importantly, Rafael Augusto Montañez Lancheros, commonly known as “Don Rafa.”  Don Rafa was identified by law‑enforcement officials as the leader of a narco‑trafficking guerrilla group that was politically aligned with President Petro’s administration.

During Jiménez’s investigation he learned that the property was being used as a day‑rental center for migrants and as a hub for drug trafficking, prostitution, and other illicit activities.  The third floor had been sealed off by the health department, and the local police had previously conducted, but apparently failed to complete, searches and seizures.  When Jiménez inquired about the property, a police officer warned him to stay away, indicating that the location was “dangerous” and that further probing could place him at risk.

## 1.3. Sequence of Threats, Assaults, and the Family’s Flight to the United States  

Starting in late May 2022 the Jiménez household began receiving threatening phone calls that referenced their Liberal Party affiliation.  In early June a fellow party activist was kidnapped and murdered after reporting similar threats, yet the Jiménez family continued their political work.  The escalation of intimidation continued:

* **June 2022 – Threatening brochure:** A pamphlet left at the factory read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.”  
* **July 2022 – Gunpoint at the factory:** While Jiménez worked the night shift two men detained him at gunpoint, accusing him of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and demanding that he leave the neighborhood.  
* **August 2022 – Second factory visit:** The same men returned, this time assaulting an employee and demanding to know the Jiménezes’ whereabouts, explicitly linking the threats to Don Rafa.  
* **August 2022 – School incident:** A man seized young son Luis Jiménez by the mouth at his school, shouting “run to your dad” before being shoved away by his father.

Following the school incident the family sought temporary refuge at Jiménez’s mother’s rural home for three weeks.  During that stay they reported the incident to a local police patrol officer.  The officer’s response was dismissive: he suggested the police were “part of the problem,” advised the family to leave the area, and offered no concrete protection.  Concluding that the threats were unlikely to abate, the Jiménezes departed Colombia in December 2022, entering the United States without inspection or parole.

## 1.4. Initial Removal Proceedings: Notices to Appear, IJ Denial, and BIA Affirmation  

Three months after arriving in the United States, the Department of Homeland Security served each petitioner with an individual **Notice to Appear (NTA)**, charging them with removability under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA).  In August 2023 the petitioners admitted the factual allegations in the NTA and the Immigration Judge (IJ) entered a removal order finding them **inadmissible** under § 212(a)(6)(C)(i) for entering without inspection.

The petitioners then filed **asylum** and **withholding‑of‑removal** applications, naming their minor children as derivative beneficiaries.  At the merits hearing the IJ found the petitioners **credible** but concluded that they failed to meet the three statutory elements required for asylum or withholding:

1. **Past persecution** – the IJ held that the threats and isolated assaults did not rise to the level of persecution.  
2. **Nexus to a protected ground** – the IJ determined that the motive for the attacks was primarily Jiménez’s involvement in the property dispute, not his political opinion.  
3. **Government protection** – the IJ found insufficient evidence that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect the family, noting the petitioners’ limited attempts to seek police assistance.

The IJ also denied **Protection Under the Convention Against Torture (CAT)**.  The petitioners appealed the IJ’s decision to the **Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA)**, raising the same three substantive grounds and a procedural claim that the IJ had violated their due‑process right to counsel.  The BIA upheld the IJ’s findings, affirming the denial of asylum and withholding, and dismissed the due‑process claim.

## 1.5. Scope of the Appellate Review: Issues Preserved and Issues Waived  

Under **8 U.S.C. § 1252**, the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit has jurisdiction to review “final orders of removal” and, by extension, consolidated asylum claims.  The petitioners filed a petition for review of the BIA’s decision.  Their brief limited the appellate challenge to **three substantive issues**:

1. Whether the BIA erred in finding **no past persecution**.  
2. Whether the BIA erred in concluding that the alleged persecution **lacked a nexus** to a protected political opinion.  
3. Whether the BIA erred in finding that the petitioners **did not demonstrate a well‑founded fear** of future persecution because the Colombian government was **unable or unwilling** to protect them.

The petitioners **did not appeal** the BIA’s denial of CAT protection, nor did they raise the procedural claim concerning counsel on appeal.  Consequently, those issues were **waived** and are outside the scope of the appellate court’s review.  The Tenth Circuit therefore limited its analysis to the three preserved claims and applied the appropriate standards of review: **substantial‑evidence** for factual findings and deference to the BIA’s legal conclusions unless contrary to controlling precedent.
