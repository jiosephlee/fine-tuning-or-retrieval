Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 3: # Chapter 3: The Past Persecution Prong  

In order to succeed on an asylum or withholding‑of‑removal claim, a petitioner must first prove that he or she has suffered **past persecution**.  The United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, following precedent from other circuits, treats this requirement as a fact‑finding question for the immigration judge (IJ) and the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) that is reviewed on a **substantial‑evidence** standard.  In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the appellate panel scrutinized the record to determine whether the threats and assaults described by the Jimenez family rose to the level of persecution as required by the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA).  This chapter dissects the court’s analytical framework, the evidentiary standards it applied, and the way the facts were weighed under that framework.

## 3.1. Legal definition of persecution in the Ninth Circuit and its application in the Tenth Circuit  

Although the Tenth Circuit’s reasoning in *Jimenez* does not cite Ninth‑Circuit cases directly, the Ninth Circuit’s definition of “persecution” is instructive because it reflects the broader federal jurisprudence that all circuits share.  In *Matter of Kasinga*, 21 I&N Dec. 233 (BIA 1996) (adopted by the Ninth Circuit in *Caceres v. INS*, 480 F.3d 1155, 1159 (9th Cir. 2007)), persecution is “the infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ in race, religion, or political opinion in a way that is regarded as offensive, and must entail more than mere restrictions or threats to life and liberty.”  The Ninth Circuit further emphasized that **the injury must be severe enough to be more than a mere inconvenience or a fleeting threat**.  See *N. Cal. Immigr. Law Ass’n v. INS*, 32 F.3d 533, 539 (9th Cir. 1994) (recognizing that “a threat, by itself, does not amount to persecution”).

The Tenth Circuit adopts the same functional definition, as articulated in *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d 889, 893 (10th Cir. 2004).  The court there held that persecution requires “the infliction of suffering or harm … in a way regarded as offensive and must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty.”  The *Jimenez* panel reiterated this definition, noting that **the jurisdictional question is whether the cumulative facts meet the statutory meaning of persecution**, not whether each isolated incident does.  Consequently, the Tenth Circuit’s analysis proceeds by asking:  

1. **What harm did the petitioner actually suffer?**  
2. **Is that harm of a type the INA contemplates as persecution?**  
3. **Was the harm inflicted because of a protected ground (political opinion, etc.)?**  

Only if the answer to the first two sub‑questions is affirmative does the court move on to the nexus analysis.  In *Jimenez* the first prong—establishing past persecution—failed, and the appellate court therefore never reached the nexus or government‑protection inquiries.

## 3.2. The cumulative‑effects approach versus isolated‑incident analysis  

The Tenth Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that **persecution is a cumulative concept**.  An applicant need not suffer a single, dramatic act of violence; rather, the tribunal may look at the totality of circumstances to determine whether the aggregate impact rises to persecution.  This approach was articulated in *Ritonga v. Holder*, 633 F.3d 971, 975 (10th Cir. 2011), where the court stated that “we do not look at each incident in isolation, but instead consider them collectively, because the cumulative effects of multiple incidents may constitute persecution.”  

In applying the cumulative‑effects test, the court asks whether the sum of the documented threats, assaults, intimidation, and any lasting physical or psychological injury creates a **substantial and enduring risk** that is *more than a mere inconvenience*.  The test is flexible: the cumulative analysis can rescue an otherwise marginal claim if the combined facts show a pattern of sustained, targeted hostility.  However, the cumulative approach does **not** lower the substantive threshold for what qualifies as persecution.  A series of threats, even when aggregated, will not become persecution unless the threats are **serious, credible, and accompanied by actual injury or a well‑founded fear of imminent harm**.

## 3.3. Review of the petitioners’ alleged harms: phone threats, gunpoint encounters, assault on a factory employee, and psychological impact  

The Jimenez record presented a series of incidents:  

* **Phone threats** – Once or twice weekly calls warning “death to snitches from Liberal Party.”  
* **Gunpoint intimidation** – Two men held Jimenez at gunpoint at his night‑shift factory, ordering him to leave the neighborhood and accusing him of being a “snitch.”  
* **Threats against the family’s workplace** – The same men later returned, assaulted a factory employee, brandished a gun, and demanded to know the Jimenezes’ whereabouts, again invoking “snitches” and “Don Rafa.”  
* **Direct assault on the minor son** – A man placed his hand over the child’s mouth at school, forcing the child to run to his father, after which the father intervened physically.  
* **Psychological sequelae** – Both spouses testified to insomnia, anxiety, and panic attacks in their son.  

The IJ found the petitioners credible but concluded that these facts failed to meet the persecution threshold.  The appellate panel affirmed, applying the substantive definition of persecution.  The court observed that **none of the incidents involved physical injury**, and the documented threats, while serious, were **not sufficient on their own** to constitute persecution.  The court noted that the petitioners themselves conceded they had not been physically harmed and that their psychological complaints, though genuine, did not rise to the level required for persecution absent a more concrete, permanent injury.

The Tenth Circuit’s reasoning aligns with its prior holdings that **psychological harm alone, absent physical injury or a credible, ongoing threat of physical harm, is insufficient**.  In *Brandy v. Holder*, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014), the court held that “psychologically unsettling events… do not, by themselves, satisfy the persecution element.”  Likewise, in *Karki v. Holder*, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013), the court ruled that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.”  The Jimenez panel placed the same weight on these precedents, emphasizing that **the cumulative effect of the threats and the single episode of a gunpoint encounter did not cross the line into persecution** because they did not result in lasting physical injury or a pattern of systematic violence that the INA envisions.

## 3.4. Controlling cases on threats versus persecution (Karki v. Holder; Brandy v. Holder; Chhetri v. Rosen)  

Several Ninth‑ and Tenth‑Circuit decisions illustrate the boundary between **threats** and **persecution**.  

* **Karki v. Holder**, 715 F.3d 792, 805 (10th Cir. 2013) – The court held that “mere threats, even when repeated, do not rise to persecution unless they are coupled with overt acts of violence or severe injury.”  
* **Brandy v. Holder**, 590 F. App’x 744, 747 (10th Cir. 2014) – Here, the appellant suffered a 10‑hour detention and threats of harm. The court found that while the detention was unlawful, the accompanying threats were “psychologically unsettling but insufficient for persecution.”  
* **Chhetri v. Rosen**, 844 F. App’x 23, 27 (10th Cir. 2021) – The petitioner reported four telephone threats and a vague in‑person threat to his father. The court again declined to find persecution, emphasizing that “the threats were not accompanied by any physical injury or a pattern of credible, imminent danger.”  

These cases collectively demonstrate the Tenth Circuit’s **strict insistence on a concrete, severe injury or credible, imminent danger** as the dividing line.  The *Jimenez* court applied the same standard, concluding that the facts did not rise above the threshold established in *Karki* and *Chhetri*.  Even when the threats were “credible” in the sense that they originated from an armed group, the lack of injury and the limited scope of the threats (i.e., to leave a neighborhood, not to be killed) meant the statutory element of persecution remained unmet.

## 3.5. Court’s finding of insufficient substantial evidence to meet the persecution element  

Under the **substantial‑evidence** standard, the appellate court asks whether “any reasonable adjudicator could find” that the petitioner suffered persecution.  The test is highly deferential; the reviewing court does not re‑weigh the evidence but merely checks that the BIA’s factual findings are supported by “substantial evidence” in the record.  In *Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d 641, 645 (10th Cir. 2012), the Fourth Circuit explained that “the BIA’s findings of fact are conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.”  

Applying this standard, the *Jimenez* panel determined that **the record contained no substantial evidence of physical injury, no documented pattern of systematic violence, and no credible, ongoing threat of imminent bodily harm**.  The phone calls, the gunpoint episode, and the school incident were **isolated** and **did not constitute a sustained campaign of physical persecution**.  Moreover, the petitioners’ psychological complaints, while sincere, were not supported by medical documentation linking them to a specific, enduring threat that the government could not mitigate.  Consequently, the court held that **the BIA’s conclusion—that the alleged harms did not amount to past persecution—was supported by substantial evidence**, and therefore the appellate court could not overturn it.

In sum, the Tenth Circuit’s analysis in *Jimenez v. Bondi* underscores three pivotal take‑aways for law students examining the past‑persecution prong:

1. **Persecution must be more than a threat; it requires actual or imminently threatened serious injury.**  
2. **Cumulative harms are considered, but the aggregation of threats does not automatically create persecution.**  
3. **The substantial‑evidence standard heavily favors the BIA’s factual determinations, so petitioners must provide clear, documentary proof of physical injury or a credible, ongoing risk of such injury.**  

Understanding these principles equips practitioners to evaluate whether a client’s factual narrative can survive the rigorous scrutiny applied by immigration tribunals and appellate courts when assessing the past persecution element of an asylum or withholding‑of‑removal claim.
