Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### How does the Tenth Circuit treat the discretionary nature of asylum when reviewing BIA decisions?
Question:
The court repeatedly refers to asylum as a discretionary form of relief. Does this discretion mean the appellate court can only look at legal errors and not substitute its own judgment on the merits? How does this discretion interact with the substantial‑evidence standard for factual findings?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Tenth Circuit treats asylum as a *discretionary* form of relief, but that label does not mean the appellate court is limited to a pure “legal‑error only” review.  As Judge Tymkovich explains in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the court’s review is governed by the statutory scheme in 8 U.S.C. § 1252 and by its own precedent on standards of review.

---

### 1.  Discretion does not strip the court of factual review

The opinion repeatedly calls asylum “a discretionary form of relief which may be granted by the Attorney General to eligible non‑citizens” (¶ B).  This statement reflects the **policy** that the Attorney General (or, by delegation, the BIA) may deny asylum even when the applicant meets the statutory definition of “refugee.”  However, the *discretionary* label **does not eliminate the BIA’s duty to make factual findings that are supported by the record**.

The court cites its own standard:

> “We review the BIA’s findings of fact under a **substantial‑evidence** standard.  Under this standard, the BIA’s findings of fact are conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.”  (*Jimenez*, ¶ A).

Thus, while the ultimate grant of asylum is discretionary, the **underlying factual determinations**—whether the applicant suffered past persecution, whether there is a nexus to a protected ground, whether the government is unable or unwilling to protect, and whether internal relocation is feasible—are still subject to the usual de novo factual review (substantial‑evidence).  The appellate court may overturn a BIA finding only if the record lacks substantial evidence, not merely because the agency exercised its discretion.

---

### 2.  The court reviews **legal errors** in addition to factual ones

Because asylum is discretionary, the BIA has a **broader margin of appreciation** on policy questions (e.g., how to balance the “well‑founded fear” requirement against internal relocation).  The Tenth Circuit therefore applies **a more searching review of legal conclusions** than it does of factual findings.  In *Jimenez* the court notes:

> “Our review of the Board’s denial of asylum relief is limited to ‘the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.’”  (*Jimenez*, ¶ A).

The court can **vacate** the BIA’s decision if it finds that the Board misapplied the law— for example, if it required a higher standard of proof than the statutory “preponderance of the evidence” or misinterpreted the nexus requirement.  But when the issue is *factual* (e.g., whether the threats constituted persecution), the court is bound by the substantial‑evidence test.

---

### 3.  How discretion and the substantial‑evidence standard interact in practice

The opinion illustrates the interaction in each of the four asylum elements:

| Element | Discretionary aspect | Review standard applied |
|---------|----------------------|--------------------------|
| **Past persecution** | The BIA may decide that the aggregate of threats does **not** rise to persecution, even if the applicant claims otherwise. | Substantial‑evidence: the court asked whether “any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude” that the threats constitute persecution. It concluded there was substantial evidence they did not. |
| **Nexus to a protected ground** | The Board may deem the political motive “tangential” and therefore refuse asylum. | Substantial‑evidence: the court looked at the record showing the primary motive was a personal/property dispute, not politics. |
| **Unable or unwilling government** | The BIA can exercise discretion in weighing scant evidence of government protection. | Substantial‑evidence: the court examined the record of police statements and the lack of a formal report, finding sufficient evidence that the government was not shown to be unable or unwilling. |
| **Internal relocation** | Even if relocation is theoretically possible, the Board may decide it is not “reasonable” for the applicant. | Substantial‑evidence: the court found evidence (family in the countryside, short‑term safe stay) that supported the Board’s conclusion that relocation was feasible. |

In each case the court **did not substitute its own judgment on the merits**; it merely asked whether the BIA’s factual findings were supported by substantial evidence.  Where the Board’s conclusions were supported, the court affirmed despite the discretionary nature of asylum.

---

### 4.  Bottom line for your question

* **Discretionary relief ≠ pure legal‑error review.**  The Tenth Circuit still applies the substantial‑evidence standard to the BIA’s factual findings.  The court may overturn a finding only when the record lacks sufficient evidence, not simply because the agency exercised discretion.

* **Legal errors are reviewed de novo.**  If the BIA misapplies the statutory elements (e.g., the “central reason” nexus test), the court can correct that error even though asylum is discretionary.

* **The discretion shows up in the *weight* given to factual findings**, not in the *standard* of review.  The appellate court respects the BIA’s judgment so long as it is reasonably supported by the record; it does not “re‑weigh” the evidence.

Therefore, in the Tenth Circuit the discretionary character of asylum limits the **scope** of the review (the court looks for substantial‑evidence support) but does not eliminate **both** factual and legal scrutiny.  The court may intervene on legal misinterpretations, and it may vacate factual findings only when they are unsupported by substantial evidence.
