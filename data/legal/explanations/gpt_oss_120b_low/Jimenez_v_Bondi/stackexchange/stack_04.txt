Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### What constitutes ‘unable or unwilling’ government protection for asylum and withholding claims?
Question:
The opinion emphasizes police reports and the applicant’s efforts to seek protection. How much weight do courts give to a lack of formal reporting versus claims that reporting would be futile or dangerous? Are there precedents where an applicant’s fear of retaliation for reporting satisfied the “unable or unwilling” prong even without an official police complaint?
Answer:
**Answer**

The *Jimenez v. Bondi* opinion makes clear that a *failure to file a police report* is not per se fatal to an asylum or withholding claim, but it is a **critical piece of the “unable or unwilling” inquiry**.  The Court treats the petitioner's **effort (or lack thereof) to obtain government protection** as one of the two “heavy” factors that weigh in deciding whether the foreign government is unable or unwilling to protect the applicant (see **§ E, Government Protection**, 130 F.4th at 861‑62).

### 1. What the opinion says about “unable or unwilling”

The Court sets out the analytical framework as follows:

1. **Two “heavy” factors**:  
   - Whether the government **stopped or tried to stop the persecutors**; and  
   - Whether the government **offered protection** (e.g., protective custody).  

2. **Notice and opportunity**:  The government must have had **notice of the persecution and an opportunity to respond** (ibid. at 861‑62).  Consequently, a **recorded police report** (or some comparable official complaint) is the ordinary way to prove that notice was given.

3. **Failure to report is not automatically fatal**:  The Court expressly states that “a failure to report can undercut a claim … but it is not necessarily fatal” (citing **Aviles‑Gonzalez v. Garland**, 2024 WL 3066987 at *2*).  An applicant can still satisfy the protection prong by showing that **reporting would have been futile or dangerous**.

### 2. How much weight the Court gives to *lack* of formal reporting?

In *Jimenez*, the Court emphasizes that **Petitioners “made minimal, if any, efforts to reach out to the government”** (¶ E).  The record shows only two informal conversations with individual police officers, **without any formal complaint**.  The Court treats that as **substantial evidence** that the government **was not given notice** and therefore **cannot be judged** as “unable or unwilling” (¶ E).  Because the **lack of a report was not accompanied by any credible evidence that reporting would have been futile or dangerous**, the Court gives the absence of a report *great weight* in rejecting the claim.

### 3. When fear of futility or danger can substitute for a report

The opinion draws on *Aviles‑Gonzalez* and states that an applicant may satisfy the protection requirement **“by demonstrating that filing a police report would have been futile or dangerous.”**  However, the Court requires **specific, contemporaneous evidence** of that risk—e.g., testimony that the police are known to be **complicit with the persecutors**, that the applicant **has been threatened for reporting**, or that the **government has a pattern of refusing protection** in similar circumstances.

In *Jimenez* the Court finds the contrary:

* The record shows **government action** (searches and seizures) on the very property tied to the persecutors, suggesting the state was **aware and trying to intervene**.  
* The only statements from police were **generic warnings** (“you should leave”) rather than **explicit threats** for reporting.  
* No evidence was offered that a **formal complaint** would have exposed the family to greater danger.

Because **no concrete proof** was presented that a formal report would have been futile or put the family at greater risk, the Court **rejects** the “futility” argument and gives **substantial weight** to the lack of reporting.

### 4. Precedents where fear of retaliation satisfied the prong

The opinion references **Aviles‑Gonzalez** (2024) as the primary precedent allowing a claim to survive without a report **if the applicant can show**:

* The **persecutors are allied with the government**, or  
* The government **punishes or ignores complainants** in a systematic way.

In *Aviles‑Gonzalez*, the Fifth Circuit (cited by the Tenth Circuit) held that an applicant from Honduras could establish “unable or unwilling” by demonstrating that **any police report would have led to retaliation**, supported by **country‑condition evidence** and **personal testimony** of prior reprisals against complainants.

However, *Jimenez* underscores that **such evidence must be on the record**.  Mere speculation that “the police are corrupt” is insufficient; the applicant must **show**—through testimony, affidavits, or reliable country‑condition reports—that **reporting would have directly endangered the applicant**.

### 5. Practical take‑away for future claimants

| Factor | How *Jimenez* weighs it | What you need to show |
|--------|------------------------|-----------------------|
| Formal report (or equivalent notice) | **Heavy** – absence = strong evidence government not given chance | Either file a police report **or** produce contemporaneous evidence that you **could not have** filed without imminent danger or futility. |
| Evidence that reporting would be futile/dangerous | **Not enough** on its own; must be **specific and contemporaneous** | Affidavits or testimony describing **direct threats** for filing, **systemic police‑perpetrator collusion**, or **country‑condition reports** documenting that reporting leads to retaliation. |
| Government’s actual response (or lack thereof) | **Key** – the court looks for concrete actions (or inaction) after notice | Show that after you reported (or after you attempted), the government **failed to act** or **actively participated** in the persecution. |

### Conclusion

In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the Tenth Circuit gives **substantial weight** to a petitioner’s **failure to obtain official notice** of the persecution, and it **does not treat that failure as fatal** only when the petitioner can **clearly demonstrate**—with specific, on‑record evidence—that reporting would have been **futile or would have exposed them to further danger**.  Absent such proof, the lack of a formal police complaint strongly supports a finding that the government was **neither shown to be unable nor unwilling** to protect the applicant.  Therefore, to satisfy the “unable or unwilling” prong without a police report, a claimant must provide **contemporaneous, detailed evidence** that the act of reporting would have been **dangerous or ineffective**; speculative or post‑hoc assertions are insufficient.
