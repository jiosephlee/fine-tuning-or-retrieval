Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### What is the significance of the “well‑founded fear” presumption from past persecution in asylum cases?
Question:
The opinion notes that past persecution gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of a well‑founded future fear. How strong is this presumption in the Tenth Circuit, and what level of evidence is required to overcome it if the applicant’s past persecution claim is weak or borderline?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Tenth Circuit treats the “well‑founded fear” presumption that flows from a showing of past persecution as a **rebuttable presumption, not a conclusive one**.  In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 58 F.4th 908 (10th Cir. 2025), the court reiterated the standard set out in *Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d 641, 646 (10th Cir. 2012): an applicant who proves past persecution “gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution.”  The presumption is therefore **strong enough to shift the burden to the government**—the government must produce evidence that the applicant’s fear is not well‑founded—but **it does not relieve the applicant of any evidentiary responsibility**.  

### How strong is the presumption in the Tenth Circuit?

1. **Burden‑shifting, not burden‑eliminating.**  
   Once past persecution is established, the government must show, by a *preponderance of the evidence*, that the applicant could safely relocate within the country or that the fear of future persecution is not well‑founded.  The court in *Jimenez* emphasized that “the presumption is rebuttable” and that “the government may rebut an applicant’s presumption … if it shows … that the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country” (see *Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.3d at 646).

2. **The presumption is not dispositive.**  
   The Tenth Circuit has repeatedly held that the government’s evidence may overcome the presumption even when the applicant’s past‑persecution claim is “weak or borderline.”  In *Jimenez* the court found “substantial evidence” that the petitioners’ alleged threats and emotional distress did **not** rise to the level of past persecution; consequently, the presumption never attached.  Even where past persecution is found, the government can still defeat the claim by demonstrating that internal relocation is reasonably available (*Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.3d at 646) or that the state is able and willing to protect the applicant (*Singh v. Bondi*, 130 F.4th at 861‑62).

### What evidence must the government produce to overcome the presumption?

When the record contains a **borderline showing of past persecution**—for example, a pattern of threats, limited physical harm, or psychological injury—the government must supply evidence that satisfies two prongs:

| Government’s Evidentiary Burden | Illustrative Evidence (from *Jimenez* and related cases) |
|--------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|
| **(1) Internal relocation is feasible** – evidence that the applicant could live safely elsewhere in the home country. | Testimony or affidavits from relatives or officials indicating that the applicant (or members of the applicant’s family) have lived without harm in other regions; documentation of the applicant’s ability to obtain housing, employment, and school for children in those areas.  In *Jimenez* the court noted the petitioners could have stayed with the applicant’s mother in the countryside, showing relocation was possible. |
| **(2) The government is able and willing to protect** – proof that the state would intervene or at least not be complicit. | Police reports, official investigations, or statements from government officials showing the state took (or would take) steps to stop the persecutors; evidence that the persecutors are not agents of the state or are beyond its control.  The court in *Jimenez* pointed out that Colombian police had already attempted searches of the drug‑linked property, undermining the claim that the government was unable or unwilling to act. |
| **(3) The applicant’s fear is not well‑founded** – evidence that the threat has abated or is not likely to recur. | Recent lack of threats, disappearance of the persecutor, or a change in the applicant’s political activity that removes the motive for future harm.  The Tenth Circuit has allowed the government to rebut the presumption by showing a “fundamental change” in circumstances (*Rivera‑Barrientos*, 666 F.3d at 646). |

If the government can produce **substantial evidence**—the same standard the BIA and IJ must meet—showing any of the above, the presumption is defeated.  The applicant then must **re‑show past persecution or a well‑founded fear** on their own.

### Practical takeaway for borderline cases

1. **Document any relocation options** (family ties, housing, employment) in the home country and obtain corroborating affidavits.  
2. **Gather any official contacts** (police reports, court filings, statements from local officials) that demonstrate the government’s response—or lack thereof—to the alleged harm.  
3. **Show that the threat is ongoing** (e.g., recent communications, recent incidents) if you want the presumption to survive; otherwise, be prepared for the government to argue that the fear is no longer well‑founded.

In sum, the Tenth Circuit’s “well‑founded fear” presumption is a **significant evidentiary advantage** for an asylum seeker who can convincingly prove past persecution, but it is **easily rebutted** when the government meets its burden with credible, preponderant evidence that relocation is viable, the state can protect the applicant, or the fear has dissipated.  A weak or borderline past‑persecution claim therefore demands robust proof of the government’s inability or unwillingness to protect, otherwise the presumption will be overcome.
