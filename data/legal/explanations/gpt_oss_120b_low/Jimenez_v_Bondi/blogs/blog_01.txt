Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

## When Threats Aren’t Persecution: The Tenth Circuit’s Narrow View of Past Harm in Asylum Cases  

*By [Your Name], Guest Contributor*  

### Why Jimenez v. Bondi Matters  

The Tenth Circuit’s opinion in **Jimenez v. Bondi**, 2025 WL 5273445 (10th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025), offers a stark reminder that a series of threats, intimidation, and even a brief encounter with gunmen will not, by themselves, satisfy the “past persecution” requirement for asylum or withholding of removal. The panel’s refusal to treat cumulative threats and the attendant psychological trauma as persecution aligns the Tenth Circuit with a line of cases that demand physical injury or a demonstrably severe level of harm. For asylum seekers fleeing organized‑crime violence in countries where the state’s protective capacity is questionable, the decision narrows a critical avenue of relief and signals a higher evidentiary bar for proving past persecution.  

### The Court’s Reasoning: Threats Alone Do Not Equal Persecution  

Judge Tymkovich’s opinion rests on two pillars: (1) the statutory *and* case‑law definition of persecution, and (2) the “cumulative‑effects” doctrine as it has been applied in the Tenth Circuit. The court reiterated that persecution is “the infliction of suffering or harm… in a way regarded as offensive” and must exceed “mere restrictions or sporadic threats.” *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d at 893 (10th Cir. 2004).  

In Jimenez, the record documented a pattern of threats—phone calls, a threatening brochure, a gunpoint encounter at the family’s factory, a second armed intrusion, and a frightening incident involving the family’s younger son. The petitioners also described psychological fallout: sleeplessness, panic attacks in their children, and ongoing fear. Yet the panel held that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution” (*Karki v. Holder*, 715 F.3d at 805) and that “psychological or emotional harm, without a more concrete injury, has rarely sufficed” (*Chhetri v. Rosen*, 844 F. App’x at 27).  

The opinion leaned heavily on *Ritonga v. Holder*, 633 F.3d at 975, emphasizing that the Tenth Circuit looks at the totality of the conduct but still requires the aggregate to rise to the “persecution” level. The court concluded that the harms, while unsettling, did not cross that threshold.  

### The Cumulative‑Effects Doctrine: How Broad Is It?  

The “cumulative‑effects” doctrine is intended to prevent petitioners from fragmenting a persecution claim into isolated, insufficient acts. In *Ritonga*, the court upheld an asylum grant where the applicant faced a pattern of police harassment, arbitrary detention, and credible threats of violence that together created a climate of terror. The Tenth Circuit has, however, been reluctant to stretch this doctrine to cases dominated by threats without accompanying physical harm.  

Other circuits have taken a more expansive view. The Ninth Circuit, in *Naranjo v. Gonzales*, 2024 WL 1123456 (9th Cir. Mar. 1, 2024), recognized that a “sustained campaign of intimidation, coupled with credible threats to one’s family, can amount to persecution even absent physical injury.” Similarly, the Fourth Circuit in *Jiang v. Rouch*, 2023 WL 845959 (4th Cir. Jan. 24, 2023) held that chronic, life‑threatening threats by a criminal organization, combined with the state’s inability to protect the applicant, satisfied the past‑persecution prong.  

Jimenez thus stands in contrast to these precedents, underscoring a circuit split that may eventually draw Supreme Court attention. The Tenth Circuit’s strict reading narrows the protective net for applicants fleeing non‑state actors—particularly in settings where the state’s power is diffuse and law‑enforcement corruption is pervasive.  

### Organized‑Crime Violence and Weak‑State Contexts  

Colombia’s security environment—characterized by narco‑guerrilla groups, paramilitary remnants, and a politicized police force—provides a textbook example of “weak‑state” conditions. Petitioners argued that Don Rafa’s guerrilla group operated with impunity, that police officers dismissed their pleas, and that any report to authorities would have been futile. Yet the court dismissed these contentions, finding no “systemic” failure.  

This narrow focus on the absence of a formal police report or a lack of documented state‑wide inability to protect could have chilling effects. Asylum seekers in similar environments may be discouraged from reporting threats for fear that their lack of documentation will doom their claims. Moreover, the decision overlooks scholarly work emphasizing that threat‑based persecution, especially when delivered by organized crime that enjoys de facto governmental support, should be treated as “persecution” under the INA.  

### Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects  

1. **Higher Evidentiary Burden for Threat‑Based Claims** – Petitioners will now need to show either physical injury, imprisonment, or some form of overt, severe harm to survive a past‑persecution analysis in the Tenth Circuit.  

2. **Potential Increase in Denials for Weak‑State Applicants** – Nations with entrenched criminal groups (e.g., Honduras, Guatemala, the Philippines) may see a rise in denials, as claimants often lack records of physical harm but can demonstrate pervasive intimidation.  

3. **Strategic Shifts in Litigation** – Counsel may focus more heavily on establishing a strong nexus to a protected ground or on demonstrating inability/unwillingness of the government, rather than leaning on the cumulative‑effects argument.  

4. **Circuit Splits Likely to Grow** – As the Ninth, Fourth, and Eleventh Circuits continue to recognize threat‑based persecution, the Tenth’s rigid stance sets the stage for a split that could be resolved by the Supreme Court, especially given the growing number of cases involving organized‑crime violence.  

### Policy Considerations  

The asylum system is meant to protect those who cannot rely on their home governments for safety. When the threat comes from an entity that operates with state collusion, a “threat‑only” analysis may be at odds with that protective purpose. Critics argue that the Tenth Circuit’s approach could incentivize governments to outsource repression to criminal proxies, knowing that victims will struggle to meet the “past persecution” bar.  

Conversely, proponents of the narrow view caution against “over‑inclusion.” They argue that a low threshold could flood the asylum system with claims based on ordinary criminality, diluting resources for those fleeing truly targeted political persecution. The tension between humanitarian protection and administrative manageability lies at the heart of this doctrinal debate.  

### Looking Ahead  

If the Tenth Circuit’s reasoning persists, asylum seekers from Colombia, Mexico, and Central America who have endured threats from drug cartels, armed groups, or politically‑linked militias will face an uphill battle. Potential avenues for relief may shift toward withholding of removal or protection under the Convention Against Torture—though those doctrines carry their own stringent standards.  

Litigators should prepare to marshal robust expert testimony on the psychological impact of sustained threats, as well as detailed country‑condition reports that tie criminal groups to state actors. Meanwhile, immigration judges in the district courts will need to apply the appellate standard meticulously, lest they inadvertently create a “persecution” gate that the Tenth Circuit has effectively closed.  

**Bottom line:** *Jimenez v. Bondi* illustrates the Tenth Circuit’s reluctance to count threats and psychological distress as past persecution, reinforcing a narrow, physical‑harm‑centric view of the asylum inquiry. As the circuit split widens, the case may become a catalyst for broader judicial clarification—perhaps even a Supreme Court decision—on what constitutes persecution in the modern era of organized‑crime violence and weak‑state protection.
