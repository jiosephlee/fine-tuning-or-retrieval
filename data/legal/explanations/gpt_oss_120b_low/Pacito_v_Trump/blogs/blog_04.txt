Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

## Staying Orders and the Balance of Harms: How the Ninth Circuit Applied the *Nken* Factors in *Pacito v. Trump*

The Ninth Circuit’s September‑12, 2025 order in *Pacito v. Trump* may look at first glance like a routine stay pending appeal, but its reasoning offers a vivid snapshot of how courts are wrestling with the tension between deference to presidential immigration power and the statutory protections afforded to refugees. By unpacking the court’s application of the four‑factor *Nken* test, we can see where the balance of harms tipped, why the panel was comfortable granting a stay on the President’s moratorium but hesitant to shelter the administration from its alleged violations of the Refugee Act and the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). More importantly, the decision foreshadows how future emergency stays in immigration cases may be decided.

### Why *Pacito* matters

The case pits two distinct statutory regimes against each other. On the one hand, 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) gives the President sweeping authority to suspend the entry of any class of aliens “whenever the President finds that the entry … would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” On the other, the Refugee Act of 1980 (codified throughout the Immigration and Nationality Act) and the APA impose procedural and substantive obligations on the executive branch when it alters or defunds refugee‑resettlement programs.

The district court in Western Washington had already enjoined enforcement of the President’s refugee‑admission moratorium and ordered the State Department to reinstate terminated cooperative agreements that fund the “reception and placement” services required by 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The government appealed, and the Ninth Circuit was asked to decide whether to stay those injunctions while the appeal proceeds. The panel’s order is the only publicly available articulation of its stay analysis, and it offers a template for how courts may treat emergency relief in the volatile intersection of immigration authority and refugee protection.

### The *Nken* framework revisited

In *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), the Supreme Court distilled the stay standard into four factors:

1. **Likelihood of success on the merits**  
2. **Irreparable injury**  
3. **Balance of hardships** (injury to the moving party vs. the non‑moving party)  
4. **Public interest**

The Ninth Circuit reiterated that the first two factors are “the most critical.” Its order therefore reads almost as a checklist, but the way it weights each factor reveals the court’s underlying policy calculus.

#### 1. Likelihood of success on the merits – a quick win for the government

The panel leaned heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii* to conclude that the government is “likely to prevail” on the question of whether the President lawfully exercised the authority in § 1182(f). The court noted that the statute’s language is crystal‑clear: the President need only make a “finding” that entry would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States.” The record shows that such a finding was made in the executive order itself. Accordingly, the Ninth Circuit found that the government met the high bar of likelihood of success for at least that prong of the plaintiffs’ claims.

The panel was less confident about the APA claim. It explicitly stated that “the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA,” because the termination of funding for reception and placement services may run afoul of the statutory duties in § 1522. By singling out the APA claim, the court signaled that the stay should not blanket‑cover all of the district court’s relief—only the portions tied to the President’s immigration authority.

#### 2. Irreparable injury – the refugee’s perspective

Irreparability is where the Ninth Circuit’s analysis becomes more nuanced. The order acknowledges that refugees who have already been admitted—or who have conditional approvals—face “irreparable injury” if the moratorium is enforced. The injury is not purely monetary; it is a deprivation of a lawful right to enter the United States, a right that, once lost, cannot be restored by a later injunction.

Nevertheless, the court balanced that concern against the possible harm to the government and the broader national interest. By staying only the portions of the injunction that relate to the admission moratorium (and not the statutory funding obligations), the panel effectively limited the scope of potential irreparable harm to those refugees whose cases hinge on the admission question. For the plaintiffs challenging the APA claim, the court found that the injury—temporary loss of services—while serious, did not rise to the level of “irreparable” in the sense required for a stay.

#### 3. Balance of hardships – the “public interest” starts to overlap

The third factor collapsed into the public‑interest analysis because the panel treated the government’s “broad discretion” and foreign‑policy implications as a counter‑vulnerability. The order repeatedly cites the “broad discretion” conferred by § 1182(f) and notes that “the remaining factors…favor the United States as well.” By invoking the President’s authority to protect national security, the court effectively argued that the harm to the government in being forced to admit refugees—potentially contrary to the President’s security assessment—outweighs the hardship to the individual refugees.

Judge Lee’s partial dissent underscores the fragility of that balance. He argues that the statutory language of § 1522 is permissive, not mandatory, and that the government should not be forced to fund services it has not yet secured. His view would tilt the balance of hardships sharply in favor of the government, but the majority preferred a narrower stay that leaves the funding issue unresolved for the appeal.

#### 4. Public interest – national security versus humanitarian obligations

The public‑interest prong is perhaps the most politically charged. The majority’s decision leans heavily on the premise that a President’s determination of what is “detrimental to the interests of the United States” is entitled to great deference, especially in the context of immigration and national security. The court’s order frames the public interest as aligning with the executive’s discretion to safeguard the nation—a stance consistent with the *Hawaii* line of cases.

Conversely, the APA claim raises a different public‑interest consideration: compliance with congressional mandates to provide essential services to refugees who have already been admitted. By refusing to stay the district court’s order that reinstates cooperative agreements, the Ninth Circuit signals that the government’s failure to meet its statutory obligations is a public‑interest harm in its own right. The split outcome—stay on the admission moratorium, no stay on the funding injunction—reflects a nuanced view that the public interest is not monolithic but compartmentalized.

### Doctrinal implications

*Pacito* dovetails with a growing body of post‑*Hawaii* precedent that treats the President’s immigration powers as a “zone of deference” while still permitting judicial review of ancillary statutory duties. The Ninth Circuit’s order draws a clear line:

* **Immigration‑entry authorities** (the core of § 1182(f)) receive the highest level of deference; courts are unlikely to block a stay when the government shows a plausible presidential finding.  
* **Statutory implementation duties** (e.g., § 1522’s funding mandates) remain subject to ordinary APA review. Even when the executive enjoys discretionary authority, Congress can bind the agency with “shall” language that triggers enforcement.

Judge Lee’s dissent raises a question that will likely surface in future cases: does the word “shall” in a funding provision automatically create a mandatory spending requirement, or does it merely set conditions for the exercise of a discretionary grant authority? The majority’s reading—that § 1522 imposes at least some enforceable obligations—suggests a more expansive view of congressional intent, one that could tighten judicial scrutiny of agency funding decisions in the immigration context.

### Potential downstream effects

1. **Future emergency stays** – Plaintiffs seeking a stay of a district court injunction that blocks a presidential immigration action will now have a clearer roadmap: demonstrate a strong likelihood of success on the narrow statutory basis of the executive’s authority, and argue irreparable injury tied directly to the admission‑restriction provision. Courts may be less inclined to stay injunctions that touch on statutory funding duties, especially where the agency has not articulated a concrete plan to meet those duties.

2. **APA challenges to refugee‑resettlement funding** – Agencies that abruptly terminate contracts for refugee services may face heightened risk of injunctions that survive stays, because courts are signaling willingness to enforce the “shall” obligations in § 1522. Agencies will likely need to produce a more robust evidentiary record showing that they have a viable replacement plan before seeking a stay on a funding‑related injunction.

3. **Presidential deference limits** – While the Ninth Circuit reaffirms the broad deference accorded to the President, the split opinion underscores that deference is not absolute. When the President’s actions intersect with specific statutory mandates, especially those that allocate resources, courts may draw a line and apply the ordinary *Nken* calculus without the “presumption of validity” that often protects immigration orders.

4. **Litigation strategy for refugee advocacy groups** – Groups can now structure their complaints to bifurcate the challenge: a constitutional or statutory claim attacking the President’s entry suspension, and a parallel APA claim targeting the agency’s funding termination. By doing so, they increase the chance that at least part of the district court’s relief will survive any stay.

### Final thoughts

The Ninth Circuit’s September order in *Pacito v. Trump* is a microcosm of the broader judicial balancing act that defines contemporary immigration litigation. The panel’s application of the *Nken* factors affirms the high bar set for courts to halt a President’s immigration decision, yet it also demonstrates that statutory obligations—particularly those concerning the welfare of refugees already admitted—retain teeth.

For practitioners, the takeaway is clear: when seeking—or resisting—an emergency stay, the key is to focus on the precise statutory language that underlies the injunction. Is the contested provision a pure grant of discretion, or does it contain enforceable “shall” duties? The answer will dictate which *Nken* factor dominates the analysis and, ultimately, whether the stay will stand. As the *Pacito* saga moves toward a merits opinion, the litigation will likely clarify the contours of § 1522’s mandatory versus permissive language, setting the stage for the next wave of disputes over how far presidential deference can stretch when Congress has spoken about refugee assistance in unequivocal terms.
