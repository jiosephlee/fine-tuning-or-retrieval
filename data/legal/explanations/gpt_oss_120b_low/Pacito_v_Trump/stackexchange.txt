Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### What is the standard for granting a stay pending appeal and how did the Ninth Circuit apply it in Pacito v. Trump?
Question:
The opinion cites the four‑factor test from *Nken v. Holder* and says the first two factors are “most critical.” I’m confused about how the court weighed each factor here, especially the likelihood of success on the merits for the government’s claim that the President’s authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) is unreviewable. Did the court consider the plaintiffs’ arguments under the APA at all when deciding the stay?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Ninth Circuit applied the classic four‑factor stay test from *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), and treated the first two factors—(1) likelihood of success on the merits and (2) irreparable injury—as “most critical.”  The panel’s order (Pacito v. Trump, 9th Cir. Sept. 12 2025) spells out how each factor was weighed.

---

### 1. Likelihood of success on the merits  

The government’s appeal rests on two separate arguments:

| Issue | Government’s claim | Ninth Circuit’s assessment |
|-------|-------------------|----------------------------|
| **A. Authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)** | The President’s suspension of refugee admissions is “unreviewable” because § 1182(f) gives the President broad, discretionary power to suspend entry of any class of aliens “whenever … detrimental to the interests of the United States.” | The court held this claim **strongly favorable** to the government.  The order says the government “is likely to prevail” on the plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of the Executive Order, citing *Hawaii v. Trump*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018) for the proposition that a “searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications” is not required.  The panel emphasized that the statutory text of § 1182(f) is “clear” and that the President “undoubtedly fulfilled” the minimal factual finding required by the statute.  Consequently, the court treated the success‑on‑the‑merits factor as **satisfied** for the immigration‑authority portion of the case. |
| **B. APA challenges to the defunding of USRAP** | Plaintiffs allege that the termination of funding and cooperative agreements violates the Administrative Procedure Act because the agency failed to follow required rule‑making and notice‑and‑comment procedures. | The order expressly **rejects** a likelihood of success on this prong.  It states: “the government is **not likely to prevail** on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA.”  The court therefore found the government’s APA claim **weak**, but because the first factor is assessed **overall**, the strong showing on the § 1182(f) claim outweighed the APA deficiency.  The panel notes that the APA issue will be addressed in the merits opinion, but for purposes of the stay it does not defeat the government’s overall showing of likely success. |

Thus, the Ninth Circuit concluded that, taken as a whole, the government makes a “strong showing” of success on the merits because the core claim—presidential authority under § 1182(f)—is almost foreclosed.

---

### 2. Irreparable injury  

The court found that, without a stay, the government would suffer irreparable harm.  Two points are made:

1. **Operational disruption** – The district court’s injunction forces the State Department to halt the suspension of refugee admissions and to continue funding cooperative agreements, which the government says it cannot sustain given the $800 million rescission and its lack of a replacement resettlement agency.  The order remarks that the government “cannot engage in a searching inquiry” into the President’s justification, implying that the injunction would cause immediate and ongoing disruption to executive policy.

2. **Financial and logistical injury** – The government’s inability to secure a new agency “as the record shows” would leave it unable to meet statutory service obligations, creating a structural break that cannot be remedied later.

Because the government would be forced to operate a program it claims is beyond its statutory authority and without the required funding, the court held the injury to be **irreparable**.

---

### 3. Balance of hardships  

Having found the government likely to succeed and to suffer irreparable injury, the court turned to the remaining two, less‑critical factors:

* **Hardship to the plaintiffs** – The plaintiffs would lose the benefit of the injunction (i.e., the ability to obtain or retain refugee status and services).  However, the panel viewed these harms as “substantive but not irreversible” because the refugees could still seek relief after the appeal is resolved, and the services at issue are statutory obligations that the government can ultimately satisfy.

* **Public‑interest considerations** – The order cites the “domestic and foreign interests of the United States” that flow from the President’s “broad discretion” under § 1182(f).  The court concludes that the public interest “favours the United States” in allowing the executive to implement its immigration policy, especially during a period of national security concern.

Together, these factors tip the balance in favor of a stay.

---

### 4. Result  

Applying the four‑factor test, the Ninth Circuit **granted a stay** of the district court’s preliminary injunctions (both the February 28 and March 24 orders).  The stay is limited only by the “limited carve‑out” the panel previously left untouched (the injunction still applies to conditionally approved refugees), but otherwise the injunctions are stayed in their entirety.

---

### 5. Did the court consider the APA arguments?  

Yes.  The order explicitly addresses the APA claim and notes that the government is **“not likely to prevail”** on that particular challenge.  The court therefore **did consider** the plaintiffs’ APA arguments, but it held that the weakness of that claim does **not defeat** the stay because the stronger § 1182(f) claim satisfies the first‑factor requirement.  The opinion therefore makes clear that a deficiency in one prong of the merits analysis does not automatically preclude a stay when another prong is overwhelmingly persuasive.

---

**Bottom line:**  
The Ninth Circuit used the *Nken* four‑factor test, gave the first two factors (likelihood of success and irreparable injury) the most weight, found the government’s § 1182(f) claim decisive, acknowledged a weak APA claim, and concluded that the balance of hardships and public‑interest considerations favored a stay.  Consequently, the district court’s injunctions were stayed pending appeal.

### Does 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) give the President unreviewable discretion to suspend refugee admissions, or is the action subject to any judicial review?
Question:
The majority says the President “has undoubtedly fulfilled” the statutory finding requirement and that the court should not conduct a “searching inquiry” into his justification. Is that a declaration that the provision is non‑justiciable, or does the court still retain authority to review whether the finding was arbitrary or contrary to law?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The Ninth Circuit treats the presidential “finding” required by **8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)** as a *procedural* prerequisite that the President satisfies on his own behalf, and it accords that finding **extreme deference**.  In *Pacito v. Trump* the panel expressly states that the President “has undoubtedly fulfilled” the statutory finding requirement and that the court “cannot engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.”  Accordingly, the court treats the exercise of the § 1182(f)‑authority as **non‑justiciable** in the sense that it will not review the substantive merits of the President’s determination that refugee admissions would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States.”  

However, the panel does **not** declare the entire provision unreviewable.  It reserves the right to review the executive action **to the extent** it implicates other statutory schemes—most notably the Refugee Act’s resettlement‑service mandates in **8 U.S.C. § 1522** and the Administrative Procedure Act.  The order therefore draws a line:

* **§ 1182(f) itself** – the President’s finding and the consequent suspension of refugee admissions are presumed valid and are not subject to a substantive “searching” review.  
* **Collateral statutory requirements** – the same executive order may be challenged as violating § 1522’s service‑provision obligations (or APA procedural requirements), and the court can review those claims.

In sum, **§ 1182(f) gives the President near‑absolute discretion that is insulated from ordinary judicial scrutiny**, but that discretion does not immunize the President (or the executive branch) from review of **independent statutory violations** that arise from the same action.  The Ninth Circuit’s approach follows the Supreme Court’s immigration deference doctrine (see *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018)) and applies the four‑factor stay analysis accordingly.  

---

### How the opinion reaches this conclusion

1. **Statutory text** – The panel quotes § 1182(f): the President “may… suspend the entry of… any class of aliens… *whenever the President finds* … would be detrimental….”  The only statutory condition is the *finding*; no further standard of reasonableness is written into the statute.

2. **Deference doctrine** – Citing *Trump v. Hawaii*, the court notes that the authority “exudes deference” and “vests [the President] with ample power” to impose entry restrictions.  This mirrors the Supreme Court’s holding that the political branches “are best suited to balance the foreign‑policy and national‑security interests at stake.”

3. **No “searching inquiry”** – The order expressly says the court “cannot engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.”  That language signals a **pre‑judgment of non‑justiciability** for the § 1182(f) finding itself.

4. **Reservation of review on other grounds** – The panel immediately pivots to a different issue: whether the administration has complied with **§ 1522** (mandatory‑language “shall” provisions concerning reception and placement services) and the **APA**.  The court states that “the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA,” and it therefore **orders the reinstatement of cooperative agreements** required by § 1522.

5. **Practical effect** – The stay order leaves the injunction on the suspension of refugee admissions **in place**, while only lifting the injunctions that affect the provision of resettlement services.  That split underscores that the court treats the admission suspension as **unreviewable**, but the ancillary service‑provision obligations remain **reviewable**.

---

### Why this is not a blanket bar to judicial review

*The opinion does not say that *all* actions taken under § 1182(f) are immune from any court scrutiny.* It limits the immunity to the **President’s discretionary finding** and the **substantive suspension** that flows from it.  The court still exercises jurisdiction over:

* **Statutory compliance** – § 1522 imposes conditioning language (“shall… to the extent of available appropriations”) that the court can enforce, as shown by its order to reinstate funding agreements.
* **Procedural requirements** – The APA claim that the agency’s termination of contracts was “arbitrary and capricious” remains alive and is the basis for the court’s partial relief.

Thus, the Ninth Circuit’s approach is consistent with the broader Supreme Court line that **immigration decisions are highly deferential**, but **the executive branch is not a law‑free zone**; it must still obey other independent statutory duties.

---

### Bottom line for a law student

- **§ 1182(f) = high‑level, largely unreviewable presidential discretion** (the finding that refugee admission is “detrimental” is presumed valid; courts will not second‑guess it).
- **Judicial review is still available for separate statutory violations** (e.g., failure to provide mandated resettlement services under § 1522 or procedural flaws under the APA).
- The *Pacito* order reflects this two‑track analysis: it **leaves the suspension of admissions untouched** while **ordering the reinstatement of services** because the latter implicates obligations outside the scope of § 1182(f).

### Is 8 U.S.C. § 1522 a mandatory funding command or merely an authorization for refugee resettlement services?
Question:
Judge Lee argues that the word “authorization” in the title makes the provision permissive, while the majority treats the statutory “shall” language in § 1522(a) as creating a mandatory duty. How do courts typically interpret the interplay between the permissive language in the title and the mandatory language in the body of a statute?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Courts treat a permissive word in a statute’s title as **ordinary descriptive language** and do **not** let it defeat a clear‑on‑its‑face mandatory command in the operative text. In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit followed this well‑settled rule: the title “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees” was deemed merely a label, while the “shall” language of 8 U.S.C. § 1522(a) was read as creating a binding duty to provide the specified services, subject only to the limitation “to the extent of available appropriations.” Judge Lee’s dissent is an outlier; the majority’s approach reflects the mainstream interpretive practice.

**Why the title is not controlling**

1. **Statutory titles are not operative provisions**. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that a statute’s short title or caption “does not control the meaning of the substantive provisions” (*United States v. Caines*, 475 U.S. 475, 483 (1986)). The title is a *heading* meant to identify the chapter, not to prescribe the legal obligations of the section.

2. **Reading the text as a whole**. Courts first look at the plain language of the operative text. In § 1522(a) the statute says the Director “**shall**…make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement…,” “**shall**…provide refugees with the opportunity to acquire sufficient English language training…,” etc. The repeated use of the mandatory verb *shall* creates a clear command. The phrase “to the extent of available appropriations” merely qualifies the *scope* of the duty (i.e., the duty is limited by the amount of money Congress actually provides), it does not transform the duty into a discretionary “authorization.”

3. **Contextual interpretation**. The Ninth Circuit noted that the “shall” provisions are part of the *conditions and considerations* that attach to any funding the government decides to provide under the later subsections (§ 1522(b)–(e)). The majority read those conditions as mandatory **if** the government exercises the authority to fund. That is a standard conditional‑mandate construction, not a discretionary grant.

4. **Consistency with legislative history and purpose**. The Refugee Act of 1980 was designed to assure a “comprehensive system of assistance” for refugees after admission. The statutory scheme therefore reflects Congress’s intent that, **when funds are appropriated**, the Executive must deliver the enumerated services. The title’s use of “authorization” tracks the broader grant‑making authority, but it does not negate the specific grant‑making obligations that follow.

**How courts treat the clash in practice**

- **Majority rule**: When a statute’s title uses a permissive term (e.g., “authorization,” “enabling,” “provision for”) but the operative language contains “shall,” “must,” or other mandatory verbs, courts give effect to the mandatory language. The title is treated as *extrinsic* material that can illuminate purpose but cannot override clear operative text.  
- **Dissenting view**: A minority may argue that the title signals congressional intent that the provision be discretionary, especially when the mandatory language is coupled with a funding limitation. This view is permissible as a *theoretical* argument but has rarely prevailed when the operative text is unambiguous.

**Illustration from *Pacito v. Trump***  

- **Majority reasoning** (paraphrased from the order): “Section 1522(a) uses the statutory command ‘shall’ and therefore imposes a duty on the Director, subject only to the appropriations limitation. The title’s word ‘authorization’ is merely descriptive and does not excuse the Government from satisfying the statutory conditions when it chooses to fund the programs.”  
- **Judge Lee’s dissent**: He contends that the title renders the provision permissive and that § 1522(a) only sets conditions *if* the Executive decides to fund the programs. The dissent is explicit that he reads the title as controlling the substantive meaning, a position contrary to the majority and to prevailing case law.

**Take‑away for your analysis**

When you encounter a statutory provision whose title says “Authorization for …” but the body contains “shall” language, the proper analytical steps are:

1. **Identify the operative clause** – look for “shall,” “must,” “will,” etc.  
2. **Determine whether the mandatory language is qualified** – e.g., “to the extent of available appropriations” limits the *amount* of performance, not the *existence* of the duty.  
3. **Check the hierarchy** – titles are subordinate to operative text; they may aid interpretation but do not trump clear commands.  
4. **Apply precedent** – cite *Caines* and any circuit authority that treats titles as non‑controlling, and, where available, cite *Pacito v. Trump* for a concrete application.

In sum, under the Ninth Circuit’s reasoning in *Pacito*, 8 U.S.C. § 1522 imposes a **mandatory** duty to provide the enumerated refugee‑resettlement services (subject to appropriations), despite the permissive word “authorization” appearing in the section’s title.

### How does the APA apply to the State Department’s termination of cooperative agreements for refugee resettlement?
Question:
The Ninth Circuit notes that the government is “not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA.” What specific APA provisions (e.g., notice-and-comment, arbitrary and capricious standard) are implicated when the executive abruptly ends funding contracts, and how might the plaintiffs prove a violation?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Ninth Circuit’s order in *Pacito v. Trump* points to two distinct APA provisions that the plaintiffs rely on to attack the State Department’s abrupt termination of the refugee‑resettlement cooperative agreements:

1. **The “arbitrary and capricious” standard of § 706(1) of the APA** – the courts may set aside agency actions that are without a rational connection to the facts or law, that fail to consider relevant factors, or that are based on an error of law.  
2. **The notice‑and‑comment requirement of § 553(b) (the ‘‘informal rulemaking’’ clause)** – when an agency changes a policy that has the force of a rule, it must publish the change, allow interested parties to comment, and provide a reasoned explanation.

The Ninth Circuit notes that **“the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA.”** The opinion does not spell out which APA provision is the weaker point for the government, but the surrounding discussion makes it clear that the court is focusing on the statutory mandate in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and the agency’s failure to comply with the procedural requirements of the APA when it terminated the agreements “abruptly” without a rulemaking process.

Below is a breakdown of how each provision is implicated and what the plaintiffs must show to prove a violation.

---

### 1. Arbitrary‑and‑Capricious Standard (§ 706(1) APA)

**What the APA requires**

An agency action is unlawful if the agency:

* (a) failed to examine the relevant data;
* (b) offered an explanation that runs counter to the evidence;
* (c) made a decision contrary to the deliberative record; or
* (d) omitted to consider an important aspect of the problem.

(See *Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass’n v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 463 U.S. 322, 331‑32 (1983).)

**How it applies here**

* The State Department “terminated all cooperative agreements” on **February 26, 2025**, immediately after the district court’s preliminary injunction (see *Discussion*, ¶ 7).  
* The agencies that run the agreements – the Office of Refugee Resettlement (within HHS) and the three nonprofit partners – are statutorily obligated under **8 U.S.C. § 1522** to provide reception and placement services *to the extent of available appropriations* (see opinion, ¶ 12‑13).  
* The court record shows that the State Department could not point to a **“new agency”** that would reliably fulfill those statutory duties; its own briefing admitted “uncertainty regarding its ability to provide the reception and placement services” (¶ 15).  

To survive an arbitrary‑and‑capricious challenge, the government must therefore **demonstrate a reasoned decision‑making process** – i.e., that it considered the statutory requirement, evaluated the impact on refugees, and concluded that terminating the agreements was the least‑disruptive way to satisfy § 1522. The order emphasizes that the government “has not shown that it can provide the mandated services” and therefore **failed to show a rational connection** between the termination and the statutory goal of providing services.  

**What the plaintiffs must prove**

1. **Statutory duty** – that § 1522 creates a mandatory (not merely permissive) obligation to furnish reception and placement services.  
2. **Failure to consider** – that the State Department did not analyze how terminating the agreements would affect its ability to meet that duty.  
3. **No reasoned justification** – that the agency offered no explanatory record, relied on an “active preparation” of a request for proposals (which never materialized), and therefore the decision was **arbitrary**.  

If the plaintiffs can establish these points, the court can set aside the termination as arbitrary and capricious under § 706(1).

---

### 2. Notice‑and‑Comment Rulemaking (§ 553(b) APA)

**What the APA requires**

When an agency adopts, amends, or repeals a rule that has the force and effect of law, it must:

* Publish the rule in the Federal Register;
* Provide for public comment;
* Consider the comments; and
* Publish a statement of basis and purpose.

(See § 553(b)(1)‑(C); *Christiansburg Garment Co. v. EEOC*, 434 U.S. 412, 424 (1978).)

**How it applies here**

* The cooperative agreements are **grant‑type instruments** that, under the APA, are “rules” when the agency establishes **policy criteria** for selecting, continuing, or terminating awardees (see *FCC v. Fox Television Stations*, 453 U.S. 629, 647 (1981)).  
* The State Department’s **instant termination** of all agreements on February 26, 2025, was **not preceded by a notice in the Federal Register**, nor was there any comment period. The order notes that the agency “abruptly ends funding contracts” (see the question).  
* The district court’s preliminary injunction was based in part on the fact that the termination **bypassed the procedural safeguards** the APA mandates for changing a funding program.  

**What the plaintiffs must prove**

1. **The agreements are “rules”** – that the cooperative‑agreement program creates binding obligations and that the termination changes the terms of those obligations.  
2. **Lack of notice‑and‑comment** – that the State Department failed to publish a proposed rule change, provide a comment period, and issue a final rule explaining the basis for termination.  
3. **Material impact** – that the change is “significant” (i.e., it eliminates all existing funding for reception and placement services) and therefore triggers the APA’s procedural requirements.  

If the plaintiffs can show the agency ignored § 553(b), a court may vacate the termination as **procedurally invalid**.

---

### Why the Ninth Circuit Says the Government Will Likely Lose on an APA Claim

The order specifically says the government is “not likely to prevail on **at least one** of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA.” The reasoning in the opinion suggests two possible weak spots:

* **Arbitrary‑and‑capricious** – because the agency cannot point to a reasoned analysis showing that terminating the agreements satisfies the statutory duty in § 1522.  
* **Notice‑and‑comment** – because the termination was an “abrupt” shutdown without any rulemaking process.

Either deficiency would be enough for the court to enjoin the termination, which is exactly what the district court ordered and what the Ninth Circuit’s stay presently preserves.

---

### Summary Checklist for Plaintiffs

| APA Provision | How the State Dept. allegedly violated it | Evidence plaintiffs must produce |
|---------------|-------------------------------------------|---------------------------------|
| **§ 706(1) – Arbitrary & capricious** | No factual record, no analysis of § 1522 duty, no viable alternative identified. | Statutory text of § 1522; agency memos showing lack of consideration; absence of a reasoned decision‑making record. |
| **§ 553(b) – Notice‑and‑comment** | Immediate termination without Federal Register notice, comment period, or statement of basis. | Copies of the cooperative‑agreement rules; timeline of termination; lack of any published notice; testimony that the agency never initiated rulemaking. |

If the plaintiffs can establish either violation, the Ninth Circuit’s order indicates that the district court’s injunction is likely to be upheld on APA grounds, even though the court grants the government deference on the underlying immigration‑policy question.

### What is the legal effect of a district court’s preliminary injunction when the appellate court stays it “in its entirety except as noted,” and how does that affect the parties’ obligations on the ground?
Question:
After the appellate stay, the district court orders are stayed, but the order also mentions a limited carve‑out for conditionally approved refugees. How should the parties interpret which actions are permissible during the stay, especially regarding ongoing terminations of cooperative agreements?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The Ninth Circuit’s order stays *both* district‑court preliminary injunctions **in their entirety** except for the narrow “carve‑out” that the court expressly preserved. While the stay is in effect, the parties must treat the injunctions as **suspended** for all persons and actions *except* for the class of “conditionally‑approved refugees” identified in the district court’s February 28 order. In practice this means:

* The government may **continue** to terminate (or not reinstate) cooperative agreements that serve only refugees *who have not* been conditionally approved before 20 Jan 2025.  
* The government **may not** terminate, suspend, or otherwise interfere with any services or agreements that affect those conditionally‑approved refugees; those agreements must remain in place and the government must comply with the district court’s directive to reinstate them.

Below is a step‑by‑step unpacking of why the stay operates this way and what each side must do on the “ground” (i.e., in the field).

---

### 1. What the stay actually says

> “The district court’s February 28, 2025 and March 24, 2025 preliminary injunctions are **stayed in their entirety, except as noted** below.” (Court order, ¶ 1)

The order then **specifies** the only exception:

> “…*to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by USCIS before January 20, 2025.*” (Court order, ¶ 1‑2)

No other language creates additional carve‑outs. Thus the stay **mirrors** the district court’s own “limited carve‑out” language that the panel had previously left untouched.

### 2. Legal effect of a “stay in its entirety except as noted”

A stay is a **suspension** of the operative effect of the underlying order. *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009) (stay “halts the operation” of the order pending appeal). When the stay language says “in its entirety except as noted,” the suspension applies to **every provision** of the underlying injunction **but** the provisions **specifically saved** by the “except as noted” language remain **live**.

Consequently:

| Underlying injunction | Status under the stay |
|-----------------------|-----------------------|
| Prohibition on the President’s suspension of refugee admissions (Exec. Order §§ 3‑4) | **Suspended** – the government may proceed with the suspension. |
| Prohibition on the Department of State’s termination of *all* cooperative agreements (Feb. 28 order) | **Suspended** – the government may terminate agreements that are not covered by the carve‑out. |
| Order to *reinstate* the cooperative agreements that were terminated **and that affect conditionally‑approved refugees** (Mar. 24 order) | **Not suspended** – the government must comply with this reinstatement requirement. |

### 3. Who is covered by the carve‑out?

The stay’s exception quotes the district court’s language verbatim: it applies **only** to “individuals who were *conditionally approved* for refugee status by USCIS before January 20, 2025.” That class is:

* **Conditionally approved refugees** – persons who had received a conditional approval (i.e., a “conditional refugee” status) prior to the executive order’s effective date.  
* It does **not** cover: (a) refugees already admitted and residing in the United States, (b) refugees who were merely **applicants** (not yet conditionally approved), or (c) any other asylum‑seeker class.

The district court’s February 28 order explicitly tied the injunction’s scope to that group; the appellate stay merely preserves that limitation.

### 4. Practical implications for the parties

#### Government (Defendant)

1. **May continue** to enforce the executive order’s suspension of new refugee admissions, because that portion of the injunction is stayed.  
2. **May continue** to terminate any cooperative agreements that *do not* serve the conditionally‑approved refugees. The July 14 district‑court enforcement order (which sought to create a framework for the carve‑out) remains stayed, so the government is not required to follow that framework while the stay is in place.  
3. **Must reinstate** *any* cooperative agreements that provide reception and placement services to the conditionally‑approved refugees. The March 24 injunction’s reinstatement directive is **not** stayed, so the government is obligated to act on it immediately. Failure to do so would be a violation of the still‑effective portion of the injunction and could expose the government to contempt sanctions.

#### Plaintiffs (Refugees and NGOs)

1. **Can rely** on the reinstated cooperative agreements for the conditionally‑approved refugees; they may seek enforcement (e.g., a motion for contempt) if the government fails to reinstate them.  
2. **Cannot enforce** the broader injunction prohibiting the suspension of the refugee program or the termination of all other agreements, because those provisions are stayed. Any attempt to block the government’s continued termination of unrelated agreements would be dismissed as “inapplicable while the stay is operative.”  
3. **Should monitor** the government’s compliance with the reinstatement requirement; the court’s order expressly directs the government to provide “the usual and customary services” under the prior agreements.

### 5. Why the court adopted this limited approach

The Ninth Circuit’s discussion of the stay factors (Nken) shows it found the government likely to succeed on the merits of the presidential‑authority claims and therefore granted a broad stay of the injunction. However, the panel **rejected** the government’s request to stay the “limited carve‑out” because the plaintiffs had shown a likelihood of success on their APA‑based challenges concerning the statutory duty to provide reception and placement services. Hence the stay **preserves** the portion of the injunction that protects the conditionally‑approved refugees while suspending everything else.

---

**Bottom line:**  
During the appellate stay, **everything** the district court ordered is on hold **except** the requirement that the government **re‑establish** cooperative agreements for the **conditionally‑approved refugees** identified in the February 28 order. The government may proceed with the broader suspension of the refugee program and may terminate other agreements, but it must immediately reinstate the agreements that affect that narrow class of refugees. The parties should therefore limit their actions to those boundaries until the appeal is resolved.

### Can the President’s suspension of refugee admissions under § 1182(f) be challenged on due‑process or equal‑protection grounds, or are those claims pre‑empted by the immigration discretion doctrine?
Question:
The opinion focuses on statutory authority, but the plaintiffs might argue constitutional violations. Is there any precedent that allows constitutional claims to survive a claim of broad presidential discretion in immigration matters?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The Ninth Circuit’s order in *Pacito v. Trump* makes clear that a claim that the President’s suspension of refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) violates the Due‑Process Clause or the Equal‑Protection Clause is **unlikely to survive**. The court treats the statutory grant of authority as a “broad discretion” that “exudes deference” and therefore bars ordinary constitutional challenges to the exercise of that discretion. The opinion cites *Trump v. Hawaii* and the “immigration discretion doctrine” as the controlling authority: once the President satisfies the minimal statutory predicate—finding that admission would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States”—the courts may not “engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Consequently, any constitutional claim that is premised on the substance of the suspension is pre‑empted by the statutory grant of discretion.

**Why the court reaches that result**

1. **Statutory text controls** – § 1182(f) says the President *may* suspend entry of any class of aliens “whenever … he finds that the entry … would be detrimental to the interest of the United States.” The court reads this language as giving the President *unreviewable* discretion once the *finding* requirement is met. The order states, “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ … The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here” (citing *Hawaii*, 585 U.S. at 683‑84).

2. **Deference doctrine** – The panel treats the provision exactly as the Supreme Court treated the travel‑ban provision in *Trump v. Hawaii*: the statute confers “broad discretion” that “exudes deference,” and the courts “cannot engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” (Order at 2‑3). Under that doctrine, constitutional challenges that question the *substance* of the President’s determination are foreclosed.

3. **No “intangible‑rights” exception** – The order does not rely on any “intangible‑rights” or “fundamental‑right” analysis that would carve out a judicial review exception. The only discussion of constitutional claims is in the context of the stay analysis, where the court notes that “the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order …” (Order, discussing the merits). The court therefore presumes the constitutional claims will fail on the merits.

4. **Scope of the decision** – The order stays the district court’s injunction that attempted to block the suspension, but it **does not address** any procedural‑due‑process or equal‑protection arguments directly. Instead, the panel’s reasoning makes clear that any such claim would be dismissed as “pre‑empted” by the statutory grant of authority.

**Relevant precedent that supports this view**

* *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018) – the Court upheld a presidential proclamation suspending entry of certain nationals, holding that the INA’s delegation “exudes deference” and that the courts may not second‑guess the President’s judgment about national‑security interests.

* *Kleindienst v. Department of State*, 340 U.S. 410 (1951) – while not a due‑process case, it establishes the “facially legitimate and bona fide reason” standard for reviewing discretionary immigration decisions and reinforces the principle that courts give great leeway to the executive.

* *Mathews v. Eldridge*, 424 U.S. 819 (1976) – the Supreme Court’s balancing test for procedural due‑process, but the Ninth Circuit in *Pacito* notes that the *Mathews* test is irrelevant where the statute itself provides the President with unreviewable discretion.

**Conclusion**

Within the confines of *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit follows the immigration‑discretion doctrine articulated in *Trump v. Hawaii*: once the President satisfies the statutory “finding” requirement of § 1182(f), the courts lack jurisdiction to entertain constitutional challenges that question the *reasonableness* or *content* of that finding. Accordingly, due‑process or equal‑protection claims aimed at overturning the refugee‑admissions suspension are effectively pre‑empted by the broad statutory discretion granted to the President.

### What is the significance of the “limited carve‑out” for individuals conditionally approved for refugee status before Jan. 20, 2025, in the stay order?
Question:
The panel stayed the preliminary injunction “to the extent the district court’s order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved….” I’m unclear on why the court treated that group differently and what legal reasoning supports carving out that subset while staying the rest.
Answer:
**Answer**

The “limited carve‑out”‑the portion of the stay that *does not* apply to people who were **conditionally approved for refugee status before 20 January 2025**‑exists because the Ninth Circuit concluded that, while the government is likely to succeed on the President’s authority to suspend refugee admissions, it is **not** likely to succeed on the plaintiffs’ statutory claim that the government must continue to fund “reception and placement” services for those refugees who have already been admitted.

### Why the court treated that group differently

1. **Two distinct legal questions**  
   The district court’s preliminary injunction covered (a) the President’s suspension of refugee admissions under **Exec. Order 14163** and (b) the termination of the State Department’s cooperative agreements that provide resettlement services.  

   The Ninth Circuit applied the *stay‑analysis* (Nken v. Holder) separately to each prong.  
   * For the **suspension of admissions**, the panel found the government “likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge,” because § 1182(f) gives the President wide discretion once he makes the required “finding” that entry would be detrimental. (See “The government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order 14163’s suspension….”)  
   * For the **reception‑and‑placement services**, the panel said “the government is **not likely to prevail** on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA.” The reason is the uncertainty about whether § 1522 imposes a mandatory duty to fund those services. (See “For reasons to be explained … the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA.”)

2. **Statutory interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522**  
   The majority read § 1522 as creating a **statutory duty** to provide certain services to refugees **who have already been admitted**. Because Congress appropriated funds for those services, the government must keep the cooperative agreements in place until a new provider can be found. (See “the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services … to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.”)

   Judge Lee’s dissent argues that § 1522 is merely an **authorization**, not a mandate, but the majority’s majority view controls the stay order.

3. **Effect on the stay**  
   Because the court believes the government will likely win on the immigration‑admission issue but may lose on the statutory‑service issue, it **stays the entire district‑court injunction except** for the subset of individuals whose status was *conditionally approved before the Jan. 20, 2025 cutoff*. Those individuals are the ones for whom § 1522’s mandatory‑service reading applies; they have a concrete expectation of receiving resettlement services that the government may be obligated to fund.

### Legal reasoning that supports the carve‑out

- **Four‑factor stay test** (Nken): the court found the government satisfied the first two factors (likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable injury) for the *admissions* prong, but **did not** for the *services* prong.  
- **Deference to the President** (Trump v. Hawaii): the court emphasizes that the President’s authority under § 1182(f) “exudes deference,” so the injunction blocking the suspension is presumptively improper.  
- **Statutory duty under § 1522**: The majority treats the language “shall … to the extent of available appropriations” as creating a **mandatory** obligation to provide reception and placement services *once* a refugee has been admitted. Because the government has terminated the cooperative agreements, reinstatement is required for those already admitted (or conditionally approved) before the moratorium took effect.

### Practical significance

- **People conditionally approved before Jan. 20, 2025** retain the right to the resettlement services that were already promised under the pre‑moratorium program. The government must keep the existing contracts alive until a new provider is in place.  
- **All other plaintiffs** (those whose applications were pending after Jan. 20, 2025 or who were not yet conditionally approved) remain subject to the stay of the district court’s injunction; the suspension of refugee admissions and the termination of the cooperative agreements continue while the appeal is pending.  
- The carve‑out therefore **limits the scope of the stay** to avoid irreparable harm to a class of refugees who have a vested interest in receiving statutorily mandated assistance, while preserving the broader deference the court gives to the President’s immigration discretion.

In short, the “limited carve‑out” reflects the panel’s nuanced view that the government’s authority to block refugee admissions is strong, but its statutory obligations to fund resettlement services for refugees already conditionally approved are uncertain. The stay therefore protects that latter class of refugees while leaving the rest of the injunction on hold.

### How does the “broad discretion” doctrine from *Trump v. Hawaii* interact with the statutory requirements of the Refugee Act of 1980 in this case?
Question:
The majority leans heavily on deference to the President, yet the Refugee Act sets out a comprehensive framework for admission and assistance. Does the Refugee Act limit the President’s discretion under § 1182(f), or can the President override the Act’s provisions by simply finding a detriment to U.S. interests?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Ninth Circuit’s order in *Pacito v. Trump* makes clear that the “broad discretion” doctrine articulated in *Trump v. Hawaii* applies to the President’s power under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), but that discretion is not unlimited once the statute that governs refugee resettlement—the Refugee Act of 1980—is taken into account.

### 1. Presidential discretion under § 1182(f)

The panel relies heavily on *Hawaii* (585 U.S. 667 (2018)) for the proposition that when the President “finds that the entry of any class of aliens would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” the statutory language of § 1182(f) gives him “ample power” to suspend entry and “exudes deference” to the executive.  The order quotes *Hawaii* verbatim:

> “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ that the entry … would be detrimental … The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” (Order at 2‑3)

Accordingly, the court treats the President’s determination in Exec. Order 14163 as a valid exercise of the authority granted by § 1182(f).  For the purpose of a stay, the panel says the government is “likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions,” and therefore the broad‑discretion doctrine supports staying the district court’s injunction that blocks the suspension.

### 2. Statutory framework of the Refugee Act

The order then turns to the Refugee Act, specifically 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which creates a statutory scheme for *domestic* resettlement services (employment training, cash assistance, placement, etc.).  The panel notes that § 1522 imposes **mandatory** duties:

* § 1522(a)(1)(A) uses “shall” and ties the obligations to “the extent of available appropriations.”
* §§ 1522(d)(2)(B) and 1522(e)(7)(A) likewise contain “shall” language requiring the Director to assume responsibility for unaccompanied minors and to develop interim‑support projects.

The Ninth Circuit treats these provisions as **mandatory** in the sense that, once the government decides to fund refugee resettlement, it must provide the services described.  The panel therefore concludes that—while the President may lawfully suspend *admissions*—the government cannot simply abandon the *statutory* duty to provide services to refugees who have already been admitted.

### 3. Interaction of the two regimes

The order therefore draws a line between two separate statutory regimes:

| **Statute** | **Effect of Presidential discretion** | **Court’s reading of the statute** |
|-------------|----------------------------------------|------------------------------------|
| § 1182(f) (immigration) | President may suspend entry of a class of aliens if he finds it detrimental; courts give “broad discretion” and defer to the executive. | Deference is proper; injunction on the suspension is stayed. |
| § 1522 (refugee assistance) | The statute does **not** give the President discretion to eliminate services; it creates a *mandatory* framework that draws on appropriated funds. | The statute imposes an enforceable duty; the district court’s injunction that blocks termination of cooperative agreements must be **partially lifted**. |

In other words, *Trump v. Hawaii* shields the President from judicial review of the **admission** moratorium, but it does **not** immunize the executive from compliance with the Refugee Act’s **service‑provision** obligations.  The Ninth Circuit therefore vacated the stay of the district court’s order that required the State Department to reinstate the cooperative agreements that fund reception and placement services.

### 4. Why the Refugee Act limits the President’s discretion

The panel’s reasoning rests on two textual observations:

1. **Mandatory language** – The use of “shall” in §§ 1522(a), (d)(2)(B), and (e)(7)(A) creates a binding duty, not merely a permissive authorization.
2. **Conditional funding clause** – Although the duties are “to the extent of available appropriations,” the appropriations bill for FY 2025 (Pub. L. No. 119‑4) did appropriate $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, showing that Congress intended the services to be funded and therefore operational.

Consequently, the President cannot rely on the broad‑discretion doctrine to avoid the statutory requirement that refugees who are already in the United States receive the assistance Congress prescribed.

### 5. Practical outcome

* The injunction that blocks the suspension of refugee admissions remains **stay‑ed** because the President’s action falls squarely within the discretionary authority of § 1182(f) as interpreted in *Hawaii*.
* The injunction that ordered the State Department to **reinstate** the cooperative agreements for domestic resettlement **remains in force**, because the Refugee Act imposes a mandatory, fund‑supported duty that the executive cannot simply discard.

**Bottom line:** *Trump v. Hawaii* gives the President sweeping leeway to halt refugee **admissions**, but the Refugee Act of 1980 creates a separate, mandatory statutory scheme for **assistance** to refugees already admitted.  The Ninth Circuit’s order respects the President’s discretion on the former while enforcing Congress’s mandate on the latter.
