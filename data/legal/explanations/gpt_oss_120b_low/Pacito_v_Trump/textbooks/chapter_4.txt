Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 4: # Court’s Analysis of Presidential Authority and Stay Standards  

In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit was called upon to decide whether to stay the district court’s preliminary injunctions that blocked President Trump’s executive order suspending refugee admissions and defunding the United States Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP).  The panel’s order offers a clear illustration of two fundamental procedural doctrines that law students must master: the doctrine of deference to the President in immigration matters, and the four‑factor test that governs the issuance of a stay pending appeal.  This chapter walks through the court’s reasoning, showing how the panel applied each principle to the facts before it.

---

## Deference Doctrine in Immigration (Trump v. Hawaii) and Its Application Here  

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), codified a high level of judicial deference to the political branches when they exercise the broad discretion granted by the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA).  The Court held that the “President’s authority to suspend the entry of aliens…exudes deference” and that courts should not engage in a “searching inquiry” into the justifications underlying a presidential finding.  The Ninth Circuit’s order invokes *Hawaii* repeatedly, treating the statutory grant of power in 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) as a “textual” authority that limits judicial review to whether the President has satisfied the bare statutory prerequisite—the “find” that admission of a class of aliens would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States.”

In *Pacito*, the panel noted that the executive order at issue (Exec. Order No. 14163) was issued under the explicit authority of § 1182(f).  The order states that the President “determined” that refugee admissions would be detrimental and therefore suspended them pending further findings.  The court therefore asked a narrow question: **Did the President make the statutory finding required by § 1182(f)?**  The record showed a clear statement of the finding in the order itself, satisfying the text of the statute.  Consequently, the panel concluded that the President had met the only substantive requirement, and that any further assessment of the political or factual reasonableness of the determination was barred by the deference doctrine articulated in *Hawaii*.

---

## Interpretation of the “Find” Requirement in § 1182(f)  

Section 1182(f) provides that “[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may…suspend the entry…as he shall deem necessary.”  The Ninth Circuit read the word “find” in its ordinary, plain‑language sense: the President must make a formal, on‑record determination that the class of aliens (here, refugees) poses a detriment.  The court emphasized that the statute does not require the President to articulate supporting evidence or to demonstrate a particular level of factual certainty.  The mere articulation of a finding, coupled with the issuance of a proclamation or executive order, satisfies the statutory condition.

The panel therefore rejected the plaintiffs’ argument that the executive order was facially invalid because the President had not provided a detailed factual basis.  Citing *Hawaii*, the court held that the statutory text “does not impose a duty on the President to provide a ‘rational basis’ or to substantiate the finding with evidence.”  The finding in Exec. Order 14163—“entry into the United States of refugees … would be detrimental to the interests of the United States”—met the statutory threshold, making the order presumptively lawful unless the plaintiffs could show that the President exceeded the scope of the authority (e.g., by suspending refugee admissions for a reason unrelated to national interest).  The court found no such allegation in the record, and thus concluded that the government was “likely to prevail on the merits” of the challenge to the injunction.

---

## Application of Nken v. Holder Stay Factors  

Having determined that the government satisfied the statutory “find” requirement, the panel turned to the procedural question of whether the district court’s injunction should be stayed pending appeal.  The Ninth Circuit applied the four‑factor test articulated in *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), which requires a court to consider:

1. **Likelihood of success on the merits**  
2. **Likelihood of irreparable injury if the stay is denied**  
3. **Balance of hardships**  
4. **Public interest**  

The order stresses that the first two factors are “the most critical.”  The panel evaluated each factor in turn.

### 1. Likelihood of Success on the Merits  

As discussed, the court found the President’s statutory authority under § 1182(f) to be clear and uncontroversial.  The government’s argument that the plaintiffs could not succeed on the merits of their challenge to the suspension of refugee admissions was therefore “strong.”  The panel quoted *Hawaii*—“the language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority”—to underscore that the merits issue was essentially foreclosed in the government’s favor.

### 2. Likelihood of Irreparable Injury  

The court next examined whether the government would suffer an injury that could not be remedied by later monetary damages if the stay were denied.  The district court’s injunction had halted the President’s ability to suspend the refugee program, thereby compelling the executive branch to continue processing and admitting refugees despite the President’s finding of detriment.  The panel concluded that continuing the admissions program would cause “irreparable injury” to the government’s national‑security interests and to the executive’s discretion to manage immigration—injuries that could not be undone once refugees entered the United States.  Moreover, the injunction forced the government to expend resources on a policy the President had deemed contrary to the nation’s interests, a injury the court deemed both immediate and irreversible.

### 3. Balance of Hardships  

Having found that the first two factors heavily favored the government, the panel gave less weight to the remaining factors but still addressed them.  The plaintiffs argued that the injunction protected refugees from being barred from entry and from losing resettlement services.  The court acknowledged these hardships but held that they were “substantial” but not “irreparable,” especially given the government’s statutory authority to suspend admissions.  Conversely, the government would suffer not only the abstract injury to its discretion but also concrete administrative disruption and potential security risks, which the court deemed to outweigh the plaintiffs’ asserted hardships.

### 4. Public Interest  

The public‑interest analysis was intertwined with the national‑security considerations already noted.  The panel emphasized that the President’s discretion in immigration is rooted in the sovereign power to protect the nation, a core public interest.  While the court recognized the humanitarian interests of the refugees, it determined that the overarching public interest in allowing the President to act on his statutory authority to restrict entry carried greater weight in the balance.

---

## Why the Court Found the Government Likely to Succeed on the Merits of the Suspension Challenge  

Summarizing the merits analysis, the Ninth Circuit’s order identifies three pillars supporting the government’s likelihood of success:

1. **Statutory Textualism** – The plain language of § 1182(f) requires only a presidential “finding” of detriment.  The executive order supplied that finding, satisfying the statutory condition.  
2. **Supreme Court Precedent** – *Trump v. Hawaii* commands deference to the President’s immigration judgments, barring courts from second‑guessing the merits of the finding.  
3. **Absence of Overreach** – The court found no allegation that the President exceeded the scope of § 1182(f) (e.g., by banning all refugees for a purpose unrelated to national interest).  Consequently, the injunction appeared to enjoin a power the President was plainly authorized to exercise.

Because the merits issue was essentially a question of statutory construction that the court saw resolved in the government’s favor, the first Nken factor tipped heavily toward granting a stay.

---

## Assessment of Irreparable Injury to the Government and the Public Interest  

The irreparable‑injury analysis hinged on the idea that once refugees are admitted, the government cannot retroactively “undo” that admission.  The court characterized the continued operation of the refugee admissions program as a “harm that is not merely monetary” but one that affects the nation’s sovereign right to control its borders.  This injury is “irreparable” because it directly frustrates the President’s statutorily delegated power and cannot be repaired by later relief.

In the public‑interest calculus, the court placed the President’s discretion at the apex of the nation’s interests in immigration control.  While humanitarian concerns are undeniably important, the *Hawaii* line of authority makes clear that the judiciary’s role is not to substitute its own policy preferences for those of the political branches.  Accordingly, the panel concluded that the public interest “lies with the government’s ability to enforce the statutory immigration scheme as envisioned by Congress.”

---

### Putting It All Together  

The Ninth Circuit’s order in *Pacito v. Trump* demonstrates how a court blends substantive immigration doctrine with procedural stay analysis.  By invoking *Trump v. Hawaii* and the plain‑text reading of § 1182(f), the panel affirmed that the President’s authority to suspend refugee admissions is largely insulated from judicial scrutiny.  Applying the *Nken* stay test, the court gave decisive weight to the government’s strong likelihood of success on the merits and to the irreparable injury the government would suffer without a stay.  The remaining factors—balance of hardships and public interest—followed from those conclusions, leading the panel to issue a stay of the district court’s injunctions pending appeal.

Understanding this reasoning equips a law student to recognize when courts will defer to the executive in immigration matters and how the stay standards operate as a gatekeeper to appellate review.  In future cases, the same four‑factor analysis will be applied, but the weight given to each factor will depend heavily on the underlying merits of the statutory authority at issue.
