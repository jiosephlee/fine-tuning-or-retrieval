Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 3: # Chapter 3: The Court’s Analysis of the Disputed DuPont Factors  

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit reviewed the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s (the “Board”) likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis and found that the Board’s treatment of three of the thirteen DuPont factors was inconsistent with controlling law. This chapter walks you through the court’s step‑by‑step reasoning on each of those factors—why the court affirmed the Board’s conclusion on the second factor, why it vacated the Board’s conclusions on the sixth and first factors, and what the court instructed the Board to do on remand. Understanding this portion of the opinion requires a grasp of (1) the evidentiary standard the court applies to the Board’s factual findings, (2) the legal content of each factor, and (3) the way the factors are interrelated in a likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis.

---  

## 3.1. Factor Two – Similarity of Goods/Services  

### 3.1.1. Board’s factual findings and definition of “credit‑card services”  

The Board began its analysis by asking whether the goods and services offered under the contested marks were “similar” within the meaning of the Lanham Act. Because “credit‑card services” no longer appears as a discrete entry in the Trademark ID Manual, the Board first reconstructed the scope of that term. Relying on industry practice and internal USPTO explanations, the Board defined “credit‑card services” to include (1) issuance of credit cards, (2) counseling about credit‑card debt, (3) processing of credit‑card transactions, (4) authorization and monitoring, (5) account management, and (6) provision of credit scores. This definition appears at **J.A. 25** of the opinion.

### 3.1.2. Legal standard for similarity of services under the Lanham Act  

Similarity does not require identity. The Federal Circuit has held that the “services need not be identical—the evidence need only establish that the respective products are related in some manner … or that the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source” (*Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC*, 668 F.3d at 1369). The key question is whether the average consumer, viewing the marks in the relevant commercial context, would think the same company provides the services.

### 3.1.3. Court’s affirmation: substantial‑evidence support  

The court examined the Board’s factual record. The Board had identified that “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” are defined in dictionaries to encompass extending credit, a function that overlaps with the issuance of credit cards. Moreover, the Board cited third‑party registrations that covered both credit‑card and banking services, showing that the USPTO itself treats those services as potentially co‑offered under a single mark. The appellate court concluded that this record provided **substantial evidence**—the standard defined by *Consol. Edison* as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Because the Board’s factual findings were supported by expert testimony and documentary evidence, the court held that the Board’s conclusion that the services are “highly similar” satisfies the de novo legal standard for factor two. Consequently, the court **affirmed** the Board’s finding on this factor.  

---  

## 3.2. Factor Six – Number and Nature of Similar Marks on Similar Goods  

### 3.2.1. Board’s limited scope to credit‑card marks only  

For the sixth factor, the Board counted only those third‑party marks that used “Aspire” (or a variant) **in connection with credit‑card services**. The Board identified nine such marks and held that this number was insufficient to render the mark “weak” or to show that consumers were accustomed to distinguishing among similar marks. The Board deliberately excluded the remaining 42 registrations that paired “Aspire” with other financial services—such as banking, loans, or financing—labeling them “essentially irrelevant” because they were not tied to “credit‑card services.” This limitation is discussed at **J.A. 47‑51**.  

### 3.2.2. The requirement to consider all marks used on *similar* goods, not just identical ones  

The Federal Circuit’s precedent makes clear that the sixth factor looks at the **breadth of use of similar marks on similar goods or services**. The court has repeatedly emphasized that “similar” does not equal “identical.” See *Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc.*, 961 F.2d at 204 (recognizing that the factor applies where the goods are merely related). Moreover, the factor is intended to gauge the **commercial strength** of the senior mark; a crowded field of similar marks can signal weakness, whereas a sparsely populated field may suggest stronger protection.  

In *Apex Bank*, the appellate court observed that the Board had already concluded, under factor two, that the parties’ services are “highly similar” and even “partially legally identical.” Given that finding, the same scope of similarity must carry over to the sixth factor. By restricting the analysis to credit‑card services alone, the Board applied a narrower definition of similarity than it did in its own earlier analysis—a procedural inconsistency that the court deemed a legal error.  

### 3.2.3. Court’s finding of legal error and the need for a broader analysis  

The court held that the Board’s failure to consider the 42 additional “Aspire” marks—used for banking and financing services—contravened the requirement to evaluate **all similar marks on similar goods**. Because the services are already deemed similar, the relevant public is not limited to “credit‑card consumers” but includes the broader group of consumers who purchase banking and financing services. The court therefore **vacated** the Board’s sixth‑factor finding and **remanded** for a new analysis that (1) adopts the broader scope of services identified in factor two, and (2) assesses whether the totality of “Aspire” marks on those services creates a “crowded field” sufficient to show weakness of the senior mark.  

---  

## 3.3. Factor One – Overall Commercial Impression of the Marks  

### 3.3.1. Interdependence of factor six and factor one via commercial strength/weakness  

Factor one evaluates the “similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” While this factor is primarily a visual‑and‑aural comparison, the court has recognized that a mark’s **commercial strength or weakness**—often derived from factor six—feeds into the overall impression. A weak mark is more likely to be deemed “generic” or “descriptive,” which can diminish its commercial impression and increase the probability of confusion. In *In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC*, 866 F.3d at 1323, the court explained that the commercial impression analysis must consider the mark’s overall market presence.  

### 3.3.2. Why vacating factor six necessitates vacating factor one  

Because the Board’s sixth‑factor analysis was legally flawed, the commercial strength determination that underlies the first‑factor analysis is unreliable. The court reasoned that any reassessment of the number and nature of similar marks could shift the perception of the senior mark’s strength, thereby altering the “commercial impression” of both marks. As a result, the court could not safely leave the first‑factor finding untouched. Accordingly, the opinion states: “Because commercial impression informs the analysis under the first DuPont factor, we must similarly vacate the Board’s analysis here” (**J.A. 7**).  

### 3.3.3. Directions for remand – reconsideration in light of a revised sixth‑factor analysis  

The court instructed the Board to redo its first‑factor analysis after completing a proper sixth‑factor evaluation. The Board must now (1) re‑examine the appearance, sound, and connotation of “ASPIRE” versus “ASPIRE BANK,” (2) consider whether the broader field of “Aspire” marks on banking and financing services influences the overall commercial impression, and (3) integrate any revised finding on commercial strength into its assessment of likelihood of confusion. The remand instructions are succinct: “We thus vacate the Board’s findings with respect to DuPont factors six and one and remand for the Board to consider the number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods and the appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression of the marks in light of its finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.”  

---  

### Summary of the Court’s Step‑by‑Step Evaluation  

1. **Factor Two (Similarity of Services)** – The Board’s factual findings were well‑supported; the court applied the de novo legal standard and affirmed the Board’s conclusion.  
2. **Factor Six (Number/Nature of Similar Marks)** – The Board’s analysis was limited to a subset of marks inconsistent with its own earlier finding of service similarity. The court identified a legal error, vacated the finding, and remanded for a broader, correct analysis.  
3. **Factor One (Overall Commercial Impression)** – Because the commercial‑strength assessment depends on factor six, the court vacated the first‑factor finding and required the Board to reconsider it after the sixth‑factor analysis is corrected.  

By structuring its review in this way, the Federal Circuit preserved the Board’s well‑founded conclusions where the evidentiary record was adequate, while ensuring that the remaining factors were evaluated in a coherent and legally consistent manner. This approach reflects the appellate court’s dual mandate: to respect the Board’s expertise when supported by substantial evidence, and to correct procedural or legal misapplications that could distort the ultimate likelihood‑of‑confusion determination.
