Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

## Ciminelli's Shadow: Defining Protected Property Interests in Securities Fraud

In the wake of the Supreme Court’s restrictive turn on federal fraud statutes, prosecutors have faced a significant hurdle in charging schemes that do not involve the traditional theft of cash or tangible assets. The Fifth Circuit’s recent decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* offers a crucial clarification on how these limitations apply in the modern context of social media-driven securities fraud. While the Supreme Court’s 2023 decision in *Ciminelli v. United States* barred convictions based solely on the deprivation of economic information, the Fifth Circuit has signaled that this barrier does not immunize "pump and dump" schemes where defendants induce victims to part with actual money. By distinguishing between the impermissible "right-to-control" theory and the permissible "fraudulent-inducement" theory, the court has preserved a vital tool for combating market manipulation, even as it adheres to the Court’s textualist constraints.

The case arises from a classic "pump and dump" scheme involving high-profile social media influencers. The government alleged that the defendants, who held themselves out as skilled traders, purchased securities and then posted false information to artificially inflate their prices. Once retail investors bought in based on these misrepresentations, the defendants sold their holdings for a profit, allegedly netting $114 million. The district court dismissed the indictment, reasoning that the scheme only deprived followers of potentially valuable economic information rather than a traditional property interest. This dismissal relied heavily on *Ciminelli*, which held that the "right to control" one’s assets is not a property interest protected by federal fraud statutes. The Fifth Circuit, however, found this reading too narrow for the facts at hand.

Judge Engelhardt’s opinion carefully navigates the doctrinal minefield left by *Ciminelli*. The court acknowledges that depriving a victim of information alone is insufficient for a fraud conviction. However, the Fifth Circuit emphasizes that the indictment here did not rely on a right-to-control theory. Instead, the charging document alleged a fraudulent-inducement theory, where the defendants used material misstatements to trick victims into purchasing securities. In the court’s view, inducing a follower to buy a stock is not merely withholding information; it is actively tricking the victim into handing over money or property. This distinction is vital. Under *Ciminelli*, the property interest must be traditional. By framing the fraud as the inducement to part with cash for a security, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment properly alleged the deprivation of a protected property interest, thereby avoiding the *Ciminelli* bar.

The decision also leans heavily on another recent Supreme Court ruling, *Kousisis v. United States*, to address the defendants' argument regarding intent. The defendants contended that because they intended to profit rather than cause their followers to suffer a net economic loss, the requisite intent to defraud was missing. The Fifth Circuit rejected this, citing *Kousisis* for the proposition that federal fraud statutes do not require the defendant to seek to leave the victim economically worse off. As the court noted, a fraud is complete when the defendant induces the deprivation of money or property under false pretenses, regardless of whether the victim receives something of equal value in return. This interpretation prevents defendants from shielding themselves by arguing that their victims received market-value securities, even if those securities were purchased based on lies.

This ruling has significant downstream implications for how federal prosecutors approach financial crimes in the digital age. By clarifying that fraudulent inducement to purchase securities survives *Ciminelli*, the Fifth Circuit ensures that the rise of social media trading does not create a safe harbor for manipulators. The decision reinforces that the core of fraud is the deceptive acquisition of property, not necessarily the infliction of a net loss. However, it also places a premium on the specificity of indictments. Prosecutors must now ensure they allege the inducement to part with property explicitly, rather than relying on broader theories of information deprivation. As lower courts grapple with applying *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* across various circuits, *Constantinescu* serves as a roadmap for distinguishing between impermissible theories of informational loss and permissible theories of property deprivation.

Ultimately, *Constantinescu* demonstrates that while *Ciminelli* narrowed the scope of federal fraud, it did not eliminate it. The Fifth Circuit’s reasoning suggests that as long as the government can show a defendant schemed to obtain money or property through deception, the charge stands, even in complex financial markets. This approach balances the Supreme Court’s demand for traditional property interests with the practical realities of modern securities fraud. For defendants, the warning is clear: the line between influencing a market and defrauding investors is drawn at the moment a victim is tricked into handing over their money, regardless of what they receive in exchange.
