Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

## Ciminelli's Shadow: Defining Protected Property Interests in Securities Fraud

In the wake of the Supreme Court’s restrictive turn on federal fraud statutes, prosecutors have faced a significant hurdle in charging schemes that do not involve the traditional theft of cash or tangible assets. The Fifth Circuit’s recent decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* offers a crucial clarification on how these limitations apply in the modern context of social media-driven securities fraud. While the Supreme Court’s 2023 decision in *Ciminelli v. United States* barred convictions based solely on the deprivation of economic information, the Fifth Circuit has signaled that this barrier does not immunize "pump and dump" schemes where defendants induce victims to part with actual money. By distinguishing between the impermissible "right-to-control" theory and the permissible "fraudulent-inducement" theory, the court has preserved a vital tool for combating market manipulation, even as it adheres to the Court’s textualist constraints.

The case arises from a classic "pump and dump" scheme involving high-profile social media influencers. The government alleged that the defendants, who held themselves out as skilled traders, purchased securities and then posted false information to artificially inflate their prices. Once retail investors bought in based on these misrepresentations, the defendants sold their holdings for a profit, allegedly netting $114 million. The district court dismissed the indictment, reasoning that the scheme only deprived followers of potentially valuable economic information rather than a traditional property interest. This dismissal relied heavily on *Ciminelli*, which held that the "right to control" one’s assets is not a property interest protected by federal fraud statutes. The Fifth Circuit, however, found this reading too narrow for the facts at hand.

Judge Engelhardt’s opinion carefully navigates the doctrinal minefield left by *Ciminelli*. The court acknowledges that depriving a victim of information alone is insufficient for a fraud conviction. However, the Fifth Circuit emphasizes that the indictment here did not rely on a right-to-control theory. Instead, the charging document alleged a fraudulent-inducement theory, where the defendants used material misstatements to trick victims into purchasing securities. In the court’s view, inducing a follower to buy a stock is not merely withholding information; it is actively tricking the victim into handing over money or property. This distinction is vital. Under *Ciminelli*, the property interest must be traditional. By framing the fraud as the inducement to part with cash for a security, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment properly alleged the deprivation of a protected property interest, thereby avoiding the *Ciminelli* bar.

The decision also leans heavily on another recent Supreme Court ruling, *Kousisis v. United States*, to address the defendants' argument regarding intent. The defendants contended that because they intended to profit rather than cause their followers to suffer a net economic loss, the requisite intent to defraud was missing. The Fifth Circuit rejected this, citing *Kousisis* for the proposition that federal fraud statutes do not require the defendant to seek to leave the victim economically worse off. As the court noted, a fraud is complete when the defendant induces the deprivation of money or property under false pretenses, regardless of whether the victim receives something of equal value in return. This interpretation prevents defendants from shielding themselves by arguing that their victims received market-value securities, even if those securities were purchased based on lies.

This ruling has significant downstream implications for how federal prosecutors approach financial crimes in the digital age. By clarifying that fraudulent inducement to purchase securities survives *Ciminelli*, the Fifth Circuit ensures that the rise of social media trading does not create a safe harbor for manipulators. The decision reinforces that the core of fraud is the deceptive acquisition of property, not necessarily the infliction of a net loss. However, it also places a premium on the specificity of indictments. Prosecutors must now ensure they allege the inducement to part with property explicitly, rather than relying on broader theories of information deprivation. As lower courts grapple with applying *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* across various circuits, *Constantinescu* serves as a roadmap for distinguishing between impermissible theories of informational loss and permissible theories of property deprivation.

Ultimately, *Constantinescu* demonstrates that while *Ciminelli* narrowed the scope of federal fraud, it did not eliminate it. The Fifth Circuit’s reasoning suggests that as long as the government can show a defendant schemed to obtain money or property through deception, the charge stands, even in complex financial markets. This approach balances the Supreme Court’s demand for traditional property interests with the practical realities of modern securities fraud. For defendants, the warning is clear: the line between influencing a market and defrauding investors is drawn at the moment a victim is tricked into handing over their money, regardless of what they receive in exchange.



# Intent to Profit, Not Harm: Kousisis Reshapes the Mens Rea of Federal Fraud

The Supreme Court’s recent term has significantly recalibrated the boundaries of federal fraud statutes, and the Fifth Circuit has quickly moved to apply these new constraints in a high-stakes securities case. In *United States v. Constantinescu*, decided on October 2, 2025, the court provided a crucial early application of the Supreme Court’s 2025 decision in *Kousisis v. United States*. The ruling clarifies that a defendant’s intent to profit from a scheme is sufficient to establish the requisite intent to defraud, even absent an intent to cause the victim net economic loss. This decision is pivotal for white-collar prosecutors and defense attorneys alike, as it delineates the permissible scope of "fraudulent inducement" claims following the property interest limitations set forth in *Ciminelli*.

The case involves a classic "pump and dump" scheme executed through modern social media channels. The indictment alleged that a group of influencers, including defendants Constantinescu and Matlock, artificially inflated the prices of specific securities by posting false and misleading information to their large followings. After inducing followers to purchase the securities, the defendants sold their own holdings for a profit, allegedly netting $114 million. The district court initially dismissed the indictment, reasoning that the allegations failed to state an offense because they sought to deprive followers of valuable economic information rather than a traditional property interest.

On appeal, the Fifth Circuit reversed, relying heavily on the Supreme Court’s twin decisions in *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*. The court acknowledged that *Ciminelli* barred convictions based solely on the "right-to-control" theory, which treats the deprivation of economic information as a property loss. However, the Fifth Circuit distinguished the current indictment as alleging a "fraudulent-inducement theory." Under this theory, the defendants used material misrepresentations to trick victims into parting with money or property—specifically, by purchasing securities. The court emphasized that this theory protects money and property directly, unlike the intangible right to control assets rejected in *Ciminelli*.

The more doctrinally significant aspect of the opinion lies in the court’s analysis of the intent to defraud. The defendants argued that the indictment was deficient because it alleged they intended to profit from the scheme, not to harm their victims. The Fifth Circuit, citing *Kousisis*, rejected this distinction. The Supreme Court in *Kousisis* had recently held that fraud statutes do not require a defendant to seek to leave the victim economically worse off. Instead, a violation occurs when a defendant schemes to obtain the victim’s money or property through deception, regardless of whether the victim receives something of equal value in return.

Applying *Kousisis*, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the defendants’ object to obtain money was enough to satisfy the *mens rea* requirement. The court noted that the fraud is complete when the defendant induces the deprivation of money under false pretenses, even if the victim receives market-value securities. This rejects a "mirror image" requirement where the victim’s loss must strictly correlate to the defendant’s gain. By confirming that an intent to profit suffices as an intent to defraud, the Fifth Circuit clarifies that the government need not prove the defendants sought to injure the victims’ bottom lines, only that they sought to obtain value through deception.

This decision has immediate downstream effects for federal fraud litigation. It signals to lower courts that the "right-to-control" limitation from *Ciminelli* does not swallow the broader fraudulent-inducement theory. Prosecutors can pursue schemes where victims part with money for goods or services that might arguably have value, provided the transaction was induced by lies. Conversely, defense counsel must pivot from arguing that no property interest was harmed to arguing that no deception occurred or that the transaction lacked the requisite nexus to a security. The *Constantinescu* opinion serves as a critical bridge between the high-level principles of *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* and their practical application in complex securities fraud cases.

Ultimately, *Constantinescu* reinforces the vitality of the federal fraud statutes in the digital age. By anchoring its reasoning in *Kousisis*, the Fifth Circuit ensures that the intent to defraud remains focused on the deceptive acquisition of property rather than the net economic outcome for the victim. As more cases navigate the post-*Kousisis* landscape, this opinion will likely serve as a key citation for defining the boundaries of permissible fraud charges in the securities context.



# The Digital Pump and Dump: Prosecuting Social Media Schemes Under 18 U.S.C. § 1348

## The Rise of the Finfluencer and the Fraud Statute

In an era where financial advice is increasingly dispensed through social media algorithms rather than regulated brokerage offices, federal prosecutors face a unique challenge in policing market manipulation. Today, the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit addressed this tension in *United States v. Constantinescu*, reversing a district court’s dismissal of an indictment against a group of social media influencers accused of orchestrating a massive “pump and dump” scheme. The case matters not only for the defendants involved, who allegedly profited $114 million from their trading activities, but for the broader question of how traditional federal fraud statutes adapt to the digital economy. By clarifying the interplay between the securities fraud statute, 18 U.S.C. § 1348, and recent Supreme Court precedents, the Fifth Circuit has provided a critical roadmap for enforcing securities laws against the modern “finfluencer.”

The facts underlying the case are emblematic of the new challenges facing securities regulators. The government alleged that the defendants, who held themselves out as skilled traders with large followings, would purchase specific securities, post false and misleading information on social media to artificially inflate the price, and then sell their holdings for a profit. While the mechanics of a pump and dump are not new, the medium—social media platforms where trust is monetized through influence—presents distinct legal hurdles. The district court initially dismissed the indictment, concluding that the allegations failed to state an offense because they described a scheme to deprive followers of valuable economic information rather than a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit’s reversal signals that this distinction may be fatal to defendants only if the prosecution fails to properly frame the nature of the property interest at stake.

## Navigating Ciminelli and Kousisis

The core of the Fifth Circuit’s analysis rests on navigating the Supreme Court’s recent tightening of federal fraud statutes. The court relied heavily on *Ciminelli v. United States* (2023) and *Kousisis v. United States* (2025) to determine whether the indictment sufficiently alleged a scheme to defraud. In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court rejected the “right-to-control” theory of fraud, holding that the deprivation of potentially valuable economic information alone does not constitute the deprivation of a property interest protected by federal fraud statutes. This decision initially raised concerns among prosecutors that schemes involving the withholding or manipulation of information might fall outside the scope of criminal liability.

However, the Fifth Circuit distinguished *Constantinescu* from *Ciminelli* by emphasizing the specific theory of liability alleged in the indictment. Judge Engelhardt, writing for the court, noted that the government was not relying on a right-to-control theory. Instead, the indictment alleged a “fraudulent-inducement theory,” under which defendants used material misstatements to trick victims into a transaction requiring them to hand over money or property. The court found that while *Ciminelli* protects against criminalizing the mere deprivation of information, it does not immunize schemes where that information is used to induce the transfer of cash. By alleging that the defendants induced followers to purchase securities through lies, the government properly alleged a deprivation of a protected property interest—the money used to buy the stock—rather than just the information itself.

## Fraudulent Inducement in the Digital Age

Beyond the property interest question, the Fifth Circuit also addressed the defendants’ argument regarding intent to defraud. The defendants contended that they intended to profit from the scheme rather than cause harm to their followers, suggesting that because the followers received market-value securities in exchange for their money, no fraud occurred. The court firmly rejected this distinction, leaning on *Kousisis*. In that decision, the Supreme Court held that the fraud statutes do not require a defendant to seek to leave the victim economically worse off. A defendant violates the statute when they scheme to obtain the victim’s money or property, regardless of whether they provide something of equal value in return.

This holding is particularly significant for pump and dump schemes, where the victim often receives a security that has value at the moment of purchase, even if that value is artificially inflated. The Fifth Circuit reasoned that the fraud is complete when the defendant induces the deprivation of money under materially false pretenses. The fact that the defendants sought to enrich themselves rather than explicitly bankrupt their followers was a distinction without a difference. As the court noted, the indictment sufficiently alleged that the defendants had money in mind and used deception to obtain it. This interpretation ensures that the “intent to defraud” element does not become a loophole for schemes where the perpetrator claims they merely wanted to make a profit without actively intending to destroy the victim’s portfolio.

## Downstream Implications for Prosecutors

The decision in *Constantinescu* offers clear guidance for future indictments involving digital market manipulation. It confirms that 18 U.S.C. § 1348, which borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes, remains a viable tool for prosecuting social media trading schemes, provided the charging documents are drafted with precision. Prosecutors must ensure they allege a fraudulent-inducement theory that focuses on the transfer of money or property, rather than a vague claim that the victim was deprived of the right to control their assets. The Fifth Circuit’s reversal suggests that as long as the indictment plausibly alleges that the victim parted with cash based on lies, the *Ciminelli* limitation on information deprivation will not bar the case.

Furthermore, the ruling reinforces the Supreme Court’s recent trajectory in *Kousisis* that economic harm is not a prerequisite for fraud liability. This lowers the evidentiary burden for the government in proving the specific intent to cause net pecuniary loss. For defendants, the decision narrows the defense that they were merely providing bad investment advice or engaging in high-risk trading strategies. If those strategies are predicated on material misrepresentations designed to induce purchases, they fall squarely within the scope of federal securities fraud. As the regulatory landscape continues to grapple with the rise of unregulated financial influencers, *Constantinescu* serves as a reminder that federal fraud statutes are adaptable to modern tactics, provided the traditional elements of property deprivation and deceptive intent are properly pleaded.
