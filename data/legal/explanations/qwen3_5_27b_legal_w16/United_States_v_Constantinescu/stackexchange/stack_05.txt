Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Is actual economic loss to the victim required for a securities fraud conviction?
Question:
The opinion states that 'fraud statutes do not require a victim to suffer an economic loss from a defendant’s scheme' and mentions the rejection of a 'mirror image' requirement. If the followers bought securities at market value (even if artificially inflated), does the fact that they received the stock negate the fraud? How does Kousisis allow a conviction when the victim receives something of value in return for their money?
Answer:
**No, actual economic loss to the victim is not required for a securities fraud conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 1348.**

Based on *United States v. Constantinescu*, 5th Cir. (2025), the fact that followers received securities—even at market value—does not negate the fraud. The Fifth Circuit relies on the Supreme Court’s decision in *Kousisis v. United States* (2025) to establish that the fraud statutes focus on the **deceptive inducement** to part with money or property, rather than the victim’s net economic outcome.

### The Legal Standard: Fraudulent Inducement vs. Right-to-Control

To understand why the receipt of value does not defeat the charge, you must distinguish between two theories of fraud discussed in the opinion:

1.  **Right-to-Control Theory (Rejected):** In *Ciminelli v. United States*, the Supreme Court held that fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests. A scheme to merely deprive a victim of "potentially valuable economic information" (the right to control assets) is insufficient for conviction.
2.  **Fraudulent-Inducement Theory (Upheld):** In *Kousisis*, the Supreme Court confirmed that a defendant commits fraud when they use a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract requiring them to hand over money or property.

*Constantinescu* clarifies that the indictment here relies on the **fraudulent-inducement theory**. The defendants allegedly used false information to induce followers to purchase securities. This is distinct from merely withholding information; it is an active deception to obtain the followers' money.

### Why Receipt of Value Does Not Negate Fraud

You asked how *Kousisis* allows a conviction when the victim receives something of value in return. The *Constantinescu* opinion addresses this directly in Section III.B:

*   **No Requirement of Net Loss:** The fraud statutes do not require the defendant to seek to leave the victim "economically worse off." A defendant violates the statute when they scheme to "obtain" the victim's money or property, regardless of whether they provide something of equal value in return.
*   **No "Mirror Image" Requirement:** The court cites *United States v. Baker* to note there is no "mirror image" requirement where the victim's loss must supply the defendant's gain. Even if the victim receives stock worth the price paid, the fraud is complete if the transaction was induced by materially false pretenses.
*   **Intent to Obtain:** The critical element is the intent to obtain money or property. As the opinion states, "defendants’ object was to obtain money, and they did so by fraudulently inducing their followers to purchase securities." The fact that the defendants intended to profit rather than actively harm the victim's bottom line is "a distinction without a difference."

### Conclusion

Under *Constantinescu*, a securities fraud conviction does not hinge on the victim suffering a financial loss or receiving less than market value. The crime is complete when the defendant uses deception to induce a victim to part with money or property. Therefore, the followers' receipt of stock does not obviate the fraud charges because the statute protects the integrity of the transaction itself, not just the economic result.
