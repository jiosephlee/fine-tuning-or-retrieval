Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Why does the Fifth Circuit borrow from mail and wire fraud precedents to interpret 18 U.S.C. § 1348?
Question:
Section III notes there is 'scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit' and that § 1348 'borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes.' Is there a specific legislative history or statutory language that mandates this borrowing? Does this mean all elements of § 1348 are identical to §§ 1341 and 1343, or are there specific nuances in securities fraud that limit this analogy?
Answer:
# The Fifth Circuit relies on mail and wire fraud precedents because § 1348 statutorily "borrows key concepts" from those statutes.

Based on the opinion in *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit's reliance on mail (§ 1341) and wire (§ 1343) fraud precedents to interpret the securities fraud statute (§ 1348) is grounded in the statutory relationship between the laws and the lack of specific circuit precedent.

### Statutory Basis and Judicial Practice
The opinion explicitly states that while there is "scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit," § 1348 **"borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes."** 

Consequently, the court notes that "courts have given the terms similar treatment," often relying on mail and wire fraud cases to analyze securities fraud charges. The opinion cites *United States v. Greenlaw* for this proposition. The text does not cite specific legislative history mandating this approach; rather, it treats the borrowing of concepts as a matter of statutory interpretation and established judicial practice within the circuit to fill the gap where specific § 1348 caselaw is lacking.

### Are the Elements Identical?
The opinion indicates that the core elements are structurally identical, but their application is subject to specific limitations regarding property interests.

**1. Core Elements**
The court identifies two primary elements for securities fraud, defining them using mail and wire fraud jurisprudence (specifically *United States v. Evans*):
*   **Scheme to Defraud:** Requires a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others to obtain something of value.
*   **Intent to Defraud:** Requires an intent to deceive and cause some harm to result from the deceit.

**2. Nuances and Limitations**
While the elements are borrowed, the opinion highlights nuances derived from Supreme Court precedent that limit the analogy, particularly regarding what constitutes a protected property interest:

*   **Property Interest:** The opinion distinguishes between the "right-to-control theory" (rejected in *Ciminelli*) and the "fraudulent-inducement theory" (accepted). Under *Ciminelli*, depriving a victim of "potentially valuable economic information" is not a traditional property interest. However, § 1348 still covers schemes that fraudulently induce victims to part with "money or property" (e.g., purchasing securities). Thus, while the *structure* of the fraud charge is borrowed from §§ 1341/1343, the *object* of the fraud must remain a traditional property interest, not merely information.
*   **Intent to Harm:** The opinion clarifies that "intent to defraud" does not require an intent to cause net economic loss to the victim. Citing *Kousisis*, the court notes that a defendant violates the statute if they scheme to "obtain" the victim's money or property, even if they provide something of equal value in return. This aligns with mail and wire fraud standards but is specifically applied here to ensure that a "pump and dump" scheme constitutes fraud even if the victims receive market-value securities initially.

### Conclusion
The Fifth Circuit borrows from mail and wire fraud precedents because § 1348 is statutorily constructed to incorporate those concepts. However, this borrowing is not absolute; the court applies the borrowed elements through the lens of recent Supreme Court decisions (*Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*) to ensure that the charged conduct involves a deprivation of traditional property interests rather than merely economic information.
