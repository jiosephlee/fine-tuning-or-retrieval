Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### How does Ciminelli define 'traditional property interest' in the context of fraud statutes?
Question:
The opinion relies heavily on Ciminelli v. United States to distinguish between economic information and property. Ciminelli held that the 'right to control' assets isn't a traditional property interest. I'm struggling to understand the boundary here. If the defendants deprived followers of valuable economic information (the right to know the true price), why isn't that a property interest under Ciminelli? What specific characteristic makes money or securities 'traditional property' while information is not?
Answer:
Based on the Fifth Circuit’s opinion in *United States v. Constantinescu*, *Ciminelli* defines a "traditional property interest" by distinguishing between abstract rights to information and tangible assets like money or securities.

According to the opinion, *Ciminelli* held that federal fraud statutes protect only **traditional property interests**. The Supreme Court explicitly ruled that **"the right to valuable economic information... is not a traditional property interest."** Consequently, a "right-to-control theory"—where the alleged property interest is the victim's **"right to control [his] assets"** or the information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions—cannot support a fraud conviction.

The boundary you are struggling with turns on the **object of the fraud**, not merely the existence of deception. The opinion outlines two distinct theories:

1.  **Right-to-Control Theory (Not Protected):** This theory alleges a defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information (e.g., the true price of a security). Under *Ciminelli*, this information is not "property."
2.  **Fraudulent-Inducement Theory (Protected):** This theory alleges a defendant used a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires **"handing over her money or property."** This *is* a traditional property interest.

In *Constantinescu*, the court reversed the dismissal because the indictment did not rely on the deprivation of information alone. Instead, it alleged a scheme to **induce followers to purchase securities**. While the defendants did withhold information about the true price, the indictment properly alleged they sought to obtain **money or property** (the cash used to buy the securities) through deception.

Therefore, under *Ciminelli* as applied here, information itself is not property, but the money or securities transferred as a result of the deception are. The fraud is complete when the defendant induces the deprivation of money or property under false pretenses, regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss.
