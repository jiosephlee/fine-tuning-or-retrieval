Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What is the standard of review for a district court's dismissal of an indictment?
Question:
In Section II, the Fifth Circuit states they review the sufficiency of an indictment de novo. I'm confused about how this standard interacts with the three requirements listed (enumerating elements, informing defendant, double jeopardy defense). Does de novo review mean the appellate court ignores the district court's reasoning entirely, or just the conclusion? How does this standard specifically apply when the district court dismisses based on a failure to state an offense rather than a lack of evidence?
Answer:
**Answer**

Based on *United States v. Constantinescu*, the standard of review for a district court's dismissal of an indictment is **de novo**.

### What *De Novo* Review Means in This Context
In Section II, the Fifth Circuit explicitly states: "We review the sufficiency of an indictment de novo." The opinion clarifies that this standard requires the appellate court to make an independent legal determination rather than deferring to the district court's analysis. Specifically, the court must:

1.  **Take the allegations of the indictment as true.**
2.  **Determine whether an offense has been stated.**

This means the appellate court does not simply review whether the district court made a reasonable error; it decides the legal question of sufficiency itself. As demonstrated in the opinion, the Fifth Circuit independently analyzed whether the indictment alleged a "scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud" under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, ultimately reversing the district court’s conclusion that the indictment failed to allege a property interest.

### Interaction with the Three Requirements
The *de novo* standard applies directly to the three requirements for a valid indictment listed in Section II (*United States v. Gaytan*):
1.  Enumerating each prima facie element of the charged offense;
2.  Fairly informing the defendant of the charges; and
3.  Providing a double jeopardy defense.

Because the review is *de novo*, the appellate court independently assesses whether the text of the indictment satisfies these requirements. In *Constantinescu*, the court concluded that the indictment sufficiently alleged each material element of the offense, satisfying the first requirement, and thus reversing the dismissal.

### Failure to State an Offense vs. Lack of Evidence
Your question regarding "failure to state an offense" versus "lack of evidence" is addressed by the court's instruction to "take the allegations of the indictment as true."

*   **Failure to State an Offense:** This is a legal challenge to the sufficiency of the *indictment's allegations*. Under *de novo* review, the appellate court examines whether the text alleges every material element of the crime. In *Constantinescu*, the district court dismissed because it concluded the indictment failed to allege a scheme to deprive victims of a "traditional property interest." The Fifth Circuit reviewed this legal conclusion *de novo* and found the indictment sufficient because it alleged a "fraudulent-inducement theory" involving money and property.
*   **Lack of Evidence:** The *de novo* standard for indictment sufficiency does not weigh evidence. As the opinion notes, the court looks at whether the indictment "enumerate[s] each prima facie element." It does not require proof that the evidence will support a conviction at trial, only that the charges legally state an offense.

In summary, *de novo* review means the appellate court ignores the district court's legal conclusion regarding sufficiency and makes its own determination based solely on whether the indictment's allegations, taken as true, state an offense.
