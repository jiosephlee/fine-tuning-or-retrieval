Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What distinguishes 'fraudulent-inducement' from the rejected 'right-to-control' theory?
Question:
Section III.A explains that the indictment alleges a 'fraudulent-inducement theory' rather than a 'right-to-control theory.' The court says fraudulent-inducement is 'not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.' Can someone clarify the legal distinction between these two theories? If both involve deception, why does one protect property (inducement) while the other only protects information (control), and why does that distinction matter for the sufficiency of the indictment?
Answer:
The core distinction lies in **what interest the fraud statute protects**: traditional property interests (money/assets) versus the intangible right to control information.

In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit clarifies this distinction by relying on the Supreme Court’s recent decisions in *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*. Here is the breakdown of the two theories and why the distinction determines the sufficiency of the indictment.

### 1. The Rejected Theory: Right-to-Control
As explained in Section III.A, the **right-to-control theory** was rejected in *Ciminelli*. Under this theory, the government attempts to prove fraud by showing the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of "potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions."

*   **The Property Interest:** The victim’s property is defined as "the right to control [his] assets."
*   **The Holding:** The Supreme Court held that the "right to valuable economic information" is **not a traditional property interest**. Therefore, depriving someone of information alone—even if it influences their economic decisions—cannot form the basis of a federal fraud conviction.

### 2. The Accepted Theory: Fraudulent-Inducement
The **fraudulent-inducement theory**, confirmed in *Kousisis*, focuses on the actual transfer of value. Under this theory, a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property."

*   **The Property Interest:** This theory protects **"money and property."**
*   **The Holding:** The Court in *Constantinescu* notes that this theory "is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory" because it targets the deprivation of actual money or property, which *is* a protected traditional property interest.

### 3. Why the Distinction Matters for the Indictment
The distinction matters because 18 U.S.C. § 1348 (the securities fraud statute) prohibits schemes to obtain **"any money or property."**

*   **If the indictment relied on Right-to-Control:** It would fail because it would only allege the deprivation of information (which is not "money or property" under *Ciminelli*). This was the district court’s error.
*   **Because the indictment relies on Fraudulent-Inducement:** It succeeds because it alleges the defendants induced followers to **purchase securities**. As the opinion states, this requires the followers to "part with their money or property."

In short, while both theories involve deception, only fraudulent-inducement alleges a scheme to obtain a **traditional property interest** (money/securities) rather than merely manipulating the victim's **informational control**. Because the indictment in *Constantinescu* alleged the defendants tricked victims into handing over money to buy securities, it sufficiently states an offense under the fraudulent-inducement theory.
