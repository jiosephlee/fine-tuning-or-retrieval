Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What is the standard of review for a district court's dismissal of an indictment?
Question:
In Section II, the Fifth Circuit states they review the sufficiency of an indictment de novo. I'm confused about how this standard interacts with the three requirements listed (enumerating elements, informing defendant, double jeopardy defense). Does de novo review mean the appellate court ignores the district court's reasoning entirely, or just the conclusion? How does this standard specifically apply when the district court dismisses based on a failure to state an offense rather than a lack of evidence?
Answer:
**Answer**

Based on *United States v. Constantinescu*, the standard of review for a district court's dismissal of an indictment is **de novo**.

### What *De Novo* Review Means in This Context
In Section II, the Fifth Circuit explicitly states: "We review the sufficiency of an indictment de novo." The opinion clarifies that this standard requires the appellate court to make an independent legal determination rather than deferring to the district court's analysis. Specifically, the court must:

1.  **Take the allegations of the indictment as true.**
2.  **Determine whether an offense has been stated.**

This means the appellate court does not simply review whether the district court made a reasonable error; it decides the legal question of sufficiency itself. As demonstrated in the opinion, the Fifth Circuit independently analyzed whether the indictment alleged a "scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud" under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, ultimately reversing the district court’s conclusion that the indictment failed to allege a property interest.

### Interaction with the Three Requirements
The *de novo* standard applies directly to the three requirements for a valid indictment listed in Section II (*United States v. Gaytan*):
1.  Enumerating each prima facie element of the charged offense;
2.  Fairly informing the defendant of the charges; and
3.  Providing a double jeopardy defense.

Because the review is *de novo*, the appellate court independently assesses whether the text of the indictment satisfies these requirements. In *Constantinescu*, the court concluded that the indictment sufficiently alleged each material element of the offense, satisfying the first requirement, and thus reversing the dismissal.

### Failure to State an Offense vs. Lack of Evidence
Your question regarding "failure to state an offense" versus "lack of evidence" is addressed by the court's instruction to "take the allegations of the indictment as true."

*   **Failure to State an Offense:** This is a legal challenge to the sufficiency of the *indictment's allegations*. Under *de novo* review, the appellate court examines whether the text alleges every material element of the crime. In *Constantinescu*, the district court dismissed because it concluded the indictment failed to allege a scheme to deprive victims of a "traditional property interest." The Fifth Circuit reviewed this legal conclusion *de novo* and found the indictment sufficient because it alleged a "fraudulent-inducement theory" involving money and property.
*   **Lack of Evidence:** The *de novo* standard for indictment sufficiency does not weigh evidence. As the opinion notes, the court looks at whether the indictment "enumerate[s] each prima facie element." It does not require proof that the evidence will support a conviction at trial, only that the charges legally state an offense.

In summary, *de novo* review means the appellate court ignores the district court's legal conclusion regarding sufficiency and makes its own determination based solely on whether the indictment's allegations, taken as true, state an offense.

### How does Ciminelli define 'traditional property interest' in the context of fraud statutes?
Question:
The opinion relies heavily on Ciminelli v. United States to distinguish between economic information and property. Ciminelli held that the 'right to control' assets isn't a traditional property interest. I'm struggling to understand the boundary here. If the defendants deprived followers of valuable economic information (the right to know the true price), why isn't that a property interest under Ciminelli? What specific characteristic makes money or securities 'traditional property' while information is not?
Answer:
Based on the Fifth Circuit’s opinion in *United States v. Constantinescu*, *Ciminelli* defines a "traditional property interest" by distinguishing between abstract rights to information and tangible assets like money or securities.

According to the opinion, *Ciminelli* held that federal fraud statutes protect only **traditional property interests**. The Supreme Court explicitly ruled that **"the right to valuable economic information... is not a traditional property interest."** Consequently, a "right-to-control theory"—where the alleged property interest is the victim's **"right to control [his] assets"** or the information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions—cannot support a fraud conviction.

The boundary you are struggling with turns on the **object of the fraud**, not merely the existence of deception. The opinion outlines two distinct theories:

1.  **Right-to-Control Theory (Not Protected):** This theory alleges a defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information (e.g., the true price of a security). Under *Ciminelli*, this information is not "property."
2.  **Fraudulent-Inducement Theory (Protected):** This theory alleges a defendant used a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires **"handing over her money or property."** This *is* a traditional property interest.

In *Constantinescu*, the court reversed the dismissal because the indictment did not rely on the deprivation of information alone. Instead, it alleged a scheme to **induce followers to purchase securities**. While the defendants did withhold information about the true price, the indictment properly alleged they sought to obtain **money or property** (the cash used to buy the securities) through deception.

Therefore, under *Ciminelli* as applied here, information itself is not property, but the money or securities transferred as a result of the deception are. The fraud is complete when the defendant induces the deprivation of money or property under false pretenses, regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss.

### What distinguishes 'fraudulent-inducement' from the rejected 'right-to-control' theory?
Question:
Section III.A explains that the indictment alleges a 'fraudulent-inducement theory' rather than a 'right-to-control theory.' The court says fraudulent-inducement is 'not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory.' Can someone clarify the legal distinction between these two theories? If both involve deception, why does one protect property (inducement) while the other only protects information (control), and why does that distinction matter for the sufficiency of the indictment?
Answer:
The core distinction lies in **what interest the fraud statute protects**: traditional property interests (money/assets) versus the intangible right to control information.

In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit clarifies this distinction by relying on the Supreme Court’s recent decisions in *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*. Here is the breakdown of the two theories and why the distinction determines the sufficiency of the indictment.

### 1. The Rejected Theory: Right-to-Control
As explained in Section III.A, the **right-to-control theory** was rejected in *Ciminelli*. Under this theory, the government attempts to prove fraud by showing the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of "potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions."

*   **The Property Interest:** The victim’s property is defined as "the right to control [his] assets."
*   **The Holding:** The Supreme Court held that the "right to valuable economic information" is **not a traditional property interest**. Therefore, depriving someone of information alone—even if it influences their economic decisions—cannot form the basis of a federal fraud conviction.

### 2. The Accepted Theory: Fraudulent-Inducement
The **fraudulent-inducement theory**, confirmed in *Kousisis*, focuses on the actual transfer of value. Under this theory, a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property."

*   **The Property Interest:** This theory protects **"money and property."**
*   **The Holding:** The Court in *Constantinescu* notes that this theory "is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory" because it targets the deprivation of actual money or property, which *is* a protected traditional property interest.

### 3. Why the Distinction Matters for the Indictment
The distinction matters because 18 U.S.C. § 1348 (the securities fraud statute) prohibits schemes to obtain **"any money or property."**

*   **If the indictment relied on Right-to-Control:** It would fail because it would only allege the deprivation of information (which is not "money or property" under *Ciminelli*). This was the district court’s error.
*   **Because the indictment relies on Fraudulent-Inducement:** It succeeds because it alleges the defendants induced followers to **purchase securities**. As the opinion states, this requires the followers to "part with their money or property."

In short, while both theories involve deception, only fraudulent-inducement alleges a scheme to obtain a **traditional property interest** (money/securities) rather than merely manipulating the victim's **informational control**. Because the indictment in *Constantinescu* alleged the defendants tricked victims into handing over money to buy securities, it sufficiently states an offense under the fraudulent-inducement theory.

### Does an intent to profit satisfy the 'intent to defraud' element under Kousisis?
Question:
In Section III.B, the court cites Kousisis to reject the argument that defendants must intend to harm the victim. The opinion states that intending to profit is sufficient because the defendants 'had money in mind.' I'm confused about the mens rea here. If a defendant intends to make money without intending to hurt the victim's bottom line, how does that constitute an 'intent to defraud' rather than just aggressive trading? What is the specific mental state required under Kousisis?
Answer:
**Yes, under *United States v. Constantinescu*, an intent to profit satisfies the "intent to defraud" element.**

The Fifth Circuit explicitly rejects the argument that a defendant must intend to cause economic harm to the victim to establish intent to defraud. In Section III.B, the court relies on *Kousisis v. United States* to clarify that the requisite mental state focuses on the defendant's intent to **obtain** money or property through deception, rather than an intent to leave the victim economically worse off.

### The *Kousisis* Standard
According to *Constantinescu*, the Supreme Court’s decision in *Kousisis* established that the fraud statutes do not require a victim to suffer an economic loss from the defendant’s scheme. The court explains:

> A defendant violates the fraud statutes when he “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.”

Consequently, the court states that the defendants' argument—that they intended to profit rather than harm their victims—is a “distinction without a difference.” As long as the indictment alleges that the defendants’ object was to obtain money by fraudulently inducing followers to purchase securities, the "intent to defraud" element is met.

### Intent to Profit vs. Intent to Harm
The confusion regarding "aggressive trading" versus fraud hinges on the **fraudulent-inducement theory** applied in this opinion. Unlike the "right-to-control" theory rejected in *Ciminelli* (which focused on the deprivation of information), the fraudulent-inducement theory protects money and property.

Under this theory, the specific mental state required is:
1.  **Intent to deceive:** Making material misrepresentations (e.g., false posts about trading positions).
2.  **Intent to obtain:** Scheming to get the victim to part with money or property.

The court notes that the indictment sufficiently alleges this by showing the defendants "had money in mind" when they pumped and dumped the securities. The fact that the victim receives market-value securities in return does not negate the fraud, because the statute does not impose a "mirror image" requirement where the victim's loss must supply the defendant's gain.

### Conclusion
To satisfy the "intent to defraud" element under *Kousisis* as applied in *Constantinescu*, the government need only prove the defendant intended to deceive the victim to obtain money or property. An intent to profit from that deception is sufficient; a specific intent to harm the victim's bottom line is not required.

### Is actual economic loss to the victim required for a securities fraud conviction?
Question:
The opinion states that 'fraud statutes do not require a victim to suffer an economic loss from a defendant’s scheme' and mentions the rejection of a 'mirror image' requirement. If the followers bought securities at market value (even if artificially inflated), does the fact that they received the stock negate the fraud? How does Kousisis allow a conviction when the victim receives something of value in return for their money?
Answer:
**No, actual economic loss to the victim is not required for a securities fraud conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 1348.**

Based on *United States v. Constantinescu*, 5th Cir. (2025), the fact that followers received securities—even at market value—does not negate the fraud. The Fifth Circuit relies on the Supreme Court’s decision in *Kousisis v. United States* (2025) to establish that the fraud statutes focus on the **deceptive inducement** to part with money or property, rather than the victim’s net economic outcome.

### The Legal Standard: Fraudulent Inducement vs. Right-to-Control

To understand why the receipt of value does not defeat the charge, you must distinguish between two theories of fraud discussed in the opinion:

1.  **Right-to-Control Theory (Rejected):** In *Ciminelli v. United States*, the Supreme Court held that fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests. A scheme to merely deprive a victim of "potentially valuable economic information" (the right to control assets) is insufficient for conviction.
2.  **Fraudulent-Inducement Theory (Upheld):** In *Kousisis*, the Supreme Court confirmed that a defendant commits fraud when they use a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract requiring them to hand over money or property.

*Constantinescu* clarifies that the indictment here relies on the **fraudulent-inducement theory**. The defendants allegedly used false information to induce followers to purchase securities. This is distinct from merely withholding information; it is an active deception to obtain the followers' money.

### Why Receipt of Value Does Not Negate Fraud

You asked how *Kousisis* allows a conviction when the victim receives something of value in return. The *Constantinescu* opinion addresses this directly in Section III.B:

*   **No Requirement of Net Loss:** The fraud statutes do not require the defendant to seek to leave the victim "economically worse off." A defendant violates the statute when they scheme to "obtain" the victim's money or property, regardless of whether they provide something of equal value in return.
*   **No "Mirror Image" Requirement:** The court cites *United States v. Baker* to note there is no "mirror image" requirement where the victim's loss must supply the defendant's gain. Even if the victim receives stock worth the price paid, the fraud is complete if the transaction was induced by materially false pretenses.
*   **Intent to Obtain:** The critical element is the intent to obtain money or property. As the opinion states, "defendants’ object was to obtain money, and they did so by fraudulently inducing their followers to purchase securities." The fact that the defendants intended to profit rather than actively harm the victim's bottom line is "a distinction without a difference."

### Conclusion

Under *Constantinescu*, a securities fraud conviction does not hinge on the victim suffering a financial loss or receiving less than market value. The crime is complete when the defendant uses deception to induce a victim to part with money or property. Therefore, the followers' receipt of stock does not obviate the fraud charges because the statute protects the integrity of the transaction itself, not just the economic result.

### Why does the Fifth Circuit borrow from mail and wire fraud precedents to interpret 18 U.S.C. § 1348?
Question:
Section III notes there is 'scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit' and that § 1348 'borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes.' Is there a specific legislative history or statutory language that mandates this borrowing? Does this mean all elements of § 1348 are identical to §§ 1341 and 1343, or are there specific nuances in securities fraud that limit this analogy?
Answer:
# The Fifth Circuit relies on mail and wire fraud precedents because § 1348 statutorily "borrows key concepts" from those statutes.

Based on the opinion in *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit's reliance on mail (§ 1341) and wire (§ 1343) fraud precedents to interpret the securities fraud statute (§ 1348) is grounded in the statutory relationship between the laws and the lack of specific circuit precedent.

### Statutory Basis and Judicial Practice
The opinion explicitly states that while there is "scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit," § 1348 **"borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes."** 

Consequently, the court notes that "courts have given the terms similar treatment," often relying on mail and wire fraud cases to analyze securities fraud charges. The opinion cites *United States v. Greenlaw* for this proposition. The text does not cite specific legislative history mandating this approach; rather, it treats the borrowing of concepts as a matter of statutory interpretation and established judicial practice within the circuit to fill the gap where specific § 1348 caselaw is lacking.

### Are the Elements Identical?
The opinion indicates that the core elements are structurally identical, but their application is subject to specific limitations regarding property interests.

**1. Core Elements**
The court identifies two primary elements for securities fraud, defining them using mail and wire fraud jurisprudence (specifically *United States v. Evans*):
*   **Scheme to Defraud:** Requires a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others to obtain something of value.
*   **Intent to Defraud:** Requires an intent to deceive and cause some harm to result from the deceit.

**2. Nuances and Limitations**
While the elements are borrowed, the opinion highlights nuances derived from Supreme Court precedent that limit the analogy, particularly regarding what constitutes a protected property interest:

*   **Property Interest:** The opinion distinguishes between the "right-to-control theory" (rejected in *Ciminelli*) and the "fraudulent-inducement theory" (accepted). Under *Ciminelli*, depriving a victim of "potentially valuable economic information" is not a traditional property interest. However, § 1348 still covers schemes that fraudulently induce victims to part with "money or property" (e.g., purchasing securities). Thus, while the *structure* of the fraud charge is borrowed from §§ 1341/1343, the *object* of the fraud must remain a traditional property interest, not merely information.
*   **Intent to Harm:** The opinion clarifies that "intent to defraud" does not require an intent to cause net economic loss to the victim. Citing *Kousisis*, the court notes that a defendant violates the statute if they scheme to "obtain" the victim's money or property, even if they provide something of equal value in return. This aligns with mail and wire fraud standards but is specifically applied here to ensure that a "pump and dump" scheme constitutes fraud even if the victims receive market-value securities initially.

### Conclusion
The Fifth Circuit borrows from mail and wire fraud precedents because § 1348 is statutorily constructed to incorporate those concepts. However, this borrowing is not absolute; the court applies the borrowed elements through the lens of recent Supreme Court decisions (*Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*) to ensure that the charged conduct involves a deprivation of traditional property interests rather than merely economic information.
