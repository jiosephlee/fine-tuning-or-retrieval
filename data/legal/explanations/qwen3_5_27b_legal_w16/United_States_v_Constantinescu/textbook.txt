Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Factual Background and Procedural Posture

## Overview of the Defendants and Their Social Media Influence
The case involves eight individuals indicted by the federal government: Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight. According to the superseding indictment, these defendants were not merely private investors but public figures who cultivated significant influence in the financial markets. Each defendant maintained large followings across various social media platforms, where they held themselves out to be skilled stock traders. They frequently posted their trading activities to these audiences, establishing a relationship of trust and authority with their followers. This social media presence is a critical factual component of the case, as the government alleges the defendants leveraged this influence to manipulate market behavior. For a law student reading this opinion, it is important to note that the defendants' status as influencers is not incidental; it is the mechanism through which the alleged fraud was executed. The indictment relies on the premise that the defendants used their platforms to disseminate information that their followers would rely upon when making investment decisions.

## Allegations of the 'Pump and Dump' Securities Scheme
The core of the criminal allegations centers on a specific type of market manipulation known as a “pump and dump” scheme. The government alleges that the defendants coordinated to purchase specific securities and then artificially inflate their prices before selling them for a profit. The mechanics of the scheme, as detailed in the indictment, involved a three-step process. First, the defendants would purchase a security. Second, they would “pump” the price of that security by posting false and misleading information about it on social media. These posts were designed to induce other investors—the defendants' followers—to purchase the security, thereby artificially increasing its price through market demand. Third, once the price was inflated, the defendants would “dump” their holdings by secretly selling the security for a profit. The indictment alleges that the defendants profited $114 million from this scheme. Understanding these factual allegations is essential because the legal dispute turns on whether these actions constitute a fraud on property or merely a deprivation of information. The indictment characterizes the defendants' conduct as inducing followers to part with money through deception, rather than simply withholding information.

## Specific Indictment Charges under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348, 1349, and 2
The defendants were charged under specific provisions of the United States Code. The primary charges include conspiracy to commit securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, and multiple counts of securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 2. Section 1348 is the federal securities fraud statute, which prohibits schemes to defraud any person in connection with any security. Section 1349 addresses the conspiracy to commit the underlying offense. Section 2 generally pertains to principals and aiding and abetting, ensuring liability extends to those who assist in the commission of the crime. Procedurally, the case moved forward with a superseding indictment, which replaced or amended the original charges to reflect the government's specific allegations regarding the scheme. Following the indictment, one defendant, Daniel Knight, pleaded guilty. The remaining defendants did not enter a plea but instead challenged the legal sufficiency of the charges against them. This divergence in procedural posture—where one defendant accepts liability while the others contest the charges—is common in multi-defendant conspiracies and sets the stage for the remaining defendants' motion to dismiss.

## District Court Dismissal and Rationale
The remaining defendants filed a motion to dismiss the indictment, arguing that the government had failed to state a valid offense under federal law. The district court granted this motion, dismissing the indictment entirely. The district court’s rationale provides the central legal conflict that the Fifth Circuit must resolve. The court concluded that the indictment failed to allege that the defendants schemed to deprive victims of a “traditional property interest.” Instead, the district court viewed the allegations as merely claiming that the defendants sought to deprive their followers of potentially valuable economic information. In the context of fraud law, the distinction between depriving a victim of property (like money) and depriving them of information (the right to control their assets) is legally significant. By dismissing the indictment, the district court effectively ruled that the government’s theory of the case did not meet the statutory requirements for securities fraud. This dismissal was a pretrial ruling on the legal sufficiency of the charging document, meaning the court decided the case based on the text of the indictment without holding a trial on the facts.

## Government's Appeal and Appellate Jurisdiction
Following the district court’s dismissal, the government filed a timely appeal to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit. Under federal criminal procedure, the government is generally permitted to appeal a district court order dismissing an indictment, as this prevents the government from being barred from prosecuting the case without a trial. The appellate court’s role in this context is to determine whether the indictment was legally sufficient to proceed. The Fifth Circuit reviews the sufficiency of an indictment *de novo*, meaning the appellate court reviews the legal question independently without deferring to the district court’s conclusion. In this review, the court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and determine whether an offense has been stated. The indictment must enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense, inform the defendant of the charges, and provide a double jeopardy defense. With this standard of review in mind, the Fifth Circuit examined whether the district court correctly interpreted the securities fraud statute in light of the specific allegations regarding the defendants' social media scheme.

# Legal Standards and Statutory Framework

## De Novo Standard of Review for Indictment Sufficiency

When an appellate court evaluates whether a criminal indictment is legally sufficient, it applies a specific standard of review that dictates how closely the court scrutinizes the lower court's decision. In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit clarified that it reviews the sufficiency of an indictment *de novo*. This standard means the appellate court does not defer to the district court’s legal conclusions but instead examines the matter anew. Under this standard, the court is required to take the factual allegations contained within the indictment as true. The court’s task is not to determine the truth of the facts, but to determine whether, assuming those facts are true, an offense has been properly stated. This approach ensures that the legal adequacy of the charging document is judged against the statutory requirements without the filter of the district court’s initial ruling.

## Three Requirements for a Sufficient Indictment

To survive a motion to dismiss, an indictment must satisfy three specific requirements established by Fifth Circuit precedent. First, the indictment must enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense. This ensures that the prosecution has formally charged a crime that exists under the law. Second, the indictment must fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him. This requirement protects the defendant’s ability to prepare a defense by providing clear notice of the accusations. Third, the indictment must provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions. This prevents the government from prosecuting the defendant twice for the same offense based on the same set of facts. In sum, for an indictment to be sufficient, it must allege each material element of the offense. If the indictment fails to meet these criteria, it fails to state an offense, and the dismissal of the indictment is appropriate.

## Elements of Securities Fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348

The substantive law governing the charges in *Constantinescu* is the securities fraud statute, 18 U.S.C. § 1348. This statute prohibits two primary types of conduct: schemes to defraud any person in connection with any security, and schemes to obtain money or property through false or fraudulent pretenses in connection with the purchase or sale of any security. Based on this statutory language and controlling circuit precedent, the Fifth Circuit identifies two core elements required to prove securities fraud. The first element is a "scheme to defraud." This requires a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the entity to be deceived. The second element is an "intent to defraud." This requires an intent to deceive and an intent to cause some harm to result from the deceit. Therefore, a defendant commits securities fraud when they engage in deception and obtaining the victim’s money or property was an object of that fraud.

## Relationship Between Securities Fraud and Mail/Wire Fraud Statutes

Because there is scant caselaw specifically construing the securities fraud statute within the Fifth Circuit, courts look to related statutes for guidance. The Fifth Circuit notes that § 1348 borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes. Consequently, courts have given the terms within the securities fraud statute similar treatment to those found in mail and wire fraud cases. This interpretive approach allows courts to rely on established mail and wire fraud jurisprudence when analyzing securities fraud charges. By treating the terms similarly, the court can apply well-developed legal principles regarding what constitutes a "scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud" to the context of securities transactions. This relationship is critical for understanding how the court defines the boundaries of fraud in this specific context.

## Introduction to Supreme Court Precedents: *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*

The legal analysis in *Constantinescu* is heavily framed by two recent Supreme Court decisions: *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States*. In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court held that a fraud conviction cannot be based solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information. The Court rejected the "right-to-control theory," which argued that the right to control one’s assets is a property interest protected by fraud statutes. The Court clarified that the right to valuable economic information is not a traditional property interest. Conversely, in *Kousisis*, the Supreme Court addressed the "fraudulent-inducement theory." Under this theory, a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract requiring the handing over of money or property. The Court in *Kousisis* confirmed that this theory protects money and property and does not require the defendant to seek to cause the victim a net pecuniary loss. Together, these precedents distinguish between impermissible theories based on information deprivation and permissible theories based on the fraudulent inducement of property transfers.

# The Scheme to Defraud and Property Interest Requirement

## The Defendants' Argument Regarding Economic Information vs. Property

In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the central legal challenge to the indictment focused on the nature of the property interest the defendants allegedly targeted. The defendants argued that the district court correctly dismissed the indictment because it failed to allege that they schemed to deprive victims of a traditional property interest. Instead, the defendants contended that the government’s allegations merely described a scheme to deprive their social-media followers of potentially valuable economic information. According to the defendants, their goal was to enrich themselves by manipulating market prices, not to injure their followers or take their property directly. They posited that because the followers received securities in exchange for their money, there was no deprivation of a protected property interest, only a loss of information that would have allowed them to make better discretionary economic decisions. This argument relied on the premise that the federal fraud statutes do not protect intangible rights to information, but rather concrete property rights.

## Supreme Court Holding in Ciminelli v. United States

To evaluate the defendants' argument, the Fifth Circuit looked to the Supreme Court’s recent decision in *Ciminelli v. United States*. In *Ciminelli*, the Court addressed the scope of federal fraud statutes, specifically holding that a fraud conviction cannot be based solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information. The *Ciminelli* case involved a "right-to-control" theory, where the government attempted to prove wire fraud by showing the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of the right to control their assets by withholding information necessary for discretionary economic decisions. The Supreme Court rejected this theory, reasoning that federal fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests. The Court explicitly stated that the right to valuable economic information is not a traditional property interest. Consequently, the right-to-control theory cannot form the basis for a fraud conviction under federal law. This precedent established a critical boundary: the government cannot prosecute fraud merely by alleging that a defendant deprived a victim of information that would have influenced their economic choices.

## Distinguishing Right-to-Control Theory from Fraudulent-Inducement Theory

The Fifth Circuit distinguished the facts in *Constantinescu* from the *Ciminelli* ruling by identifying a different legal theory present in the indictment: the fraudulent-inducement theory. While the right-to-control theory focuses on the deprivation of information or the right to control assets, the fraudulent-inducement theory focuses on the transfer of money or property. Under the fraudulent-inducement theory, a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into entering a contract that requires handing over money or property. The Supreme Court, in *Kousisis v. United States*, clarified that this theory is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory rejected in *Ciminelli*. Instead, fraudulent-inducement protects money and property directly. The distinction is vital for the sufficiency of the indictment: if the government alleges only that information was withheld, the charge fails under *Ciminelli*. However, if the government alleges that deception was used to induce a transfer of funds or assets, the charge proceeds under the fraudulent-inducement theory, which remains valid.

## Application of Fraudulent-Inducement Theory to the Indictment

Applying this distinction to the *Constantinescu* indictment, the court found that the government did not rely on the rejected right-to-control theory. The indictment did not merely allege that the defendants withheld information from their followers. Rather, it alleged that the defendants induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities. The factual allegations described a "pump and dump" scheme where defendants posted false and misleading information to artificially inflate security prices, causing investors to buy in. This conduct fits squarely within the fraudulent-inducement framework because the deception was the mechanism used to trick the victims into a transaction. The court noted that the indictment alleged the defendants used material misstatements to induce the followers to part with their money or property to purchase the securities. Because the indictment explicitly described an inducement to purchase, rather than a mere withholding of information, it avoided the pitfalls of *Ciminelli*.

## Why the Indictment Alleges Deprivation of Money or Property

Ultimately, the court concluded that the indictment sufficiently alleged the deprivation of a protected property interest because the followers were induced to part with their money. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Kousisis* reinforced that fraud statutes do not require the victim to suffer a net economic loss for a conviction to stand. Even if the victims received securities of market value in exchange for their money, the fraud was complete when the defendant induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses. In *Constantinescu*, the defendants' object was to obtain money by fraudulently inducing their followers to buy securities. The indictment alleged that the defendants posted false information "so that other investors were induced to purchase the security." This allegation demonstrates that the defendants had money in mind and succeeded in obtaining it through deception. Therefore, the indictment properly alleged a scheme to defraud followers of money and property, satisfying the property interest requirement despite the defendants' arguments regarding economic information.

# The Intent to Defraud and Economic Harm Requirement

Having established that the indictment alleged a deprivation of a protected property interest rather than merely economic information, the Fifth Circuit turned to the second critical element of securities fraud: the intent to defraud. This section of the opinion addresses whether the government sufficiently alleged that the defendants possessed the specific intent required by the fraud statutes. The court focused on the relationship between a defendant’s motive to profit and the legal requirement to cause harm to the victim. In doing so, the court relied heavily on recent Supreme Court jurisprudence to clarify that a fraud conviction does not require proof that the defendant sought to leave the victim with a net economic loss.

## Elements of Intent to Defraud: Deception and Harm

To understand the court’s analysis, one must first grasp the statutory elements of intent as defined within the Fifth Circuit. Citing *United States v. Greenlaw*, the opinion establishes that securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 requires a “scheme to defraud” enacted with an “intent to defraud.” The court breaks down the intent element into two distinct components derived from *United States v. Evans*. First, the defendant must have an intent to deceive. Second, the defendant must have an intent to cause some harm to result from the deceit. In the context of *Constantinescu*, the defendants conceded that the indictment adequately alleged deception; they admitted to posting false and misleading information about securities on social media. Consequently, the legal dispute centered entirely on the second prong: whether the indictment alleged an intent to cause harm, or whether the defendants’ intent to enrich themselves was legally insufficient to satisfy the statute.

## The Defendants' Argument Regarding Profit vs. Victim Harm

The defendants challenged the sufficiency of the indictment by arguing that their motive was self-enrichment rather than victim injury. Their position was that the indictment merely alleged they intended to deprive their followers of valuable economic information to make a profit, without intending to injure the followers financially. The core of their argument was that the followers purchased securities that retained their market value. Because the victims received stock worth what they paid for it, the defendants contended there was no intent to cause a net pecuniary loss. They argued that under the fraud statutes, a defendant must seek to hurt the victim’s bottom line to establish the requisite intent to defraud. In their view, a scheme designed solely to generate profit for the schemer, even through deception, does not constitute fraud if the victim does not suffer an actual economic detriment.

## Supreme Court Holding in *Kousisis v. United States*

To resolve this dispute, the Fifth Circuit looked to the Supreme Court’s decision in *Kousisis v. United States*, decided in 2025. In *Kousisis*, the defendant had lied to a state department of transportation to obtain contracts, falsely representing that his company would subcontract with a disadvantaged business. When the lie was discovered, the government charged him under a “fraudulent-inducement theory.” This theory posits that a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract requiring the victim to hand over money or property. The Supreme Court in *Kousisis* clarified the scope of the fraud statutes in relation to this theory. The Court distinguished fraudulent inducement from the “right-to-control” theory rejected in *Ciminelli*. While *Ciminelli* barred convictions based solely on the deprivation of information, *Kousisis* confirmed that the fraudulent-inducement theory remains a valid basis for prosecution because it protects tangible money and property.

## Rejection of the Requirement for Net Pecuniary Loss

The pivotal holding in *Kousisis* relevant to *Constantinescu* was the Court’s rejection of the argument that a fraud conviction requires the defendant to seek to leave the victim economically worse off. The Supreme Court held that a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he schemes to “obtain” the victim’s “money or property,” regardless of whether he seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss. The Court reasoned that the fraud statutes do not impose a requirement that the victim suffer a net loss. Even if the defendant provides something of equal value in return—such as money, property, or services—the fraud is complete if the defendant induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses. This principle directly undermined the defendants’ argument in *Constantinescu* that the followers’ receipt of market-value securities negated the intent to defraud.

## Sufficiency of Alleging Intent to Obtain Money via Deception

Applying the *Kousisis* framework, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment in *Constantinescu* sufficiently alleged the intent to defraud. The indictment charged that defendants posted false information so that investors were induced to purchase securities, thereby handing over their money to the defendants’ scheme. The court noted that the defendants’ object was to obtain money, evidenced by their profit motive of $114 million. Under *Kousisis*, having “money in mind” and inducing a victim to part with that money satisfies the intent element, even if the defendant’s goal was personal profit rather than victim loss. The court emphasized that the fraud statutes do not require a “mirror image” where the victim’s loss must supply the defendant’s gain. Because the indictment alleged that defendants fraudulently induced followers to purchase securities, it adequately alleged an intent to defraud, rendering the district court’s dismissal improper.

# Disposition and Legal Significance

## Court's Decision to Reverse and Remand
In the final section of the opinion, the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals addresses the procedural outcome of the case. The district court had previously dismissed the superseding indictment against the defendants, ruling that the government failed to state an offense because the allegations did not demonstrate a deprivation of a traditional property interest. Upon review, the appellate court concluded that the district court erred in its legal analysis of the indictment’s sufficiency. Consequently, the Fifth Circuit issued an order to REVERSE the district court’s judgment. This reversal vacates the dismissal, meaning the legal barrier preventing the prosecution is removed. Furthermore, the court REMANDED the case to the district court for proceedings consistent with the appellate opinion. This remand instruction directs the lower court to resume the criminal proceedings, allowing the case to move forward toward trial or further pretrial motions, now that the indictment has been validated as legally sufficient.

## Confirmation of Indictment Sufficiency
The core of the court’s disposition rests on its confirmation that the indictment meets the necessary legal standards to proceed. Under the Fifth Circuit’s standard of review, the court examined the indictment *de novo*, taking all allegations as true to determine if an offense was stated. The court found that the indictment successfully enumerated the prima facie elements of the charged offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and § 1349. Specifically, the opinion confirms that the document fairly informed the defendants of the charges and provided a basis for a double jeopardy defense. The court determined that the allegations of a "scheme to defraud" and an "intent to defraud" were adequately pleaded. By rejecting the defendants' argument that the indictment failed to allege a deprivation of property, the court solidified the standing of the charges. This confirmation is critical for law students to understand: it demonstrates that an indictment need not prove guilt at the pleading stage, but must only allege the material elements of the crime in a way that survives a facial challenge based on statutory interpretation.

## Implications for Future 'Pump and Dump' Prosecutions
The decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* carries significant weight for future prosecutions involving "pump and dump" schemes, particularly those utilizing modern communication channels. The facts of this case involved defendants with large social media followings who allegedly manipulated security prices by posting false information to induce purchases before selling their own holdings for profit. By reversing the dismissal, the Fifth Circuit signals that federal prosecutors can successfully charge such schemes under the securities fraud statute, provided they frame the allegations correctly. The ruling validates the government's theory that artificially inflating prices through deception to sell securities constitutes a fraud scheme. This removes a potential defense strategy where defendants might argue that because they sold "real" securities, no property interest was violated. The opinion clarifies that the manipulation of the market price through deception to facilitate the defendant's sale is sufficient to allege a crime, thereby strengthening the government's hand in combating market manipulation by influential traders.

## Clarification of Fraud Statutes in the Context of Social Media
While the case involves social media, the legal clarification provided by the court focuses on the statutory interpretation of fraud rather than the specific technology used. The opinion explains that 18 U.S.C. § 1348 borrows key concepts from mail and wire fraud statutes, requiring a scheme to defraud enacted with intent to defraud. The court distinguishes between the "right-to-control theory," which was rejected by the Supreme Court in *Ciminelli* because it relies on the deprivation of valuable economic information alone, and the "fraudulent-inducement theory" applied here. In the context of social media, the court treats the defendants' posts as the mechanism of misrepresentation used to trick victims into a contract (purchasing the security). The opinion clarifies that the medium of the fraud—social media posts—does not alter the fundamental requirement that the scheme must involve obtaining money or property. Thus, the ruling ensures that fraud statutes remain applicable to digital environments where the deception occurs online but the financial transaction involves traditional securities.

## Guidance for Drafting Securities Fraud Indictments Post-Kousisis
For law students and practitioners, this opinion offers specific guidance on drafting securities fraud indictments following the Supreme Court's decision in *Kousisis*. The court emphasizes that an indictment must allege a "fraudulent-inducement theory," where the defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into handing over money or property. Crucially, the opinion notes that the government does not need to allege that the defendant intended to cause the victim a net pecuniary loss. In *Constantinescu*, the defendants argued they intended to profit rather than harm victims, but the court found this distinction irrelevant under *Kousisis*. Therefore, when drafting an indictment, prosecutors should focus on alleging the intent to "obtain" the victim's money or property through deception. The indictment must clearly state that the fraud was used to induce the purchase of securities, thereby protecting a traditional property interest, rather than merely alleging the deprivation of information. This distinction ensures the indictment survives a motion to dismiss based on the limitations established in *Ciminelli*.
