Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Factual Background and Procedural History

## Employment relationship: Cohen as hourly licensed attorney document reviewer

To understand the legal claims at issue, one must first establish the nature of the employment relationship between the parties. The plaintiff, Bruce Cohen, began working for the defendant, Consilio, LLC, in 2018. Consilio operates as a nationwide legal document review company with offices located in Minnesota. Cohen’s role within the company was that of an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer. This classification is significant because it places Cohen within a category of workers who are generally eligible for overtime compensation under federal and state wage laws, rather than being classified as salaried exempt employees. The opinion notes that Cohen was employed alongside other Minnesota-based hourly licensed attorney document reviewers, establishing a potential group of similarly situated employees for the purposes of the lawsuit. The specific duties and compensation structure of this role form the factual foundation for the alleged wage violations.

## The 2019 overtime policy change eliminating premium pay

The central factual dispute arises from a change in Consilio’s compensation policy. In July 2019, Consilio sent an email to Cohen and other Minnesota-based employees in his position. This communication informed them that the company would be instituting a new premium overtime policy effective in August 2019. The substance of this new policy was critical to Cohen’s claims: it eliminated overtime premium pay entirely. Instead of receiving time-and-a-half or other premium rates for hours worked beyond the standard workweek, the policy clarified that Cohen and his fellow employees would be paid “at [their] base pay rate” for all hours worked. Cohen alleges that this policy change resulted in the failure to pay overtime wages owed to him and the putative class of employees. This unilateral alteration of the wage structure serves as the alleged wrongful conduct that triggered the subsequent litigation.

## Statutes invoked: MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims

In August 2020, Cohen filed suit on behalf of himself and a putative class, grounding his allegations in three distinct Minnesota statutes. First, he invoked the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), specifically Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101 and 181.171, subd. 1. Second, he alleged violations of the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), citing Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25 and 177.27, subd. 8. Third, Cohen relied on the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), referencing Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, and 177.27. Each of these statutes provides a separate legal basis for recovering unpaid wages and associated penalties. Understanding that these are three separate statutory claims is essential, as the court treats the remedies and procedural requirements for each differently. The invocation of multiple statutes allows the plaintiff to seek redress under varying legal theories, though the court must analyze the specific language and intent of each statute independently.

## Remedies sought: Overtime, liquidated damages, penalties, and injunctive relief

Cohen’s complaint sought a variety of remedies corresponding to the statutory violations alleged. Primarily, he sought the recovery of unpaid overtime wages. In addition to the back wages, he requested liquidated damages, which are statutory damages often equal to the amount of unpaid wages, intended to compensate the employee for the delay in payment. Beyond compensatory measures, Cohen sought statutory penalties under each of the three acts. These penalties function differently than liquidated damages; they are often punitive in nature and designed to deter future violations. Finally, Cohen sought injunctive relief, requesting a court order requiring Consilio to cease the alleged illegal policy. The distinction between these remedies is procedurally vital. While wages and liquidated damages are typically paid to the employee to make them whole, statutory penalties often go to the state government or the Commissioner of Labor, which complicates the plaintiff’s standing to pursue them if the underlying wage claim is satisfied.

## Pre-judgment stipulation: Full payment of wages and liquidated damages

A critical procedural development occurred prior to the district court’s judgment. Consilio paid Cohen and the other Minnesota licensed attorney document reviewers a total of $256,010.01 to cover the overtime wages and liquidated damages demanded pursuant to Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Cohen personally received $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages. Following these payments, the parties entered into a stipulation acknowledging that Cohen had “been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit” and that he did not dispute payment of any liquidated damages entitled under the MFLSA. However, the parties could not agree on the statutory penalties, with Cohen arguing he was entitled to significant sums under the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA. The parties further stipulated that the most efficient path forward was for the court to decide the summary judgment motion on all claims before further discovery, acknowledging that a grant of summary judgment would fully resolve the case. This stipulation resolved the compensatory portion of the dispute but left the penalty claims and the question of jurisdiction active.

## District Court's grant of summary judgment to Consilio

Following the stipulation and the pre-judgment payments, Consilio moved for summary judgment on all three statutory claims. The district court granted Consilio’s motion for summary judgment on the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims. In federal civil litigation, summary judgment is a dispositive motion that allows a case to be resolved without a trial. It is proper only if there are no genuine issues of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. By granting the motion, the district court determined that, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to Cohen, Consilio was legally entitled to win on all claims. This ruling effectively dismissed Cohen’s lawsuit entirely. The grant of summary judgment is the final procedural step in the district court that Cohen challenges on appeal, making the standard of review for this decision a key component of the appellate analysis.

## Scope of the Eighth Circuit appeal

Cohen appealed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit. The appellate court’s review is de novo, meaning the court examines the legal questions anew without deferring to the district court’s conclusions. The scope of the appeal encompasses the dismissal of all three statutory claims and the denial of the requested penalties and injunctive relief. The Eighth Circuit’s disposition reflects the complex procedural posture created by the pre-judgment stipulation. The court affirmed the district court’s judgment regarding the MPWA and MWTA claims, agreeing that Cohen could not recover the penalties sought under those statutes. However, regarding the MFLSA claim, the court vacated the summary judgment and remanded the case for further proceedings. This split disposition highlights that while the court resolved the substantive rights under two statutes, it identified a potential jurisdictional issue regarding the MFLSA claim that required the district court to address first.

# Statutory Interpretation of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA)

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit addressed a critical question of statutory interpretation regarding the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA). Specifically, the court had to determine whether an individual employee possesses an independent right to seek "average daily wage penalties" for unpaid overtime, or whether that enforcement authority is reserved exclusively for the Commissioner of Labor. This issue arose after Consilio had paid Cohen his owed wages and liquidated damages but disputed liability for statutory penalties. The district court granted summary judgment to Consilio, concluding that Cohen could not recover these penalties. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit affirmed this portion of the judgment, relying on a close reading of the statutory text and the structure of the MPWA. Understanding this section of the opinion requires an analysis of how courts interpret statutory silence and the specific roles assigned to government officials versus private litigants within a regulatory scheme.

## Textual analysis of Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a) regarding penalties

The court’s analysis begins with the plain language of the statute alleged to have been violated. Cohen argued that Minn. Stat. § 181.101 authorized employees to seek average daily wage penalties, but the Eighth Circuit focused on the specific text of subsection (a). The opinion highlights that § 181.101(a) references the Commissioner regarding penalties, rather than the employee. The statute provides that penalties are only available if the Commissioner first serves a demand for payment on behalf of the employee. If the employer fails to pay within ten days of this service, the Commissioner is then authorized to charge and collect the wages earned plus a penalty. This textual distinction is vital for law students to observe: the statute creates a procedural gatekeeping mechanism where the Commissioner initiates the penalty process. The court notes that while the section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages, the mechanism for securing penalties is tied directly to the Commissioner’s administrative action, not a private civil suit by the employee.

## The Commissioner's exclusive role in serving demand and collecting penalties

Building on the textual analysis, the opinion clarifies the functional roles assigned to the Commissioner versus the employee under § 181.101. The court explains that the money collected by the Commissioner, including any penalties, must be paid to the employee concerned. However, the authority to collect that money initially resides with the Commissioner. The statute explicitly states that it does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages, but it does not explicitly grant the employee the right to prosecute a claim for penalties under this specific section. This distinction separates the recovery of the underlying debt (wages) from the imposition of the sanction (penalties). The court emphasizes that under § 181.101(a), the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the Commissioner, with any collected funds subsequently distributed to the employee. Consequently, a private plaintiff cannot bypass the Commissioner’s demand process to seek penalties directly in a civil action.

## Comparison with other MPWA sections (§ 181.13, § 181.14) granting employee penalty rights

To further support its interpretation, the court engages in a comparative analysis of the MPWA, looking at how the legislature drafted other sections within the same act. The opinion points to Minn. Stat. §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14, subd. 2, which govern discharged employees and those who quit or resign, respectively. In those specific sections, the legislature explicitly empowered the employee to recover unpaid wages and, upon demand, to "charge and collect a penalty." By contrasting these sections with § 181.101, the court highlights a deliberate difference in statutory language. Where the legislature intended to give employees the direct right to collect penalties, it wrote that right into the statute. The absence of such language in § 181.101 suggests that the legislature did not intend to grant the same authority to employees in the context of general wage violations covered by that section. This comparative reading reinforces the conclusion that the right to penalties in § 181.101 is not available to private plaintiffs.

## Legislative intent: The 'knows how' canon of statutory construction

The court relies on a specific canon of statutory construction to infer legislative intent from the differences in the statutory text. Citing *Lagasse v. Horton*, the opinion applies the principle that the legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the legislature "knows how" to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so. Because the legislature explicitly provided for employee-collected penalties in §§ 181.13 and 181.14, its failure to include similar language in § 181.101 is interpreted as a conscious choice to withhold that authority. This canon helps the court resolve the ambiguity of whether the general redress provision in § 181.171 overrides the specific limitations in § 181.101. The court concludes that the specific limitations regarding the Commissioner’s role in § 181.101 control, and the legislature’s silence on employee penalty rights in that section confirms they were not intended to be granted.

## Precedent on Commissioner enforcement authority (Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.)

The Eighth Circuit grounds its statutory interpretation in binding precedent from the Minnesota Supreme Court. The opinion cites *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.*, which established that when the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect. The court applies this rule to § 181.101, finding the text regarding the Commissioner’s role to be clear. Additionally, the court references *Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc.*, a district court case explaining that under § 181.101(a), the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the Commissioner. By relying on *Allan*, the Eighth Circuit demonstrates that it is not creating new law but rather applying established rules of statutory construction to the text before it. This reliance on precedent ensures that the interpretation aligns with Minnesota state law principles regarding the MPWA.

## Holding: Employee lacks standing to seek MPWA penalties directly

The final outcome of this statutory analysis is the affirmation of the district court’s summary judgment on the MPWA claim. The court holds that only the Commissioner may seek daily average wage penalties under § 181.101. Consequently, Cohen, as an individual employee, lacked the authority to pursue these penalties directly in his civil action. This holding resolves the dispute over the $172,080 in average daily wage penalties Cohen sought. While Cohen retained the right to sue for unpaid wages, the court determined that the statutory scheme reserved the enforcement of penalties to the state agency. This decision underscores the importance of reading statutes as a whole and respecting the specific enforcement mechanisms the legislature has established for different types of violations within the same act.

# Jurisdictional Challenges: Standing and Mootness under the MFLSA

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals confronted a critical procedural hurdle that often arises in statutory enforcement actions: the jurisdictional requirement of a live case or controversy under Article III of the Constitution. While the plaintiff, Cohen, had successfully secured payment of all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages from his employer, Consilio, he continued to pursue statutory penalties. Because these penalties were payable to the state rather than to Cohen personally, the court had to determine whether Cohen retained a sufficient personal stake in the outcome to maintain federal jurisdiction. This chapter analyzes the court’s reasoning regarding standing, mootness, and the voluntary cessation doctrine as applied to Cohen’s claim under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA).

## Distinction between private damages and civil penalties payable to the state

To understand the jurisdictional difficulty in this case, one must first distinguish between the different types of relief available under the MFLSA. In many employment disputes, an employee seeks compensation for unpaid wages and damages directly payable to them. In *Cohen*, the parties stipulated that Consilio had paid Cohen all overtime wages and liquidated damages he was owed under the MFLSA. Consequently, Cohen received $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages. However, Cohen continued to argue that he was entitled to statutory penalties. The court noted a crucial distinction regarding the destination of these funds: unlike the wages and liquidated damages, the parties agreed that any statutory penalties imposed under the MFLSA were payable to the Commissioner of the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry, not to Cohen. This distinction is vital because federal courts require a plaintiff to demonstrate a personal stake in the litigation. When the relief sought benefits the government rather than the plaintiff directly, the court must scrutinize whether the plaintiff retains the necessary Article III standing to press the claim.

## Article III standing requirements for citizen plaintiffs (Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw)

The Eighth Circuit looked to Supreme Court precedent to determine whether a citizen plaintiff can pursue civil penalties payable to the state. The court cited *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, which established that civil penalties may still "afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct." The rationale is that penalties can encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones, thereby providing a form of redress to the plaintiff through deterrence. However, the court emphasized that a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim and for each specific form of relief sought. Even if penalties can provide redress through deterrence, Cohen must still show that he has a personal interest in obtaining those penalties. The court expressed serious concern regarding its jurisdiction to decide the penalty issue because Cohen had already been compensated for the direct financial harm, raising the question of whether he still suffered an injury-in-fact sufficient to support standing.

## Mootness doctrine: Whether payment of wages extinguishes the controversy

The concept of standing is closely linked to the doctrine of mootness. As the court explained, the requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness). If subsequent events make it "absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur," a plaintiff's claim may become moot, depriving the court of jurisdiction. In *Cohen*, the payment of all overtime wages and liquidated damages created a strong argument that the controversy was resolved. If the employer has paid the employee what was owed, and the employee seeks only penalties payable to the state, the court must determine if the case remains live. The court noted that Cohen’s claim for penalties might become moot if it were clear that Consilio would not violate the overtime laws again. This highlights the procedural reality that federal courts are not licensed to retain jurisdiction over cases where parties plainly lack a continuing interest.

## The 'voluntary cessation' exception to mootness

Despite the argument that payment of wages might moot the case, the court recognized the "voluntary cessation" exception to the mootness doctrine. It is well-settled law that a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not automatically deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of that practice. This rule prevents defendants from evading judicial review simply by stopping the illegal conduct after being sued. However, for the case to remain viable, the defendant must not be able to show that it is "absolutely clear" that the wrongful conduct will not recur. In *Cohen*, the court acknowledged that while Consilio had paid the wages, the status of the underlying overtime policy was critical. If the policy remained in place, Cohen’s claim for civil penalties might still be viable because the threat of future violation could persist. The voluntary cessation doctrine thus places the burden on the defendant to prove that the violation has been permanently corrected to the point of mootness.

## The unresolved factual dispute regarding the current overtime policy

The Eighth Circuit identified a specific factual gap that prevented it from resolving the jurisdictional question on appeal. The parties agreed that Cohen had been paid all wages and liquidated damages, but whether Consilio’s violative overtime policy was still in place remained in dispute. The court explained that if the policy remains, Cohen’s claim for civil penalties may still be viable under *Friends of the Earth*. Conversely, if the policy is no longer in place and it is "absolutely clear" that the wrongful conduct will not recur, the MFLSA claim may be moot. Because the record did not clearly establish whether Consilio had rescinded the policy or whether the conduct could reasonably be expected to recur, the appellate court could not definitively rule on the jurisdictional issue. This unresolved factual dispute regarding the current status of the overtime policy was the primary reason the court could not affirm or reverse the district court’s summary judgment on the MFLSA claim.

## Remand instructions: District Court to determine continuing jurisdiction

Due to the uncertainty regarding the continuing nature of the controversy, the Eighth Circuit vacated the district court’s grant of summary judgment as to Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remanded the case for further proceedings. The court instructed the district court to determine its jurisdiction before proceeding on the merits of the penalty claim. This procedural disposition ensures that the lower court addresses the factual questions regarding the overtime policy and the application of the mootness doctrine first. The court emphasized that federal courts cannot decide cases where jurisdiction is unclear. By remanding for a determination of continuing jurisdiction, the Eighth Circuit ensured that the case would only proceed if Cohen could demonstrate that a live controversy remained, thereby adhering to Article III limitations. This outcome underscores the importance of jurisdictional thresholds in statutory enforcement cases where direct damages have been satisfied.

# Waiver, Procedural Default, and Final Disposition

## MWTA Claim: Dismissal Due to Lack of Argument or Statutory Authority (Waiver)

In addressing the final substantive claim, the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) notice claim, the Eighth Circuit illustrates the critical procedural concept of waiver. Cohen alleged that Consilio failed to provide statutorily required notice regarding the change in its overtime policy, seeking penalties under Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032. However, on appeal, Cohen complained primarily that the district court included little to no discussion of this claim. The appellate court noted that Cohen had stipulated he received all pay Consilio owed him and, crucially, pointed to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under the MWTA.

This scenario demonstrates how appellate courts treat claims that are not adequately supported by legal argument. The court cited *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, explaining that when a party’s brief does not support an assertion with argument or legal authority, the issue is waived. By failing to provide a meaningful argument in support of reversal, Cohen effectively abandoned the claim. The court’s decision to not disturb the district court’s order dismissing this claim underscores a fundamental rule of appellate practice: the court will not research legal theories or statutory provisions on behalf of a litigant. If a party asserts a statutory violation but fails to identify the specific statutory provision granting the relief sought, or fails to argue why the lower court erred, the appellate court may deem the issue waived and affirm the dismissal.

## Injunctive Relief: Improperly Raised in Summary Judgment Briefing Rather Than Complaint

The court’s handling of Cohen’s request for injunctive relief further highlights the importance of procedural timing and the distinction between pleadings and briefing. In his original complaint, Cohen sought specific forms of injunctive relief. However, during the summary judgment briefing phase, Cohen changed course, requesting different relief—specifically, an affirmative injunctive order that Consilio rescind its 2019 overtime policy. The district court did not consider this new request, and Cohen argued on appeal that he was entitled to it.

The Eighth Circuit rejected this argument, holding that the issue was not properly before them. The court emphasized that the district court understandably did not consider a claim that was not raised in the initial pleadings. This distinction is vital for law students to understand: the complaint defines the scope of the litigation. Attempting to introduce new claims or significantly alter the scope of requested relief in opposition briefs, rather than through formal amendments to the complaint, is procedurally improper. The court noted that Cohen sought to change the nature of the relief sought at a late stage in the litigation, bypassing the procedural mechanisms designed to ensure fair notice to the opposing party.

## Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a) and Amendment of Pleadings

To support its ruling on the injunctive relief claim, the court referenced Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a) and the Eighth Circuit’s decision in *InfoDeli, LLC*. Rule 15(a) governs the amendment of pleadings, establishing the proper mechanism for a party to modify their claims or requests for relief after the initial complaint has been filed. The court explained that new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs.

This procedural rule serves to maintain order and fairness in the judicial process. By requiring amendments to be filed as formal pleadings, the rule ensures that the opposing party has adequate notice of the claims they must defend against and an opportunity to respond through discovery or additional briefing. When a party attempts to raise a new claim for the first time in a summary judgment brief, they circumvent these safeguards. In *Cohen*, because the request to rescind the policy was a deviation from the original complaint that was not processed through an amendment, the appellate court could not consider it. This serves as a cautionary example for litigators: if the scope of relief changes, the pleadings must be updated formally before the court can adjudicate the new request.

## Final Disposition: Affirm in Part, Vacate and Remand in Part

The final section of the opinion details the court’s judgment, which reflects the mixed outcome of the various claims analyzed throughout the decision. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s order regarding the MPWA claim, the MWTA claim, and the request for injunctive relief. These claims were resolved on the merits or dismissed due to procedural waiver, leaving no further issues for the district court to address regarding them. However, the court vacated the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA).

The vacatur of the MFLSA claim was driven by the jurisdictional concerns regarding mootness and standing discussed in the substantive analysis section. Because it remained unclear whether Consilio’s violative overtime policy was still in place, the appellate court could not determine if Cohen retained a continuing interest in the case. Consequently, the case was remanded to the district court to determine its jurisdiction. This disposition—affirming in part and vacating in part—demonstrates how an appellate court can resolve multiple claims differently based on their specific legal and factual postures. The affirmation ends the litigation on the waived and meritless claims, while the remand sends the live jurisdictional question back to the trial court for further proceedings.

## Special Concurrence: Judge Kobes' Concerns on Standing and Attorney's Fees

The opinion concludes with a special concurrence by Circuit Judge Kobes, which offers additional insight into the judicial reasoning surrounding the MFLSA claim. Judge Kobes agreed with the majority’s decision to vacate the judgment and remand the case but expressed specific reservations regarding the standing analysis. He noted that he was not convinced Cohen had standing to seek statutory penalties under *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services*, distinguishing the penalties in that case from those available under the MFLSA. He observed that neither party had briefed this specific issue, highlighting the importance of thorough briefing on standing requirements.

Additionally, Judge Kobes addressed issues not fully covered in the majority opinion: attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. He concurred in vacating the district court’s ruling on these ancillary matters as well, ensuring that the remand would allow the district court to address them in light of the jurisdictional determination. This concurrence reinforces that while the majority focused on the primary jurisdictional hurdle (mootness), other aspects of the litigation, such as the award of fees, must also be reconsidered on remand to ensure a complete and fair resolution of the case.
