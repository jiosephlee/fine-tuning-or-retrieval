Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Don't Save It for the Brief: Waiver and Pleading Requirements in Employment Appeals

## The High Cost of Procedural Oversight

In the high-stakes world of employment litigation, substantive rights often take a backseat to procedural mechanics. A plaintiff may have a meritorious claim for unpaid wages, yet lose the battle because they failed to properly preserve an argument or amend their complaint. The United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit’s recent decision in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, issued October 6, 2025, serves as a stark reminder of this reality. While the case involves complex interpretations of Minnesota wage statutes, the most instructive aspects for practitioners lie in the court’s strict application of waiver doctrines and pleading standards. In *Cohen*, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the dismissal of significant claims not because the employer necessarily won on the merits of the wage theft allegations, but because the plaintiff failed to follow the rules of appellate briefing and civil procedure.

## The Factual Landscape

To understand the procedural pitfalls, one must first understand the posture of the case. Cohen, an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer, sued his employer, Consilio, LLC, alleging violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA). The core dispute involved overtime pay. Crucially, before the appellate court reviewed the case, Consilio paid Cohen all alleged overtime wages and liquidated damages. The parties stipulated that Cohen had been paid for all wages and damages he claimed under the MFLSA. However, a dispute remained regarding statutory penalties and injunctive relief. The district court granted summary judgment to Consilio on all claims, and Cohen appealed. While the Eighth Circuit vacated the judgment regarding the MFLSA penalties due to potential mootness issues, it affirmed the dismissal of the MWTA penalties and the request for injunctive relief on procedural grounds.

## Waiver Through Lack of Authority

The Eighth Circuit’s handling of Cohen’s MWTA claim highlights a recurring frustration for appellate courts: the failure of counsel to support assertions with legal authority. Cohen argued that Consilio failed to provide statutorily required notice of a change in its overtime policy, entitling him to penalties under the MWTA. However, the district court had granted summary judgment on this claim with little discussion, and Cohen’s appellate brief failed to provide a meaningful argument or statutory authority to support reversal.

Writing for the panel, Circuit Judge Kelly noted that Cohen pointed to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under the specific MWTA provisions cited. The court applied the well-established principle that an issue is waived if a party’s brief does not support an assertion with argument or legal authority. Citing *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, the Eighth Circuit declined to disturb the district court’s order. This aspect of the opinion reinforces that appellate courts are not research engines for counsel. Even if a statutory interpretation might be favorable to an employee, the claim dies if the appellant fails to provide the legal scaffolding required to sustain it. For employment practitioners, this underscores the necessity of rigorously briefing every claim, even those that might seem secondary to the main wage recovery, as failure to cite authority results in immediate waiver.

## Pleading Requirements and Rule 15

Perhaps the most significant lesson from *Cohen* for employment litigators concerns the scope of injunctive relief. In his original complaint, Cohen sought specific forms of injunctive relief. However, during the summary judgment briefing, Cohen changed course and requested different relief—specifically, an order rescinding the 2019 overtime policy. The district court did not consider this new request, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed that the issue was not properly before them.

The court was explicit that new claims or significant changes in the relief sought must be raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs. Relying on *InfoDeli, LLC* and Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a), the Eighth Circuit made clear that the complaint defines the boundaries of the litigation. Allowing parties to shift the relief sought in briefing stages would undermine the notice function of pleadings and potentially prejudice the opposing party. This holding is a critical reminder that the complaint is the roadmap for the case. If an employee realizes that the specific relief sought in the initial filing is insufficient or needs modification based on the employer’s conduct or defense strategy, they must formally amend the complaint. Relying on the flexibility of briefing to introduce new remedial requests is a procedural strategy that will not survive appellate scrutiny.

## Implications for Employment Practitioners

The *Cohen* decision sends a clear message to employment attorneys on both sides of the aisle: procedural discipline is as vital as substantive legal knowledge. In wage and hour cases, where statutory penalties and injunctive relief can significantly alter the financial exposure of an employer, the stakes for preservation are high. The Eighth Circuit’s affirmation of the MWTA dismissal demonstrates that statutory interpretation arguments will not save a claim that lacks proper briefing support. Similarly, the rejection of the injunctive relief claim reinforces the strict boundaries of Rule 15.

For plaintiffs’ counsel, the takeaway is to ensure that every claim in the complaint is supported by a robust briefing strategy that cites controlling authority, and that any evolution in the requested relief is formalized through an amendment rather than a motion brief. For defense counsel, the case provides a strong basis for arguing waiver when plaintiffs attempt to expand their remedies late in the litigation process. Ultimately, *Cohen* illustrates that in the Eighth Circuit, as in many others, the law favors the vigilant. A meritorious claim for wage theft is of little use if the procedural vehicle carrying it is dismantled by waiver and pleading errors.
