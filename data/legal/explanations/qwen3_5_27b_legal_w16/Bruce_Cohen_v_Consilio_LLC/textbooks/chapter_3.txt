Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 3: # Jurisdictional Challenges: Standing and Mootness under the MFLSA

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals confronted a critical procedural hurdle that often arises in statutory enforcement actions: the jurisdictional requirement of a live case or controversy under Article III of the Constitution. While the plaintiff, Cohen, had successfully secured payment of all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages from his employer, Consilio, he continued to pursue statutory penalties. Because these penalties were payable to the state rather than to Cohen personally, the court had to determine whether Cohen retained a sufficient personal stake in the outcome to maintain federal jurisdiction. This chapter analyzes the court’s reasoning regarding standing, mootness, and the voluntary cessation doctrine as applied to Cohen’s claim under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA).

## Distinction between private damages and civil penalties payable to the state

To understand the jurisdictional difficulty in this case, one must first distinguish between the different types of relief available under the MFLSA. In many employment disputes, an employee seeks compensation for unpaid wages and damages directly payable to them. In *Cohen*, the parties stipulated that Consilio had paid Cohen all overtime wages and liquidated damages he was owed under the MFLSA. Consequently, Cohen received $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages. However, Cohen continued to argue that he was entitled to statutory penalties. The court noted a crucial distinction regarding the destination of these funds: unlike the wages and liquidated damages, the parties agreed that any statutory penalties imposed under the MFLSA were payable to the Commissioner of the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry, not to Cohen. This distinction is vital because federal courts require a plaintiff to demonstrate a personal stake in the litigation. When the relief sought benefits the government rather than the plaintiff directly, the court must scrutinize whether the plaintiff retains the necessary Article III standing to press the claim.

## Article III standing requirements for citizen plaintiffs (Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw)

The Eighth Circuit looked to Supreme Court precedent to determine whether a citizen plaintiff can pursue civil penalties payable to the state. The court cited *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, which established that civil penalties may still "afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct." The rationale is that penalties can encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones, thereby providing a form of redress to the plaintiff through deterrence. However, the court emphasized that a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim and for each specific form of relief sought. Even if penalties can provide redress through deterrence, Cohen must still show that he has a personal interest in obtaining those penalties. The court expressed serious concern regarding its jurisdiction to decide the penalty issue because Cohen had already been compensated for the direct financial harm, raising the question of whether he still suffered an injury-in-fact sufficient to support standing.

## Mootness doctrine: Whether payment of wages extinguishes the controversy

The concept of standing is closely linked to the doctrine of mootness. As the court explained, the requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness). If subsequent events make it "absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur," a plaintiff's claim may become moot, depriving the court of jurisdiction. In *Cohen*, the payment of all overtime wages and liquidated damages created a strong argument that the controversy was resolved. If the employer has paid the employee what was owed, and the employee seeks only penalties payable to the state, the court must determine if the case remains live. The court noted that Cohen’s claim for penalties might become moot if it were clear that Consilio would not violate the overtime laws again. This highlights the procedural reality that federal courts are not licensed to retain jurisdiction over cases where parties plainly lack a continuing interest.

## The 'voluntary cessation' exception to mootness

Despite the argument that payment of wages might moot the case, the court recognized the "voluntary cessation" exception to the mootness doctrine. It is well-settled law that a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not automatically deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of that practice. This rule prevents defendants from evading judicial review simply by stopping the illegal conduct after being sued. However, for the case to remain viable, the defendant must not be able to show that it is "absolutely clear" that the wrongful conduct will not recur. In *Cohen*, the court acknowledged that while Consilio had paid the wages, the status of the underlying overtime policy was critical. If the policy remained in place, Cohen’s claim for civil penalties might still be viable because the threat of future violation could persist. The voluntary cessation doctrine thus places the burden on the defendant to prove that the violation has been permanently corrected to the point of mootness.

## The unresolved factual dispute regarding the current overtime policy

The Eighth Circuit identified a specific factual gap that prevented it from resolving the jurisdictional question on appeal. The parties agreed that Cohen had been paid all wages and liquidated damages, but whether Consilio’s violative overtime policy was still in place remained in dispute. The court explained that if the policy remains, Cohen’s claim for civil penalties may still be viable under *Friends of the Earth*. Conversely, if the policy is no longer in place and it is "absolutely clear" that the wrongful conduct will not recur, the MFLSA claim may be moot. Because the record did not clearly establish whether Consilio had rescinded the policy or whether the conduct could reasonably be expected to recur, the appellate court could not definitively rule on the jurisdictional issue. This unresolved factual dispute regarding the current status of the overtime policy was the primary reason the court could not affirm or reverse the district court’s summary judgment on the MFLSA claim.

## Remand instructions: District Court to determine continuing jurisdiction

Due to the uncertainty regarding the continuing nature of the controversy, the Eighth Circuit vacated the district court’s grant of summary judgment as to Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remanded the case for further proceedings. The court instructed the district court to determine its jurisdiction before proceeding on the merits of the penalty claim. This procedural disposition ensures that the lower court addresses the factual questions regarding the overtime policy and the application of the mootness doctrine first. The court emphasized that federal courts cannot decide cases where jurisdiction is unclear. By remanding for a determination of continuing jurisdiction, the Eighth Circuit ensured that the case would only proceed if Cohen could demonstrate that a live controversy remained, thereby adhering to Article III limitations. This outcome underscores the importance of jurisdictional thresholds in statutory enforcement cases where direct damages have been satisfied.
