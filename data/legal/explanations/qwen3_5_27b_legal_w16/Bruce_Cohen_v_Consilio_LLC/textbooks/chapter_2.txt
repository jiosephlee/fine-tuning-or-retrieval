Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 2: # Statutory Interpretation of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA)

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit addressed a critical question of statutory interpretation regarding the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA). Specifically, the court had to determine whether an individual employee possesses an independent right to seek "average daily wage penalties" for unpaid overtime, or whether that enforcement authority is reserved exclusively for the Commissioner of Labor. This issue arose after Consilio had paid Cohen his owed wages and liquidated damages but disputed liability for statutory penalties. The district court granted summary judgment to Consilio, concluding that Cohen could not recover these penalties. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit affirmed this portion of the judgment, relying on a close reading of the statutory text and the structure of the MPWA. Understanding this section of the opinion requires an analysis of how courts interpret statutory silence and the specific roles assigned to government officials versus private litigants within a regulatory scheme.

## Textual analysis of Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a) regarding penalties

The court’s analysis begins with the plain language of the statute alleged to have been violated. Cohen argued that Minn. Stat. § 181.101 authorized employees to seek average daily wage penalties, but the Eighth Circuit focused on the specific text of subsection (a). The opinion highlights that § 181.101(a) references the Commissioner regarding penalties, rather than the employee. The statute provides that penalties are only available if the Commissioner first serves a demand for payment on behalf of the employee. If the employer fails to pay within ten days of this service, the Commissioner is then authorized to charge and collect the wages earned plus a penalty. This textual distinction is vital for law students to observe: the statute creates a procedural gatekeeping mechanism where the Commissioner initiates the penalty process. The court notes that while the section provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages, the mechanism for securing penalties is tied directly to the Commissioner’s administrative action, not a private civil suit by the employee.

## The Commissioner's exclusive role in serving demand and collecting penalties

Building on the textual analysis, the opinion clarifies the functional roles assigned to the Commissioner versus the employee under § 181.101. The court explains that the money collected by the Commissioner, including any penalties, must be paid to the employee concerned. However, the authority to collect that money initially resides with the Commissioner. The statute explicitly states that it does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages, but it does not explicitly grant the employee the right to prosecute a claim for penalties under this specific section. This distinction separates the recovery of the underlying debt (wages) from the imposition of the sanction (penalties). The court emphasizes that under § 181.101(a), the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the Commissioner, with any collected funds subsequently distributed to the employee. Consequently, a private plaintiff cannot bypass the Commissioner’s demand process to seek penalties directly in a civil action.

## Comparison with other MPWA sections (§ 181.13, § 181.14) granting employee penalty rights

To further support its interpretation, the court engages in a comparative analysis of the MPWA, looking at how the legislature drafted other sections within the same act. The opinion points to Minn. Stat. §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14, subd. 2, which govern discharged employees and those who quit or resign, respectively. In those specific sections, the legislature explicitly empowered the employee to recover unpaid wages and, upon demand, to "charge and collect a penalty." By contrasting these sections with § 181.101, the court highlights a deliberate difference in statutory language. Where the legislature intended to give employees the direct right to collect penalties, it wrote that right into the statute. The absence of such language in § 181.101 suggests that the legislature did not intend to grant the same authority to employees in the context of general wage violations covered by that section. This comparative reading reinforces the conclusion that the right to penalties in § 181.101 is not available to private plaintiffs.

## Legislative intent: The 'knows how' canon of statutory construction

The court relies on a specific canon of statutory construction to infer legislative intent from the differences in the statutory text. Citing *Lagasse v. Horton*, the opinion applies the principle that the legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the legislature "knows how" to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so. Because the legislature explicitly provided for employee-collected penalties in §§ 181.13 and 181.14, its failure to include similar language in § 181.101 is interpreted as a conscious choice to withhold that authority. This canon helps the court resolve the ambiguity of whether the general redress provision in § 181.171 overrides the specific limitations in § 181.101. The court concludes that the specific limitations regarding the Commissioner’s role in § 181.101 control, and the legislature’s silence on employee penalty rights in that section confirms they were not intended to be granted.

## Precedent on Commissioner enforcement authority (Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.)

The Eighth Circuit grounds its statutory interpretation in binding precedent from the Minnesota Supreme Court. The opinion cites *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.*, which established that when the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect. The court applies this rule to § 181.101, finding the text regarding the Commissioner’s role to be clear. Additionally, the court references *Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc.*, a district court case explaining that under § 181.101(a), the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the Commissioner. By relying on *Allan*, the Eighth Circuit demonstrates that it is not creating new law but rather applying established rules of statutory construction to the text before it. This reliance on precedent ensures that the interpretation aligns with Minnesota state law principles regarding the MPWA.

## Holding: Employee lacks standing to seek MPWA penalties directly

The final outcome of this statutory analysis is the affirmation of the district court’s summary judgment on the MPWA claim. The court holds that only the Commissioner may seek daily average wage penalties under § 181.101. Consequently, Cohen, as an individual employee, lacked the authority to pursue these penalties directly in his civil action. This holding resolves the dispute over the $172,080 in average daily wage penalties Cohen sought. While Cohen retained the right to sue for unpaid wages, the court determined that the statutory scheme reserved the enforcement of penalties to the state agency. This decision underscores the importance of reading statutes as a whole and respecting the specific enforcement mechanisms the legislature has established for different types of violations within the same act.
