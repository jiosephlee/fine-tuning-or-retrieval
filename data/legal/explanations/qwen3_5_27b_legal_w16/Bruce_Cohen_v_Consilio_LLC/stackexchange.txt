Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Why does the Eighth Circuit limit penalty recovery under MPWA § 181.101 to the Commissioner?
Question:
In Cohen v. Consilio, the court held that only the Commissioner can seek 'average daily wage penalties' under Minn. Stat. § 181.101, even though § 181.171 says an employer is liable for 'compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.' I'm confused about how the court distinguishes this section from other MPWA sections like § 181.13(a) where employees can explicitly charge and collect penalties. Does the court rely on the expressio unius canon here, or is there a specific administrative law principle limiting private enforcement of penalties in this context?
Answer:
The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *Cohen v. Consilio* limits penalty recovery under Minn. Stat. § 181.101 to the Commissioner based on **statutory text and legislative intent inferred from statutory structure**, rather than a general administrative law principle.

While the court does not explicitly name the *expressio unius* canon, it applies the underlying logic: because the legislature explicitly authorized private penalty collection in other MPWA sections but omitted that language in § 181.101, the omission was intentional.

Here is the breakdown of the court’s reasoning:

### 1. The Plain Text of § 181.101
The court begins with the plain language of the statute. Under Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a), the mechanism for collecting average daily wage penalties is exclusively administrative:
*   Penalties are only available if the **Commissioner** first serves a demand for payment on behalf of the employee.
*   If payment is not made within ten days, the **Commissioner** may charge and collect the wages and the penalty.
*   While money collected is paid to the employee, the right to *seek* and *collect* the penalty is vested in the Commissioner.

The court notes that the statute reserves only the ability to "prosecut[e] a claim for wages" to the employee, not penalties.

### 2. The Role of § 181.171
You asked how the court distinguishes this from § 181.171, which allows a civil action for "compensatory damages and other appropriate relief." The court clarifies that § 181.171 does not create independent penalty rights.

Section 181.171 states that an employer is liable for civil penalties "provided for in the section violated." Because § 181.101 (the section allegedly violated) does not provide for *employee-collected* penalties—only Commissioner-collected penalties—§ 181.171 cannot be used to bootstrap a private right of action for those penalties. As the court explains, the general enforcement provision (§ 181.171) incorporates the specific remedies of the violated section (§ 181.101), and § 181.101 limits penalty enforcement to the Commissioner.

### 3. Statutory Comparison (Legislative Intent)
The court addresses your question about *expressio unius* by comparing § 181.101 to other MPWA sections. The opinion cites Minn. Stat. § 181.13(a) and § 181.14, subd. 2, which explicitly allow discharged or resigning employees to "charge and collect a penalty."

Relying on *Lagasse v. Horton*, the court applies the principle that "the Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature 'knows how' to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so." Since the legislature knew how to write a private penalty right into the MPWA (as seen in § 181.13(a)) but chose not to include it in § 181.101, the court concludes the limitation to the Commissioner was deliberate.

### Conclusion
The Eighth Circuit does not rely on a broad administrative law principle limiting private enforcement. Instead, it relies on **textualism and statutory construction**. The court holds that the specific language of § 181.101 controls over the general language of § 181.171, and the legislature’s choice to grant private penalty rights in some MPWA sections but not § 181.101 confirms that employees cannot recover average daily wage penalties under that specific section.

### Can a private plaintiff establish Article III standing to seek civil penalties payable to the state under Laidlaw?
Question:
The court vacated the MFLSA claim due to jurisdictional concerns about standing for civil penalties payable to the Commissioner, citing Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw. I understand Laidlaw allows citizen suits for penalties to deter future violations, but the court notes penalties here go to the state, not the plaintiff. Can someone clarify the specific test for standing when the relief sought (penalties) benefits the government rather than the plaintiff directly? Does the 'redressability' prong fail if the money doesn't go to the plaintiff?
Answer:
**Yes, under *Laidlaw*, a private plaintiff can establish standing for civil penalties payable to the state, provided the penalties offer redress through deterrence of future violations.**

Based on the Eighth Circuit’s opinion in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the fact that penalties are payable to the Commissioner rather than the plaintiff does not automatically defeat Article III standing. However, standing is contingent on whether the plaintiff has a continuing interest in the litigation (i.e., the violation is ongoing or could recur).

Here is the breakdown of the legal standard as applied in *Cohen*:

### 1. Redressability Does Not Require Payment to the Plaintiff
The court explicitly acknowledges the holding in *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw*, which governs this issue. In Section II.B, the court states:

> "Civil penalties, even if they are paid to the government, may 'afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct' because such penalties may 'encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.'"

Therefore, the "redressability" prong of standing is satisfied not by the plaintiff receiving the funds, but by the penalty’s ability to deter the defendant from repeating the conduct that caused the plaintiff's injury.

### 2. Standing Must Be Shown for Each Form of Relief
While *Laidlaw* permits this theory of standing, the court emphasizes that a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for "each form of relief that is sought" (*Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc.*). In *Cohen*, because the parties stipulated that Cohen had been paid all wages and liquidated damages, the only remaining relief sought was statutory penalties payable to the state.

### 3. The Critical Factor: Recurrence of the Violation
The court vacated the summary judgment and remanded the MFLSA claim specifically to determine jurisdiction because the record was unclear on whether the injury was moot. Standing depends on whether the wrongful conduct could reasonably be expected to recur.

As the court noted:
> "Cohen’s claim for penalties 'might become moot if subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur'... However, if Consilio’s policy is no longer in place and it is 'absolutely clear' that its wrongful conduct will not recur, Cohen’s MFLSA claim may in fact be moot."

If the employer has ceased the violative policy and it is "absolutely clear" it will not return, the plaintiff lacks a continuing interest, and the claim becomes moot even if penalties are sought.

### 4. Circuit Skepticism
It is worth noting that while the majority applied the *Laidlaw* framework, Judge Kobes concurred specially to express doubt. He noted that there may be "differences between the penalties in that case [*Laidlaw*] and those available under the MFLSA," suggesting that the applicability of *Laidlaw* to this specific statutory scheme is not settled in this circuit.

### Summary
To establish standing for penalties payable to the state under this opinion:
1.  **Redressability:** You must argue the penalty deters future violations, not that you receive the money.
2.  **Live Controversy:** You must show the violation is ongoing or capable of recurrence; if the conduct has stopped and won't return, the claim is moot.
3.  **Specific Relief:** You must prove standing specifically for the penalty claim, separate from any wage claims that may already be resolved.

### How does voluntary cessation of overtime policy affect mootness in wage theft cases?
Question:
The court remanded the MFLSA claim to determine if the case is moot because Consilio paid the wages and liquidated damages. The opinion cites the standard that voluntary cessation doesn't moot a case unless it is 'absolutely clear' the conduct won't recur. Since the parties stipulated Cohen was paid, why isn't the case moot? What factual findings on remand would satisfy the 'capable of repetition yet evading review' or 'voluntary cessation' exceptions to mootness in this context?
Answer:
# Payment of Wages Does Not Automatically Moot a Claim for Penalties If the Policy Remains

The short answer is that while the stipulation resolved Cohen’s claim for **back pay and liquidated damages**, it did not resolve the question of whether the **underlying overtime policy** remains in effect. Under the Eighth Circuit’s analysis in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the case is not automatically moot because the defendant’s voluntary cessation of the *harm* (non-payment) does not necessarily mean they have ceased the *challenged practice* (the policy itself).

Here is the breakdown of the legal standard and the specific factual findings required on remand based on the opinion.

### The Legal Standard: Voluntary Cessation
The opinion relies on the Supreme Court’s standard from *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc.* regarding mootness. As stated in Section II.B of the opinion:

> A plaintiff’s claim for penalties “might become moot if subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.”

However, the court emphasizes the "voluntary cessation" doctrine:

> It is well settled that “a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice.”

In this context, paying Cohen his owed wages is a form of cessation regarding the *financial injury*, but it does not guarantee that the *policy* causing the injury has been abandoned. If the policy remains, the threat of future injury persists, preserving standing for civil penalties which are intended to "deter them from committing future ones."

### Why the Stipulation Was Insufficient
The parties stipulated that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages. However, the Eighth Circuit noted a critical gap in the record:

> Whether Consilio’s violative overtime policy is still in place remains in dispute.

The stipulation addressed the *remedy* for past conduct (money), but not the *status* of the conduct itself (the policy). Because civil penalties under the MFLSA are payable to the Commissioner to encourage the discontinuance of violations, Cohen retains a stake in the case if the policy continues to threaten future violations.

### Required Factual Findings on Remand
The Eighth Circuit vacated the summary judgment and remanded for the district court to determine jurisdiction. Based on the opinion’s reasoning in Section II.B, the district court must make findings regarding the **voluntary cessation exception** to determine if the case is moot. Specifically, the court must determine:

1.  **Current Status of the Policy:** Is Consilio’s overtime policy that eliminated premium pay still in effect?
    *   *If yes:* The claim is likely not moot because the "ongoing unlawful conduct" persists, allowing for penalties to deter future violations.
2.  **Future Recurrence:** If the policy has been changed or removed, is it "absolutely clear" that the wrongful conduct will not recur?
    *   *If no:* The voluntary cessation exception applies, and the case remains live.
    *   *If yes:* The case may be moot, as the court is not "license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest."

### Note on "Capable of Repetition Yet Evading Review"
While your question references the "capable of repetition yet evading review" exception, the provided opinion does not explicitly analyze this standard. Instead, the court grounds its remand instruction entirely on the **voluntary cessation** standard and the status of the policy. The opinion suggests that if the policy remains, the injury is "ongoing," which satisfies the requirement for standing and mootness analysis under *Laidlaw* without needing to invoke the repetition exception.

### Summary for the Law Student
Do not confuse **payment of damages** with **cessation of the challenged practice**. In wage theft cases involving statutory penalties, paying the worker cures the past harm but does not moot the case if the employer retains the policy that caused the harm. The district court on remand must verify whether the policy exists and whether its recurrence is "absolutely clear" to avoid before dismissing the claim as moot.

### Does failing to cite statutory authority for penalties constitute waiver on appeal?
Question:
Regarding the MWTA claim, the court affirmed the dismissal because Cohen 'points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute' and offered 'no meaningful argument.' I thought summary judgment appeals get reviewed de novo. Is this a waiver issue under appellate procedure rules, or did the court find the lack of statutory citation meant there was no genuine issue of material fact? What is the threshold for 'meaningful argument' to avoid waiver on appeal?
Answer:
**Yes, failing to cite statutory authority and provide meaningful argument constitutes waiver on appeal, even when the underlying standard of review is de novo.**

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit explicitly treated the appellant's failure to cite statutory authority for penalties as a waiver issue, rather than a merits-based determination on the summary judgment standard.

### The Interplay Between De Novo Review and Appellate Waiver
You are correct that the court reviews grants of summary judgment *de novo* (Section II). However, *de novo* review does not obligate the appellate court to construct legal arguments for the appellant. As the court explains in Section II.C regarding the MWTA claim:

> "Cohen complains that the district court 'included little to no discussion of this claim.' But Cohen stipulated that he received all pay Consilio owes him, and he points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute. With no meaningful argument in support of reversal, we will not disturb the district court's order dismissing this claim."

The court distinguished this from the MPWA and MFLSA claims, where it engaged in statutory analysis. For the MWTA claim, the court declined to reach the merits of whether a genuine issue of material fact existed because Cohen failed to meet his burden of briefing the legal basis for the penalty.

### The Threshold for "Meaningful Argument"
The opinion establishes the threshold for avoiding waiver by citing *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000). According to the *Cohen* opinion, the standard requires an appellant to support assertions with both **argument** and **legal authority**.

Specifically, the court held that:
1.  **Statutory Authority:** The appellant must identify the specific statutory provision authorizing the relief sought (here, civil penalties under the MWTA). Cohen "points to no statutory authority."
2.  **Argument:** The appellant must provide reasoning supporting reversal. Cohen offered "no meaningful argument in support of reversal."

When a brief lacks these elements, the court applies the waiver doctrine: "inasmuch as [the party's] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it."

### Conclusion
The dismissal of the MWTA claim was not a finding that there was no genuine issue of material fact, but rather a procedural determination that Cohen waived the issue by failing to brief the legal authority for penalties. Under Eighth Circuit precedent cited in the opinion, *de novo* review of a summary judgment order does not cure an appellant's failure to provide statutory authority or meaningful argument on appeal.

### Can a plaintiff raise new injunctive relief requests in summary judgment briefing?
Question:
Cohen sought injunctive relief to rescind the overtime policy, but the court said the issue is 'not properly before us' because he changed the relief sought in his summary judgment briefing rather than amending the complaint. I know Fed. R. Civ. P. 15 governs amendments, but why can't you clarify or expand relief requests in opposition to summary judgment? At what point does a request for injunctive relief become procedurally defaulted?
Answer:
**No, you generally cannot raise new or significantly different requests for injunctive relief in summary judgment briefing.**

Based on the Eighth Circuit’s opinion in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, 8th Cir. 2025, a plaintiff must raise new claims or altered requests for relief through a formal amendment to the complaint under Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a), rather than through opposition briefs.

### The Legal Standard
The court in *Cohen* relied on the principle that pleading requirements are distinct from briefing arguments. While summary judgment briefing allows parties to argue why the existing claims should succeed or fail, it is not the proper vehicle for introducing new substantive requests.

As the Eighth Circuit stated in Section II.D of the opinion:
> "New claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs."

The court cited *InfoDeli, LLC*, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025), to support the proposition that procedural rules require formal amendments (Rule 15) to add or significantly alter the scope of relief sought.

### Application in *Cohen*
In *Cohen*, the plaintiff initially sought "certain specific forms of injunctive relief" in his complaint. However, during summary judgment briefing, Cohen "changed course and request[ed] different relief"—specifically, an affirmative order to rescind the 2019 overtime policy.

Because this request differed from what was pleaded in the complaint, the district court did not consider it. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit affirmed that the issue was "not properly before us." The court treated the shift in relief sought as a new claim that was procedurally defaulted because it was not raised via an amended complaint.

### When Does Procedural Default Occur?
Based on this opinion, a request for injunctive relief becomes procedurally defaulted when:

1.  **It deviates from the complaint:** The relief requested in briefing is different from the specific relief sought in the operative complaint.
2.  **It is raised in briefing:** The request is introduced for the first time in summary judgment opposition rather than through a Rule 15 motion to amend.

If a plaintiff wishes to "clarify or expand" relief to the point where it constitutes a new request (as in *Cohen*), they must amend the complaint. Doing so in briefing results in the court refusing to consider the request.

### What is the procedural effect of a concurring opinion that vacates issues the majority affirms?
Question:
The majority opinion concludes with 'We otherwise affirm,' but Judge Kobes' concurrence states, 'I also vacate the district court's ruling as to attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest.' As a student, I'm confused about the binding effect of this. Does the concurrence's view on fees become part of the mandate, or does the majority's 'affirm' control? How does the Eighth Circuit handle discrepancies between the majority judgment and a concurrence regarding specific remedies?
Answer:
**The majority opinion controls the judgment and mandate; the concurrence does not.**

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the procedural effect of Judge Kobes' concurrence is limited to his individual view. It does not alter the binding judgment of the Eighth Circuit, which is established by the majority opinion.

**1. The Majority Opinion Constitutes the Judgment**
The text of the opinion distinguishes between the Court's holding and a judge's individual concurrence. The majority opinion concludes with the operative directive:

> "We vacate the district court’s order regarding Cohen’s claim under the MFLSA and remand for further proceedings. **We otherwise affirm.**"

This "We otherwise affirm" language is the judgment of the Court. It indicates that all issues not explicitly vacated (such as the MPWA, MWTA, and injunctive relief claims) are affirmed. Because the majority did not explicitly vacate the ruling on attorney’s fees, costs, or prejudgment interest, those items fall within the scope of the "otherwise affirm" directive.

**2. Concurrences Do Not Alter the Mandate**
Judge Kobes' opinion is labeled "KOBES, Circuit Judge, **concurring specially**." Unlike the majority, which speaks for the Court using "We," the concurrence speaks for the judge using "I" ("I also vacate...").

In appellate procedure, a concurring opinion explains the judge's reasoning or additional views but does not modify the majority's disposition unless the majority opinion itself incorporates the concurrence's language into the final order. Here, the majority's final order is distinct and separate from the concurrence. Therefore, the district court must follow the majority's mandate to affirm the fees, costs, and interest, not the concurrence's request to vacate them.

**3. Application to the Mandate**
When the Eighth Circuit issues its mandate to the district court, it will reflect the majority's disposition:
*   **MFLSA Claim:** Vacated and remanded (per majority).
*   **Fees, Costs, Interest:** Affirmed (per majority's "otherwise affirm").
*   **Concurrence View:** Not binding on the lower court.

**Conclusion**
The majority's "We otherwise affirm" controls. The concurrence's view on fees does not become part of the mandate. The district court must proceed based on the majority's order to affirm the fees, costs, and prejudgment interest.
