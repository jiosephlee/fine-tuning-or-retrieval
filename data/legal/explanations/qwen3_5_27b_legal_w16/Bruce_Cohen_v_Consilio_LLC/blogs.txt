Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Who Gets the Penalties? 8th Circuit Limits Private Enforcement of Minnesota Wage Laws

## The Leverage of Penalties in Wage Litigation

Private enforcement is often the engine that drives compliance with wage and hour laws. In class action litigation, the threat of statutory penalties frequently provides the leverage necessary to secure settlements or judgments that exceed mere back pay. However, the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit has issued a decision that significantly narrows the scope of private enforcement under Minnesota state law. In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, decided on October 6, 2025, the court held that individual employees cannot seek "average daily wage penalties" under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA). Furthermore, the court raised serious jurisdictional questions regarding whether an employee retains standing to seek statutory penalties under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) once the underlying wage violation has been cured. For plaintiffs’ counsel operating in the Eighth Circuit, this decision removes a key tool from the litigation arsenal and shifts the balance of power toward employers and state regulators.

## Statutory Interpretation of the MPWA

The core of the dispute turned on the interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 181.101, which governs the payment of wages under the MPWA. Cohen, an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer, argued that the plain language of the statute authorized employees to seek penalties in addition to unpaid wages. The Eighth Circuit, writing through Circuit Judge Kelly, disagreed, relying heavily on the specific text of the statute and the broader statutory scheme.

The court noted that § 181.101(a) explicitly references the Commissioner regarding penalties. Under the statute, the Commissioner must first serve a demand for payment on behalf of an employee. Only if the employer fails to pay within ten days of that demand may the Commissioner charge and collect wages and a penalty. While the statute preserves an employee's right to prosecute a claim for *wages*, the Eighth Circuit concluded that the authority to enforce *penalties* is reserved exclusively for the Commissioner. The court bolstered this textual reading with a legislative intent argument, pointing to other sections of the Minnesota wage statutes, such as §§ 181.13 and 181.14, where the legislature explicitly empowered discharged or resigning employees to charge and collect penalties. The court reasoned that where the legislature knows how to grant private penalty rights, its silence in § 181.101 must be read as an intentional limitation. This textualist approach effectively severs the link between a private wage claim and the recovery of daily wage penalties under the MPWA, leaving that enforcement power solely in the hands of the state.

## Standing, Mootness, and the MFLSA Claim

While the MPWA holding was definitive, the court’s treatment of Cohen’s MFLSA claim highlighted the complex intersection of remedial statutes and Article III standing. Cohen had stipulated that Consilio had paid all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages. However, he continued to pursue statutory penalties, which the parties agreed would be payable to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen personally. This created a jurisdictional puzzle: if Cohen has no personal financial stake in the penalties, does he have standing to sue for them?

The Eighth Circuit cited *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services*, which allows citizen plaintiffs to seek civil penalties even if paid to the government, provided the penalties afford redress by deterring future violations. However, the court noted that standing must persist throughout the litigation. If Consilio’s violative overtime policy has been rescinded and it is "absolutely clear" that the conduct will not recur, Cohen’s claim for penalties could be moot. Because the record was unclear on whether the policy remained in place, the Eighth Circuit vacated the district court’s summary judgment on the MFLSA claim and remanded for a determination of jurisdiction. Circuit Judge Kobes concurred specially, expressing skepticism that Cohen had standing under *Laidlaw* given the differences between environmental penalties and wage penalties, but agreed that the district court needed to resolve the mootness question first.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The *Cohen* decision has immediate and significant implications for employment litigation in Minnesota and the Eighth Circuit. By holding that private plaintiffs cannot recover average daily wage penalties under the MPWA, the court has reduced the potential financial exposure for employers in private suits. This reduction in exposure may diminish the incentive for plaintiffs to file such actions, particularly in cases where the unpaid wages themselves are not substantial enough to justify the cost of litigation without the multiplier effect of penalties.

Moreover, the standing inquiry regarding the MFLSA penalties signals a stricter scrutiny of "citizen plaintiff" standing in the wage context. While *Laidlaw* provides a pathway for private enforcement of public rights, the Eighth Circuit’s remand suggests that courts will closely examine whether the defendant’s voluntary cessation of the challenged practice renders the claim moot. Employers may find that promptly paying owed wages and liquidated damages is not only a compliance measure but also a litigation strategy to potentially moot out penalty claims.

Ultimately, *Cohen* reinforces a trend of limiting private enforcement mechanisms in favor of administrative oversight. By channeling penalty enforcement to the Commissioner, the Eighth Circuit has effectively prioritized state administrative action over private litigation for these specific remedies. For practitioners, this means that the threat of private penalty actions under the MPWA is largely extinguished, and reliance on the MFLSA for penalties will require a careful showing of ongoing violation to survive a mootness challenge. The decision serves as a reminder that while wage statutes provide substantive rights, the procedural avenues to enforce them are subject to rigorous statutory and constitutional constraints.



# Made Whole but Not Mooted? Standing for Civil Penalties After Back Pay

In wage theft litigation, the path to resolution often hinges on whether an employer can cure its violation by paying what is owed. In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit confronted a nuanced jurisdictional question that arises when an employer pays all back wages and liquidated damages to an employee, but the employee continues to seek statutory penalties payable to the state. The court’s decision highlights the enduring tension between the doctrine of mootness and the public enforcement mechanisms embedded in labor statutes. While the Eighth Circuit affirmed summary judgment on most of Cohen’s claims, it vacated the ruling on the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) claim, signaling that paying an employee’s individual damages does not automatically extinguish a plaintiff’s standing to seek civil penalties.

The case began when Cohen, an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer, sued Consilio, LLC, alleging the company failed to pay overtime wages in violation of Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the MFLSA, and the Wage Theft Act (MWTA). During the litigation, Consilio paid Cohen and other class members the disputed overtime wages and liquidated damages. The parties stipulated that Cohen had been paid all wages and damages owed to him personally. However, a dispute remained regarding statutory penalties, which under Minnesota law are payable to the Commissioner of Labor rather than the individual employee. The district court granted summary judgment for Consilio on all claims, reasoning that the payment of back wages resolved the matter. Cohen appealed, arguing he remained entitled to penalties and injunctive relief despite being made whole for his personal losses.

The Eighth Circuit’s analysis began with statutory interpretation regarding the MPWA and MWTA claims. On the MPWA claim, the court held that the plain language of the statute reserves the right to seek average daily wage penalties for the Commissioner, not private employees. The court noted that the legislature knew how to empower employees with a right to penalties when it wished to do so, as seen in other sections of the Minnesota wage statutes, but did not do so here. Similarly, the court affirmed the dismissal of the MWTA claim because Cohen failed to provide statutory authority supporting private penalties under that act. These holdings reinforce a strict textualist approach to state wage statutes, limiting private enforcement to the recovery of actual wages and liquidated damages unless the legislature explicitly authorizes private penalty claims.

The core of the opinion, however, rests on the MFLSA claim and the jurisdictional implications of *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services*. The Eighth Circuit expressed serious concern regarding its jurisdiction to decide the penalty claim given that Cohen had been fully compensated for his personal injury. Citing *Laidlaw*, the court recognized that civil penalties payable to the government can still afford redress to citizen plaintiffs by encouraging defendants to discontinue violations and deterring future conduct. Consequently, Cohen’s claim might not be moot even if he received his back pay, provided the allegedly wrongful behavior could reasonably be expected to recur. The court emphasized that voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of the power to determine its legality. Because it remained unclear whether Consilio’s violative overtime policy was still in place, the Eighth Circuit vacated the summary judgment and remanded for the district court to determine if Cohen retained standing and if the case was moot.

This remand offers a significant analytical pivot for wage theft litigation in the Eighth Circuit. The court’s reliance on *Laidlaw* suggests that employers cannot rely solely on paying back wages to kill a lawsuit if statutory penalties remain at stake. If the policy persists, the plaintiff retains a continuing interest in the litigation through the mechanism of civil penalties, even if those funds do not go directly into the plaintiff’s pocket. This creates a strategic dilemma for employers who might otherwise settle wage claims to avoid the risk of penalties. It also places a factual burden on the district court to assess the current status of the employer’s policies, ensuring that courts do not retain jurisdiction over cases where the parties plainly lack a continuing interest.

Judge Kobes’ special concurrence adds a layer of skepticism to the majority’s approach. While agreeing with the remand, Judge Kobes questioned whether Cohen actually has standing to seek statutory penalties under *Laidlaw* in this specific context, noting that the parties did not brief the issue. This signals that the standing question remains open and fact-sensitive. The concurrence also highlighted the need to address attorney’s fees and costs on remand, further complicating the potential resolution. The split in the concurrence suggests that while the majority is willing to allow the case to proceed to test the mootness question, there is judicial hesitation about the breadth of citizen-plaintiff standing in wage theft cases where the primary relief sought benefits the state rather than the individual.

The downstream effects of *Cohen v. Consilio* will likely influence how employers structure settlements and how plaintiffs frame their complaints in the Eighth Circuit. Employers may need to be more cautious about simply paying back wages, as it may not resolve the litigation if penalties are statutorily available. Plaintiffs, conversely, must be prepared to demonstrate that the underlying policy violation persists to maintain standing for penalty claims. As the district court works through the remand, the Eighth Circuit’s decision clarifies that being made whole financially does not necessarily mean a case is mooted legally, preserving a pathway for public enforcement through private litigation even after individual damages are satisfied.



# Don't Save It for the Brief: Waiver and Pleading Requirements in Employment Appeals

## The High Cost of Procedural Oversight

In the high-stakes world of employment litigation, substantive rights often take a backseat to procedural mechanics. A plaintiff may have a meritorious claim for unpaid wages, yet lose the battle because they failed to properly preserve an argument or amend their complaint. The United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit’s recent decision in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, issued October 6, 2025, serves as a stark reminder of this reality. While the case involves complex interpretations of Minnesota wage statutes, the most instructive aspects for practitioners lie in the court’s strict application of waiver doctrines and pleading standards. In *Cohen*, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the dismissal of significant claims not because the employer necessarily won on the merits of the wage theft allegations, but because the plaintiff failed to follow the rules of appellate briefing and civil procedure.

## The Factual Landscape

To understand the procedural pitfalls, one must first understand the posture of the case. Cohen, an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer, sued his employer, Consilio, LLC, alleging violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA). The core dispute involved overtime pay. Crucially, before the appellate court reviewed the case, Consilio paid Cohen all alleged overtime wages and liquidated damages. The parties stipulated that Cohen had been paid for all wages and damages he claimed under the MFLSA. However, a dispute remained regarding statutory penalties and injunctive relief. The district court granted summary judgment to Consilio on all claims, and Cohen appealed. While the Eighth Circuit vacated the judgment regarding the MFLSA penalties due to potential mootness issues, it affirmed the dismissal of the MWTA penalties and the request for injunctive relief on procedural grounds.

## Waiver Through Lack of Authority

The Eighth Circuit’s handling of Cohen’s MWTA claim highlights a recurring frustration for appellate courts: the failure of counsel to support assertions with legal authority. Cohen argued that Consilio failed to provide statutorily required notice of a change in its overtime policy, entitling him to penalties under the MWTA. However, the district court had granted summary judgment on this claim with little discussion, and Cohen’s appellate brief failed to provide a meaningful argument or statutory authority to support reversal.

Writing for the panel, Circuit Judge Kelly noted that Cohen pointed to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under the specific MWTA provisions cited. The court applied the well-established principle that an issue is waived if a party’s brief does not support an assertion with argument or legal authority. Citing *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, the Eighth Circuit declined to disturb the district court’s order. This aspect of the opinion reinforces that appellate courts are not research engines for counsel. Even if a statutory interpretation might be favorable to an employee, the claim dies if the appellant fails to provide the legal scaffolding required to sustain it. For employment practitioners, this underscores the necessity of rigorously briefing every claim, even those that might seem secondary to the main wage recovery, as failure to cite authority results in immediate waiver.

## Pleading Requirements and Rule 15

Perhaps the most significant lesson from *Cohen* for employment litigators concerns the scope of injunctive relief. In his original complaint, Cohen sought specific forms of injunctive relief. However, during the summary judgment briefing, Cohen changed course and requested different relief—specifically, an order rescinding the 2019 overtime policy. The district court did not consider this new request, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed that the issue was not properly before them.

The court was explicit that new claims or significant changes in the relief sought must be raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs. Relying on *InfoDeli, LLC* and Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a), the Eighth Circuit made clear that the complaint defines the boundaries of the litigation. Allowing parties to shift the relief sought in briefing stages would undermine the notice function of pleadings and potentially prejudice the opposing party. This holding is a critical reminder that the complaint is the roadmap for the case. If an employee realizes that the specific relief sought in the initial filing is insufficient or needs modification based on the employer’s conduct or defense strategy, they must formally amend the complaint. Relying on the flexibility of briefing to introduce new remedial requests is a procedural strategy that will not survive appellate scrutiny.

## Implications for Employment Practitioners

The *Cohen* decision sends a clear message to employment attorneys on both sides of the aisle: procedural discipline is as vital as substantive legal knowledge. In wage and hour cases, where statutory penalties and injunctive relief can significantly alter the financial exposure of an employer, the stakes for preservation are high. The Eighth Circuit’s affirmation of the MWTA dismissal demonstrates that statutory interpretation arguments will not save a claim that lacks proper briefing support. Similarly, the rejection of the injunctive relief claim reinforces the strict boundaries of Rule 15.

For plaintiffs’ counsel, the takeaway is to ensure that every claim in the complaint is supported by a robust briefing strategy that cites controlling authority, and that any evolution in the requested relief is formalized through an amendment rather than a motion brief. For defense counsel, the case provides a strong basis for arguing waiver when plaintiffs attempt to expand their remedies late in the litigation process. Ultimately, *Cohen* illustrates that in the Eighth Circuit, as in many others, the law favors the vigilant. A meritorious claim for wage theft is of little use if the procedural vehicle carrying it is dismantled by waiver and pleading errors.
