Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Can a private plaintiff establish Article III standing to seek civil penalties payable to the state under Laidlaw?
Question:
The court vacated the MFLSA claim due to jurisdictional concerns about standing for civil penalties payable to the Commissioner, citing Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw. I understand Laidlaw allows citizen suits for penalties to deter future violations, but the court notes penalties here go to the state, not the plaintiff. Can someone clarify the specific test for standing when the relief sought (penalties) benefits the government rather than the plaintiff directly? Does the 'redressability' prong fail if the money doesn't go to the plaintiff?
Answer:
**Yes, under *Laidlaw*, a private plaintiff can establish standing for civil penalties payable to the state, provided the penalties offer redress through deterrence of future violations.**

Based on the Eighth Circuit’s opinion in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the fact that penalties are payable to the Commissioner rather than the plaintiff does not automatically defeat Article III standing. However, standing is contingent on whether the plaintiff has a continuing interest in the litigation (i.e., the violation is ongoing or could recur).

Here is the breakdown of the legal standard as applied in *Cohen*:

### 1. Redressability Does Not Require Payment to the Plaintiff
The court explicitly acknowledges the holding in *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw*, which governs this issue. In Section II.B, the court states:

> "Civil penalties, even if they are paid to the government, may 'afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct' because such penalties may 'encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.'"

Therefore, the "redressability" prong of standing is satisfied not by the plaintiff receiving the funds, but by the penalty’s ability to deter the defendant from repeating the conduct that caused the plaintiff's injury.

### 2. Standing Must Be Shown for Each Form of Relief
While *Laidlaw* permits this theory of standing, the court emphasizes that a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for "each form of relief that is sought" (*Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc.*). In *Cohen*, because the parties stipulated that Cohen had been paid all wages and liquidated damages, the only remaining relief sought was statutory penalties payable to the state.

### 3. The Critical Factor: Recurrence of the Violation
The court vacated the summary judgment and remanded the MFLSA claim specifically to determine jurisdiction because the record was unclear on whether the injury was moot. Standing depends on whether the wrongful conduct could reasonably be expected to recur.

As the court noted:
> "Cohen’s claim for penalties 'might become moot if subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur'... However, if Consilio’s policy is no longer in place and it is 'absolutely clear' that its wrongful conduct will not recur, Cohen’s MFLSA claim may in fact be moot."

If the employer has ceased the violative policy and it is "absolutely clear" it will not return, the plaintiff lacks a continuing interest, and the claim becomes moot even if penalties are sought.

### 4. Circuit Skepticism
It is worth noting that while the majority applied the *Laidlaw* framework, Judge Kobes concurred specially to express doubt. He noted that there may be "differences between the penalties in that case [*Laidlaw*] and those available under the MFLSA," suggesting that the applicability of *Laidlaw* to this specific statutory scheme is not settled in this circuit.

### Summary
To establish standing for penalties payable to the state under this opinion:
1.  **Redressability:** You must argue the penalty deters future violations, not that you receive the money.
2.  **Live Controversy:** You must show the violation is ongoing or capable of recurrence; if the conduct has stopped and won't return, the claim is moot.
3.  **Specific Relief:** You must prove standing specifically for the penalty claim, separate from any wage claims that may already be resolved.
