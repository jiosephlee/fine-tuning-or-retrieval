Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Why does the Eighth Circuit limit penalty recovery under MPWA § 181.101 to the Commissioner?
Question:
In Cohen v. Consilio, the court held that only the Commissioner can seek 'average daily wage penalties' under Minn. Stat. § 181.101, even though § 181.171 says an employer is liable for 'compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.' I'm confused about how the court distinguishes this section from other MPWA sections like § 181.13(a) where employees can explicitly charge and collect penalties. Does the court rely on the expressio unius canon here, or is there a specific administrative law principle limiting private enforcement of penalties in this context?
Answer:
The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *Cohen v. Consilio* limits penalty recovery under Minn. Stat. § 181.101 to the Commissioner based on **statutory text and legislative intent inferred from statutory structure**, rather than a general administrative law principle.

While the court does not explicitly name the *expressio unius* canon, it applies the underlying logic: because the legislature explicitly authorized private penalty collection in other MPWA sections but omitted that language in § 181.101, the omission was intentional.

Here is the breakdown of the court’s reasoning:

### 1. The Plain Text of § 181.101
The court begins with the plain language of the statute. Under Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a), the mechanism for collecting average daily wage penalties is exclusively administrative:
*   Penalties are only available if the **Commissioner** first serves a demand for payment on behalf of the employee.
*   If payment is not made within ten days, the **Commissioner** may charge and collect the wages and the penalty.
*   While money collected is paid to the employee, the right to *seek* and *collect* the penalty is vested in the Commissioner.

The court notes that the statute reserves only the ability to "prosecut[e] a claim for wages" to the employee, not penalties.

### 2. The Role of § 181.171
You asked how the court distinguishes this from § 181.171, which allows a civil action for "compensatory damages and other appropriate relief." The court clarifies that § 181.171 does not create independent penalty rights.

Section 181.171 states that an employer is liable for civil penalties "provided for in the section violated." Because § 181.101 (the section allegedly violated) does not provide for *employee-collected* penalties—only Commissioner-collected penalties—§ 181.171 cannot be used to bootstrap a private right of action for those penalties. As the court explains, the general enforcement provision (§ 181.171) incorporates the specific remedies of the violated section (§ 181.101), and § 181.101 limits penalty enforcement to the Commissioner.

### 3. Statutory Comparison (Legislative Intent)
The court addresses your question about *expressio unius* by comparing § 181.101 to other MPWA sections. The opinion cites Minn. Stat. § 181.13(a) and § 181.14, subd. 2, which explicitly allow discharged or resigning employees to "charge and collect a penalty."

Relying on *Lagasse v. Horton*, the court applies the principle that "the Legislature’s action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature 'knows how' to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so." Since the legislature knew how to write a private penalty right into the MPWA (as seen in § 181.13(a)) but chose not to include it in § 181.101, the court concludes the limitation to the Commissioner was deliberate.

### Conclusion
The Eighth Circuit does not rely on a broad administrative law principle limiting private enforcement. Instead, it relies on **textualism and statutory construction**. The court holds that the specific language of § 181.101 controls over the general language of § 181.171, and the legislature’s choice to grant private penalty rights in some MPWA sections but not § 181.101 confirms that employees cannot recover average daily wage penalties under that specific section.
