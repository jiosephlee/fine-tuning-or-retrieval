Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Does failing to cite statutory authority for penalties constitute waiver on appeal?
Question:
Regarding the MWTA claim, the court affirmed the dismissal because Cohen 'points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute' and offered 'no meaningful argument.' I thought summary judgment appeals get reviewed de novo. Is this a waiver issue under appellate procedure rules, or did the court find the lack of statutory citation meant there was no genuine issue of material fact? What is the threshold for 'meaningful argument' to avoid waiver on appeal?
Answer:
**Yes, failing to cite statutory authority and provide meaningful argument constitutes waiver on appeal, even when the underlying standard of review is de novo.**

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit explicitly treated the appellant's failure to cite statutory authority for penalties as a waiver issue, rather than a merits-based determination on the summary judgment standard.

### The Interplay Between De Novo Review and Appellate Waiver
You are correct that the court reviews grants of summary judgment *de novo* (Section II). However, *de novo* review does not obligate the appellate court to construct legal arguments for the appellant. As the court explains in Section II.C regarding the MWTA claim:

> "Cohen complains that the district court 'included little to no discussion of this claim.' But Cohen stipulated that he received all pay Consilio owes him, and he points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute. With no meaningful argument in support of reversal, we will not disturb the district court's order dismissing this claim."

The court distinguished this from the MPWA and MFLSA claims, where it engaged in statutory analysis. For the MWTA claim, the court declined to reach the merits of whether a genuine issue of material fact existed because Cohen failed to meet his burden of briefing the legal basis for the penalty.

### The Threshold for "Meaningful Argument"
The opinion establishes the threshold for avoiding waiver by citing *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000). According to the *Cohen* opinion, the standard requires an appellant to support assertions with both **argument** and **legal authority**.

Specifically, the court held that:
1.  **Statutory Authority:** The appellant must identify the specific statutory provision authorizing the relief sought (here, civil penalties under the MWTA). Cohen "points to no statutory authority."
2.  **Argument:** The appellant must provide reasoning supporting reversal. Cohen offered "no meaningful argument in support of reversal."

When a brief lacks these elements, the court applies the waiver doctrine: "inasmuch as [the party's] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it."

### Conclusion
The dismissal of the MWTA claim was not a finding that there was no genuine issue of material fact, but rather a procedural determination that Cohen waived the issue by failing to brief the legal authority for penalties. Under Eighth Circuit precedent cited in the opinion, *de novo* review of a summary judgment order does not cure an appellant's failure to provide statutory authority or meaningful argument on appeal.
