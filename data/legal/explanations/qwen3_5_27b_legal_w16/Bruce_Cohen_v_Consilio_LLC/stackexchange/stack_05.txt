Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Can a plaintiff raise new injunctive relief requests in summary judgment briefing?
Question:
Cohen sought injunctive relief to rescind the overtime policy, but the court said the issue is 'not properly before us' because he changed the relief sought in his summary judgment briefing rather than amending the complaint. I know Fed. R. Civ. P. 15 governs amendments, but why can't you clarify or expand relief requests in opposition to summary judgment? At what point does a request for injunctive relief become procedurally defaulted?
Answer:
**No, you generally cannot raise new or significantly different requests for injunctive relief in summary judgment briefing.**

Based on the Eighth Circuit’s opinion in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, 8th Cir. 2025, a plaintiff must raise new claims or altered requests for relief through a formal amendment to the complaint under Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a), rather than through opposition briefs.

### The Legal Standard
The court in *Cohen* relied on the principle that pleading requirements are distinct from briefing arguments. While summary judgment briefing allows parties to argue why the existing claims should succeed or fail, it is not the proper vehicle for introducing new substantive requests.

As the Eighth Circuit stated in Section II.D of the opinion:
> "New claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs."

The court cited *InfoDeli, LLC*, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025), to support the proposition that procedural rules require formal amendments (Rule 15) to add or significantly alter the scope of relief sought.

### Application in *Cohen*
In *Cohen*, the plaintiff initially sought "certain specific forms of injunctive relief" in his complaint. However, during summary judgment briefing, Cohen "changed course and request[ed] different relief"—specifically, an affirmative order to rescind the 2019 overtime policy.

Because this request differed from what was pleaded in the complaint, the district court did not consider it. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit affirmed that the issue was "not properly before us." The court treated the shift in relief sought as a new claim that was procedurally defaulted because it was not raised via an amended complaint.

### When Does Procedural Default Occur?
Based on this opinion, a request for injunctive relief becomes procedurally defaulted when:

1.  **It deviates from the complaint:** The relief requested in briefing is different from the specific relief sought in the operative complaint.
2.  **It is raised in briefing:** The request is introduced for the first time in summary judgment opposition rather than through a Rule 15 motion to amend.

If a plaintiff wishes to "clarify or expand" relief to the point where it constitutes a new request (as in *Cohen*), they must amend the complaint. Doing so in briefing results in the court refusing to consider the request.
