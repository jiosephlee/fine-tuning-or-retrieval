Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### How does the Federal Circuit distinguish between questions of law and fact in DuPont factor analysis?
Question:
In the opinion, the court states that 'Likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors.' However, it also says 'Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact.' I am confused about how the court applies de novo review to the likelihood of confusion conclusion while reviewing the DuPont factor findings for substantial evidence. Can someone clarify how these standards interact in practice?
Answer:
**The Short Answer**

The opinion resolves this tension by establishing a two-tiered review standard: the **DuPont factors are treated as factual findings** reviewed for *substantial evidence*, while the ultimate **likelihood of confusion conclusion is a question of law** reviewed *de novo*.

In practice, the court accepts the Board’s factual findings regarding the factors if supported by evidence, but then independently applies the legal standard of "likelihood of confusion" to those facts. However, if the Board applies a legal standard incorrectly *within* a factor (such as defining the scope of "similar goods"), that specific analysis is reviewed *de novo* as a legal error.

**Detailed Explanation**

**1. The Standard of Review (Section II)**
The opinion explicitly sets out the framework in Section II. The court states:
> "We review the [B]oard’s legal conclusions de novo, and its findings of fact for substantial evidence."

It further clarifies the relationship between the factors and the conclusion:
> "Likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors."
> "Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence when called into question on appeal."

This creates a hierarchy: The DuPont factors provide the factual substrate. The likelihood of confusion is the legal conclusion drawn from that substrate.

**2. How the Standards Interact in Practice**
The interaction is best illustrated by the court’s handling of **DuPont Factor 6** (Section III.B).

*   **Factual Findings (Substantial Evidence):** The Board found that there were nine third-party marks using "Aspire" for credit card services. The court did not dispute this number; it accepted the underlying evidence regarding the existence of these marks.
*   **Legal Application (De Novo):** The Board, however, limited its analysis to marks used on "credit card services" and excluded marks used on broader "banking and financing services." The court found this to be a **legal error**.
    *   The Board had already made a factual finding under **Factor 2** that the parties' services were "highly similar" (Section III.A).
    *   The court reasoned that the legal standard for "similar goods" in Factor 6 must be consistent with the factual finding in Factor 2. By narrowing the scope in Factor 6, the Board applied an incorrect legal standard.

As the court notes:
> "When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar... the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors. We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor."

**3. The Resulting Remedy**
Because the Board's analysis of Factor 6 contained a legal flaw (defining the scope of similarity incorrectly), the court **vacated** that finding. Since Factor 6 affects the commercial strength of the mark, which in turn affects the commercial impression under **Factor 1**, the court also vacated Factor 1.

However, for **Factor 2**, where the Board’s factual finding (services are highly similar) was supported by substantial evidence and the legal analysis was correct, the court **affirmed**.

**Summary**
To answer your specific confusion: The court reviews the *existence and weight* of the evidence for each factor for substantial evidence. But it reviews the *ultimate conclusion* of likelihood of confusion, and any *legal definitions* applied to those factors (like the scope of "similar goods"), *de novo*. If a factual finding relies on an incorrect legal standard, the court will correct it, as seen with the vacatur of Factor 6.

### Can 'credit card services' and 'banking services' be considered 'legally identical' if one party doesn't offer credit cards?
Question:
Apex Bank does not offer credit cards, only banking and financing services. However, the Board found the parties' services to be 'legally identical, in part.' The court affirmed this finding under DuPont Factor 2. How can services be legally identical when one party's business model (issuing credit cards via a bank partner) differs from the other's (direct banking services), especially when Apex explicitly stated they do not offer credit cards?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** Yes. Under *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, services can be found "legally identical, in part" even if one party does not currently offer the specific product, provided the legal definition of their broader service category encompasses that product. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board's finding because the dictionary definitions of "banking" and "finance" include extending credit via credit cards, creating a legal overlap regardless of Apex's specific business model.

### The Legal Standard: DuPont Factor 2

To understand this holding, you must look at the standard for the second *DuPont* factor (similarity of goods/services). As the opinion states in **Section III.A**, the Federal Circuit clarified that:

> "The services need not be identical—the evidence need only establish that 'the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.'"

The Court cited *Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC* for the proposition that strict identity is not required. However, in this specific case, the Board went further and found the services were "legally identical, in part."

### Why the Court Affirmed "Legally Identical"

Your confusion stems from the fact that Apex explicitly does not issue credit cards. However, the Board’s analysis (affirmed by the Court) relied on the **legal scope of the service classifications**, not just the specific current offerings of the applicant.

The opinion outlines the Board's reasoning in **Section III.A**:

1.  **Broad Definition of Credit Card Services:** The Board defined "credit card services" to include "issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases... managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores" (J.A. 25).
2.  **Dictionary Definitions of Banking:** Crucially, the Board looked at the dictionary definitions for "banking," "bank," and "finance." The opinion notes:
    > "The Board determined that because the dictionary definitions for 'banking,' 'bank,' and 'finance' encompass extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards, Apex and CC Serve’s services are 'legally identical, in part.'" (J.A. 27–28)
3.  **Substantial Evidence:** The Court reviewed this finding for "substantial evidence." Because the legal definition of Apex's service category ("banking and financing services") encompasses the specific activity CC Serve performs ("credit card services"), there is a legal overlap.

### Reconciling with Apex's Business Model

You asked how this holds when Apex "explicitly stated they do not offer credit cards." The opinion addresses this in **Section III.A** when discussing Apex's argument on appeal:

> "Apex argues that the parties' services are 'not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,' especially because CC Serve is partnering with banks to manage credit card programs—'not providing banking services' itself... We find Apex's argument unavailing."

The Court rejected the argument that the lack of direct competition or current product overlap negates the legal similarity of the service categories. The determination of "legally identical, in part" rests on the fact that **banking services legally include the capacity to issue credit cards**, even if Apex chooses not to exercise that capacity currently.

### Conclusion

The Court affirmed the Board's finding because **DuPont Factor 2 assesses the relationship between the service categories**, not merely the specific product mix of the companies involved. Since the legal definition of "banking and financing services" encompasses "issuing credit cards," the services are legally identical in that specific aspect, satisfying the standard for similarity under the Lanham Act.

*Note: While the Court affirmed Factor 2, it vacated Factors 1 and 6 because the Board inconsistently narrowed the scope of "similar services" for those factors (see Section III.B).*

### Why must the scope of third-party marks in DuPont Factor 6 match the scope of goods similarity in Factor 2?
Question:
The court vacated the Board's Factor 6 analysis because the Board limited third-party marks to 'credit card services' while Factor 2 found 'banking and financing services' to be highly similar. The court stated, 'When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar... the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors.' Is there a specific doctrine requiring this consistency, or is this a general principle of administrative law?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, there is no specific named "doctrine" of administrative law requiring this consistency. Instead, the Court treats it as a requirement of **internal consistency within the *DuPont* likelihood-of-confusion analysis**.

The Court’s reasoning rests on the relationship between the factual findings made in Factor 2 and the legal standard applied in Factor 6.

### The Legal Standard for Factor 6
The sixth *DuPont* factor considers "the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." As the Court notes, citing *Olde Tyme Foods* and *Juice Generation*, this factor does not require identical goods, only **similar** ones. The purpose is to determine if the field is crowded, which would suggest the mark is weak and entitled to a narrower scope of protection.

### The Requirement for Consistency
The Board committed a legal error by applying different scopes of "similarity" to different factors.
1.  **Factor 2 (Similarity of Services):** The Board found the parties' services ("credit card services" vs. "banking and financing services") to be **"highly similar"** and partially "legally identical."
2.  **Factor 6 (Third-Party Marks):** The Board restricted its review to marks used only on "credit card services," excluding those used on the broader "banking and financing services."

The Court held that once the Board makes a factual finding in Factor 2 that the services are highly similar, it cannot arbitrarily narrow that definition for Factor 6. The opinion states:

> "When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar... in its analysis of the second DuPont factor... the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors."

### Conclusion
The Court reasoned that there is **"no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor."**

Therefore, this is not a general principle of administrative law (such as *Chevron* deference or arbitrary and capricious review) but rather a **substantive requirement of the *DuPont* test itself**. The factors must be applied coherently; a finding of high similarity in one factor cannot be undermined by a narrower definition of similarity in another factor without justification. Because the Board failed to maintain this consistency, the Court vacated the Factor 6 analysis and remanded for reconsideration using the broader scope established in Factor 2.

### Does a remand on DuPont Factor 6 automatically require a remand on Factor 1 regarding commercial impression?
Question:
The court vacated Factor 1 (similarity of marks) solely because reconsideration of Factor 6 (third-party marks) 'may result in a different determination of the mark's commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression.' Is there a binding precedent that links the strength of a mark (Factor 6) directly to the commercial impression analysis (Factor 1) in this manner?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit treated the remand of DuPont Factor 6 as necessitating a remand of Factor 1, but this requirement was grounded in the specific interdependence of mark strength and commercial impression rather than a universal rule applicable to all Factor 6 remands.

**The Legal Standard and Factors**
The opinion defines the first DuPont factor as an analysis of the "similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression." The Court notes that this factor focuses on whether consumer confusion is likely based on the overall "commercial impression."

The sixth DuPont factor considers the "number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." Citing *Palm Bay Imps.* and *Juice Generation*, the Court explains that evidence under this factor is relevant to demonstrate that a mark is "relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection."

**The Linkage in *Apex Bank***
In Section III.C, the Court explicitly links the two factors. The Board had erred in Factor 6 by improperly narrowing the scope of third-party marks considered. The Court reasoned that correcting this error meant "reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness."

Because the Court found that "commercial impression informs the analysis under the first DuPont factor," and because the strength of the mark affects that impression, the Court concluded it "must similarly vacate the Board’s analysis" of Factor 1.

**Conclusion**
*Apex Bank* establishes that a remand on Factor 6 requires a remand on Factor 1 when the corrected Factor 6 analysis could alter the assessment of the mark's strength. While the opinion does not state this is "automatic" in every instance, it holds that when Factor 6 evidence informs the mark's strength, and that strength informs the commercial impression, the two factors are legally intertwined, necessitating a joint remand to ensure the commercial impression analysis reflects the corrected strength assessment.

### What is the proper methodology for defining services when a Trademark ID Manual entry has been deleted?
Question:
The Board noted that the entry for 'credit card services' was deleted from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve's registration. Consequently, the Board used dictionary definitions to define the services. Is this the standard approach for defining goods and services in an opposition proceeding when the official manual classification has changed or been removed?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s methodology of using dictionary definitions to define services when the relevant Trademark ID Manual entry has been deleted.

In this case, the Board faced a situation where the entry for "credit card services" was deleted from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration had already issued. To determine the scope of CC Serve’s services for the likelihood-of-confusion analysis, the Board relied on dictionary definitions. The Board defined "credit card services" to encompass "issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores."

The Federal Circuit reviewed this determination under the **second *DuPont* factor**, which assesses the similarity of the parties’ goods and services. The Court noted that services need not be identical; rather, the evidence must establish that the products are related in some manner or that the marketing circumstances could give rise to a mistaken belief of a common source.

The Court held that substantial evidence supported the Board’s finding that the parties’ services were highly similar based on the definitions used. Specifically, the opinion states: "Because the entry for 'credit card services' was deleted from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board first determined the meaning of the identified services... We affirm the Board’s finding as to the second DuPont factor."

However, the opinion provides an important caveat regarding the consistency of this definition across other factors. While the Court affirmed the definition used for the second factor, it vacated the Board’s analysis of the **sixth *DuPont* factor** (the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods). The Court reasoned that because the Board had already found the services to be "highly similar" (and partially "legally identical") under the second factor, it erred by narrowing the scope of "similar goods" too strictly when considering third-party marks under the sixth factor. The Court instructed that the Board should retain the same scope of similarity across factors.

**Summary for Practice:**
According to *Apex Bank*, using dictionary definitions to define services is a permissible methodology when the Trademark ID Manual entry is no longer available, provided the resulting definition is supported by substantial evidence. However, once a scope of similarity is established for one *DuPont* factor, that scope should generally remain consistent when analyzing other factors, such as the commercial strength of the mark based on third-party use.

### What is the quantitative threshold for a 'crowded field' defense under DuPont Factor 6?
Question:
The Board rejected Apex's argument that CC Serve's mark was weak because there were only nine Aspire-formative marks for credit card services. The court cited cases where 14 or 26 marks were considered 'considerable.' Is there a specific numerical threshold established in Federal Circuit case law for determining when a field is 'crowded' enough to demonstrate mark weakness?
Answer:
# No Bright-Line Numerical Threshold Exists

Based on the opinion in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, there is **no specific numerical threshold** established in Federal Circuit case law for determining when a field is "crowded" enough to demonstrate mark weakness under DuPont Factor 6.

While the Board in this case compared the number of third-party marks to figures from prior decisions, the Federal Circuit vacated the Board's analysis because it focused on the wrong scope of goods, not because of the specific number of marks itself.

### The Board’s Reliance on Prior Numbers
The Board initially concluded that nine "Aspire-formative" marks for credit card services were insufficient to prove a crowded field. In reaching this conclusion, the Board cited prior Federal Circuit cases as benchmarks for what constitutes "considerable" evidence of weakness:
*   **14 marks:** Cited from *Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U.*, 797 F.3d 1363 (Fed. Cir. 2015), where the court discussed "voluminous evidence" highlighting fourteen notable examples.
*   **26 marks:** Cited from *Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC*, 794 F.3d 1334 (Fed. Cir. 2015), where the court referred to 26 third-party marks as "a considerable number."

The Board reasoned that nine marks did not rise to the level of "considerable" or "ubiquitous" use found in those cases.

### The Court’s Correction: Scope Over Count
The Federal Circuit did not affirm or reject the Board's numerical comparison as a binding rule. Instead, the Court vacated the Board’s finding on Factor 6 because the Board defined the relevant market too narrowly.

The Court held that the Board erred by restricting the universe of third-party marks to only those relating to "credit card services," while excluding marks related to broader "banking and financing services." This was inconsistent with the Board’s own finding under **DuPont Factor 2**, which determined the parties' services were "highly similar" and "legally identical, in part."

The opinion clarifies the legal standard for Factor 6:
> "The sixth DuPont factor requires the Board to consider similar marks for similar goods and services... We have held that the sixth DuPont factor does not require identical goods—only similar ones."

### Conclusion for Practitioners
*Apex Bank* indicates that the "crowded field" defense is not determined by a magic number (e.g., 14 or 26 marks). Rather, the analysis depends on:
1.  **The Scope of Similar Goods:** The Board must consider third-party marks on *similar* goods, not just *identical* goods.
2.  **Consistency:** The definition of "similar goods" used for Factor 6 must align with the finding of similarity under Factor 2.

In this case, the Court remanded for the Board to reconsider the factor in light of the broader scope of "banking and financing services," which would include the 42 additional third-party marks Apex argued were relevant. Therefore, the quantitative threshold remains flexible and context-dependent on the definition of the relevant market.
