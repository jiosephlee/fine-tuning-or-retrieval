Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# TTAB Must Apply Consistent Definitions of 'Similar Goods' Across DuPont Factors

The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) is tasked with applying a multifactor test to determine whether a new trademark application creates a likelihood of confusion with an existing mark. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, decided on September 25, 2025, the Federal Circuit addressed a subtle but critical procedural inconsistency in how the Board applies these factors. Writing for the court, Circuit Judge Hughes held that the Board cannot define the scope of "similar goods" broadly when weighing the similarity of the parties' services under the second *DuPont* factor, only to narrow that definition when evaluating third-party evidence under the sixth *DuPont* factor. This decision reinforces the necessity of analytical coherence in trademark likelihood-of-confusion determinations, ensuring that the scope of the relevant marketplace remains consistent throughout the analysis.

The dispute arose when Apex Bank sought to register the ASPIRE BANK marks for banking and financing services. CC Serve, which held a prior registration for the word mark ASPIRE in connection with credit card services, opposed the application. The Board sustained the opposition, finding a likelihood of confusion. On appeal, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding regarding the second *DuPont* factor, which assesses the similarity of the parties' goods and services. The Board had determined that credit card services and banking services were highly similar, noting that dictionary definitions of banking encompass extending credit through cards. Substantial evidence supported this conclusion, and the court found no error in the Board’s determination that the services were legally identical in part.

However, the court identified a legal flaw in how the Board applied the sixth *DuPont* factor, which considers the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods. While the Board had accepted that banking and credit card services were highly similar for the purpose of comparing the parties' marks, it restricted the universe of third-party marks it considered to only those used in connection with credit card services. The Board excluded third-party marks using ASPIRE in the broader banking and financing sector, deeming them irrelevant. The Federal Circuit rejected this approach, reasoning that if the services are sufficiently similar to weigh heavily in favor of confusion under the second factor, the same standard of similarity must apply when evaluating third-party marks under the sixth factor. The court vacated the Board’s findings on the first and sixth factors and remanded for reconsideration.

This ruling underscores a fundamental principle of trademark doctrine: the definition of the relevant marketplace must remain stable throughout the likelihood-of-confusion analysis. The sixth factor serves to gauge the commercial strength of the senior mark by looking at how crowded the field is. If consumers encounter many similar marks in a crowded marketplace, they are generally more adept at distinguishing between sources, which weakens the senior mark’s claim to exclusive rights. By narrowing the scope of third-party marks while simultaneously broadening the scope of the parties' services, the Board artificially inflated the strength of CC Serve’s mark. The Federal Circuit’s correction ensures that the Board cannot shift the goalposts to favor a finding of confusion. If the market is broad enough to include banking services for the purpose of comparing the applicant and the opponent, it must be broad enough to include third-party banking marks when assessing the senior mark’s exclusivity.

The doctrinal implications of *Apex Bank* extend to how applicants and practitioners structure their evidence during opposition proceedings. Traditionally, applicants challenging a likelihood-of-confusion finding often struggle to prove that a senior mark is weak due to third-party use. This decision clarifies that the definition of "similar goods" used to establish the competitive relationship between the parties cannot be used to exclude third-party evidence that falls within that same competitive relationship. Practitioners should be encouraged to submit a broader array of third-party registrations and uses that align with the broader definition of similarity accepted by the Board in other factors. Furthermore, it places a higher burden on the Board to maintain internal consistency across its findings. A finding that services are "legally identical" for one factor creates a factual predicate that constrains the analysis of other factors.

Ultimately, *Apex Bank* serves as a reminder that the *DuPont* factors are not isolated checkboxes but interconnected elements of a single legal inquiry. The Federal Circuit’s insistence on consistency prevents the Board from engaging in a form of analytical cherry-picking that could distort the likelihood-of-confusion calculus. By vacating the lower court’s findings on the first and sixth factors, the court leaves open the possibility that the inclusion of broader third-party evidence could alter the commercial impression of the ASPIRE mark. As the case returns to the Board, it will be interesting to see whether the expanded universe of third-party marks is sufficient to tip the balance against a finding of confusion. For now, the ruling stands as a necessary check on the Board’s discretion, ensuring that the scope of similarity remains a fixed point of reference rather than a variable tool for reaching a predetermined outcome.



# Interdependence of DuPont Factors: Why a Remand on Mark Strength Necessitated Reconsideration of Commercial Impression

In trademark law, the likelihood-of-confusion analysis is rarely a mechanical exercise. It is a holistic inquiry where the interplay between various factors often determines the outcome more than any single element in isolation. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, decided September 25, 2025, the Federal Circuit reinforced this principle by vacating the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s analysis of two distinct DuPont factors based on their legal interdependence. While the court affirmed the Board’s finding that the parties’ services were highly similar, it held that the Board’s narrow definition of the relevant market for third-party marks rendered its assessment of mark strength—and consequently, the commercial impression of the marks—legally flawed. This decision serves as a crucial reminder to the TTAB that consistency in defining the relevant universe of goods and services is essential across all factors of the likelihood-of-confusion test.

The dispute arose when Apex Bank sought to register the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for banking and financing services. CC Serve Corp., which owns a registration for the ASPIRE mark in connection with credit card services, opposed the application, arguing a likelihood of confusion under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act. The Board sustained the opposition, relying heavily on the similarity of the services and the strength of the ASPIRE mark. On appeal, Apex Bank challenged the Board’s application of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. Circuit Judge Hughes, writing for the panel, affirmed the Board’s conclusion regarding the second factor, which assesses the similarity of the parties’ goods or services. The court found substantial evidence supporting the Board’s determination that credit card services and general banking services are highly similar, noting that dictionary definitions and third-party registrations supported the view that these services could emanate from a single source.

However, the Federal Circuit identified a critical inconsistency in how the Board applied the concept of similarity when moving from Factor 2 to Factor 6. Factor 6 requires an analysis of the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods. While the Board had broadly defined the services as highly similar under Factor 2 to support a finding of confusion, it narrowed the scope of its inquiry under Factor 6 to only those third-party marks used specifically on credit card services. The Board identified nine Aspire-formative marks in that narrow category and deemed this insufficient to show that CC Serve’s mark was weak. Apex Bank argued that the Board should have considered 42 additional third-party marks using Aspire in the broader financial services industry. The Federal Circuit agreed with Apex, holding that the Board erred by imposing a more stringent legal standard for similarity under Factor 6 than it had applied under Factor 2. If the services are legally identical or highly similar enough to weigh heavily in favor of confusion under Factor 2, the court reasoned, the Board must consider third-party marks on those same similar goods when evaluating mark strength under Factor 6.

This ruling on Factor 6 had immediate downstream consequences for Factor 1, which evaluates the similarity of the marks in their entireties regarding appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. The Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s analysis of Factor 1 not because the Board erred in its initial comparison of the marks, but because the determination of mark strength directly influences the commercial impression analysis. If the scope of third-party marks is expanded on remand, the evidence may demonstrate that the ASPIRE mark is weaker than the Board originally concluded. A weaker mark is entitled to a narrower scope of protection, which alters the overall commercial impression of the competing marks. By vacating Factor 1 alongside Factor 6, the court underscored that the DuPont factors are not independent silos. A change in the factual landscape regarding mark strength necessitates a fresh look at how consumers perceive the similarity of the marks in question.

The doctrinal implications of *Apex Bank* extend beyond the specific facts of credit card versus banking services. The decision places a constraint on the TTAB’s discretion to define the relevant market differently for different factors within the same likelihood-of-confusion analysis. Practitioners can now point to this opinion to challenge Board decisions that find goods highly similar for the purpose of establishing proximity but then ignore third-party uses on those same goods when assessing mark strength. This consistency requirement prevents the Board from cherry-picking definitions of similarity to reach a predetermined outcome. Furthermore, the ruling reinforces the holistic nature of the DuPont test, reminding adjudicators that the commercial impression of a mark is dynamic and dependent on the strength of that mark within the relevant marketplace. As the case proceeds on remand, the outcome may hinge on whether the inclusion of the broader set of financial services marks sufficiently weakens the ASPIRE mark to distinguish it from Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK brand.



# The Scope of Financial Services in Trademark Law: Credit Cards vs. Banking

In the rapidly evolving landscape of financial technology, the lines between traditional banking and specialized credit services are increasingly blurred. For trademark practitioners, this convergence raises critical questions about the scope of protection afforded to marks within the financial sector. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, decided September 25, 2025, the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit addressed precisely this issue. The court affirmed a Trademark Trial and Appeal Board finding that credit card services and general banking services are highly similar for likelihood-of-confusion purposes, while simultaneously correcting the Board’s inconsistent application of the *DuPont* factors regarding third-party marks. This decision signals that the Federal Circuit will scrutinize the Board’s internal consistency when defining the relevant market, offering important guidance for fintech startups and legacy institutions alike.

## The Convergence of Banking and Credit Services

The core of the dispute centered on Apex Bank’s attempt to register the marks ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services, against CC Serve Corp.’s prior registration of ASPIRE for credit card services. The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board sustained CC Serve’s opposition, finding a likelihood of confusion under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act. On appeal, Apex challenged the Board’s analysis of the similarity of services, arguing that CC Serve merely partners with banks to manage credit programs and does not provide banking services itself.

The Federal Circuit rejected this argument, affirming the Board’s application of the second *DuPont* factor. The court highlighted that the services need not be identical to be legally similar; they must simply be related in a manner that could give rise to a mistaken belief of common source. Notably, the Board’s analysis was bolstered by the fact that the specific entry for “credit card services” had been deleted from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration. Consequently, the Board relied on dictionary definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance,” which encompass extending credit and issuing credit cards. The court found substantial evidence supporting the conclusion that the parties’ services were “legally identical, in part.” This reliance on dictionary definitions over outdated ID Manual entries suggests that the substantive nature of the services, rather than the specific classification codes, will drive the similarity inquiry in the financial sector.

## Consistency in the DuPont Analysis

While the court affirmed the finding on service similarity, it vacated the Board’s analysis of the sixth *DuPont* factor, which considers the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods. The Board had determined that the relevant public for the sixth factor was limited strictly to consumers of “credit card services,” excluding third-party marks that used ASPIRE for broader banking and financing services. The Federal Circuit identified this as a legal error rooted in inconsistency.

The court reasoned that once the Board found the parties’ services to be highly similar under the second factor, it could not impose a more stringent standard of identity under the sixth factor. If banking and credit card services are legally identical in part for the purpose of assessing confusion between the parties, then the universe of third-party marks must reflect that same scope of similarity. By limiting the third-party evidence to only those marks used on credit card services, the Board artificially narrowed the field and potentially overstated the commercial strength of CC Serve’s mark. The court vacated the findings on the sixth factor and, consequently, the first factor regarding the overall commercial impression of the marks, remanding for the Board to consider the broader landscape of financial services marks.

## Implications for Fintech and Brand Strategy

The doctrinal implications of *Apex Bank* are significant for companies operating at the intersection of traditional finance and fintech. First, the affirmation of the second factor reinforces a broad interpretation of financial services. Companies cannot easily insulate their marks by arguing that they are merely a service provider or partner rather than a direct issuer if the dictionary definitions and market realities suggest functional overlap. This broadens the scope of potential oppositions in the financial sector, meaning a bank launching a new credit product may face challenges from a fintech company with a similar mark, and vice versa.

Second, the remand on the sixth factor establishes a consistency requirement for TTAB proceedings. Practitioners can now argue that if the Board finds goods or services similar under one factor, it must maintain that definition when evaluating the strength of the mark through third-party usage. This prevents the Board from having it both ways: finding services close enough to confuse consumers while simultaneously finding the market narrow enough to ignore crowded third-party usage. For applicants, this suggests that compiling evidence of third-party use across the broader spectrum of financial services—not just the specific sub-category—may be crucial to overcoming a likelihood-of-confusion finding.

## Downstream Effects on Trademark Prosecution

Ultimately, *Apex Bank* serves as a reminder that the *DuPont* factors are not isolated checkboxes but an integrated analysis where factual findings must remain consistent. As financial products continue to merge, with banks offering fintech apps and fintechs offering banking accounts, the definition of “similarity” will likely continue to expand. The Federal Circuit’s insistence on aligning the scope of the sixth factor with the second factor ensures that the strength of a mark is evaluated against the actual marketplace reality. For the Federal Circuit, the question is not just whether the services are similar enough to confuse, but whether the Board’s definition of that similarity holds steady throughout the entire likelihood-of-confusion inquiry. As the case returns to the Board, the outcome will depend on whether the broader inclusion of third-party marks in the banking sector dilutes the distinctiveness of the ASPIRE mark enough to allow Apex’s registration to proceed.
