Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Interdependence of DuPont Factors: Why a Remand on Mark Strength Necessitated Reconsideration of Commercial Impression

In trademark law, the likelihood-of-confusion analysis is rarely a mechanical exercise. It is a holistic inquiry where the interplay between various factors often determines the outcome more than any single element in isolation. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, decided September 25, 2025, the Federal Circuit reinforced this principle by vacating the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s analysis of two distinct DuPont factors based on their legal interdependence. While the court affirmed the Board’s finding that the parties’ services were highly similar, it held that the Board’s narrow definition of the relevant market for third-party marks rendered its assessment of mark strength—and consequently, the commercial impression of the marks—legally flawed. This decision serves as a crucial reminder to the TTAB that consistency in defining the relevant universe of goods and services is essential across all factors of the likelihood-of-confusion test.

The dispute arose when Apex Bank sought to register the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for banking and financing services. CC Serve Corp., which owns a registration for the ASPIRE mark in connection with credit card services, opposed the application, arguing a likelihood of confusion under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act. The Board sustained the opposition, relying heavily on the similarity of the services and the strength of the ASPIRE mark. On appeal, Apex Bank challenged the Board’s application of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. Circuit Judge Hughes, writing for the panel, affirmed the Board’s conclusion regarding the second factor, which assesses the similarity of the parties’ goods or services. The court found substantial evidence supporting the Board’s determination that credit card services and general banking services are highly similar, noting that dictionary definitions and third-party registrations supported the view that these services could emanate from a single source.

However, the Federal Circuit identified a critical inconsistency in how the Board applied the concept of similarity when moving from Factor 2 to Factor 6. Factor 6 requires an analysis of the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods. While the Board had broadly defined the services as highly similar under Factor 2 to support a finding of confusion, it narrowed the scope of its inquiry under Factor 6 to only those third-party marks used specifically on credit card services. The Board identified nine Aspire-formative marks in that narrow category and deemed this insufficient to show that CC Serve’s mark was weak. Apex Bank argued that the Board should have considered 42 additional third-party marks using Aspire in the broader financial services industry. The Federal Circuit agreed with Apex, holding that the Board erred by imposing a more stringent legal standard for similarity under Factor 6 than it had applied under Factor 2. If the services are legally identical or highly similar enough to weigh heavily in favor of confusion under Factor 2, the court reasoned, the Board must consider third-party marks on those same similar goods when evaluating mark strength under Factor 6.

This ruling on Factor 6 had immediate downstream consequences for Factor 1, which evaluates the similarity of the marks in their entireties regarding appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. The Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s analysis of Factor 1 not because the Board erred in its initial comparison of the marks, but because the determination of mark strength directly influences the commercial impression analysis. If the scope of third-party marks is expanded on remand, the evidence may demonstrate that the ASPIRE mark is weaker than the Board originally concluded. A weaker mark is entitled to a narrower scope of protection, which alters the overall commercial impression of the competing marks. By vacating Factor 1 alongside Factor 6, the court underscored that the DuPont factors are not independent silos. A change in the factual landscape regarding mark strength necessitates a fresh look at how consumers perceive the similarity of the marks in question.

The doctrinal implications of *Apex Bank* extend beyond the specific facts of credit card versus banking services. The decision places a constraint on the TTAB’s discretion to define the relevant market differently for different factors within the same likelihood-of-confusion analysis. Practitioners can now point to this opinion to challenge Board decisions that find goods highly similar for the purpose of establishing proximity but then ignore third-party uses on those same goods when assessing mark strength. This consistency requirement prevents the Board from cherry-picking definitions of similarity to reach a predetermined outcome. Furthermore, the ruling reinforces the holistic nature of the DuPont test, reminding adjudicators that the commercial impression of a mark is dynamic and dependent on the strength of that mark within the relevant marketplace. As the case proceeds on remand, the outcome may hinge on whether the inclusion of the broader set of financial services marks sufficiently weakens the ASPIRE mark to distinguish it from Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK brand.
