Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 2: # Legal Standards and Review Framework

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit reviewed a decision by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (Board) regarding the registration of the word and design marks ASPIRE BANK. To understand the court’s analysis, a law student must first grasp the statutory basis for the Board’s authority, the substantive test used to determine trademark infringement, and the procedural standards governing appellate review. Section II of the opinion establishes these foundational legal standards, distinguishing between questions of law and questions of fact, and defining the evidentiary threshold required to sustain a trademark opposition. This chapter outlines the statutory framework of the Lanham Act, the multi-factor test for likelihood of confusion, and the specific standard of review applied by the Federal Circuit when evaluating Board decisions.

## Lanham Act Section 2(d) and Likelihood of Confusion

The substantive legal basis for the dispute in *Apex Bank* is Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d). As the Federal Circuit explains, this statute governs trademark oppositions and requires the refusal of registration when "confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods." In this case, CC Serve initiated an opposition against Apex’s intent-to-use applications, alleging that Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK marks were confusingly similar to CC Serve’s registered ASPIRE mark for credit card services. The Board sustained this opposition, concluding that consumer confusion was likely. Consequently, the central legal question for the Board, and subsequently the court, was whether the evidence supported a finding of likelihood of confusion under Section 2(d). The statute does not require proof of actual confusion; rather, it mandates a predictive judgment about whether consumers are likely to be confused regarding the source of the goods or services.

## The DuPont Factors Framework (13 Factors)

To determine whether likelihood of confusion exists, the Board and the Federal Circuit apply a multi-factor test established in *In re E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.*, 476 F.2d 1357 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The opinion notes that likelihood of confusion is a question of law, but it is based on findings of relevant underlying facts, specifically findings under the thirteen *DuPont* factors. Each of these factors presents a question of fact. While the specific facts of *Apex Bank* involved the Board’s analysis of the first, second, and sixth factors, the framework encompasses a broader inquiry into the nature of the marks, the channels of trade, the sophistication of consumers, and other relevant circumstances. The court emphasizes that the ultimate determination of likelihood of confusion is a legal conclusion drawn from these factual findings. Therefore, understanding the *DuPont* framework is essential to understanding how the Board weighed the evidence and how the court evaluates the Board’s conclusions.

## Standard of Review: De Novo for Law, Substantial Evidence for Facts

The Federal Circuit applies a bifurcated standard of review when hearing appeals from the Board, a distinction critical to understanding the court’s disposition in *Apex Bank*. The court reviews the Board’s legal conclusions *de novo*, meaning the court gives no deference to the Board’s interpretation of the law. Conversely, the court reviews the Board’s findings of fact for substantial evidence. The opinion defines substantial evidence as "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." This standard is not a free pass for the Board; the evidence must be sufficient to support the factual findings that underpin the legal conclusion of likelihood of confusion. In *Apex Bank*, the court applied this standard to the Board’s factual determinations regarding the similarity of services and the number of similar marks in use. If the Board’s legal application of the *DuPont* factors was erroneous, the court would correct it *de novo*, but if the factual findings were supported by substantial evidence, the court would generally uphold them unless the legal conclusion drawn from those facts was incorrect.

## Relevance of DuPont Factors: Not All Factors Must Be Considered

A common misconception in trademark law is that a tribunal must mechanically consider all thirteen *DuPont* factors in every case. The opinion in *Apex Bank* clarifies that the Board need not consider every factor, only those "that are relevant and of record." This flexibility allows the Board to focus its analysis on the factors that are dispositive in a specific context. For instance, in *Apex Bank*, the appeal focused on the first, second, and sixth factors because those were the specific areas where the Board’s analysis was challenged. The court acknowledges that while each factor presents a question of fact, the weight given to each factor depends on the specific circumstances of the case. This principle ensures that the likelihood of confusion analysis remains a holistic inquiry rather than a rigid checklist, allowing the Board to tailor its findings to the evidence presented in the opposition record.

## Burden of Proof in Trademark Oppositions

While the opinion focuses on the standard of review, the procedural posture of the case illuminates the burden inherent in a trademark opposition. In *Apex Bank*, CC Serve initiated the opposition, and the Board sustained it under Section 2(d). This procedural history indicates that the Board must find that the evidence presented in the opposition record supports a conclusion that confusion is likely before it can refuse registration. The Board’s duty is to evaluate whether the concurrent use of the marks on respective goods creates a likelihood of confusion. In this context, the Board’s decision to sustain the opposition implies that the evidence met the statutory threshold for refusal. On appeal, the Federal Circuit examines whether that determination was supported by substantial evidence and whether the Board applied the correct legal standards in making that determination. Thus, the burden in this framework rests on the Board to ensure its findings of fact are adequate to support the legal conclusion that Section 2(d) requires a refusal of registration.
