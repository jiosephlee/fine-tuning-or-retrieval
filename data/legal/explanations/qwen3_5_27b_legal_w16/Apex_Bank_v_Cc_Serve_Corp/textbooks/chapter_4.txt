Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 4: # Vacated Analysis: Third-Party Marks and Commercial Strength (DuPont Factors 6 and 1)

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit addressed a critical procedural and legal error made by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (Board) regarding the scope of third-party mark evidence. While the Board correctly analyzed the similarity of the parties' services under the second DuPont factor, it committed a legal error in its application of the sixth DuPont factor concerning the number and nature of similar marks in use. This error necessitated a vacatur of the Board’s findings on both the sixth and first DuPont factors. This chapter examines the legal principles governing the assessment of mark strength through third-party use, the inconsistency in the Board’s definition of similarity across different factors, and the consequential impact on the overall likelihood of confusion analysis.

## Purpose of the Sixth DuPont Factor: Assessing Mark Strength via Third-Party Use

The sixth DuPont factor requires the Board to consider the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods or services. As explained in the opinion, this factor serves a specific evidentiary purpose in the likelihood of confusion analysis: it helps determine the commercial strength of the prior user’s mark. Citing precedent such as *Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772*, the opinion clarifies that evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection. The underlying theory is that when a field is crowded with similar marks, consumers become more adept at distinguishing between them. Consequently, they are less likely to be confused by the similarity between two specific marks. As noted in *Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC*, evidence that consumers have been educated to distinguish between marks in this way tends to indicate a lack of commercial strength. Conversely, if few similar marks exist, the prior user’s mark may be considered strong, warranting broader protection against confusion.

## Board's Error: Limiting Scope to 'Credit Card Services' Only

In the proceedings below, Apex submitted exhibits showing third-party uses of marks including the word “Aspire” in connection with credit card-related services and, more broadly, the financial services industry. The Board, however, narrowed the scope of its analysis significantly. It defined the relevant public strictly as “consumers of ‘credit card services’” and focused solely on marks identified for those specific services. The Board deemed the forty-two other third-party marks using “Aspire” for other banking and financing services to be “essentially irrelevant.” The Board acknowledged that there were nine “Aspire-formative marks” for credit card services but concluded that this number did not rise to the level of “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use required to demonstrate weakness. By excluding the broader category of financial service marks, the Board effectively insulated CC Serve’s mark from a more comprehensive assessment of its distinctiveness in the marketplace. The Federal Circuit identified this exclusion as a legal flaw, noting that the Board improperly restricted the universe of marks it considered.

## Inconsistency with Factor 2 Finding of High Service Similarity

The core of the Federal Circuit’s reasoning rests on the inconsistency between the Board’s analysis of the second DuPont factor and its analysis of the sixth DuPont factor. Under the second factor, which assesses the similarity of the parties' goods and services, the Board had already determined that CC Serve’s credit card services and Apex’s banking and financing services were highly similar, to the point of being “legally identical, in part.” The Board relied on this high degree of similarity to conclude that the second factor weighed heavily in favor of a likelihood of confusion. However, when analyzing the sixth factor, the Board applied a narrower definition of similarity, restricting its review to only credit card services. The Federal Circuit held that the sixth DuPont factor does not require identical goods, only similar ones. Because the Board had already made a factual finding that the services were highly similar for the purpose of the second factor, it should have retained that same scope when considering the similarity of third-party marks under the sixth factor. The court reasoned there was no justification for imposing a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth factor than the one applied under the second factor.

## Impact on Commercial Impression and the First DuPont Factor

The error in the sixth DuPont factor analysis had a direct ripple effect on the first DuPont factor, which considers the similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. The opinion explains that the commercial impression of a mark is informed by its commercial strength. If the sixth factor analysis changes—for instance, if more third-party marks are considered, potentially weakening the prior user’s mark—this can alter the overall commercial impression of the marks at issue. Because the Board’s finding on the sixth factor was legally flawed, the resulting determination regarding the mark’s commercial strength was unreliable. Consequently, the Board’s analysis of the first factor, which relied on that assessment of strength and impression, was also compromised. The Federal Circuit noted that reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor might result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength, which would in turn affect the overall commercial impression required for the first factor analysis.

## Reasoning for Vacating Factor 6 and Remanding Factor 1

Based on these legal errors, the Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s findings with respect to the sixth DuPont factor. The court ordered a remand for the Board to reconsider the appropriate scope of third-party marks eligible for consideration, specifically in view of the factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar. The Board was instructed to consider the number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods without the improper restriction to credit card services alone. Furthermore, the court vacated the Board’s analysis of the first DuPont factor and remanded it for reconsideration. On remand, the Board must evaluate the appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression of the marks in light of the corrected scope of third-party evidence. This disposition ensures that the likelihood of confusion analysis is grounded in a consistent legal standard regarding service similarity and accurately reflects the commercial reality of the marketplace. The court affirmed the Board’s analysis only as to the second DuPont factor, where it found no error in the legal analysis and sufficient substantial evidence to support the finding of high service similarity.
