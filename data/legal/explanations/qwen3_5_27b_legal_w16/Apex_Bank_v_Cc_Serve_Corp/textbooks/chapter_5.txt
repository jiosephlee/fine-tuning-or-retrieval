Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 5: # Final Disposition and Significance

## Judgment: Affirmed-in-Part, Vacated-in-Part, and Remanded

The Federal Circuit concluded its review of the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s decision with a split disposition, illustrating how appellate courts handle mixed errors in administrative fact-finding and legal analysis. The court affirmed the Board’s determination regarding the second DuPont factor, which assessed the similarity of the parties’ services. Because substantial evidence supported the Board’s finding that Apex Bank’s banking services and CC Serve’s credit card services were highly similar, the court found no error in this portion of the decision. However, the court vacated the Board’s findings regarding the first and sixth DuPont factors. A vacatur means the court is setting aside the Board’s specific conclusions on those factors due to legal error. Consequently, the case was remanded to the Board. A remand instructs the lower tribunal to reconsider the case in light of the appellate court’s legal guidance. In this context, the Board must re-evaluate the likelihood of confusion, specifically ensuring its analysis of the marks’ similarity and third-party usage aligns with its established finding that the underlying services are highly similar.

## Remand Instructions: Reconsider Scope of Third-Party Marks

The core instruction for the Board on remand concerns the scope of third-party marks to be considered under the sixth DuPont factor. The Federal Circuit identified a specific legal flaw in the Board’s original analysis: the Board had defined the relevant universe of third-party marks too narrowly. While the Board found under the second factor that the parties’ services were broadly similar (encompassing banking and financing), it restricted the sixth factor analysis to marks used only on "credit card services." The court instructed the Board to expand this scope on remand. The Board must consider third-party marks used on similar goods and services, which, consistent with the second factor analysis, includes the broader category of banking and financing services. This instruction ensures that the evidence regarding market crowding and consumer education reflects the actual marketplace in which the parties operate, rather than an artificially limited subset of that market.

## Allocation of Costs to Appellant

In addition to the merits of the trademark dispute, the court addressed the allocation of litigation costs. The opinion concludes with a specific order: "Costs to appellant." This procedural disposition means that Apex Bank, as the appellant, is responsible for the costs associated with the appeal. In trademark appeals, the award of costs is often discretionary, but it generally follows the outcome of the litigation. Although Apex Bank succeeded in vacating the Board’s findings on two factors, the court did not reverse the likelihood of confusion determination outright. Because the appeal did not result in a complete victory for Apex—the opposition remains sustained pending the remand proceedings—the court assigned the costs to the appellant. This highlights that a partial procedural victory does not automatically shift the financial burden of the appeal to the prevailing party on the ultimate issue of registration.

## Significance for Defining 'Similar Goods' Across DuPont Factors

This opinion establishes a significant principle regarding the consistency required in defining "similarity" across the various DuPont factors. The Federal Circuit emphasized that the Board cannot apply a broad standard for similarity when analyzing the relationship between the parties’ goods or services (Factor 2) and then apply a narrow standard when analyzing third-party marks (Factor 6). The court reasoned that if the Board determines services are highly similar or legally identical for the purpose of the second factor, it must retain that same scope of similarity when evaluating the relevant public and third-party uses under the sixth factor. This prevents a logical inconsistency where the Board finds the parties are in the same market for the purpose of finding confusion, but then excludes competitors from that same market when assessing the strength of the senior mark. Future trademark opposition proceedings must ensure that the definition of the relevant goods and services remains uniform throughout the likelihood of confusion analysis.

## Interplay Between Service Similarity and Mark Strength Analysis

Finally, the decision underscores the interdependence of the DuPont factors, specifically the relationship between service similarity, mark strength, and commercial impression. The court vacated the first DuPont factor (similarity of the marks) not because of a direct error in the mark comparison itself, but because the error in the sixth factor (third-party marks) affected the analysis of mark strength. The presence of numerous third-party marks can weaken a senior mark, which in turn influences the overall commercial impression and the likelihood of confusion under the first factor. Because the Board’s flawed scope of third-party marks led to an incorrect assessment of CC Serve’s mark strength, the court determined that the analysis of the marks’ overall commercial impression was also compromised. This teaches that the DuPont factors are not isolated silos; an error in assessing market conditions or mark strength can ripple through the analysis, necessitating a remand of related factors to ensure a coherent likelihood of confusion determination.
