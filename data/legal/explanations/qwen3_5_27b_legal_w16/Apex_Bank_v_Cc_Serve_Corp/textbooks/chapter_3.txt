Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 3: # Affirmed Analysis: Similarity of Services (DuPont Factor 2)

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit reviewed a decision by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (Board) regarding a likelihood-of-confusion opposition. While the Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s analysis regarding the strength of the mark and the similarity of the marks themselves, the court affirmed the Board’s finding on the second *DuPont* factor: the similarity of the parties’ goods and services. This section of the opinion provides a critical lesson in how appellate courts review factual findings in trademark cases and how the Board defines the scope of services when determining the likelihood of confusion. Understanding this affirmed portion is essential because it established the baseline relationship between the parties’ commercial offerings, which influenced the court’s decision to remand other factors for reconsideration.

## Board's Definition of 'Credit Card Services' and 'Banking Services'

The threshold issue in analyzing the second *DuPont* factor is accurately defining the goods or services at issue. In this case, the Board faced a complication because the specific entry for “credit card services” had been deleted from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration was issued. Consequently, the Board could not rely solely on the standard classification and instead had to determine the meaning of the identified services based on the evidence. The Board defined CC Serve’s “credit card services” broadly, encompassing the issuance of credit cards for financing purchases, counseling regarding credit card debt, processing payments and transactions, authorization, monitoring, managing accounts, and providing access to credit scores.

Conversely, Apex Bank sought registration for “banking and financing services.” The Board noted that the legal standard for this factor does not require the services to be identical. As the court cites from *Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC*, the evidence need only establish that the products are related in some manner or that the circumstances surrounding their marketing could give rise to a mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source. By defining both sets of services expansively, the Board set the stage to compare the functional overlap between CC Serve’s credit card management and Apex’s proposed banking operations. This definitional step is crucial for law students to understand, as the scope of the goods or services often dictates the strength of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis.

## Finding of 'Legally Identical, in Part' Services

After defining the services, the Board concluded that the parties’ services were “legally identical, in part.” This finding relied heavily on dictionary definitions for terms such as “banking,” “bank,” and “finance.” The Board reasoned that these definitions encompass extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards. Because Apex’s proposed “banking and financing services” inherently included the types of activities CC Serve was already performing under its ASPIRE mark, the Board found a high degree of similarity.

To support this conclusion, the Board also examined third-party registrations. The Board identified registrations that covered both credit card services and banking and financing services, using this evidence to demonstrate that the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark. This finding was significant because it established that consumers might reasonably expect a single entity to provide both banking and credit card services. The Board ultimately determined that this high degree of similarity weighed heavily in favor of finding a likelihood of confusion. This specific phrasing, “legally identical, in part,” highlights the Board’s willingness to find overlap based on the functional nature of the services rather than requiring them to be marketed in the exact same channel.

## Apex's Argument: Services Are Not Directly Competitive

On appeal, Apex Bank challenged the Board’s conclusion regarding the similarity of services. Apex argued that the parties’ services were not the same and were not directly competitive with each other. A central component of Apex’s argument focused on the operational distinction between the two companies. Apex pointed out that CC Serve does not provide banking services itself; rather, CC Serve joins with a bank, and that bank issues the ASPIRE-branded credit cards. Apex contended that because CC Serve is a servicer partnering with banks, its services are distinct from Apex’s actual banking services.

This argument attempts to narrow the scope of the second *DuPont* factor by emphasizing the competitive relationship between the parties. In trademark law, if services are not competitive, the likelihood of confusion may be reduced. Apex sought to persuade the Federal Circuit that the Board erred by ignoring this structural difference. However, the Board’s analysis had already accounted for the nature of CC Serve’s services by defining them as “credit card services” which included managing accounts and processing transactions, activities that overlap significantly with the proposed banking services. Apex’s appeal thus required the Federal Circuit to determine whether the Board’s broader definition was supported by the record.

## Court's Application of Substantial Evidence Standard

The Federal Circuit’s review of the Board’s factual findings is governed by the substantial evidence standard. As outlined in the opinion’s standard of review section, the court reviews the Board’s findings of fact to determine if they are supported by substantial evidence, defined as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” The court does not reweigh the evidence de novo but rather ensures the Board’s conclusion was reasonable based on the record.

Applying this standard to the second *DuPont* factor, the Federal Circuit examined whether the Board carefully considered the descriptions of each party’s services. The court found that the Board had indeed done so and that substantial evidence supported the finding that the services were highly similar. The court rejected Apex’s argument that the services were not competitive, noting that the Board’s definition of “credit card services” and “banking services” was consistent with the evidence presented. This section of the opinion teaches students that appellate courts afford deference to the Board’s factual determinations regarding the nature of goods and services, provided those determinations are grounded in the record. The court found no legal error in the Board’s analysis, which is a prerequisite for affirmance.

## Affirmance of the Board's Finding on Service Similarity

Ultimately, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding as to the second *DuPont* factor. The court concluded that the Board did not err in its legal analysis and that substantial evidence supported the finding that the parties’ services are highly similar. This affirmance was a pivotal part of the disposition, as it established that the services were related enough to support a likelihood of confusion, even though the court vacated the findings on the strength of the mark and the similarity of the marks themselves.

By affirming this factor, the court acknowledged that the overlap between credit card services and banking services was significant enough to weigh heavily in favor of confusion. This decision contrasts with the court’s treatment of the sixth *DuPont* factor, where the court vacated the Board’s decision for being too narrow in defining the relevant universe of third-party marks. The affirmation of Factor 2 demonstrates that while the Board may err in the scope of evidence considered for mark strength, its factual findings on the nature of the services themselves can remain intact if supported by substantial evidence. For the law student, this highlights the importance of distinguishing between factual findings, which are reviewed deferentially, and legal conclusions or the scope of evidence, which may be reviewed more strictly.
