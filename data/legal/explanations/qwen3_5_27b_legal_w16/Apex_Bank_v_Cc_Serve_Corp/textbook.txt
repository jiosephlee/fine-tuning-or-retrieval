Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Factual and Procedural Background

This chapter outlines the factual circumstances and procedural history leading to the Federal Circuit’s decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* Understanding the parties, the specific marks involved, and the administrative pathway from the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) to the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) is essential for analyzing the court’s likelihood-of-confusion analysis. The dispute centers on whether Apex Bank could register its proposed marks given CC Serve Corp.’s prior registration of a similar mark.

## Parties: Apex Bank (Appellant) and CC Serve Corp. (Opposer)

The litigation involves two distinct entities operating within the financial services sector. CC Serve Corp., the opposer in the administrative proceedings and appellee in this appeal, is a company that offers credit card services to customers. It is the owner of a registered trademark for the word mark ASPIRE, which was issued in 1998 with an effective priority date of October 17, 1996. Apex Bank, the appellant, is a retail bank chartered in Tennessee with eighteen branch locations. While Apex currently offers personal and business checking and savings accounts, home mortgages, and consumer and business loans, the record indicates that it does not currently offer credit cards. Apex initiated the trademark application process in anticipation of offering an internet bank under a different brand, making it the party seeking registration and subsequently appealing the Board’s refusal.

## Trademarks: ASPIRE BANK (Apex) vs. ASPIRE (CC Serve)

The core of the dispute concerns the similarity between two word marks. CC Serve holds Registration No. 2126948 for the standard character mark ASPIRE. Apex filed intent-to-use applications to register two marks: the ASPIRE BANK word mark and the ASPIRE BANK design mark. Apex sought registration for these marks under the designation of “[b]anking and financing services.” The similarity between the word “ASPIRE” in CC Serve’s mark and the phrase “ASPIRE BANK” in Apex’s proposed marks formed the basis for CC Serve’s claim that consumer confusion was likely. The procedural history confirms that CC Serve’s mark was a prior registered mark, while Apex’s marks were applied for in August 2019 and subsequently published for opposition.

## Services: Banking/Financing vs. Credit Card Services

The nature of the services offered by each party is a critical factual component of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis. CC Serve’s services are described as credit card services, which involve joining with a bank to issue ASPIRE-branded credit cards and associated accounts to customers, with CC Serve servicing the accounts. In contrast, Apex Bank’s existing services include checking, savings, mortgages, and loans, but explicitly exclude credit cards. However, Apex’s intent-to-use applications were for “banking and financing services.” The Board later determined that the dictionary definitions for banking and finance encompass extending credit through credit cards, leading to a finding that the services were “legally identical, in part.” This factual overlap regarding the scope of services provided the context for the Board’s initial decision and the Federal Circuit’s subsequent review.

## USPTO Prosecution and CC Serve's Letter of Protest

The administrative process began when Apex filed its intent-to-use applications with the USPTO in August 2019. During the prosecution of these applications, CC Serve submitted a letter of protest asserting that Apex’s proposed marks were confusingly similar to its own registered mark. Despite this protest, the examining attorney approved the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for publication. The marks were published in the Official Gazette on December 17, 2019. This procedural step is significant because it demonstrates that the USPTO examiner did not find the protest sufficient to refuse registration at the examination stage, prompting CC Serve to escalate the challenge through a formal opposition proceeding.

## TTAB Opposition Proceedings and Sustained Opposition

Following publication, CC Serve initiated an opposition against Apex’s marks in February 2020. The opposition was filed under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), which requires the refusal of registration when confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user. The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) reviewed the evidence and sustained the opposition. The Board concluded that consumer confusion between the marks was likely, thereby refusing registration of Apex’s marks. This decision by the Board constituted the final administrative action from which Apex sought judicial review.

## Appeal to the Federal Circuit and Jurisdiction

Dissatisfied with the TTAB’s decision, Apex Bank appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The Federal Circuit exercised jurisdiction over this appeal pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B), which grants the court exclusive jurisdiction over appeals from final decisions of the TTAB. In reviewing the Board’s decision, the Federal Circuit applies a specific standard of review: legal conclusions are reviewed de novo, while findings of fact are reviewed for substantial evidence. Substantial evidence is defined as such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion. This procedural framework guided the court’s analysis of whether the Board correctly applied the likelihood-of-confusion factors to the facts presented by the parties.

# Legal Standards and Review Framework

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit reviewed a decision by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (Board) regarding the registration of the word and design marks ASPIRE BANK. To understand the court’s analysis, a law student must first grasp the statutory basis for the Board’s authority, the substantive test used to determine trademark infringement, and the procedural standards governing appellate review. Section II of the opinion establishes these foundational legal standards, distinguishing between questions of law and questions of fact, and defining the evidentiary threshold required to sustain a trademark opposition. This chapter outlines the statutory framework of the Lanham Act, the multi-factor test for likelihood of confusion, and the specific standard of review applied by the Federal Circuit when evaluating Board decisions.

## Lanham Act Section 2(d) and Likelihood of Confusion

The substantive legal basis for the dispute in *Apex Bank* is Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d). As the Federal Circuit explains, this statute governs trademark oppositions and requires the refusal of registration when "confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods." In this case, CC Serve initiated an opposition against Apex’s intent-to-use applications, alleging that Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK marks were confusingly similar to CC Serve’s registered ASPIRE mark for credit card services. The Board sustained this opposition, concluding that consumer confusion was likely. Consequently, the central legal question for the Board, and subsequently the court, was whether the evidence supported a finding of likelihood of confusion under Section 2(d). The statute does not require proof of actual confusion; rather, it mandates a predictive judgment about whether consumers are likely to be confused regarding the source of the goods or services.

## The DuPont Factors Framework (13 Factors)

To determine whether likelihood of confusion exists, the Board and the Federal Circuit apply a multi-factor test established in *In re E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co.*, 476 F.2d 1357 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The opinion notes that likelihood of confusion is a question of law, but it is based on findings of relevant underlying facts, specifically findings under the thirteen *DuPont* factors. Each of these factors presents a question of fact. While the specific facts of *Apex Bank* involved the Board’s analysis of the first, second, and sixth factors, the framework encompasses a broader inquiry into the nature of the marks, the channels of trade, the sophistication of consumers, and other relevant circumstances. The court emphasizes that the ultimate determination of likelihood of confusion is a legal conclusion drawn from these factual findings. Therefore, understanding the *DuPont* framework is essential to understanding how the Board weighed the evidence and how the court evaluates the Board’s conclusions.

## Standard of Review: De Novo for Law, Substantial Evidence for Facts

The Federal Circuit applies a bifurcated standard of review when hearing appeals from the Board, a distinction critical to understanding the court’s disposition in *Apex Bank*. The court reviews the Board’s legal conclusions *de novo*, meaning the court gives no deference to the Board’s interpretation of the law. Conversely, the court reviews the Board’s findings of fact for substantial evidence. The opinion defines substantial evidence as "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." This standard is not a free pass for the Board; the evidence must be sufficient to support the factual findings that underpin the legal conclusion of likelihood of confusion. In *Apex Bank*, the court applied this standard to the Board’s factual determinations regarding the similarity of services and the number of similar marks in use. If the Board’s legal application of the *DuPont* factors was erroneous, the court would correct it *de novo*, but if the factual findings were supported by substantial evidence, the court would generally uphold them unless the legal conclusion drawn from those facts was incorrect.

## Relevance of DuPont Factors: Not All Factors Must Be Considered

A common misconception in trademark law is that a tribunal must mechanically consider all thirteen *DuPont* factors in every case. The opinion in *Apex Bank* clarifies that the Board need not consider every factor, only those "that are relevant and of record." This flexibility allows the Board to focus its analysis on the factors that are dispositive in a specific context. For instance, in *Apex Bank*, the appeal focused on the first, second, and sixth factors because those were the specific areas where the Board’s analysis was challenged. The court acknowledges that while each factor presents a question of fact, the weight given to each factor depends on the specific circumstances of the case. This principle ensures that the likelihood of confusion analysis remains a holistic inquiry rather than a rigid checklist, allowing the Board to tailor its findings to the evidence presented in the opposition record.

## Burden of Proof in Trademark Oppositions

While the opinion focuses on the standard of review, the procedural posture of the case illuminates the burden inherent in a trademark opposition. In *Apex Bank*, CC Serve initiated the opposition, and the Board sustained it under Section 2(d). This procedural history indicates that the Board must find that the evidence presented in the opposition record supports a conclusion that confusion is likely before it can refuse registration. The Board’s duty is to evaluate whether the concurrent use of the marks on respective goods creates a likelihood of confusion. In this context, the Board’s decision to sustain the opposition implies that the evidence met the statutory threshold for refusal. On appeal, the Federal Circuit examines whether that determination was supported by substantial evidence and whether the Board applied the correct legal standards in making that determination. Thus, the burden in this framework rests on the Board to ensure its findings of fact are adequate to support the legal conclusion that Section 2(d) requires a refusal of registration.

# Affirmed Analysis: Similarity of Services (DuPont Factor 2)

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit reviewed a decision by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (Board) regarding a likelihood-of-confusion opposition. While the Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s analysis regarding the strength of the mark and the similarity of the marks themselves, the court affirmed the Board’s finding on the second *DuPont* factor: the similarity of the parties’ goods and services. This section of the opinion provides a critical lesson in how appellate courts review factual findings in trademark cases and how the Board defines the scope of services when determining the likelihood of confusion. Understanding this affirmed portion is essential because it established the baseline relationship between the parties’ commercial offerings, which influenced the court’s decision to remand other factors for reconsideration.

## Board's Definition of 'Credit Card Services' and 'Banking Services'

The threshold issue in analyzing the second *DuPont* factor is accurately defining the goods or services at issue. In this case, the Board faced a complication because the specific entry for “credit card services” had been deleted from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration was issued. Consequently, the Board could not rely solely on the standard classification and instead had to determine the meaning of the identified services based on the evidence. The Board defined CC Serve’s “credit card services” broadly, encompassing the issuance of credit cards for financing purchases, counseling regarding credit card debt, processing payments and transactions, authorization, monitoring, managing accounts, and providing access to credit scores.

Conversely, Apex Bank sought registration for “banking and financing services.” The Board noted that the legal standard for this factor does not require the services to be identical. As the court cites from *Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC*, the evidence need only establish that the products are related in some manner or that the circumstances surrounding their marketing could give rise to a mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source. By defining both sets of services expansively, the Board set the stage to compare the functional overlap between CC Serve’s credit card management and Apex’s proposed banking operations. This definitional step is crucial for law students to understand, as the scope of the goods or services often dictates the strength of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis.

## Finding of 'Legally Identical, in Part' Services

After defining the services, the Board concluded that the parties’ services were “legally identical, in part.” This finding relied heavily on dictionary definitions for terms such as “banking,” “bank,” and “finance.” The Board reasoned that these definitions encompass extending credit or providing funds through the issuance of credit cards. Because Apex’s proposed “banking and financing services” inherently included the types of activities CC Serve was already performing under its ASPIRE mark, the Board found a high degree of similarity.

To support this conclusion, the Board also examined third-party registrations. The Board identified registrations that covered both credit card services and banking and financing services, using this evidence to demonstrate that the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark. This finding was significant because it established that consumers might reasonably expect a single entity to provide both banking and credit card services. The Board ultimately determined that this high degree of similarity weighed heavily in favor of finding a likelihood of confusion. This specific phrasing, “legally identical, in part,” highlights the Board’s willingness to find overlap based on the functional nature of the services rather than requiring them to be marketed in the exact same channel.

## Apex's Argument: Services Are Not Directly Competitive

On appeal, Apex Bank challenged the Board’s conclusion regarding the similarity of services. Apex argued that the parties’ services were not the same and were not directly competitive with each other. A central component of Apex’s argument focused on the operational distinction between the two companies. Apex pointed out that CC Serve does not provide banking services itself; rather, CC Serve joins with a bank, and that bank issues the ASPIRE-branded credit cards. Apex contended that because CC Serve is a servicer partnering with banks, its services are distinct from Apex’s actual banking services.

This argument attempts to narrow the scope of the second *DuPont* factor by emphasizing the competitive relationship between the parties. In trademark law, if services are not competitive, the likelihood of confusion may be reduced. Apex sought to persuade the Federal Circuit that the Board erred by ignoring this structural difference. However, the Board’s analysis had already accounted for the nature of CC Serve’s services by defining them as “credit card services” which included managing accounts and processing transactions, activities that overlap significantly with the proposed banking services. Apex’s appeal thus required the Federal Circuit to determine whether the Board’s broader definition was supported by the record.

## Court's Application of Substantial Evidence Standard

The Federal Circuit’s review of the Board’s factual findings is governed by the substantial evidence standard. As outlined in the opinion’s standard of review section, the court reviews the Board’s findings of fact to determine if they are supported by substantial evidence, defined as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” The court does not reweigh the evidence de novo but rather ensures the Board’s conclusion was reasonable based on the record.

Applying this standard to the second *DuPont* factor, the Federal Circuit examined whether the Board carefully considered the descriptions of each party’s services. The court found that the Board had indeed done so and that substantial evidence supported the finding that the services were highly similar. The court rejected Apex’s argument that the services were not competitive, noting that the Board’s definition of “credit card services” and “banking services” was consistent with the evidence presented. This section of the opinion teaches students that appellate courts afford deference to the Board’s factual determinations regarding the nature of goods and services, provided those determinations are grounded in the record. The court found no legal error in the Board’s analysis, which is a prerequisite for affirmance.

## Affirmance of the Board's Finding on Service Similarity

Ultimately, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding as to the second *DuPont* factor. The court concluded that the Board did not err in its legal analysis and that substantial evidence supported the finding that the parties’ services are highly similar. This affirmance was a pivotal part of the disposition, as it established that the services were related enough to support a likelihood of confusion, even though the court vacated the findings on the strength of the mark and the similarity of the marks themselves.

By affirming this factor, the court acknowledged that the overlap between credit card services and banking services was significant enough to weigh heavily in favor of confusion. This decision contrasts with the court’s treatment of the sixth *DuPont* factor, where the court vacated the Board’s decision for being too narrow in defining the relevant universe of third-party marks. The affirmation of Factor 2 demonstrates that while the Board may err in the scope of evidence considered for mark strength, its factual findings on the nature of the services themselves can remain intact if supported by substantial evidence. For the law student, this highlights the importance of distinguishing between factual findings, which are reviewed deferentially, and legal conclusions or the scope of evidence, which may be reviewed more strictly.

# Vacated Analysis: Third-Party Marks and Commercial Strength (DuPont Factors 6 and 1)

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit addressed a critical procedural and legal error made by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (Board) regarding the scope of third-party mark evidence. While the Board correctly analyzed the similarity of the parties' services under the second DuPont factor, it committed a legal error in its application of the sixth DuPont factor concerning the number and nature of similar marks in use. This error necessitated a vacatur of the Board’s findings on both the sixth and first DuPont factors. This chapter examines the legal principles governing the assessment of mark strength through third-party use, the inconsistency in the Board’s definition of similarity across different factors, and the consequential impact on the overall likelihood of confusion analysis.

## Purpose of the Sixth DuPont Factor: Assessing Mark Strength via Third-Party Use

The sixth DuPont factor requires the Board to consider the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods or services. As explained in the opinion, this factor serves a specific evidentiary purpose in the likelihood of confusion analysis: it helps determine the commercial strength of the prior user’s mark. Citing precedent such as *Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772*, the opinion clarifies that evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection. The underlying theory is that when a field is crowded with similar marks, consumers become more adept at distinguishing between them. Consequently, they are less likely to be confused by the similarity between two specific marks. As noted in *Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC*, evidence that consumers have been educated to distinguish between marks in this way tends to indicate a lack of commercial strength. Conversely, if few similar marks exist, the prior user’s mark may be considered strong, warranting broader protection against confusion.

## Board's Error: Limiting Scope to 'Credit Card Services' Only

In the proceedings below, Apex submitted exhibits showing third-party uses of marks including the word “Aspire” in connection with credit card-related services and, more broadly, the financial services industry. The Board, however, narrowed the scope of its analysis significantly. It defined the relevant public strictly as “consumers of ‘credit card services’” and focused solely on marks identified for those specific services. The Board deemed the forty-two other third-party marks using “Aspire” for other banking and financing services to be “essentially irrelevant.” The Board acknowledged that there were nine “Aspire-formative marks” for credit card services but concluded that this number did not rise to the level of “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use required to demonstrate weakness. By excluding the broader category of financial service marks, the Board effectively insulated CC Serve’s mark from a more comprehensive assessment of its distinctiveness in the marketplace. The Federal Circuit identified this exclusion as a legal flaw, noting that the Board improperly restricted the universe of marks it considered.

## Inconsistency with Factor 2 Finding of High Service Similarity

The core of the Federal Circuit’s reasoning rests on the inconsistency between the Board’s analysis of the second DuPont factor and its analysis of the sixth DuPont factor. Under the second factor, which assesses the similarity of the parties' goods and services, the Board had already determined that CC Serve’s credit card services and Apex’s banking and financing services were highly similar, to the point of being “legally identical, in part.” The Board relied on this high degree of similarity to conclude that the second factor weighed heavily in favor of a likelihood of confusion. However, when analyzing the sixth factor, the Board applied a narrower definition of similarity, restricting its review to only credit card services. The Federal Circuit held that the sixth DuPont factor does not require identical goods, only similar ones. Because the Board had already made a factual finding that the services were highly similar for the purpose of the second factor, it should have retained that same scope when considering the similarity of third-party marks under the sixth factor. The court reasoned there was no justification for imposing a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth factor than the one applied under the second factor.

## Impact on Commercial Impression and the First DuPont Factor

The error in the sixth DuPont factor analysis had a direct ripple effect on the first DuPont factor, which considers the similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. The opinion explains that the commercial impression of a mark is informed by its commercial strength. If the sixth factor analysis changes—for instance, if more third-party marks are considered, potentially weakening the prior user’s mark—this can alter the overall commercial impression of the marks at issue. Because the Board’s finding on the sixth factor was legally flawed, the resulting determination regarding the mark’s commercial strength was unreliable. Consequently, the Board’s analysis of the first factor, which relied on that assessment of strength and impression, was also compromised. The Federal Circuit noted that reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor might result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength, which would in turn affect the overall commercial impression required for the first factor analysis.

## Reasoning for Vacating Factor 6 and Remanding Factor 1

Based on these legal errors, the Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s findings with respect to the sixth DuPont factor. The court ordered a remand for the Board to reconsider the appropriate scope of third-party marks eligible for consideration, specifically in view of the factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar. The Board was instructed to consider the number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods without the improper restriction to credit card services alone. Furthermore, the court vacated the Board’s analysis of the first DuPont factor and remanded it for reconsideration. On remand, the Board must evaluate the appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression of the marks in light of the corrected scope of third-party evidence. This disposition ensures that the likelihood of confusion analysis is grounded in a consistent legal standard regarding service similarity and accurately reflects the commercial reality of the marketplace. The court affirmed the Board’s analysis only as to the second DuPont factor, where it found no error in the legal analysis and sufficient substantial evidence to support the finding of high service similarity.

# Final Disposition and Significance

## Judgment: Affirmed-in-Part, Vacated-in-Part, and Remanded

The Federal Circuit concluded its review of the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s decision with a split disposition, illustrating how appellate courts handle mixed errors in administrative fact-finding and legal analysis. The court affirmed the Board’s determination regarding the second DuPont factor, which assessed the similarity of the parties’ services. Because substantial evidence supported the Board’s finding that Apex Bank’s banking services and CC Serve’s credit card services were highly similar, the court found no error in this portion of the decision. However, the court vacated the Board’s findings regarding the first and sixth DuPont factors. A vacatur means the court is setting aside the Board’s specific conclusions on those factors due to legal error. Consequently, the case was remanded to the Board. A remand instructs the lower tribunal to reconsider the case in light of the appellate court’s legal guidance. In this context, the Board must re-evaluate the likelihood of confusion, specifically ensuring its analysis of the marks’ similarity and third-party usage aligns with its established finding that the underlying services are highly similar.

## Remand Instructions: Reconsider Scope of Third-Party Marks

The core instruction for the Board on remand concerns the scope of third-party marks to be considered under the sixth DuPont factor. The Federal Circuit identified a specific legal flaw in the Board’s original analysis: the Board had defined the relevant universe of third-party marks too narrowly. While the Board found under the second factor that the parties’ services were broadly similar (encompassing banking and financing), it restricted the sixth factor analysis to marks used only on "credit card services." The court instructed the Board to expand this scope on remand. The Board must consider third-party marks used on similar goods and services, which, consistent with the second factor analysis, includes the broader category of banking and financing services. This instruction ensures that the evidence regarding market crowding and consumer education reflects the actual marketplace in which the parties operate, rather than an artificially limited subset of that market.

## Allocation of Costs to Appellant

In addition to the merits of the trademark dispute, the court addressed the allocation of litigation costs. The opinion concludes with a specific order: "Costs to appellant." This procedural disposition means that Apex Bank, as the appellant, is responsible for the costs associated with the appeal. In trademark appeals, the award of costs is often discretionary, but it generally follows the outcome of the litigation. Although Apex Bank succeeded in vacating the Board’s findings on two factors, the court did not reverse the likelihood of confusion determination outright. Because the appeal did not result in a complete victory for Apex—the opposition remains sustained pending the remand proceedings—the court assigned the costs to the appellant. This highlights that a partial procedural victory does not automatically shift the financial burden of the appeal to the prevailing party on the ultimate issue of registration.

## Significance for Defining 'Similar Goods' Across DuPont Factors

This opinion establishes a significant principle regarding the consistency required in defining "similarity" across the various DuPont factors. The Federal Circuit emphasized that the Board cannot apply a broad standard for similarity when analyzing the relationship between the parties’ goods or services (Factor 2) and then apply a narrow standard when analyzing third-party marks (Factor 6). The court reasoned that if the Board determines services are highly similar or legally identical for the purpose of the second factor, it must retain that same scope of similarity when evaluating the relevant public and third-party uses under the sixth factor. This prevents a logical inconsistency where the Board finds the parties are in the same market for the purpose of finding confusion, but then excludes competitors from that same market when assessing the strength of the senior mark. Future trademark opposition proceedings must ensure that the definition of the relevant goods and services remains uniform throughout the likelihood of confusion analysis.

## Interplay Between Service Similarity and Mark Strength Analysis

Finally, the decision underscores the interdependence of the DuPont factors, specifically the relationship between service similarity, mark strength, and commercial impression. The court vacated the first DuPont factor (similarity of the marks) not because of a direct error in the mark comparison itself, but because the error in the sixth factor (third-party marks) affected the analysis of mark strength. The presence of numerous third-party marks can weaken a senior mark, which in turn influences the overall commercial impression and the likelihood of confusion under the first factor. Because the Board’s flawed scope of third-party marks led to an incorrect assessment of CC Serve’s mark strength, the court determined that the analysis of the marks’ overall commercial impression was also compromised. This teaches that the DuPont factors are not isolated silos; an error in assessing market conditions or mark strength can ripple through the analysis, necessitating a remand of related factors to ensure a coherent likelihood of confusion determination.
