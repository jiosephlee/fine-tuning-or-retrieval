Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Case Background and Procedural History

## Factual Account of the November 2020 Incident

The factual record underlying this appeal centers on events that occurred during the night of November 19 to 20, 2020. According to the opinion, the defendant, Justin Cutbank, had been drinking and using methamphetamine prior to the incident. Cutbank awakened his girlfriend, identified in the record as D.F., and pointed a firearm at her face. He directed her into a closet, making paranoid accusations regarding listening devices in the home and threats from people outside. D.F. testified that Cutbank kept her in the closet at gunpoint for what felt like hours, during which time he threatened her life and struck her across the face with the weapon. Eventually, Cutbank ordered D.F. to wake other individuals sleeping in the house, including her two teenage sons and a housemate, C.F. Following these events, Cutbank fled the residence taking D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm. These facts are critical to the opinion because they establish the context of the alleged possession and the underlying violent conduct that informed both the conviction and the sentencing enhancements.

## Police Encounter and Recovery of the Firearm

Following D.F.’s call to the police, officers responded to the scene after also receiving a report from a nearby residence that a man was refusing to leave a garage. Upon arrival, Cutbank had barricaded himself inside the garage. Over the course of several hours, Cutbank made erratic statements and claimed to possess a weapon. When officers attempted to detain him, Cutbank resisted, biting an officer in the face and neck and attempting to grab the officer’s gun. During the search of the path Cutbank likely traveled between D.F.’s home and the garage, officers recovered D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with a sawed-off barrel and stock. DNA analysis matched Cutbank to the recovered firearm. At trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified this weapon as the one they saw Cutbank with prior to the night of the incident. The recovery of the physical evidence and the witness identification formed the core of the government’s proof of possession, which the jury ultimately accepted.

## Charges under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e)

Cutbank was charged with being a felon in possession of a firearm in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). This statute prohibits individuals who have been convicted of a crime punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year from possessing any firearm or ammunition. The opinion notes that Cutbank is a felon with an extensive criminal history, satisfying the status element of the charge. Additionally, the indictment included provisions under 18 U.S.C. § 924(e), known as the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA). The ACCA mandates enhanced penalties for felons in possession of a firearm who have three or more prior convictions for violent felonies or serious drug offenses. The jury found Cutbank guilty of the possession charge and specifically found that he had at least three prior violent felony convictions. This finding triggered the enhanced penalty provisions of § 924(e), which significantly increased the statutory sentencing range applicable to the conviction.

## District Court Sentencing under the Armed Career Criminal Act

Following the conviction, the district court proceeded to sentencing using the United States Sentencing Guidelines (USSG). The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) recommended a base offense level of 26 because the offense involved a sawed-off firearm described in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a). The PSR further recommended several specific enhancements: a three-level increase for an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level increase for recklessly creating a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing police, and a four-level increase for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony offense. These adjustments resulted in a total offense level of 35. Combined with a Criminal History Category of VI, the calculated advisory guidelines sentencing range was 292 to 365 months of imprisonment. The district court sentenced Cutbank to 292 months, which fell within the advisory range. Although the jury found the ACCA statutory enhancement applicable, the opinion notes that the Guidelines calculation for the ACCA offense level would have been lower (level 34) than the calculation under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) (level 35); the Guidelines require the application of the greater offense level.

## Summary of Appellant's Claims on Appeal

On appeal, Cutbank challenged both his conviction and his sentence, arguing that the district court committed multiple errors. His claims fall into two primary categories: evidentiary and procedural errors, and a sentencing enhancement error. Regarding the trial proceedings, Cutbank argued that the admission of testimony regarding his prior possession of the rifle violated Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b), which generally prohibits evidence of other crimes to prove propensity. He also raised hearsay and Confrontation Clause issues regarding statements made by D.F. to the police, and objected to testimony regarding the death of D.F.’s sister, arguing it was unfairly prejudicial. Finally, he claimed the court improperly limited his cross-examination of witness C.F. regarding prior inconsistent statements. Regarding the sentence, Cutbank argued that the district court misapplied the four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possession in connection with another felony offense. He contended that applying this enhancement resulted in a sentence that conflicted with the statutory sentencing factors of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). The Eighth Circuit reviewed these claims under standards of abuse of discretion for evidentiary rulings and for sentencing reasonableness, ultimately affirming the district court’s judgment.

# Admissibility of Prior Possession Evidence under Rule 404(b)

## Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b) and Propensity Evidence

In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the defendant challenged the admission of testimony regarding his prior possession of the firearm used in the charged offense. To understand this challenge, a law student must first grasp the general prohibition found in Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). This rule generally bars the admission of evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts to prove a person’s character in order to show that on a particular occasion the person acted in accordance with that character. This is known as the prohibition against propensity evidence. In *Cutbank*, the defense argued that allowing witnesses to testify that they had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle before the incident violated this rule. The defense contended that this testimony suggested Cutbank had a propensity to possess firearms or to use them violently, rather than proving the specific fact of possession at the time of the charged offense.

However, the Eighth Circuit clarified that not all evidence of prior conduct falls under the 404(b) prohibition. The rule applies only to "other" acts that are extrinsic to the charged offense. If the evidence is intrinsic to the charged crime, the 404(b) restrictions do not apply. In the context of a felon in possession charge, the government must prove that the defendant knowingly possessed the firearm at the time of the offense. Evidence that the defendant possessed that specific weapon at a time immediately preceding the charged offense is often considered part of the same transaction or sequence of events. Consequently, the court must distinguish between evidence offered to show a bad character versus evidence offered to prove the essential elements of the charged crime, such as ownership or continuous control.

## The Intrinsic Evidence Doctrine in Firearm Possession Cases

The central legal doctrine applied in *Cutbank* is the intrinsic evidence exception to Rule 404(b). Intrinsic evidence is evidence that is part of the charged offense itself or is necessary to complete the story of the crime. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s determination that testimony regarding Cutbank’s prior possession of the specific Marlin .22 caliber rifle was intrinsic. Because the charge was possession of that specific firearm, evidence linking the defendant to that firearm prior to the arrest was not an "other act" in the 404(b) sense. Instead, it was evidence directly relevant to the identity of the weapon and the defendant's relationship to it.

The court reasoned that the prior sightings were not introduced to show that Cutbank was a violent person who habitually carried guns. Rather, the testimony served to connect the defendant to the specific instrumentality of the crime. In firearm possession cases, establishing that the defendant had control over the weapon in the past supports the inference that he maintained control over it during the charged incident. By classifying the evidence as intrinsic, the appellate court bypassed the strict requirements of 404(b), which would otherwise require a specific non-propensity purpose and a balancing of prejudice under Rule 403. This distinction is critical for students reading criminal appeals, as it determines the standard of review and the threshold for admissibility.

## Relevance of Prior Possession to Proving Continuous Control

The Eighth Circuit provided specific reasoning for why prior possession is relevant in this context, focusing on the concept of continuous control. The opinion states, "Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon." This reasoning addresses the element of possession, which requires both dominion and control. If a defendant is seen with a weapon repeatedly over time, it becomes more probable that the weapon found in connection with the crime belonged to them and was under their control.

In *Cutbank*, witnesses D.F. and C.F. testified that they had seen the defendant with the sawed-off rifle a "handful of times" and "once or twice" before the incident. This testimony was not merely cumulative; it provided a timeline of possession. By showing that Cutbank had the gun prior to the night of the incident, the evidence helped rebut any potential defense argument that the gun was planted or that he did not know it was there. The court viewed the prior possession as a logical precursor to the charged possession, creating a continuous narrative of ownership. Therefore, the evidence was deemed highly probative of the fact in issue—whether Cutbank possessed the firearm—without crossing into the realm of impermissible character evidence.

## Pretrial Motion to Exclude Witness Testimony on Prior Sightings

Procedurally, the issue of admissibility was litigated before the trial began. Cutbank filed a pretrial motion specifically requesting that the district court prevent D.F. and C.F. from testifying about their prior observations of the sawed-off Marlin rifle. This motion framed the legal argument around Rule 404(b), asserting that the testimony constituted improper propensity evidence. The district court denied this motion, agreeing with the government’s position that the evidence was intrinsic to the possession charge.

The procedural posture continued into the trial itself. When D.F. testified that she had seen Cutbank with the rifle a "handful of times" before the incident, Cutbank renewed his objection. The district court overruled this objection, maintaining its pretrial ruling. Similarly, when C.F. testified to seeing the gun used previously, the court overruled the defense objection. On appeal, Cutbank cited no authority to refute the district court’s pretrial ruling, and the Eighth Circuit noted that the defense failed to provide cases or analysis to support the claim that this was improper 404(b) evidence. This procedural history highlights the importance of preserving objections at trial and the deference appellate courts give to district courts’ evidentiary rulings when the defendant fails to articulate a legal basis for reversal.

## Application of United States v. Battle Precedent

To support its decision, the Eighth Circuit relied heavily on its prior precedent in *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014). In *Battle*, the court established the principle that prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of the same weapon. The *Cutbank* opinion cites *Battle* to demonstrate that this is settled law within the Eighth Circuit. The court in *Cutbank* found that the facts were analogous to *Battle*, where the link between the defendant and the specific weapon was crucial to the conviction.

By applying *Battle*, the appellate court reinforced the standard that prior possession evidence is admissible when it establishes ownership or control of the weapon involved in the charged offense. The citation serves to show that the district court did not abuse its discretion in admitting the testimony. For law students, this illustrates how appellate courts use binding circuit precedent to resolve evidentiary disputes. The *Battle* precedent provided the doctrinal foundation that allowed the *Cutbank* court to affirm the conviction without needing to engage in a complex Rule 403 balancing test, as the evidence was deemed intrinsic and therefore not subject to the 404(b) exclusionary rule in the first place.

# Trial Conduct, Hearsay, and Impeachment Procedures

In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals addressed a series of evidentiary and procedural challenges raised by the defendant regarding the conduct of his trial. Cutbank, convicted of being a felon in possession of a firearm, argued that the district court committed multiple errors in admitting testimony and managing trial procedures that deprived him of a fair trial. The appellate court reviewed these claims under an abuse of discretion standard, emphasizing that district courts possess broad latitude in managing trial proceedings. This chapter examines the specific legal principles applied by the Eighth Circuit regarding hearsay instructions, the Confrontation Clause, impeachment procedures, and the authentication of evidence, as articulated in the court’s analysis of Cutbank’s appeal.

## Hearsay Statements and Limiting Jury Instructions

One of the procedural issues raised by Cutbank concerned the admission of hearsay statements made by the victim, D.F., to police officers prior to the trial. Before trial, Cutbank requested a specific jury instruction stating that the jury could not consider D.F.’s statements to police as substantive evidence of his actions until D.F. herself testified to the events. The government argued that the statements were necessary to establish the context for police actions and that a standard instruction at the close of trial would be sufficient. The district court denied the request for a specific pre-testimony instruction.

On appeal, the Eighth Circuit reviewed the district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion. The court explained that a trial court is not required to instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the precise moment it is admitted, provided the court gives an appropriate instruction at the close of the trial. In this case, the appellate court noted that the record demonstrated the district court did not actually admit hearsay during the trial. Because D.F. testified in person, the need for a limiting instruction regarding her prior statements was mitigated. The Eighth Circuit held that there was no abuse of discretion in the district court’s management of the jury instructions, as the standard practice of instructing at the close of trial was followed and the record did not show improper hearsay admission.

## Confrontation Clause Requirements for Declarants

Closely related to the hearsay issue was Cutbank’s argument regarding the Confrontation Clause of the Sixth Amendment. Cutbank was concerned that D.F.’s statements to the police might be considered without her direct testimony, potentially violating his right to confront witnesses against him. The Eighth Circuit clarified the standard for satisfying the Confrontation Clause in this context. The court held that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the hearsay declarants actually appear in court and testify in person.

In *Cutbank*, D.F. did appear in court and testified regarding the events of the night in question. Because the declarant was present and subject to cross-examination, the constitutional requirement was met. The court reasoned that since D.F. testified, there was no violation of the Confrontation Clause regardless of whether her prior statements to police were referenced. This ruling underscores the principle that the primary safeguard of the Confrontation Clause is the opportunity for the defendant to cross-examine the witness live in court. Consequently, the Eighth Circuit found no error in the district court’s handling of this issue, reinforcing that the presence and testimony of the declarant cure potential confrontation concerns regarding prior statements.

## Impeachment by Prior Inconsistent Statements

The trial also involved the use of prior inconsistent statements to impeach witnesses. During cross-examination, the defense sought to challenge the credibility of the government witnesses, D.F. and C.F., by highlighting inconsistencies between their trial testimony and prior statements. For example, D.F. testified during cross-examination that some of her prior statements were inconsistent because she was not familiar with guns and did not like them. Similarly, the defense cross-examined C.F. about prior statements she made to a police investigator regarding her observation of the gun.

The Eighth Circuit addressed the propriety of using these prior statements for impeachment purposes. The court acknowledged that extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement. However, the court emphasized that while the purpose of the evidence is impeachment, the evidence itself must still meet foundational requirements. In the case of C.F., the defense attempted to introduce a police report containing her prior statements. The court sustained the government’s objection to reading portions of the report into the record without proper foundation. The Eighth Circuit affirmed that although extrinsic evidence is permissible for impeachment, it does not bypass the requirement for authentication. The defense’s attempt to use the police report was properly limited by the district court because the report was not authored by the witness and lacked the necessary authentication to be admitted as extrinsic evidence.

## Admissibility of Testimony Regarding Victim's Family History

Another evidentiary ruling involved testimony regarding the victim’s family history. During cross-examination of D.F. regarding her prior inconsistent statements about the gun, she testified that some statements were made because she "did not like guns" and that her "sister was killed by a gun." The defense moved to strike this testimony, arguing it was prejudicial. The district court denied the motion.

On appeal, Cutbank argued that this testimony regarding the death of D.F.’s sister was inadmissible. The Eighth Circuit applied the balancing test under Federal Rule of Evidence 403, which allows a court to exclude relevant evidence if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. The court found that the testimony was responsive to cross-examination regarding inconsistent prior statements, explaining why D.F. might have hesitated or erred in her earlier accounts. The Eighth Circuit noted that Cutbank pointed to no unfair prejudice or confusion resulting from the testimony, and none was apparent on the record. Furthermore, another witness, C.F., had mentioned the same fact without objection. The court concluded that the district court did not abuse its broad evidentiary discretion in allowing the testimony, as it served to explain the witness's credibility and prior statements without causing substantial prejudice.

## Authentication Requirements for Police Reports

The final procedural issue examined in this section concerned the authentication of police reports used for impeachment. As noted in the discussion of impeachment, the defense sought to cross-examine C.F. about prior statements she made to a police investigator. However, when defense counsel attempted to read portions of the resulting police report into the record, the district court sustained the government’s objection. The court required proper foundation because the report was not authored by C.F.

The Eighth Circuit affirmed this ruling, citing the principle that extrinsic evidence used for impeachment must still be authenticated. Even though the purpose of introducing the report was to impeach the witness with a prior inconsistent statement, the document itself must be properly authenticated to ensure its reliability. The court referenced *United States v. Cumbie* to support the holding that authentication is a prerequisite for admitting extrinsic evidence of prior inconsistent statements. By requiring authentication, the district court ensured that the evidence introduced was reliable and connected to the witness. The Eighth Circuit found no abuse of discretion in this ruling, noting that the defense failed to establish the necessary foundation for the police report.

In summary, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s evidentiary rulings, applying the abuse of discretion standard and the harmless error doctrine. The court determined that even if any errors occurred, they did not substantially sway the jury given the substantial testimonial and physical evidence presented by the government. The cumulative effect of the trial errors did not result in substantial prejudice to the defendant, leading to the affirmation of the conviction.

# Sentencing Guidelines Calculation and ACCA Enhancements

In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit addressed the defendant’s appeal regarding his sentence, specifically focusing on the calculation of the advisory sentencing guidelines range and the application of statutory enhancements. Cutbank, convicted as a felon in possession of a firearm, challenged the district court’s application of a specific sentencing enhancement and argued that the resulting sentence violated the statutory factors governing sentencing. To understand the court’s affirmation, a student must grasp how the United States Sentencing Guidelines (USSG) interact with statutory penalties like the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), the standards for applying specific offense characteristics, and the procedural requirements a district court must follow to ensure a sentence is both procedurally and substantively reasonable. This chapter breaks down the sentencing reasoning found in the opinion, teaching the mechanics of guideline calculations and the appellate review standards applied to sentencing decisions.

## USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Enhancement for Connection to Another Felony

A central issue in Cutbank’s appeal was the application of a four-level sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). This enhancement applies when a defendant uses or possesses a firearm in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense. A critical rule governing this enhancement, as noted in the opinion, is that it applies regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought for the other felony or a conviction was obtained. In Cutbank’s case, the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) identified that Cutbank possessed the firearm during a separate incident involving domestic abuse, kidnapping, and assault. Although Cutbank was not separately charged for these underlying acts in this specific prosecution, the facts established the "connection" required for the enhancement.

The appellate court reviewed the district court’s application of this enhancement for clear error. The opinion highlights that at the sentencing hearing, the district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements regarding Cutbank’s prior felony convictions and the circumstances of the firearm possession without objection from the defense. Because the facts in the PSR established that the gun was used in connection with another felony, the district court did not err in applying the four-level increase. This teaches students that factual findings supporting guideline enhancements are reviewed deferentially, and if the facts are uncontested or supported by the record, the enhancement stands even if the underlying conduct was not the subject of a separate conviction.

## Base Offense Level and Specific Offense Characteristics

Understanding the final sentencing range requires examining how the base offense level and specific offense characteristics were calculated to reach the total offense level of 35. The PSR recommended a base offense level of 26, driven by the nature of the weapon—a sawed-off firearm described in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a)—and Cutbank’s history of at least two prior convictions for crimes of violence. From this base, the court applied several specific offense characteristics that increased the level further. These included a three-level increase for the firearm having an altered or obliterated serial number, a two-level increase because Cutbank recklessly created a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing from police, and the aforementioned four-level increase for possession in connection with another felony.

These adjustments illustrate how the Guidelines operate as a cumulative system where specific conduct and criminal history elevate the offense level. The combination of the base level and these enhancements resulted in a total offense level of 35. When paired with a Criminal History Category of VI, derived from Cutbank’s extensive prior record, the guidelines produced an advisory sentencing range of 292 to 365 months imprisonment. The district court sentenced Cutbank to 292 months, the minimum of this range. For a law student reading this opinion, it is important to note that the calculation of these levels is a prerequisite for determining the statutory reasonableness of the sentence; errors in this mathematical or categorical step can constitute procedural error.

## Interaction Between ACCA Statutory Penalties and Guidelines

The opinion provides a clear example of the interaction between statutory sentencing penalties and the advisory Guidelines. The jury found that Cutbank had three or more prior violent felony convictions, which triggered the enhanced mandatory minimum penalties under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e). Cutbank argued that applying the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement alongside the ACCA statutory penalty resulted in a sentence that conflicted with the core principles of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). However, the court clarified the relationship between the statutory penalty and the Guidelines calculation. While the ACCA triggered statutory penalties, the Guidelines themselves have a specific provision for career criminals, USSG § 4B1.4.

The court noted that the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have produced a total offense level of 34. However, the offense level calculated under § 2K2.1, including the specific enhancements, resulted in a level of 35. The Guidelines explicitly provide that when multiple guideline sections apply, the greater offense level must be used. Consequently, the court applied the level 35 calculation rather than the ACCA guideline level of 34. This distinction is vital for students: the statutory ACCA penalty sets the floor for sentencing, but the Guidelines calculation determines the advisory range, and the court must apply the higher of the applicable guideline offense levels to ensure the correct range is established before considering the statutory minimums.

## District Court Consideration of § 3553(a) Factors

Once the Guidelines range is correctly calculated, the district court must consider the factors set forth in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) to determine the appropriate sentence. Cutbank argued that the sentence was substantively unreasonable because it was greater than necessary to comply with the purposes of sentencing. In reviewing this claim, the Eighth Circuit applied a presumption of reasonableness to sentences that fall within the applicable Guidelines range. The opinion emphasizes that the district court was aware of Cutbank’s arguments, having reviewed the PSR and sentencing memoranda, and thus it is presumed the court considered and rejected them.

The court reiterated that a district court is not required to provide a mechanical recitation of every § 3553(a) factor it weighed. Instead, the record must simply make it clear that the court actually considered the factors in determining the sentence. In this case, the district court noted its review of the PSR and the sentencing factors that could warrant a variance. Given Cutbank’s nearly dozen prior felony convictions, many involving violence and firearms, and the nature of the offense involving domestic abuse and injury to a police officer, the court found the 292-month term was not substantively unreasonable. This teaches students that appellate courts grant substantial deference to the district court’s discretion regarding the substantive reasonableness of a within-range sentence, provided the procedural steps were followed.

## Procedural Requirements for Sentencing Calculations

The final component of the sentencing analysis involves the procedural requirements a district court must satisfy to avoid reversible error. The opinion cites *Gall v. United States* and *Rosales-Mireles v. United States* to establish the procedural framework. Before a court of appeals can consider the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, it must first ensure that the district court committed no significant procedural error, such as failing to calculate or improperly calculating the Guidelines range. This procedural step is mandatory; a correct calculation of the advisory range is the foundation upon which the § 3553(a) analysis rests.

In *Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit confirmed that the district court correctly calculated the Guidelines range. Because the calculation was accurate and the court demonstrated consideration of the statutory factors, the appellate court did not need to scrutinize the substantive decision further. The opinion reinforces that while the Guidelines are advisory, the process of calculating them is binding on the district court’s procedure. If a defendant challenges the sentence, the appellate court looks first to whether the mathematical and legal framework of the Guidelines was applied correctly. Only after confirming procedural correctness does the court assess whether the sentence itself is reasonable under the circumstances of the case.

# Appellate Standards of Review and Final Disposition

## Abuse of Discretion Standard for Evidentiary and Sentencing Rulings

When a defendant appeals a district court’s decision, the Court of Appeals must first determine the standard of review applicable to the specific issue raised. In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit applied the abuse of discretion standard to review both evidentiary rulings and sentencing decisions. This standard reflects the principle that district court judges, who observe the trial firsthand and manage the proceedings, are better positioned to make certain factual and procedural determinations than appellate judges reviewing a cold record. Consequently, an appellate court will not reverse a district court’s decision unless the ruling was arbitrary, capricious, or manifestly unreasonable.

Regarding evidentiary rulings, the opinion cites *United States v. Bennett* and *Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn* to establish that the district court possesses broad discretion. For example, Cutbank argued the district court erred by not giving a specific limiting instruction regarding hearsay statements during the trial. The Eighth Circuit noted that under *Bennett*, a court need not instruct the jury at the moment evidence is admitted if an appropriate instruction is provided at the close of trial. Similarly, when the district court denied Cutbank’s motion to strike testimony about the victim’s sister being killed by a gun, the appellate court deferred to the trial judge’s assessment of prejudice under Federal Rule of Evidence 403. The Eighth Circuit emphasized that because Cutbank pointed to no unfair prejudice or confusion, and none was apparent on the record, there was no abuse of the district court’s broad evidentiary discretion.

The same deferential standard applied to the sentencing phase. The opinion references *United States v. Dunn* to note that while factual findings in a Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) are reviewed for clear error, the ultimate sentencing decision is reviewed for abuse of discretion. In Cutbank’s case, the district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements regarding prior felony convictions without objection. The Eighth Circuit held that the court did not err in finding that the facts established the firearm was used in connection with another felony. Furthermore, the appellate court reviewed the substantive reasonableness of the sentence under an abuse of discretion framework, looking at whether the district court properly considered the statutory factors.

## Harmless Error Doctrine for Improperly Admitted Evidence

Even if a district court makes an evidentiary error, reversal is not automatic. The appellate court must determine whether the error was harmless. The harmless error doctrine prevents the overturning of convictions based on technical mistakes that did not affect the outcome of the trial. In *Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit explicitly cited *United States v. DeMarce* to explain this threshold: the court will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence.

The opinion illustrates how this doctrine functions in practice. Cutbank raised multiple evidentiary challenges, including objections to testimony about prior gun possession and statements regarding the victim’s family history. The Eighth Circuit acknowledged that an erroneous evidentiary ruling will not result in reversal if the error was harmless. The court reasoned that because the government’s evidence—both testimonial and physical—was substantial, the district court’s rulings did not influence, or had only a slight influence on, the verdict. This analysis requires the appellate court to look at the totality of the evidence presented. If the remaining evidence is overwhelming, the likelihood that a specific evidentiary ruling changed the jury’s mind is low, rendering any potential error harmless.

## Presumption of Reasonableness for Within-Range Sentences

Sentencing appeals involve a two-step analysis: procedural reasonableness and substantive reasonableness. The Eighth Circuit outlined this framework by citing *Gall v. United States* and *Rosales-Mireles v. United States*. Procedural reasonableness requires the district court to correctly calculate the applicable Guidelines range and consider the factors set forth in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). Substantive reasonableness asks whether the sentence imposed is appropriate given those factors.

A critical component of this review is the presumption of reasonableness that attaches to within-guidelines sentences. The opinion notes that when a district court imposes a sentence within the applicable Guidelines range, the Court of Appeals presumes that sentence is reasonable. Citing *United States v. Goodhouse*, the Eighth Circuit applied this presumption to Cutbank’s 292-month sentence, which fell within the advisory range of 292 to 365 months. This presumption places the burden on the defendant to demonstrate that the sentence is unreasonable.

To satisfy procedural reasonableness, the district court must show it considered the § 3553(a) factors. However, the opinion clarifies, citing *United States v. Maluoth*, that the court does not need to provide a mechanical recitation of every factor. It simply must be clear from the record that the court actually considered them. In Cutbank’s case, the district court reviewed the PSR and sentencing memoranda and noted its review of the § 3553(a) factors. Citing *United States v. Masood*, the Eighth Circuit held that because the court was aware of the arguments, it is presumed to have considered and rejected them. This procedural safeguard ensures that the sentencing process is transparent without imposing rigid formalistic requirements on district judges.

## Analysis of Cumulative Error and Substantial Prejudice

Defendants sometimes argue that while individual errors might be harmless, the cumulative effect of multiple errors deprived them of a fair trial. The Eighth Circuit addressed this in *Cutbank* by applying the standard from *United States v. Keys*. Under this standard, the court will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is substantial prejudice to the defendant.

This standard reinforces the high bar required for reversal when multiple evidentiary or procedural issues are raised. In Cutbank’s appeal, he argued that the district court committed multiple errors. However, the Eighth Circuit found that the cumulative effect of these rulings did not result in substantial prejudice. The court reasoned that the government’s evidence was substantial enough to support the conviction regardless of the contested rulings. This analysis prevents defendants from securing reversals by aggregating minor or harmless errors into a claim of unfairness that the record does not support. It underscores that the focus remains on whether the errors actually impacted the jury’s verdict or the fairness of the proceedings.

## Final Affirmance of Conviction and Sentence

The final disposition of an appeal depends on whether the appellant has met the burden of showing reversible error under the applicable standards of review. In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit concluded that the district court did not abuse its discretion in its evidentiary rulings or sentencing decisions. The court found that the evidence was sufficient, the procedural safeguards were followed, and the sentence was substantively reasonable.

The opinion concludes by affirming the judgment of the district court. This disposition reflects the application of the standards discussed throughout the chapter. Because Cutbank could not demonstrate that the evidentiary errors were harmful, that the sentencing guidelines were miscalculated, or that the cumulative errors caused substantial prejudice, the appellate court upheld the lower court’s decisions. The affirmation of the 292-month sentence, triggered by the Armed Career Criminal Act and the specific sentencing enhancements, demonstrates the finality of the process when the appellate court finds no significant procedural or substantive error. For a law student, this outcome illustrates how the standards of review function as gatekeepers, ensuring that convictions and sentences stand unless the defendant can clearly show that the district court acted unreasonably or that the errors affected the outcome.
