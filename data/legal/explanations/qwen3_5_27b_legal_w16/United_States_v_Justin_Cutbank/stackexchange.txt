Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### When is prior possession of a firearm considered 'intrinsic' evidence rather than Rule 404(b) other acts evidence?
Question:
I'm reading *United States v. Cutbank* and the court allowed testimony that the defendant had possessed the sawed-off rifle a 'handful of times' before the charged incident. The defense argued this was propensity evidence under Rule 404(b), but the Eighth Circuit called it 'intrinsic evidence.' I'm confused about the distinction. Does 'intrinsic' just mean it's part of the same transaction, or is there a specific test for when prior possession proves continuous control? The court cited *United States v. Battle*, but I'm not sure how that applies here when the prior possession wasn't part of the specific charged offense date.
Answer:
**In *United States v. Cutbank*, prior possession is "intrinsic" when it establishes ownership or control of the *same* weapon charged in the indictment.**

The distinction you are asking about hinges on whether the evidence is part of the charged offense itself (intrinsic) or an "other act" used to show propensity (Rule 404(b)). Based on the Eighth Circuit’s opinion in *Cutbank*, here is the breakdown of the standard and how it applies to firearm possession cases.

### The Distinction: Intrinsic vs. Rule 404(b)
Rule 404(b) generally prohibits evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts to prove a person’s character or propensity to commit the crime. However, evidence that is **intrinsic** to the charged offense is not subject to Rule 404(b) because it is not considered an "other act."

In *Cutbank*, the court clarified that prior possession of a firearm does not automatically fall under Rule 404(b). Instead, if the prior possession is directly relevant to proving the *current* possession charge, it is treated as intrinsic evidence.

### The Standard Applied in *Cutbank*
The Eighth Circuit relied on *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014), to define the scope of intrinsic evidence in this context. According to the *Cutbank* opinion:

> “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.”

This creates a specific test for when prior possession becomes intrinsic:
1.  **Same Weapon:** The prior possession must involve the specific firearm charged in the indictment.
2.  **Relevance to Control:** The evidence must help establish that the defendant had ownership or control over that weapon leading up to the charged incident.

### Application to the Facts
In *Cutbank*, the defendant argued that testimony regarding his possession of the sawed-off rifle a "handful of times" before the charged incident was propensity evidence. The court rejected this, ruling the evidence intrinsic for two main reasons grounded in the opinion:

*   **Continuous Possession:** The testimony demonstrated "continuous possession of the firearm before the incident." This helps complete the story of the charged offense by showing the defendant did not merely encounter the gun once, but maintained control over it.
*   **Proof of the Charged Element:** Because the charge was felon in possession (18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1)), establishing that the defendant had the gun previously was directly relevant to proving he possessed it on the date of the offense.

### Conclusion
To answer your specific question: "Intrinsic" in this context does not strictly mean the prior act occurred during the exact same "transaction" or moment as the charged crime. Rather, as *Cutbank* explains, it means the prior act is **necessary to establish the element of possession itself**.

If the prior possession proves the defendant had **ownership or control of the same weapon** leading up to the charged incident, the Eighth Circuit treats it as intrinsic evidence. It is admissible not to show the defendant is a "bad person" (propensity), but to prove he actually possessed the specific gun in question.

### Does USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) require a separate conviction for the 'another felony offense' enhancement?
Question:
In the sentencing section, the court applied a four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possession 'in connection with another felony offense.' The commentary says this applies 'regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.' The facts mention Cutbank threatened his girlfriend and barricaded himself, which sounds like domestic assault or kidnapping, but I don't see a conviction for those specific acts in the opinion. How can the court enhance the sentence based on a 'felony offense' if he wasn't charged or convicted of that underlying felony? What is the burden of proof for that underlying conduct at sentencing?
Answer:
**No, a separate conviction is not required.**

Under *United States v. Cutbank*, 8th Cir. 2025, the application of the four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) does not depend on whether the defendant was charged with or convicted of the underlying "another felony offense."

### The Legal Standard
As the Eighth Circuit explains in Section III of the opinion, the relevant authority is found in the Commentary to the Sentencing Guidelines. Specifically, **§ 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C))** states that the enhancement applies if the defendant used or possessed a firearm in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense:

> "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained."

This language explicitly decouples the sentencing enhancement from the requirement of a separate criminal conviction for the underlying conduct.

### Application in *Cutbank*
In *Cutbank*, the defendant argued that the enhancement was improperly applied because he was not separately convicted of the underlying acts (such as domestic abuse or kidnapping). The court rejected this argument.

1.  **Factual Basis:** The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) detailed conduct where Cutbank pointed a gun at his girlfriend, threatened her life, and barricaded himself with police. The appellate court noted that the offense of conviction involved "domestic abuse, kidnaping, assault in the victim’s home in the presence of her children, and injuring a police officer."
2.  **Procedural Adoption:** At sentencing, the district court "adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements." Because the facts in the PSR established the underlying felony conduct, the court found no error in applying the enhancement.
3.  **Standard of Review:** The Eighth Circuit reviewed the district court's finding for clear error, citing *United States v. Dunn*. Since the PSR facts were adopted without objection and supported by the record, the appellate court affirmed that the facts established the gun was used in connection with another felony.

### Summary for Law Students
When analyzing § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B):
*   **Conviction Status:** A separate conviction for the underlying felony is **not** necessary.
*   **Proof Requirement:** The enhancement turns on whether the *conduct* constitutes a felony, not on the procedural status of a charge for that conduct.
*   **Process:** If the PSR facts describing the underlying felony are adopted by the district court (often without objection), the enhancement applies based on the Commentary's directive.

The *Cutbank* opinion confirms that the Guidelines Commentary controls here, allowing the sentencing court to enhance the penalty based on the factual circumstances of the offense, even absent a specific conviction for those circumstances.

### How does the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) interact with USSG § 4B1.4(b) when calculating the Guidelines range?
Question:
The opinion states that the jury found Cutbank had three prior violent felonies, triggering ACCA penalties. However, the PSR calculated a base offense level under § 2K2.1 resulting in a level of 35, while the 'Armed Career Criminal Guideline' (§ 4B1.4) would have produced level 34. The court says 'The Guidelines provide that the greater offense level applies.' I thought ACCA imposed a mandatory statutory minimum of 15 years. How does the Guidelines calculation interact with the ACCA statutory minimum? Does the Guidelines level determine the starting point for the statutory range, or are they separate calculations that just happen to overlap?
Answer:
Based on *United States v. Cutbank*, the interaction between the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) and the Sentencing Guidelines is governed by a "greater offense level" rule for the Guidelines calculation, while the statutory penalty is triggered separately by the jury's finding.

### 1. The Guidelines Calculation: The "Greater Offense Level" Rule
The opinion clarifies that when a defendant is subject to ACCA penalties, the court must calculate the Guidelines range using the higher of the applicable offense levels.

In *Cutbank*, the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) calculated an offense level of 35 under § 2K2.1 (felon in possession), while the specific ACCA Guideline (§ 4B1.4) would have produced a level of 34. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court's application of the higher level, citing the explicit instruction in the Guidelines:

> "The Guidelines provide that the greater offense level applies. See USSG § 4B1.4(b)."

This resolves the calculation conflict: the court does not simply use the ACCA Guideline (§ 4B1.4) because the statute was triggered; it compares the specific offense guideline (§ 2K2.1) against the ACCA guideline and selects the higher number to determine the advisory range.

### 2. Statutory Trigger vs. Guidelines Range
The opinion treats the statutory enhancement and the Guidelines calculation as distinct but converging steps in the sentencing process:

*   **Statutory Trigger:** The jury’s finding of three prior violent felonies triggered the "enhanced penalties" of the ACCA statute.
*   **Guidelines Calculation:** The PSR calculated the advisory range based on the "greater offense level" rule described above.
*   **Sentencing:** The district court imposed a sentence "within the advisory guidelines range."

The opinion emphasizes that the Guidelines range serves as the starting point for the sentencing proceeding. Citing *Gall v. United States*, the court notes:

> "a district court should begin all sentencing proceedings by correctly calculating the applicable Guidelines range."

While the opinion does not explicitly detail the mathematical interaction between the statutory minimum and the Guidelines floor, it confirms that the sentence imposed (292 months) was determined to be reasonable because it fell within the calculated Guidelines range, which was derived using the higher offense level.

### Summary
To answer your specific question: According to *Cutbank*, the Guidelines calculation and the statutory ACCA trigger are separate calculations that interact at the sentencing phase. The jury finding activates the statute, but the **Guidelines level determines the advisory range** by applying the "greater offense level" rule under § 4B1.4(b). The court then sentences within that advisory range, ensuring the sentence complies with the statutory enhancement while adhering to the Guidelines framework.

### When is a limiting instruction required for hearsay statements when the declarant testifies at trial?
Question:
Cutbank requested a limiting instruction regarding D.F.'s statements to the police before she testified, arguing hearsay and Confrontation Clause issues. The district court refused to give an instruction at the time the evidence was admitted, relying on the fact that D.F. later testified in person. The Eighth Circuit affirmed, citing *United States v. Bennett*. I thought the Confrontation Clause (Crawford) might still require specific handling of pre-trial statements even if the witness testifies. Under what circumstances is the declarant's testimony sufficient to cure the need for a limiting instruction on their prior hearsay statements?
Answer:
Based on the Eighth Circuit's opinion in *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, a limiting instruction regarding a declarant's prior statements is **not required at the time the evidence is admitted** if the district court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial, and the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the declarant testifies in person.

Here is the breakdown of the legal standards applied in *Cutbank* regarding your question:

### 1. Timing of Limiting Instructions
Under the Eighth Circuit's precedent cited in *Cutbank*, specifically *United States v. Bennett*, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), the district court has broad discretion regarding the timing of jury instructions. The court is not required to instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the moment it is admitted. Instead, the court may provide an appropriate limiting instruction at the close of trial.

In *Cutbank*, the defendant requested an instruction at the time D.F.'s statements to police were introduced to prevent the jury from considering them as substantive evidence until she testified. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court's refusal to give the instruction immediately, noting that the record showed the court did not admit hearsay during trial and that D.F. subsequently testified. The appellate court found no abuse of discretion in deferring the instruction.

### 2. Confrontation Clause Requirements
Regarding the Confrontation Clause concerns raised under *Crawford*, *Cutbank* relies on *United States v. Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010). The opinion states that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied **"when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person."**

Because D.F. testified at trial, the Eighth Circuit concluded that the constitutional requirement for confrontation was met. Consequently, the lack of an immediate limiting instruction did not violate the Confrontation Clause, provided the declarant was available for cross-examination, which she was.

### Summary for Practice
If you are litigating a similar issue in the Eighth Circuit:
*   **Requesting an Instruction:** You may request a limiting instruction at the time evidence is admitted, but the court is within its discretion to deny it if it intends to provide a comprehensive instruction at the close of trial.
*   **Confrontation Clause:** The primary safeguard for Confrontation Clause issues involving prior statements is the declarant's live testimony. If the declarant testifies and is subject to cross-examination, the Clause is generally satisfied, reducing the necessity for specific limiting instructions regarding the prior statements' admissibility.

The court in *Cutbank* emphasized that appellate review of these decisions is for **abuse of discretion**, and absent a showing that the jury was substantially swayed by the lack of an immediate instruction (harmless error analysis), the conviction will stand.

### To what extent can emotionally prejudicial background information be admitted during impeachment regarding prior inconsistent statements?
Question:
During cross-examination, D.F. testified that she didn't remember seeing the gun because her sister was killed by a gun. The defense moved to strike this under Rule 403 as prejudicial, but the court denied it. The opinion says this testimony 'responded to cross-examination regarding inconsistent prior statements.' I'm struggling to understand the Rule 403 balancing here. If the probative value is just to explain a prior inconsistency, does that outweigh the high risk of unfair prejudice regarding a family member's death? Is there a specific exception for impeachment credibility that relaxes Rule 403?
Answer:
Based on *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, there is no specific "impeachment exception" that relaxes Rule 403. Instead, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s ruling by applying the standard Rule 403 balancing test and finding that the trial court did not abuse its broad discretion.

### The Rule 403 Balancing Test
Under Federal Rule of Evidence 403, relevant evidence may be excluded if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusion of the issues, or misleading the jury. The key distinction is between "prejudice" (which is inherent in almost all damaging evidence) and **"unfair prejudice"** (which suggests an emotional decision-making process rather than a reasoned one).

### Application in *Cutbank*
In *Cutbank*, the witness (D.F.) testified during cross-examination that she did not remember seeing the gun because she was "not familiar with guns" and "did not like guns . . . [a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun." The defense moved to strike this testimony under Rule 403, arguing it was prejudicial. The Eighth Circuit upheld the denial of the motion for two primary reasons grounded in the opinion:

1.  **Responsiveness and Probative Value:** The court noted that the testimony "responded to cross-examination regarding inconsistent prior statements." The probative value lay in explaining the witness's prior inconsistent statements and assessing her credibility. Because the testimony was directly responsive to the defense's own line of questioning regarding those inconsistencies, it served a legitimate evidentiary purpose.
2.  **Lack of Apparent Unfair Prejudice:** The court explicitly stated that the defendant "points to no unfair prejudice or confusion resulting from this testimony, and none is apparent." While the information regarding the sister's death was emotionally charged, the court did not find it crossed the threshold into *unfair* prejudice under Rule 403 in this context.

### Standard of Review
The Eighth Circuit emphasized that appellate courts review evidentiary rulings for an **abuse of discretion**. Citing *Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn*, the opinion notes the district court has "broad evidentiary discretion." Unless the defendant can demonstrate that the ruling substantially swayed the verdict (harmless error standard), the appellate court will not overturn the trial court's balancing of probative value against prejudice.

### Conclusion
To answer your question directly: The probative value of explaining a prior inconsistency can outweigh the risk of prejudice, but not because of a special exception. Rather, as *Cutbank* illustrates, trial courts have significant discretion to admit such evidence if it is responsive to impeachment and if no *unfair* prejudice is apparent. The emotional nature of the background information alone does not trigger a Rule 403 exclusion if the evidence is necessary to explain the witness's credibility.

### What is the standard of review for a within-Guidelines sentence under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)?
Question:
The court affirms the sentence because it was within the advisory guidelines range and cites a 'presumption of reasonableness' from *United States v. Goodhouse*. Cutbank argued the sentence was substantively unreasonable under § 3553(a). I'm confused about the procedural posture here. If the sentence is within the range, does the appellate court only look for procedural error, or do they still review substantive reasonableness? Also, the opinion mentions the district court didn't provide a 'mechanical recitation' of factors. What is the minimum record requirement to show the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** For a within-Guidelines sentence, the Eighth Circuit applies a **presumption of reasonableness**, but it still reviews both **procedural and substantive reasonableness**. Regarding the record, the district court is **not required to provide a mechanical recitation** of the § 3553(a) factors; it is sufficient that the record clearly shows the court actually considered them.

### 1. Standard of Review: Procedural and Substantive
Even when a sentence falls within the advisory Guidelines range, the appellate court conducts a two-step review.

First, the court ensures there was no **significant procedural error**, such as failing to calculate the Guidelines range correctly. As *Cutbank* notes, citing *Rosales-Mireles v. United States*, "Before a court of appeals can consider the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, it must first ensure that the district court committed no significant procedural error."

Second, the court reviews **substantive reasonableness**. While a within-Guidelines sentence is afforded a "presumption of reasonableness" on appeal (*United States v. Goodhouse*), this does not preclude substantive review. The court in *Cutbank* explicitly affirmed that the district court "did not abuse its substantial sentencing discretion and impose a substantively unreasonable sentence." Therefore, the appellate court looks beyond procedural error to ensure the sentence is not substantively unreasonable, even if it is within the range.

### 2. Record Requirement for § 3553(a) Consideration
Regarding your question about the minimum record requirement: the district court **does not need to explicitly list or mechanically recite** every § 3553(a) factor.

*Cutbank* cites *United States v. Maluoth* to clarify this standard: "To uphold the sentence, we do not require a district court to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence."

In *Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit found the requirement satisfied because the district court "noted its review of the PSR and sentencing memoranda and reviewed § 3553(a) factors that could warrant a variance." Furthermore, citing *United States v. Masood*, the court noted that if the district court was aware of the defendant's arguments, "we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them."

**Summary:** The presumption of reasonableness applies to within-Guidelines sentences, but substantive review remains active. The record must show the court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors, but explicit, mechanical recitation is not required.
