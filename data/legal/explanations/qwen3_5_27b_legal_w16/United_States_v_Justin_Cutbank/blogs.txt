Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# The Intrinsic Evidence Exception: Admitting Prior Possession in Gun Cases

## The Battle Over Propensity vs. Intrinsic Evidence

In federal felon-in-possession prosecutions, one of the most contentious evidentiary battles often centers on whether the government can introduce evidence that the defendant possessed the weapon before the charged incident. Defense counsel frequently argue that such testimony violates Rule 404(b) by inviting the jury to convict based on the defendant’s criminal propensity rather than the specific facts of the case. However, in *United States v. Cutbank*, decided October 7, 2025, the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit reaffirmed a robust application of the "intrinsic evidence" exception. The ruling clarifies that prior possession of the specific firearm at issue is not merely other bad conduct, but is directly relevant to proving continuous ownership and control. This decision signals to practitioners that excluding evidence of prior possession of the same weapon in the Eighth Circuit will be an uphill climb, as such evidence is increasingly viewed as part of the charged offense rather than extrinsic character evidence.

## The Factual Landscape and Evidentiary Dispute

The facts underlying *Cutbank* illustrate why the government sought to introduce testimony regarding prior possession. Justin Cutbank, a felon with an extensive history, was charged with possessing a sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle. The charged conduct involved a chaotic night where Cutbank, under the influence of methamphetamine, held his girlfriend at gunpoint in a closet before fleeing to a nearby garage where he barricaded himself and assaulted police officers. During the investigation, officers recovered the rifle along with a knife and a cell phone. At trial, the girlfriend and a housemate testified that they had seen Cutbank with the specific sawed-off rifle a handful of times prior to the incident. Cutbank moved to exclude this testimony, arguing it constituted impermissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b). The district court denied the motion, ruling the evidence was intrinsic to the possession charge, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed.

## The Court’s Reasoning on Continuous Possession

The Eighth Circuit’s opinion relies heavily on the distinction between extrinsic acts used to prove character and intrinsic acts that form part of the charged conduct. Writing for the court, Judge Loken emphasized that prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control. The court cited *United States v. Battle*, a 2014 Eighth Circuit precedent, to support the proposition that evidence showing the defendant had the gun previously is not an attempt to show he is a gun-toting person generally, but rather that he maintained control over the specific instrumentality of the crime. By classifying the testimony as intrinsic, the court bypassed the stricter requirements of Rule 404(b), which typically require notice and a specific balancing of probative value against unfair prejudice. Instead, the evidence was admitted as part of the res gestae of the possession charge, effectively treating the timeline of possession as a continuous act rather than discrete criminal episodes.

## Analytical Perspective on the Intrinsic Label

From an analytical standpoint, *Cutbank* highlights the expanding utility of the intrinsic evidence doctrine in federal firearms cases. The court’s reasoning rests on a pragmatic view of possession: in cases involving a specific, traceable weapon, the history of who held that weapon is inextricably linked to the charge of current possession. However, this approach risks blurring the line between identity and character. If a defendant is seen with a gun multiple times over a long period, excluding that history might make it difficult for the government to prove intent or knowledge. Yet, admitting it carries the risk that a jury will convict based on a pattern of behavior rather than the specific night in question. The Eighth Circuit’s deference to the district court’s discretion suggests that unless the defense can show substantial prejudice that outweighs the probative value, the intrinsic label will likely shield such evidence from exclusion. This creates a high bar for defense attorneys attempting to limit the scope of the prosecution’s case to the specific dates of the alleged crime.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The doctrinal implications of *Cutbank* extend beyond the admissibility of testimony to the sentencing phase as well. The opinion also addressed a sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which applies when a firearm is possessed in connection with another felony offense. The court affirmed the enhancement, noting that the Guidelines require the greater offense level to apply when ACCA penalties are triggered. This stacking of enhancements—ACCA penalties combined with specific Guidelines adjustments for the nature of the firearm and the context of the possession—results in severe sentencing exposure. For defense strategies, the combination of the intrinsic evidence ruling and the sentencing enhancements suggests a comprehensive approach is necessary. Counsel must anticipate that evidence of prior possession will likely be admitted and focus instead on challenging the specific factual findings used to trigger sentencing enhancements, such as whether the prior acts truly constituted a "violent felony" or if the firearm was genuinely used in connection with another felony as defined by the Guidelines.

## Conclusion for Practitioners

Ultimately, *United States v. Cutbank* serves as a reminder that the intrinsic evidence exception remains a potent tool for prosecutors in the Eighth Circuit. By anchoring the admissibility of prior possession testimony in the need to prove continuous control over the specific weapon, the court has narrowed the avenues for defense motions to exclude such evidence under Rule 404(b). While the decision aligns with existing precedent like *Battle*, its application in a fact-intensive domestic violence and barricade scenario underscores the court’s willingness to admit a broad range of evidence to establish the context of the possession. For practitioners in the circuit, the takeaway is clear: motions to exclude prior possession of the charged firearm should be framed carefully, focusing on Rule 403 prejudice rather than Rule 404(b) propensity, as the latter is unlikely to succeed when the weapon in question is the same one recovered by police.



# ACCA and Guidelines: Navigating the 'Connection with Another Felony' Enhancement

In the landscape of federal firearms sentencing, few statutes carry the weight of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA). Yet, even when ACCA’s mandatory minimums are triggered, the Sentencing Guidelines continue to play a critical role in determining the advisory range. The Eighth Circuit’s recent decision in *United States v. Cutbank*, issued on October 7, 2025, offers a clear window into how courts navigate the intersection of statutory enhancements and Guidelines calculations. Specifically, the opinion highlights the application of the four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), and reinforces the high bar defendants face when challenging the substantive reasonableness of a within-range sentence.

The facts underlying *Cutbank* illustrate the type of conduct that typically drives aggressive sentencing recommendations. The defendant, a felon with an extensive criminal history, engaged in a terrifying domestic incident involving a sawed-off rifle, held his girlfriend and her children at gunpoint, and subsequently fled to a garage where he resisted arrest, biting an officer. While the conviction itself rested on 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) and the ACCA enhancement under § 924(e), the sentencing phase required the court to reconcile the statutory penalty with the Guidelines’ specific offense characteristics. The district court calculated a base offense level of 26 due to the sawed-off nature of the weapon and applied several specific enhancements, including a four-level increase for possession in connection with another felony. This calculation resulted in a total offense level of 35, which the court applied over the ACCA-specific guideline level of 34, adhering to the rule that the greater offense level controls.

The doctrinal crux of the appeal lay in Cutbank’s argument that applying the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement conflicted with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) that a sentence be sufficient, but not greater than necessary. This argument touches on a recurring tension in post-*Booker* sentencing: whether the Guidelines can be stacked in a way that renders a sentence substantively unreasonable even when it falls within the advisory range. The Eighth Circuit rejected this contention, emphasizing the procedural presumption that a within-Guidelines sentence is reasonable. The court noted that the district court had correctly calculated the range and explicitly considered the § 3553(a) factors, including the defendant’s nearly dozen prior felony convictions and the violent nature of the instant offense.

From an analytical perspective, *Cutbank* serves as a reminder of the distinct roles played by ACCA and the Guidelines. ACCA imposes a statutory floor, but it does not dictate the specific Guidelines offense level used to calculate the advisory range. The opinion clarifies that the "connection with another felony" enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) applies regardless of whether a conviction was obtained for that underlying felony, provided the facts support the connection. In this case, the underlying felony conduct was the domestic violence and kidnapping-like behavior preceding the arrest. By applying the greater of the two offense levels, the court ensured that the sentencing range reflected the full scope of the defendant’s culpability, rather than artificially capping it based on the ACCA guideline provision alone.

The downstream implications of this ruling are significant for defense counsel and sentencing practitioners. It reinforces that challenging a within-range sentence on substantive reasonableness grounds requires more than a general invocation of § 3553(a); the defendant must demonstrate that the sentence is greater than necessary given the specific circumstances. With the Eighth Circuit affirming that the district court need not provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed, the burden remains squarely on the appellant to show that the record does not reflect a genuine consideration of the statutory sentencing factors. Furthermore, the case underscores the importance of the "greater offense level applies" rule in § 4B1.4(b). When a defendant is subject to ACCA, the Guidelines calculation does not stop at the ACCA-specific provision; courts must still evaluate specific offense characteristics that might push the offense level higher, as happened here with the four-level enhancement for the connection to other felonies.

Ultimately, *Cutbank* is a straightforward affirmation of established sentencing principles, but it provides a useful data point for understanding how lower courts are applying the Guidelines in the context of serious firearm offenses. The Eighth Circuit’s reliance on the presumption of reasonableness for within-range sentences, combined with its strict adherence to the "greater offense level" rule, signals that appellate courts will remain deferential to district courts that have correctly navigated the complex interplay between statutory mandates and advisory guidelines. For defendants facing ACCA charges, this suggests that the most viable sentencing arguments may lie in the factual findings supporting specific enhancements rather than in broad challenges to the reasonableness of the resulting range.



# Confrontation Clause Compliance: The Cure of Live Testimony in Domestic Violence Cases

In the landscape of federal criminal procedure, few evidentiary battles are as recurrent as the tension between the need for victim testimony and the constitutional protections afforded to the accused. Nowhere is this more palpable than in domestic violence prosecutions involving firearms, where victims often make initial statements to police that later become the backbone of the government’s case. In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, decided by the Eighth Circuit on October 7, 2025, the court addressed precisely these friction points. While the opinion affirms a conviction under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) and a significant sentencing enhancement, its procedural holdings offer a clear roadmap for how federal courts manage Confrontation Clause challenges and Rule 403 objections when victims testify to traumatic histories. The case reaffirms a critical principle: the presence of the declarant at trial often serves as a constitutional cure for prior hearsay concerns, even when that testimony introduces emotionally charged background details.

## The Confrontation Clause and the "Live Testimony" Cure

The core constitutional issue in *Cutbank* turned on the admissibility of the victim’s prior statements to law enforcement. Cutbank argued that the district court should have instructed the jury that they could not consider the victim’s statements to police as substantive evidence until she testified in person. This is a common defense strategy in cases where the government relies on 911 calls or initial police reports to establish the context of the arrest. Under *Crawford v. Washington*, testimonial hearsay is generally inadmissible unless the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity for cross-examination. However, the Eighth Circuit found no abuse of discretion in the district court’s refusal to issue a limiting instruction prior to the victim’s testimony.

Judge Loken, writing for the panel, grounded the decision in the straightforward reality that the victim, D.F., did appear in court and testify. The court cited *United States v. Charboneau* for the proposition that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied "when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person." This reasoning underscores a practical mechanism often utilized by prosecutors: the live testimony of the witness cures the constitutional defect associated with their prior out-of-court statements. By ensuring the victim was subject to cross-examination, the government neutralized the Sixth Amendment objection. The Eighth Circuit’s analysis suggests that when a witness is present, the focus shifts from the admissibility of the prior statement to the weight the jury assigns it. This approach streamlines trial proceedings, preventing defense counsel from halting the flow of evidence with procedural objections that are rendered moot once the witness takes the stand. For practitioners, *Cutbank* reinforces that while *Crawford* imposes strict limits on testimonial hearsay, the remedy for potential violations is the witness’s availability for cross-examination, not necessarily the exclusion of their prior statements if they are ultimately present.

## Balancing Prejudice and Credibility Under Rule 403

Beyond the constitutional question, *Cutbank* offers a notable application of Federal Rule of Evidence 403 regarding the admission of prejudicial background information. During cross-examination, the defense sought to undermine the victim’s credibility by highlighting inconsistencies in her prior statements about the firearm. In response, the victim explained that she was unfamiliar with guns because her sister had been killed by one. Cutbank moved to strike this testimony, arguing it was unfairly prejudicial. The district court denied the motion, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed, finding no abuse of discretion.

This ruling highlights the delicate balancing act courts must perform when victims of violence attempt to explain their behavior or memory gaps. The defense likely argued that introducing the death of the victim’s sister would inflame the jury’s emotions against the defendant, distracting from the specific issue of possession. However, the Eighth Circuit found that the testimony was responsive to the cross-examination regarding inconsistent statements. The court noted that Cutbank pointed to no specific unfair prejudice or confusion, and none was apparent on the record. This deference to the trial court’s discretion is significant in domestic violence cases, where a victim’s trauma often informs their interaction with law enforcement and their recollection of events. By allowing the testimony, the court acknowledged that excluding such context might unfairly sanitize the witness’s testimony, making inconsistencies appear deceptive rather than rooted in genuine trauma or lack of familiarity. The decision signals that while Rule 403 protects defendants from inflammatory evidence, it does not grant them a veto over a witness’s explanation of their own credibility, provided the information is directly relevant to the inconsistency being challenged.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The doctrinal implications of *Cutbank* extend beyond the specific facts of a domestic dispute involving a sawed-off rifle. First, the opinion reinforces the Eighth Circuit’s reliance on the "live testimony" doctrine as a robust shield against Confrontation Clause challenges. This creates a strong incentive for defense counsel to focus their objections on the availability of the witness rather than the content of their prior statements if the witness is expected to testify. It suggests that procedural objections regarding limiting instructions may be viewed with skepticism by appellate courts if the constitutional right to confrontation is ultimately satisfied by the witness's presence.

Second, the Rule 403 analysis provides a framework for handling "collateral" trauma in violent felony prosecutions. As courts increasingly deal with cases involving domestic violence and gang activity, witnesses will frequently offer background details to explain their testimony. *Cutbank* suggests that appellate review will be highly deferential to district courts in these instances, provided the evidence is responsive to impeachment or credibility issues. This could lead to a broader admission of victim background stories in federal trials, potentially influencing jury perceptions in ways that defense counsel must anticipate during voir dire and opening statements.

Ultimately, *United States v. Cutbank* serves as a reminder that in federal criminal trials, procedural safeguards like the Confrontation Clause and Rule 403 are applied with a pragmatic eye toward trial fairness. The Eighth Circuit’s affirmation indicates that when the government produces a witness who can be cross-examined, and when that witness offers relevant explanations for their testimony, the scale tips heavily in favor of admission. For the defense, the lesson is clear: challenging the admissibility of victim testimony requires more than pointing to potential prejudice; it requires demonstrating that the trial court’s discretion was abused in a way that substantially swayed the verdict, a high bar in cases where the evidence of guilt, as in *Cutbank*, is substantial.
