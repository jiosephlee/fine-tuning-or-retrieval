Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### When is a limiting instruction required for hearsay statements when the declarant testifies at trial?
Question:
Cutbank requested a limiting instruction regarding D.F.'s statements to the police before she testified, arguing hearsay and Confrontation Clause issues. The district court refused to give an instruction at the time the evidence was admitted, relying on the fact that D.F. later testified in person. The Eighth Circuit affirmed, citing *United States v. Bennett*. I thought the Confrontation Clause (Crawford) might still require specific handling of pre-trial statements even if the witness testifies. Under what circumstances is the declarant's testimony sufficient to cure the need for a limiting instruction on their prior hearsay statements?
Answer:
Based on the Eighth Circuit's opinion in *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, a limiting instruction regarding a declarant's prior statements is **not required at the time the evidence is admitted** if the district court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial, and the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the declarant testifies in person.

Here is the breakdown of the legal standards applied in *Cutbank* regarding your question:

### 1. Timing of Limiting Instructions
Under the Eighth Circuit's precedent cited in *Cutbank*, specifically *United States v. Bennett*, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), the district court has broad discretion regarding the timing of jury instructions. The court is not required to instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the moment it is admitted. Instead, the court may provide an appropriate limiting instruction at the close of trial.

In *Cutbank*, the defendant requested an instruction at the time D.F.'s statements to police were introduced to prevent the jury from considering them as substantive evidence until she testified. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court's refusal to give the instruction immediately, noting that the record showed the court did not admit hearsay during trial and that D.F. subsequently testified. The appellate court found no abuse of discretion in deferring the instruction.

### 2. Confrontation Clause Requirements
Regarding the Confrontation Clause concerns raised under *Crawford*, *Cutbank* relies on *United States v. Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010). The opinion states that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied **"when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person."**

Because D.F. testified at trial, the Eighth Circuit concluded that the constitutional requirement for confrontation was met. Consequently, the lack of an immediate limiting instruction did not violate the Confrontation Clause, provided the declarant was available for cross-examination, which she was.

### Summary for Practice
If you are litigating a similar issue in the Eighth Circuit:
*   **Requesting an Instruction:** You may request a limiting instruction at the time evidence is admitted, but the court is within its discretion to deny it if it intends to provide a comprehensive instruction at the close of trial.
*   **Confrontation Clause:** The primary safeguard for Confrontation Clause issues involving prior statements is the declarant's live testimony. If the declarant testifies and is subject to cross-examination, the Clause is generally satisfied, reducing the necessity for specific limiting instructions regarding the prior statements' admissibility.

The court in *Cutbank* emphasized that appellate review of these decisions is for **abuse of discretion**, and absent a showing that the jury was substantially swayed by the lack of an immediate instruction (harmless error analysis), the conviction will stand.
