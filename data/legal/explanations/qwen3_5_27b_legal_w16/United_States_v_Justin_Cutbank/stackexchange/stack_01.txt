Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### When is prior possession of a firearm considered 'intrinsic' evidence rather than Rule 404(b) other acts evidence?
Question:
I'm reading *United States v. Cutbank* and the court allowed testimony that the defendant had possessed the sawed-off rifle a 'handful of times' before the charged incident. The defense argued this was propensity evidence under Rule 404(b), but the Eighth Circuit called it 'intrinsic evidence.' I'm confused about the distinction. Does 'intrinsic' just mean it's part of the same transaction, or is there a specific test for when prior possession proves continuous control? The court cited *United States v. Battle*, but I'm not sure how that applies here when the prior possession wasn't part of the specific charged offense date.
Answer:
**In *United States v. Cutbank*, prior possession is "intrinsic" when it establishes ownership or control of the *same* weapon charged in the indictment.**

The distinction you are asking about hinges on whether the evidence is part of the charged offense itself (intrinsic) or an "other act" used to show propensity (Rule 404(b)). Based on the Eighth Circuit’s opinion in *Cutbank*, here is the breakdown of the standard and how it applies to firearm possession cases.

### The Distinction: Intrinsic vs. Rule 404(b)
Rule 404(b) generally prohibits evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts to prove a person’s character or propensity to commit the crime. However, evidence that is **intrinsic** to the charged offense is not subject to Rule 404(b) because it is not considered an "other act."

In *Cutbank*, the court clarified that prior possession of a firearm does not automatically fall under Rule 404(b). Instead, if the prior possession is directly relevant to proving the *current* possession charge, it is treated as intrinsic evidence.

### The Standard Applied in *Cutbank*
The Eighth Circuit relied on *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014), to define the scope of intrinsic evidence in this context. According to the *Cutbank* opinion:

> “Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon.”

This creates a specific test for when prior possession becomes intrinsic:
1.  **Same Weapon:** The prior possession must involve the specific firearm charged in the indictment.
2.  **Relevance to Control:** The evidence must help establish that the defendant had ownership or control over that weapon leading up to the charged incident.

### Application to the Facts
In *Cutbank*, the defendant argued that testimony regarding his possession of the sawed-off rifle a "handful of times" before the charged incident was propensity evidence. The court rejected this, ruling the evidence intrinsic for two main reasons grounded in the opinion:

*   **Continuous Possession:** The testimony demonstrated "continuous possession of the firearm before the incident." This helps complete the story of the charged offense by showing the defendant did not merely encounter the gun once, but maintained control over it.
*   **Proof of the Charged Element:** Because the charge was felon in possession (18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1)), establishing that the defendant had the gun previously was directly relevant to proving he possessed it on the date of the offense.

### Conclusion
To answer your specific question: "Intrinsic" in this context does not strictly mean the prior act occurred during the exact same "transaction" or moment as the charged crime. Rather, as *Cutbank* explains, it means the prior act is **necessary to establish the element of possession itself**.

If the prior possession proves the defendant had **ownership or control of the same weapon** leading up to the charged incident, the Eighth Circuit treats it as intrinsic evidence. It is admissible not to show the defendant is a "bad person" (propensity), but to prove he actually possessed the specific gun in question.
