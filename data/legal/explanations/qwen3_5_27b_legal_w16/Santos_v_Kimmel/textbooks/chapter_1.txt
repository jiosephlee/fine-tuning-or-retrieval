Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Factual Background and Parties

This chapter introduces the factual narrative and procedural posture of *Santos v. Kimmel*, a dispute arising from the intersection of digital content creation, late-night television satire, and intellectual property law. To understand the legal claims and the court’s ultimate disposition, a law student must first grasp the sequence of events that gave rise to the litigation and the specific roles played by each litigant. The opinion provides a concise summary of these facts in the introductory section, which serves as the foundation for the District Court’s dismissal and the Second Circuit’s subsequent review. By analyzing these elements, students can see how factual allegations map onto legal standards, particularly regarding copyright ownership and the procedural mechanisms for dismissing claims that lack legal merit.

## The Cameo Platform and the Creation of Personalized Videos

The factual genesis of this dispute lies in the operation of the Cameo platform, a service that allows users to request personalized video messages from public figures. According to the opinion, the defendant, Jimmy Kimmel, utilized this platform to initiate contact with the plaintiff, George Santos. Crucially, the opinion notes that Kimmel did not identify himself during this process; instead, he used "various fictitious names" to submit the requests. This detail is significant because it establishes the context of the interaction: Santos believed he was fulfilling requests from private individuals rather than a television host preparing content for broadcast.

Santos fulfilled each request by creating personalized videos. These videos constitute the "original Works" referenced in the copyright claims. The opinion indicates that these works were published on the same day they were created, a fact supported by copyright registrations taken judicial notice by the court. For the purpose of understanding the copyright infringement claim, it is essential to recognize that Santos, as the creator, holds the copyright to these videos. The dispute arises not from the creation of the videos themselves, but from how they were subsequently utilized by the defendants. The opinion frames the creation of these videos as the plaintiff's commercial activity, noting that a reasonable observer would view the videos as conveying "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." This characterization of the original works' purpose is a factual premise that the court later weighs against the purpose of the secondary use in its fair use analysis.

## The 'Will Santos Say It?' Television Segments on Jimmy Kimmel Live!

The alleged infringement occurred when the defendants took the videos created by Santos and broadcast them on the late-night television program *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* ("JKL"). The opinion specifies that Kimmel aired the videos as part of a series of segments titled "Will Santos Say It?" The stated purpose of these segments, as acknowledged by Santos in his own allegations and accepted by the District Court, was "to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money." This factual distinction is critical: the defendants did not merely replay the videos; they incorporated them into a mocking series designed to critique Santos’s behavior.

From a legal perspective, this broadcast constitutes the "secondary use" of the copyrighted material. The nature of this use—satire, commentary, and mockery—distinguishes it from a straightforward reproduction of the work. The opinion highlights that the defendants were motivated by "(sarcastic) criticism and commentary" rather than a desire to "supplant" Santos’s commercially valuable right in the videos. Understanding this factual context is necessary for later chapters, as the court will analyze whether this transformative purpose outweighs the copyright holder's rights under the fair use doctrine. For now, the student must note that the factual record establishes a conflict between the plaintiff's intent in creating the videos (personalized encouragement) and the defendants' intent in using them (public commentary and satire).

## Identification of Parties: Santos, Kimmel, ABC, and Disney

The litigation involves multiple parties, each with a distinct role in the production and broadcast of the contested segments. The plaintiff is George Santos, identified in the opinion as a "Former Congressman." He is the creator of the videos and the alleged copyright holder. The defendants are Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. ("ABC"), and The Walt Disney Company. Kimmel is the host of *JKL* and the individual who initiated the Cameo requests and aired the segments. ABC is the network that airs the show, and Disney is ABC’s parent company.

Identifying all three entities as defendants is procedurally significant. In media litigation, plaintiffs often sue the individual host, the broadcasting network, and the parent corporation to ensure all potential liable parties are bound by the judgment. The opinion treats them collectively as "Defendants" for the purpose of the appeal. Understanding the relationship between these parties helps explain the scope of liability. If the court finds infringement, the question of who bears responsibility—whether the host who selected the content, the network that aired it, or the parent company that owns the network—becomes relevant. However, in this specific case, the court’s analysis focuses on the nature of the use itself rather than the individual liability of each defendant, as the fair use defense applies to the conduct of the group collectively in producing the segments.

## The District Court's Initial Dismissal of the Complaint

Before reaching the Second Circuit, this case was litigated in the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York, presided over by Judge Cote. The procedural mechanism used by the District Court to resolve the case was a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. A Rule 12(b)(6) motion asserts that the plaintiff has failed to state a claim upon which relief can be granted. In practical terms, this means that even if all the facts alleged in the complaint are true, the law does not provide a remedy for the plaintiff's grievance.

The District Court granted the motion, dismissing both the copyright infringement claims and the state law claims. Regarding the copyright claims, the District Court concluded they were barred by the fair use doctrine. Regarding the state law claims (breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement), the court found they were either preempted by the Copyright Act or failed to state a claim under applicable state law. Santos appealed this final judgment to the Second Circuit. The appellate court reviews such dismissals *de novo*, meaning the Second Circuit judges examine the legal questions anew without deferring to the District Court’s conclusions. This standard of review is explicitly noted in the opinion, which cites *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.* The *de novo* standard is important for students to understand because it signals that the appellate court will independently evaluate whether the complaint met the legal sufficiency required to survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion. The Second Circuit ultimately affirmed the District Court’s judgment, agreeing that the claims were legally insufficient.
