Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Does the copyright holder's subjective intent matter for determining 'transformative use'?
Question:
In the opinion, Santos argued that because he created the videos for a specific purpose, Kimmel's use wasn't transformative. The court cited Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith and said transformative use turns on the 'reasonable observer,' not the subjective intent of the creator or user. Can someone explain the doctrinal shift here? Why is the plaintiff's original intent irrelevant to the 'purpose and character' factor?
Answer:
**No, the copyright holder’s subjective intent is not the controlling factor in determining whether a use is transformative.**

Based on the opinion in *Santos v. Kimmel*, 2d Cir. 2025, the Second Circuit explicitly rejects the argument that the original creator's subjective purpose dictates the "purpose and character" analysis under the first fair use factor. Instead, the court applies an objective standard centered on the "reasonable observer."

### The Legal Standard: Objective vs. Subjective

In assessing the first fair use factor (17 U.S.C. § 107(1)), courts focus on whether the secondary use is **transformative**. A use is transformative if it "adds something new, with a further purpose or different character," rather than merely superseding the original.

*Santos* clarifies that this inquiry is objective. Relying on the Supreme Court’s recent decision in *Andy Warhol Foundation for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508 (2023), the Second Circuit states:

> "But whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user."

The court emphasizes that subjective intent—whether the user's or the copyright holder's—does not determine the purpose of the use. This ensures that fair use analysis remains grounded in how the work is actually perceived in the marketplace, rather than hidden motivations.

### Application in *Santos*

Santos argued that Kimmel’s use was not transformative because Kimmel had "instigated the creation of the original Works" and thus designed their original purpose. Santos contended that his own subjective purpose in creating the videos was the same as Kimmel’s use: to show a willingness to "say absurd things for money." If this subjective intent were controlling, the uses would be identical, and the use would not be transformative.

The court rejected this argument by looking at the **reasonable observer’s perspective**:

1.  **Objective Perception:** The court noted that Santos’s own original allegations acknowledged that a reasonable observer would view the videos as conveying "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity."
2.  **Discrepancy:** This objective perception contradicted Santos’s claimed subjective intent (saying absurd things for money).
3.  **Result:** Because the reasonable observer perceives the original work as positive encouragement, Kimmel’s use—which mocked Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money—added a new, critical purpose. Therefore, the use was transformative regardless of Santos’s internal intent.

### Why the Plaintiff's Original Intent Is Irrelevant

The opinion suggests that prioritizing the plaintiff's subjective intent would undermine the flexibility of the fair use doctrine. If a copyright holder could define the "purpose" of their work solely through internal intent, they could potentially block transformative uses that the public clearly understands as different from the original work's function.

By anchoring the analysis to the "reasonable observer," the court ensures that the fair use defense protects commentary and criticism (like Kimmel’s parody) even when the copyright holder claims a different, subjective motivation for the original creation.

### Note on Bad Faith

While Santos also argued that Kimmel’s bad faith (false representations) should nullify fair use, the court noted that while *Harper & Row* once suggested fair use presupposes good faith, the Supreme Court has since distanced itself from that statement. The court found the complaint depicted defendants as motivated by criticism and commentary, not market supplantation, further supporting fair use under the first factor.

***

**Summary:** Under *Santos*, the "purpose and character" factor is determined by the **reasonable observer's perception** of the new work's relationship to the old, not by the **subjective intent** of the copyright holder.
