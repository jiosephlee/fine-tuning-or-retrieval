Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

## The Reasonable Observer Prevails: Why Santos’s Subjective Intent Failed the Fair Use Test

The Second Circuit’s recent decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* offers a timely clarification of fair use doctrine in the post-*Andy Warhol Foundation* era, particularly regarding the role of subjective intent in transformative use analysis. While the case features the high-profile legal battle between former Congressman George Santos and late-night host Jimmy Kimmel, the legal significance extends far beyond the spectacle of the "Will Santos Say It?" segments. At its core, the opinion reinforces that the fair use inquiry is an objective one, centered on how a reasonable observer perceives the secondary work, rather than the subjective motivations of the original creator. For content creators, media outlets, and legal practitioners, this ruling underscores the robust protection afforded to parody and commentary, even when the underlying material was generated through deceptive means.

The crux of the dispute turned on the first fair use factor: the purpose and character of the use. Santos argued that because he created the Cameo videos to convey feelings of hope and positivity, Kimmel’s use of those same videos to mock his willingness to say absurd things for money could not be transformative. He contended that Kimmel effectively "designed" the original purpose of the works by instigating their creation. Writing for the panel, Circuit Judge Lohier rejected this argument, relying heavily on the Supreme Court’s guidance in *Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*. The Second Circuit emphasized that whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or the secondary user. Because a reasonable observer would perceive the aired segments as criticism regarding Santos’s conduct rather than the original messages of encouragement, the use was deemed transformative. This distinction is vital; it prevents copyright holders from shielding their works from criticism simply by asserting that their original creative intent was pure or benevolent.

Santos also attempted to bypass the transformative use analysis by invoking the "bad faith" doctrine, arguing that Kimmel’s use of fictitious names to solicit the videos nullified the fair use defense. He pointed to *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises*, where the Supreme Court suggested that fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing. The Second Circuit, however, noted that the Supreme Court has since distanced itself from that specific language. Furthermore, the court found that Santos’s own complaint contradicted any claim that the defendants intended to supplant his commercially valuable right in the videos. Instead, the record painted a portrait of defendants motivated by sarcastic criticism and commentary. This rejection of the bad faith argument is significant because it limits the ability of plaintiffs to derail fair use defenses by highlighting deceptive procurement tactics, provided the ultimate use remains transformative and does not usurp the market for the original work.

Beyond the copyright claims, the court’s dismissal of the state law causes of action further insulated Kimmel and ABC from liability. The breach of contract claims failed because Santos was not a party to the Cameo Terms of Service, and Illinois law presumes against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties absent an express declaration. Similarly, the fraudulent inducement claim could not survive because Santos failed to allege any actual out-of-pocket loss under New York law. While these state law dismissals are procedurally straightforward, they complement the copyright holding by closing off alternative avenues for plaintiffs who might attempt to recast copyright disputes as contract or fraud matters when the core injury is the unauthorized use of expressive content.

The downstream implications of *Santos v. Kimmel* are likely to be felt most acutely in the realm of late-night television and digital media commentary. The decision affirms that the "gotcha" nature of a prank does not automatically strip away fair use protections if the resulting broadcast adds a new purpose or character to the original work. It signals to media producers that they may rely on the transformative use defense even when the underlying footage was obtained through unconventional or deceptive means, so long as the new work functions as criticism or commentary. However, the ruling also serves as a cautionary note for public figures: once a work is published, the copyright holder’s ability to control its use diminishes significantly when the secondary use is transformative. As the Second Circuit noted, creators are unlikely to license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions, and the law does not require them to. Ultimately, the reasonable observer standard prevails, ensuring that the flexibility of fair use remains available to foster creativity and public discourse, regardless of the subjective intent behind the original creation.



# Beyond Copyright: Why Privity and Pleading Standards Doomed Santos’s State Law Claims

While the headline-grabbing aspect of *Santos v. Kimmel* is undoubtedly the Second Circuit’s affirmation of the fair use defense regarding George Santos’s Cameo videos, the decision offers a critical, if quieter, lesson in civil procedure and state law pleading. Former Congressman Santos attempted to bypass the hurdles of copyright law by framing his grievance as a series of state law violations, including breach of contract and fraudulent inducement. The Second Circuit’s recent opinion, authored by Judge Lohier, systematically dismantles these alternative theories, reinforcing that state law claims cannot simply serve as a backdoor for copyright disputes and must survive rigorous pleading standards on their own merits. For litigants and legal observers, the state law analysis provides a stark reminder of the limitations imposed by privity, implied contract doctrine, and specific damages requirements in New York.

The court’s rejection of Santos’s breach of contract claims begins with a fundamental issue of privity regarding the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos argued that Jimmy Kimmel breached the platform’s terms by using fictitious names to solicit the videos. However, the Second Circuit noted that Santos was not a party to the agreement between Kimmel and Cameo. Applying Illinois law, which governs the Terms of Service, the court highlighted the state’s strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties. The opinion emphasizes that for a third party to enforce a contract, the implication must be so strong it is practically an express declaration. The court found no such declaration in the Terms of Service, noting that other provisions explicitly contained third-party beneficiary language where intended. This distinction is vital for digital platform litigation; it signals that users cannot easily enforce platform terms against other users merely because the platform’s rules were violated, absent clear contractual intent to create third-party rights.

Even if privity were not a barrier, Santos’s claim for breach of implied-in-fact contract failed under New York law due to insufficient pleading. To survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, a complaint must allege the essential terms of the agreement and a meeting of minds. The Second Circuit found Santos’s complaint conclusory, failing to specify which provisions of an alleged implied contract were breached or how the parties agreed that Kimmel would adhere to the Terms of Service. This ruling underscores the Second Circuit’s commitment to the *Twombly/Iqbal* plausibility standard, even in state law claims. It suggests that courts will not allow plaintiffs to rely on vague assertions of an "implied" agreement when the factual record suggests no actual negotiation or mutual understanding occurred between the parties.

Perhaps the most technical yet decisive hurdle for Santos was the fraudulent inducement claim, which collapsed under New York’s strict damages requirement. Under *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, a plaintiff alleging fraudulent inducement in New York must plead an out-of-pocket loss. The Second Circuit agreed with the District Court that Santos failed to allege any actual financial loss resulting from the alleged fraud. This is a significant doctrinal point for fraud litigation in the Second Circuit; it prevents plaintiffs from converting a dispute over the nature of a transaction into a fraud claim without demonstrating tangible economic harm. In this context, where Santos was paid for the videos (or at least did not allege he lost money in the transaction), the fraud claim was legally insufficient regardless of the deceptive nature of Kimmel’s requests.

From an analytical perspective, this opinion reinforces the boundary between copyright preemption and independent state law viability. While the court did not need to resolve the preemption issue for the contract claims because they failed on state law grounds, the result effectively achieves the same outcome: state law cannot be used to circumvent the protections afforded to transformative commentary. The decision serves as a cautionary tale for plaintiffs attempting to frame high-profile media interactions as contract or fraud disputes. It highlights that courts will scrutinize the mechanics of the relationship—privity, meeting of minds, and specific damages—rather than the public relations spectacle. Downstream, this may encourage more precise pleading in similar cases involving digital platforms and celebrity interactions, but it also cements the difficulty of holding late-night satire liable under state tort and contract theories when the core activity is protected expression.



# Early Dismissal of Fair Use: Is the Second Circuit Lowering the Bar for 12(b)(6) Motions?

## Procedural Efficiency Meets Copyright Defense

The intersection of high-profile celebrity litigation and procedural doctrine often yields significant legal precedents, and the Second Circuit’s recent decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* is no exception. In affirming the dismissal of former Congressman George Santos’s copyright and state law claims against Jimmy Kimmel and ABC, the court addressed a critical procedural question: can the affirmative defense of fair use be resolved at the pleading stage under Rule 12(b)(6)? By relying on recent Second Circuit authority, specifically *Hachette Book Group, Inc. v. Internet Archive* and *TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum*, the panel signaled that fair use is not always a fact-intensive inquiry requiring discovery. When the facts alleged in the complaint clearly demonstrate transformative use, dismissal is not only permissible but appropriate. This decision reinforces a trend toward early resolution of copyright defenses, potentially altering litigation strategy for plaintiffs and defendants alike.

## The Facts and the Fair Use Analysis

The dispute arose after Kimmel used fictitious names to request personalized videos from Santos via the Cameo platform. Santos fulfilled these requests, and Kimmel subsequently aired the videos on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* as part of a mocking segment titled “Will Santos Say It?” Santos sued for copyright infringement and various state law violations. The District Court dismissed the complaint, and the Second Circuit affirmed, focusing heavily on the four statutory factors of fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107.

Central to the court’s reasoning was the first factor: the purpose and character of the use. Santos argued that the use was not transformative because Kimmel instigated the creation of the videos, effectively designing their original purpose. The Second Circuit rejected this subjective argument, emphasizing that whether a use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or the user. Citing the Supreme Court’s decision in *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*, the court noted that the reasonable observer would view the aired segments as commentary on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money, rather than the feelings of hope and positivity Santos intended in the original videos. This distinction allowed the court to conclude that the use was sufficiently transformative to support dismissal at the pleading stage.

## The Role of Bad Faith and Market Harm

Santos also attempted to undermine the fair use defense by pointing to Kimmel’s false representations, arguing this demonstrated bad faith that should nullify the defense. The court acknowledged the Supreme Court’s statement in *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises* that fair use presupposes good faith but noted that the Court has since distanced itself from that formulation. Furthermore, the court found that the complaint itself contradicted any claim that the defendants intended to supplant Santos’s commercially valuable rights. Instead, the complaint painted a picture of defendants motivated by sarcastic criticism.

Regarding the fourth factor, the effect on the potential market, the court applied the standard that fair use analysis does not ask whether the second work damages the market for the first through criticism, but whether it usurps the market by offering a competing substitute. Relying on *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.* and *Hachette*, the Second Circuit concluded that Santos failed to allege market harm because the unlikelihood that creators will license critical reviews or lampoons removes such uses from the notion of a potential licensing market. Consequently, the court held that fair use barred the copyright claims even without further factual development.

## Doctrinal Implications for Copyright Litigation

The affirmance in *Santos* carries significant weight for copyright litigation strategy, particularly regarding the burden on defendants to plead transformative use. While fair use is traditionally an affirmative defense often litigated after discovery, this opinion clarifies that it can serve as a basis for a motion to dismiss when the complaint’s own allegations establish the defense. This lowers the barrier for defendants to seek early termination of cases where the infringing material is clearly used for parody or commentary.

Moreover, the court’s rejection of the subjective intent argument aligns with the post-*Warhol* landscape, reinforcing that the focus must remain on the objective perception of the new work. This shift may discourage plaintiffs from relying on the creator’s original purpose as a shield against fair use claims. Additionally, the dismissal of the bad faith argument suggests that deceptive conduct in obtaining the underlying work, while potentially relevant to other claims, does not automatically vitiate a fair use defense in the context of transformative commentary.

## Downstream Effects on State Law Claims

Beyond copyright, the decision also provides guidance on the viability of state law claims in similar contexts. The court dismissed Santos’s breach of contract claims, noting that he was not a party to the Cameo Terms of Service and that Illinois and New York law presume against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties absent express declaration. Similarly, the fraudulent inducement claim failed because Santos did not allege out-of-pocket loss, a requirement under New York law. These rulings suggest that plaintiffs attempting to circumvent copyright preemption or fair use through state law theories will face steep hurdles if they cannot establish direct contractual privity or specific economic damages.

## Conclusion

*Santos v. Kimmel* serves as a reminder that the Copyright Act’s flexibility extends to procedural mechanisms as well as substantive defenses. By affirming the dismissal based on the face of the complaint, the Second Circuit has validated the use of Rule 12(b)(6) to resolve fair use disputes when the transformative nature of the work is evident from the pleadings. For litigators, this underscores the importance of drafting complaints that can withstand early fair use scrutiny, while for defendants, it offers a viable path to efficiency in cases involving parody and criticism. As courts continue to navigate the balance between protecting creativity and preventing the stifling of commentary, *Santos* reinforces the principle that fair use is a robust shield that can be raised at the very outset of litigation.
