Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Beyond Copyright: Why Privity and Pleading Standards Doomed Santos’s State Law Claims

While the headline-grabbing aspect of *Santos v. Kimmel* is undoubtedly the Second Circuit’s affirmation of the fair use defense regarding George Santos’s Cameo videos, the decision offers a critical, if quieter, lesson in civil procedure and state law pleading. Former Congressman Santos attempted to bypass the hurdles of copyright law by framing his grievance as a series of state law violations, including breach of contract and fraudulent inducement. The Second Circuit’s recent opinion, authored by Judge Lohier, systematically dismantles these alternative theories, reinforcing that state law claims cannot simply serve as a backdoor for copyright disputes and must survive rigorous pleading standards on their own merits. For litigants and legal observers, the state law analysis provides a stark reminder of the limitations imposed by privity, implied contract doctrine, and specific damages requirements in New York.

The court’s rejection of Santos’s breach of contract claims begins with a fundamental issue of privity regarding the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos argued that Jimmy Kimmel breached the platform’s terms by using fictitious names to solicit the videos. However, the Second Circuit noted that Santos was not a party to the agreement between Kimmel and Cameo. Applying Illinois law, which governs the Terms of Service, the court highlighted the state’s strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties. The opinion emphasizes that for a third party to enforce a contract, the implication must be so strong it is practically an express declaration. The court found no such declaration in the Terms of Service, noting that other provisions explicitly contained third-party beneficiary language where intended. This distinction is vital for digital platform litigation; it signals that users cannot easily enforce platform terms against other users merely because the platform’s rules were violated, absent clear contractual intent to create third-party rights.

Even if privity were not a barrier, Santos’s claim for breach of implied-in-fact contract failed under New York law due to insufficient pleading. To survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, a complaint must allege the essential terms of the agreement and a meeting of minds. The Second Circuit found Santos’s complaint conclusory, failing to specify which provisions of an alleged implied contract were breached or how the parties agreed that Kimmel would adhere to the Terms of Service. This ruling underscores the Second Circuit’s commitment to the *Twombly/Iqbal* plausibility standard, even in state law claims. It suggests that courts will not allow plaintiffs to rely on vague assertions of an "implied" agreement when the factual record suggests no actual negotiation or mutual understanding occurred between the parties.

Perhaps the most technical yet decisive hurdle for Santos was the fraudulent inducement claim, which collapsed under New York’s strict damages requirement. Under *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, a plaintiff alleging fraudulent inducement in New York must plead an out-of-pocket loss. The Second Circuit agreed with the District Court that Santos failed to allege any actual financial loss resulting from the alleged fraud. This is a significant doctrinal point for fraud litigation in the Second Circuit; it prevents plaintiffs from converting a dispute over the nature of a transaction into a fraud claim without demonstrating tangible economic harm. In this context, where Santos was paid for the videos (or at least did not allege he lost money in the transaction), the fraud claim was legally insufficient regardless of the deceptive nature of Kimmel’s requests.

From an analytical perspective, this opinion reinforces the boundary between copyright preemption and independent state law viability. While the court did not need to resolve the preemption issue for the contract claims because they failed on state law grounds, the result effectively achieves the same outcome: state law cannot be used to circumvent the protections afforded to transformative commentary. The decision serves as a cautionary tale for plaintiffs attempting to frame high-profile media interactions as contract or fraud disputes. It highlights that courts will scrutinize the mechanics of the relationship—privity, meeting of minds, and specific damages—rather than the public relations spectacle. Downstream, this may encourage more precise pleading in similar cases involving digital platforms and celebrity interactions, but it also cements the difficulty of holding late-night satire liable under state tort and contract theories when the core activity is protected expression.
