Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

## The Reasonable Observer Prevails: Why Santos’s Subjective Intent Failed the Fair Use Test

The Second Circuit’s recent decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* offers a timely clarification of fair use doctrine in the post-*Andy Warhol Foundation* era, particularly regarding the role of subjective intent in transformative use analysis. While the case features the high-profile legal battle between former Congressman George Santos and late-night host Jimmy Kimmel, the legal significance extends far beyond the spectacle of the "Will Santos Say It?" segments. At its core, the opinion reinforces that the fair use inquiry is an objective one, centered on how a reasonable observer perceives the secondary work, rather than the subjective motivations of the original creator. For content creators, media outlets, and legal practitioners, this ruling underscores the robust protection afforded to parody and commentary, even when the underlying material was generated through deceptive means.

The crux of the dispute turned on the first fair use factor: the purpose and character of the use. Santos argued that because he created the Cameo videos to convey feelings of hope and positivity, Kimmel’s use of those same videos to mock his willingness to say absurd things for money could not be transformative. He contended that Kimmel effectively "designed" the original purpose of the works by instigating their creation. Writing for the panel, Circuit Judge Lohier rejected this argument, relying heavily on the Supreme Court’s guidance in *Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*. The Second Circuit emphasized that whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or the secondary user. Because a reasonable observer would perceive the aired segments as criticism regarding Santos’s conduct rather than the original messages of encouragement, the use was deemed transformative. This distinction is vital; it prevents copyright holders from shielding their works from criticism simply by asserting that their original creative intent was pure or benevolent.

Santos also attempted to bypass the transformative use analysis by invoking the "bad faith" doctrine, arguing that Kimmel’s use of fictitious names to solicit the videos nullified the fair use defense. He pointed to *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises*, where the Supreme Court suggested that fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing. The Second Circuit, however, noted that the Supreme Court has since distanced itself from that specific language. Furthermore, the court found that Santos’s own complaint contradicted any claim that the defendants intended to supplant his commercially valuable right in the videos. Instead, the record painted a portrait of defendants motivated by sarcastic criticism and commentary. This rejection of the bad faith argument is significant because it limits the ability of plaintiffs to derail fair use defenses by highlighting deceptive procurement tactics, provided the ultimate use remains transformative and does not usurp the market for the original work.

Beyond the copyright claims, the court’s dismissal of the state law causes of action further insulated Kimmel and ABC from liability. The breach of contract claims failed because Santos was not a party to the Cameo Terms of Service, and Illinois law presumes against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties absent an express declaration. Similarly, the fraudulent inducement claim could not survive because Santos failed to allege any actual out-of-pocket loss under New York law. While these state law dismissals are procedurally straightforward, they complement the copyright holding by closing off alternative avenues for plaintiffs who might attempt to recast copyright disputes as contract or fraud matters when the core injury is the unauthorized use of expressive content.

The downstream implications of *Santos v. Kimmel* are likely to be felt most acutely in the realm of late-night television and digital media commentary. The decision affirms that the "gotcha" nature of a prank does not automatically strip away fair use protections if the resulting broadcast adds a new purpose or character to the original work. It signals to media producers that they may rely on the transformative use defense even when the underlying footage was obtained through unconventional or deceptive means, so long as the new work functions as criticism or commentary. However, the ruling also serves as a cautionary note for public figures: once a work is published, the copyright holder’s ability to control its use diminishes significantly when the secondary use is transformative. As the Second Circuit noted, creators are unlikely to license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions, and the law does not require them to. Ultimately, the reasonable observer standard prevails, ensuring that the flexibility of fair use remains available to foster creativity and public discourse, regardless of the subjective intent behind the original creation.
