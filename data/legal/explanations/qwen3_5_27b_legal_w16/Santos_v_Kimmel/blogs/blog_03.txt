Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Early Dismissal of Fair Use: Is the Second Circuit Lowering the Bar for 12(b)(6) Motions?

## Procedural Efficiency Meets Copyright Defense

The intersection of high-profile celebrity litigation and procedural doctrine often yields significant legal precedents, and the Second Circuit’s recent decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* is no exception. In affirming the dismissal of former Congressman George Santos’s copyright and state law claims against Jimmy Kimmel and ABC, the court addressed a critical procedural question: can the affirmative defense of fair use be resolved at the pleading stage under Rule 12(b)(6)? By relying on recent Second Circuit authority, specifically *Hachette Book Group, Inc. v. Internet Archive* and *TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum*, the panel signaled that fair use is not always a fact-intensive inquiry requiring discovery. When the facts alleged in the complaint clearly demonstrate transformative use, dismissal is not only permissible but appropriate. This decision reinforces a trend toward early resolution of copyright defenses, potentially altering litigation strategy for plaintiffs and defendants alike.

## The Facts and the Fair Use Analysis

The dispute arose after Kimmel used fictitious names to request personalized videos from Santos via the Cameo platform. Santos fulfilled these requests, and Kimmel subsequently aired the videos on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* as part of a mocking segment titled “Will Santos Say It?” Santos sued for copyright infringement and various state law violations. The District Court dismissed the complaint, and the Second Circuit affirmed, focusing heavily on the four statutory factors of fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107.

Central to the court’s reasoning was the first factor: the purpose and character of the use. Santos argued that the use was not transformative because Kimmel instigated the creation of the videos, effectively designing their original purpose. The Second Circuit rejected this subjective argument, emphasizing that whether a use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or the user. Citing the Supreme Court’s decision in *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*, the court noted that the reasonable observer would view the aired segments as commentary on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money, rather than the feelings of hope and positivity Santos intended in the original videos. This distinction allowed the court to conclude that the use was sufficiently transformative to support dismissal at the pleading stage.

## The Role of Bad Faith and Market Harm

Santos also attempted to undermine the fair use defense by pointing to Kimmel’s false representations, arguing this demonstrated bad faith that should nullify the defense. The court acknowledged the Supreme Court’s statement in *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises* that fair use presupposes good faith but noted that the Court has since distanced itself from that formulation. Furthermore, the court found that the complaint itself contradicted any claim that the defendants intended to supplant Santos’s commercially valuable rights. Instead, the complaint painted a picture of defendants motivated by sarcastic criticism.

Regarding the fourth factor, the effect on the potential market, the court applied the standard that fair use analysis does not ask whether the second work damages the market for the first through criticism, but whether it usurps the market by offering a competing substitute. Relying on *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.* and *Hachette*, the Second Circuit concluded that Santos failed to allege market harm because the unlikelihood that creators will license critical reviews or lampoons removes such uses from the notion of a potential licensing market. Consequently, the court held that fair use barred the copyright claims even without further factual development.

## Doctrinal Implications for Copyright Litigation

The affirmance in *Santos* carries significant weight for copyright litigation strategy, particularly regarding the burden on defendants to plead transformative use. While fair use is traditionally an affirmative defense often litigated after discovery, this opinion clarifies that it can serve as a basis for a motion to dismiss when the complaint’s own allegations establish the defense. This lowers the barrier for defendants to seek early termination of cases where the infringing material is clearly used for parody or commentary.

Moreover, the court’s rejection of the subjective intent argument aligns with the post-*Warhol* landscape, reinforcing that the focus must remain on the objective perception of the new work. This shift may discourage plaintiffs from relying on the creator’s original purpose as a shield against fair use claims. Additionally, the dismissal of the bad faith argument suggests that deceptive conduct in obtaining the underlying work, while potentially relevant to other claims, does not automatically vitiate a fair use defense in the context of transformative commentary.

## Downstream Effects on State Law Claims

Beyond copyright, the decision also provides guidance on the viability of state law claims in similar contexts. The court dismissed Santos’s breach of contract claims, noting that he was not a party to the Cameo Terms of Service and that Illinois and New York law presume against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties absent express declaration. Similarly, the fraudulent inducement claim failed because Santos did not allege out-of-pocket loss, a requirement under New York law. These rulings suggest that plaintiffs attempting to circumvent copyright preemption or fair use through state law theories will face steep hurdles if they cannot establish direct contractual privity or specific economic damages.

## Conclusion

*Santos v. Kimmel* serves as a reminder that the Copyright Act’s flexibility extends to procedural mechanisms as well as substantive defenses. By affirming the dismissal based on the face of the complaint, the Second Circuit has validated the use of Rule 12(b)(6) to resolve fair use disputes when the transformative nature of the work is evident from the pleadings. For litigators, this underscores the importance of drafting complaints that can withstand early fair use scrutiny, while for defendants, it offers a viable path to efficiency in cases involving parody and criticism. As courts continue to navigate the balance between protecting creativity and preventing the stifling of commentary, *Santos* reinforces the principle that fair use is a robust shield that can be raised at the very outset of litigation.
