Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Chapter 1: Factual Background and Parties

This chapter introduces the factual narrative and procedural posture of *Santos v. Kimmel*, a dispute arising from the intersection of digital content creation, late-night television satire, and intellectual property law. To understand the legal claims and the court’s ultimate disposition, a law student must first grasp the sequence of events that gave rise to the litigation and the specific roles played by each litigant. The opinion provides a concise summary of these facts in the introductory section, which serves as the foundation for the District Court’s dismissal and the Second Circuit’s subsequent review. By analyzing these elements, students can see how factual allegations map onto legal standards, particularly regarding copyright ownership and the procedural mechanisms for dismissing claims that lack legal merit.

## The Cameo Platform and the Creation of Personalized Videos

The factual genesis of this dispute lies in the operation of the Cameo platform, a service that allows users to request personalized video messages from public figures. According to the opinion, the defendant, Jimmy Kimmel, utilized this platform to initiate contact with the plaintiff, George Santos. Crucially, the opinion notes that Kimmel did not identify himself during this process; instead, he used "various fictitious names" to submit the requests. This detail is significant because it establishes the context of the interaction: Santos believed he was fulfilling requests from private individuals rather than a television host preparing content for broadcast.

Santos fulfilled each request by creating personalized videos. These videos constitute the "original Works" referenced in the copyright claims. The opinion indicates that these works were published on the same day they were created, a fact supported by copyright registrations taken judicial notice by the court. For the purpose of understanding the copyright infringement claim, it is essential to recognize that Santos, as the creator, holds the copyright to these videos. The dispute arises not from the creation of the videos themselves, but from how they were subsequently utilized by the defendants. The opinion frames the creation of these videos as the plaintiff's commercial activity, noting that a reasonable observer would view the videos as conveying "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." This characterization of the original works' purpose is a factual premise that the court later weighs against the purpose of the secondary use in its fair use analysis.

## The 'Will Santos Say It?' Television Segments on Jimmy Kimmel Live!

The alleged infringement occurred when the defendants took the videos created by Santos and broadcast them on the late-night television program *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* ("JKL"). The opinion specifies that Kimmel aired the videos as part of a series of segments titled "Will Santos Say It?" The stated purpose of these segments, as acknowledged by Santos in his own allegations and accepted by the District Court, was "to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money." This factual distinction is critical: the defendants did not merely replay the videos; they incorporated them into a mocking series designed to critique Santos’s behavior.

From a legal perspective, this broadcast constitutes the "secondary use" of the copyrighted material. The nature of this use—satire, commentary, and mockery—distinguishes it from a straightforward reproduction of the work. The opinion highlights that the defendants were motivated by "(sarcastic) criticism and commentary" rather than a desire to "supplant" Santos’s commercially valuable right in the videos. Understanding this factual context is necessary for later chapters, as the court will analyze whether this transformative purpose outweighs the copyright holder's rights under the fair use doctrine. For now, the student must note that the factual record establishes a conflict between the plaintiff's intent in creating the videos (personalized encouragement) and the defendants' intent in using them (public commentary and satire).

## Identification of Parties: Santos, Kimmel, ABC, and Disney

The litigation involves multiple parties, each with a distinct role in the production and broadcast of the contested segments. The plaintiff is George Santos, identified in the opinion as a "Former Congressman." He is the creator of the videos and the alleged copyright holder. The defendants are Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. ("ABC"), and The Walt Disney Company. Kimmel is the host of *JKL* and the individual who initiated the Cameo requests and aired the segments. ABC is the network that airs the show, and Disney is ABC’s parent company.

Identifying all three entities as defendants is procedurally significant. In media litigation, plaintiffs often sue the individual host, the broadcasting network, and the parent corporation to ensure all potential liable parties are bound by the judgment. The opinion treats them collectively as "Defendants" for the purpose of the appeal. Understanding the relationship between these parties helps explain the scope of liability. If the court finds infringement, the question of who bears responsibility—whether the host who selected the content, the network that aired it, or the parent company that owns the network—becomes relevant. However, in this specific case, the court’s analysis focuses on the nature of the use itself rather than the individual liability of each defendant, as the fair use defense applies to the conduct of the group collectively in producing the segments.

## The District Court's Initial Dismissal of the Complaint

Before reaching the Second Circuit, this case was litigated in the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York, presided over by Judge Cote. The procedural mechanism used by the District Court to resolve the case was a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. A Rule 12(b)(6) motion asserts that the plaintiff has failed to state a claim upon which relief can be granted. In practical terms, this means that even if all the facts alleged in the complaint are true, the law does not provide a remedy for the plaintiff's grievance.

The District Court granted the motion, dismissing both the copyright infringement claims and the state law claims. Regarding the copyright claims, the District Court concluded they were barred by the fair use doctrine. Regarding the state law claims (breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement), the court found they were either preempted by the Copyright Act or failed to state a claim under applicable state law. Santos appealed this final judgment to the Second Circuit. The appellate court reviews such dismissals *de novo*, meaning the Second Circuit judges examine the legal questions anew without deferring to the District Court’s conclusions. This standard of review is explicitly noted in the opinion, which cites *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.* The *de novo* standard is important for students to understand because it signals that the appellate court will independently evaluate whether the complaint met the legal sufficiency required to survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion. The Second Circuit ultimately affirmed the District Court’s judgment, agreeing that the claims were legally insufficient.

# Chapter 2: Procedural Posture and Standard of Review

This chapter examines the procedural framework governing the appeal in *Santos v. Kimmel*. George Santos appealed a final judgment from the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York, which had dismissed his complaint against Jimmy Kimmel, ABC, and Disney. The District Court dismissed the action under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, concluding that Santos failed to state a claim upon which relief could be granted. To understand the Second Circuit’s decision to affirm this dismissal, a law student must first grasp the procedural standards applied by the appellate court. The Second Circuit does not simply accept the District Court’s conclusion; instead, it applies specific standards of review and evidentiary rules that dictate what materials the appellate court may consider when evaluating the legal sufficiency of the complaint.

## Rule 12(b)(6) Failure to State a Claim Standard

The procedural posture of this case is defined by the District Court’s dismissal of Santos’s complaint for failure to state a claim under Rule 12(b)(6). A dismissal under this rule signifies that, even assuming the factual allegations in the complaint are true, the law does not provide a remedy for the injuries described. In *Santos*, the District Court concluded that the copyright claims were barred by the fair use doctrine and that the state law claims were either preempted by federal law or failed to state a claim under state law. The Second Circuit’s task was to determine whether the District Court correctly applied this standard. The opinion indicates that a claim can be dismissed at the pleading stage if the defense is so clearly established by the complaint itself that no factual development is necessary. For instance, the court notes that fair use may be "so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim." This procedural mechanism allows courts to resolve certain legal defenses, such as fair use or preemption, without proceeding to a full trial, provided the complaint’s own allegations or attached documents make the defense evident.

## De Novo Review on Appeal

When reviewing a District Court’s dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6), the Second Circuit applies a standard of review known as *de novo*. Citing *DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C.*, the court explains that it reviews the dismissal without deference to the lower court’s legal conclusions. This means the appellate court examines the legal issues as if it were hearing them for the first time. If the Second Circuit disagrees with the District Court’s interpretation of the Copyright Act or state contract law, it is free to reverse the decision. However, this *de novo* standard applies to questions of law; the court still must accept the factual allegations in the complaint as true for the purpose of the motion. The *de novo* standard is critical in this case because it allowed the Second Circuit to independently evaluate whether the fair use defense and state law pleading deficiencies were sufficient to warrant dismissal, rather than relying solely on the District Court’s analysis.

## Judicial Notice of Copyright Registrations

In evaluating the copyright infringement claims, the Second Circuit clarified the scope of evidence it could consider during a Rule 12(b)(6) review. While the court generally limits its review to the complaint and its attachments, it may take judicial notice of specific public records. The opinion cites *Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp.* and *TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum* to establish that the court may take judicial notice of "copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry." In *Santos*, this procedural tool was essential for establishing the publication dates of the videos at issue. The relevant copyright registrations showed that the videos were published on the same day they were created. This fact was pivotal for the court’s analysis of the fair use factors, specifically regarding the nature of the copyrighted work and market harm. By taking judicial notice of these registrations, the court could treat the publication dates as established facts without requiring additional evidence, reinforcing the conclusion that the copyright claims failed at the pleading stage.

## Pleading Requirements and Documents Incorporated by Reference

The Second Circuit’s review is constrained by specific rules regarding what documents and allegations constitute the record for a Rule 12(b)(6) motion. Under *DiFolco*, the court considers "the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference in the complaint." This standard ensures that the court evaluates the complaint in the context of the actual documents that define the parties’ relationship. For example, the court examined the Cameo Terms of Service and the complaint’s specific allegations regarding the videos' intended message. Furthermore, the opinion highlights that pleading requirements vary by claim. In the state law section, the court noted that Santos failed to allege the "essential terms of the parties’ contract" as required by *Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc.* Similarly, for the fraudulent inducement claim, the court applied New York law requiring an allegation of actual out-of-pocket loss, citing *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.* These examples illustrate that a complaint must not only allege facts but must do so with sufficient specificity to survive a motion to dismiss, particularly when the complaint’s own language contradicts the legal theories asserted.

# Chapter 3: Copyright Infringement and Fair Use Analysis

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit addressed whether a copyright infringement claim could survive a motion to dismiss when the defendant asserted the affirmative defense of fair use. The case arose from a series of segments on the late-night show *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* in which host Jimmy Kimmel, using fictitious names, solicited personalized videos from former Congressman George Santos via the Cameo platform. Kimmel subsequently aired these videos to mock Santos’s willingness to perform for money. The District Court dismissed Santos’s copyright claims, and the Second Circuit affirmed, holding that the fair use doctrine barred the claims even at the pleading stage. This chapter examines the Court’s application of the fair use analysis, focusing on the statutory factors and the specific reasoning regarding transformative use and market harm.

## The Four Statutory Factors of 17 U.S.C. § 107

To understand the Court’s reasoning, one must first grasp the statutory framework governing fair use. As the Second Circuit explained, the affirmative defense of fair use “excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior,” serving to avoid a rigid application of the Copyright Act that might stifle creativity. The Court grounded its analysis in 17 U.S.C. § 107, which outlines four non-exclusive factors for determining whether a use is fair. These factors are: (1) the purpose and character of the use; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.

The opinion emphasizes that fair use is a flexible concept whose application varies depending on the context. While fair use is often a fact-intensive inquiry, the Court noted that it may be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” at the pleading stage. In *Santos*, the Court reviewed the District Court’s dismissal *de novo*, considering the facts alleged in the complaint and documents incorporated by reference. The Court concluded that the undisputed facts regarding the nature of the segments and the videos themselves made the fair use defense dispositive, allowing the court to resolve the issue without a full trial on the merits.

## Purpose and Character: Transformative Use vs. Original Intent

The Court placed significant weight on the first statutory factor: the purpose and character of the use. In assessing this factor, the Second Circuit focused chiefly on the degree to which the use is “transformative.” A use is transformative if the new work adds something new, with a further purpose or different character, rather than merely superseding the objects of the original creation. In this case, the core dispute centered on the purpose of the videos. Santos argued that the use was not transformative because Kimmel had “instigated the creation of the original Works” and was therefore the “designer[]” of their original purpose. Santos contended that because Kimmel requested the videos, the purpose of the videos was inherently tied to Kimmel’s intent.

The Court rejected this argument, distinguishing between who requested the work and what the work actually conveys when used. The District Court had found that the purpose of the allegedly infringing use was to comment on the willingness of Santos to say absurd things for money. While Santos did not dispute this finding, he argued that this was also his original purpose in making the videos. The Court clarified that the transformative nature of a secondary use turns on the new context and meaning added by the secondary user, not on the subjective intent of the copyright holder regarding why they created the original work. The Court agreed with the District Court that the first factor strongly supported a finding of fair use because the segments served a critical and mocking purpose distinct from the original videos.

## Subjective Intent vs. The Reasonable Observer Test

A critical pedagogical point in *Santos* is the standard used to determine the “purpose” of a use. Santos argued that his original intent in creating the videos—to convey “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity”—should control the analysis. The Court explicitly rejected the relevance of the subjective intent of the copyright holder or the secondary user in determining the purpose of the use. Citing the Supreme Court’s decision in *Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, the Second Circuit held that the purpose of the use is determined by what a reasonable observer thinks, not by the subjective interpretation of a court or the parties involved.

In applying this reasonable observer test, the Court looked at how the videos were presented in the new context. Although Santos claimed the videos conveyed positivity, the Court noted that a reasonable observer viewing the segments on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* would understand them as part of a mocking series titled “Will Santos Say It?” The context of the late-night show transformed the meaning of the clips from sincere messages to examples of absurdity performed for payment. This distinction underscores that in fair use analysis, the objective perception of the work’s new meaning often outweighs the creator’s subjective motivation for the original work.

## The Role of Bad Faith in Fair Use Defenses

Santos further attempted to defeat the fair use defense by arguing that Kimmel’s false representations on the Cameo platform demonstrated bad faith, which should nullify the defense. Santos relied on the Supreme Court’s statement in *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters.* that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.” The Second Circuit acknowledged this precedent but noted that the Supreme Court has since distanced itself from this specific statement. More importantly, the Court analyzed whether the alleged bad faith actually undermined the fair use defense in this context.

The Court reasoned that the complaint contradicted any claim that the Defendants intended to “supplant” Santos’s commercially valuable right in the videos. Instead, the complaint painted a portrait of defendants motivated by sarcastic criticism and commentary. The Court distinguished between uses that act as a market substitute and those that serve as criticism. Because the segments were designed to mock and comment rather than to replace the market for the original videos, the alleged bad faith regarding the solicitation of the videos did not negate the transformative, critical nature of the broadcast. Thus, the first factor remained supportive of fair use despite the disputed conduct during the creation of the underlying works.

## Market Harm and Potential Licensing Markets for Parody

Finally, the Court addressed the fourth statutory factor: the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. The Court clarified the standard for market harm, noting that the inquiry is not whether the second work would damage the market for the first through parody or criticism. Instead, the question is whether the second work “usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Citing *Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive*, the Court explained that criticism and parody generally do not constitute market harm in this sense.

The Court relied on the Supreme Court’s reasoning in *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.*, noting that the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market. In *Santos*, the Court found it clear on the face of the complaint that Santos had not suffered market harm within the meaning of the fourth factor. The segments did not act as a substitute for the original Cameo videos; rather, they served as a critique of the platform and Santos’s participation in it. Consequently, because the use was transformative and did not usurp the market for the original works, the Court affirmed the District Court’s conclusion that fair use barred the copyright claims.

# Chapter 4: State Law Claims: Contract and Fraud

## Copyright Preemption of State Law Claims

In analyzing the state law claims, the Second Circuit first addressed the District Court’s dismissal of Santos’s breach of contract and breach of implied contract claims on the ground that they were preempted by the Copyright Act. Preemption occurs when federal law occupies a field of regulation or conflicts with state law, thereby invalidating the state law claim. Here, the District Court concluded that the Copyright Act preempted Santos’s contract claims, suggesting that the state law causes of action were not distinct enough from the federal copyright infringement claim. On appeal, Santos argued that his state law claims were “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim,” citing Second Circuit precedent such as *Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc.* to support the position that preemption should not apply.

However, the Second Circuit did not reach a definitive conclusion on the preemption issue. Instead, the court applied a principle of judicial economy often observed in appellate practice: when a case can be resolved on narrower or more straightforward grounds, courts will avoid deciding complex or unnecessary legal questions. Because the court determined that Santos’s state law contract claims failed to state a claim for relief under applicable state law standards, it deemed it unnecessary to resolve whether the Copyright Act preempted those claims. This approach teaches students that appellate courts may affirm a dismissal on alternative grounds if the lower court’s judgment is correct for any reason, thereby preserving the finality of the judgment without expanding or clarifying federal preemption doctrine in this specific context.

## Third-Party Beneficiary Status Under Cameo Terms (Illinois Law)

The court next examined Santos’s direct breach of contract claim, which relied on the Terms of Service for the Cameo platform. A critical threshold issue in contract law is privity of contract, which generally requires that a plaintiff be a party to the agreement to enforce its terms. In this case, the Terms of Service were an agreement between the user (Kimmel) and the platform, not between Kimmel and Santos. Consequently, Santos sought to enforce the Terms of Service as a third-party beneficiary. The opinion notes that the Terms of Service are governed by Illinois law, which establishes a strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.

Under the Illinois standard cited in *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur*, a contract must contain language so strong as to be practically an express declaration that it applies to third parties. The court observed that the Cameo Terms of Service contained other provisions that explicitly included third-party beneficiary language. The presence of such language in some sections of the contract suggested that the drafters knew how to include third-party rights when they intended to do so. Because Santos identified no express declaration within the relevant provisions that the agreement applied to him, the court found he lacked standing to enforce the Terms of Service directly. This reasoning illustrates how courts interpret contract language holistically; the inclusion of specific third-party language in one part of an agreement can imply the exclusion of such rights in other parts where the language is absent.

## Implied-in-Fact Contract Requirements (New York Law)

Santos also pursued a claim for breach of an implied-in-fact contract, which the court analyzed under New York law. Unlike an express contract, an implied-in-fact contract is inferred from the conduct of the parties and the surrounding circumstances rather than written or spoken words. To survive a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6), a plaintiff must allege, in nonconclusory language, the essential terms of the agreement and demonstrate that the parties reached a meeting of minds. The Second Circuit emphasized that Santos’s complaint failed to plead the essential terms of the putative implied contract, specifically regarding which provisions of the Terms of Service the Defendants allegedly breached.

Furthermore, the court required a plausible allegation that there was an indication of a meeting of minds constituting an agreement that Kimmel would adhere to the Terms of Service in his dealings with Santos. Citing New York case law such as *Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc.*, the court noted that the complaint did not allege specific facts showing that the Defendants intended to be bound by the Terms of Service in this context. This section of the opinion highlights the pleading standards required for contract claims in federal court; general assertions of a contract are insufficient without specific factual allegations regarding the terms and the mutual agreement to those terms. Because Santos could not establish the essential elements of an implied-in-fact contract under New York law, the claim was dismissed independently of the preemption question.

## Fraudulent Inducement and the Out-of-Pocket Loss Requirement

The final state law claim addressed by the court was for fraudulent inducement. Under New York law, the elements of fraud are strict, and the required damages are specific. The District Court dismissed this claim for failure to allege any out-of-pocket loss, a requirement established in *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.* The Second Circuit affirmed this dismissal, agreeing that Santos failed to allege he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud. The court relied on *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.*, reinforcing that New York courts require proof of actual financial loss directly resulting from the fraud to sustain a claim.

This ruling underscores the importance of damage elements in tort claims, particularly fraud. Even if a plaintiff can prove that a defendant made false representations with the intent to induce reliance, the claim may still fail if the plaintiff does not allege specific monetary harm that flows from that reliance. In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the complaint did not sufficiently plead that Santos incurred out-of-pocket expenses or lost money in a way that satisfied this statutory and common law requirement. By affirming the dismissal on this ground, the Second Circuit reinforced that the pleading standard for fraud requires specific factual support for the damages element, not just general allegations of harm or reputational injury. Consequently, all state law claims were dismissed, leading to the affirmation of the District Court’s judgment.

# Chapter 5: Holding, Disposition, and Legal Significance

## Affirmance of the District Court Judgment

The final section of any appellate opinion typically details the court's ultimate decision regarding the lower court's ruling. In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit concluded its analysis by affirming the judgment of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York. To understand this disposition, a law student must first recognize the procedural posture of the case. The District Court had dismissed Santos’s complaint under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure for failure to state a claim. When an appellate court reviews a dismissal at this stage, it applies a *de novo* standard of review, meaning the appellate court considers the matter anew without deference to the lower court’s legal conclusions. The Second Circuit confirmed this standard, noting that it considers the facts alleged in the complaint, attached documents, and matters subject to judicial notice.

The Court’s holding was twofold, addressing both the federal copyright claims and the state law claims. Regarding the copyright infringement claims, the Second Circuit agreed with the District Court that the affirmative defense of fair use barred the claims even at the pleading stage. This means that even assuming all factual allegations in the complaint were true, the law did not support a finding of infringement because the use was fair. Regarding the state law claims for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement, the Court concluded that each claim failed to state a claim for relief. Consequently, the appellate court upheld the District Court’s decision to dismiss the entire case. The term "affirm" indicates that the appellate court found no reversible error in the District Court’s judgment, leaving the dismissal intact.

## Impact on Satirical Parody and Late-Night Television

The Court’s reasoning regarding the copyright claims offers significant guidance for media law, particularly concerning the intersection of user-generated content and late-night satire. The Second Circuit affirmed the District Court’s application of the fair use doctrine, specifically focusing on the first statutory factor: the purpose and character of the use. The Court emphasized that the core inquiry is whether the secondary use is "transformative," meaning it adds something new with a further purpose or different character rather than merely superseding the original work. In this case, the Court found that Kimmel’s use of the videos was transformative because the purpose was to comment on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money, rather than to convey the original message of hope and positivity Santos intended.

This holding reinforces the protection afforded to parody and criticism under the Copyright Act. The Court rejected Santos’s argument that Kimmel’s bad faith or false representations regarding his identity nullified the fair use defense. While noting that the Supreme Court has historically linked fair use to good faith, the Second Circuit clarified that the subjective intent of the user does not determine the purpose of the use; rather, it turns on what a reasonable observer would think. Furthermore, the Court addressed the fourth fair use factor, the effect on the potential market, by distinguishing between market harm caused by criticism and market harm caused by a competing substitute. Because the segments were critical lampoons, they did not usurp the market for the original videos. This reasoning solidifies the legal standing for late-night television programs to utilize content for satirical commentary without fear of copyright liability, provided the use is transformative and does not serve as a market substitute for the original work.

## Implications for Celebrity Cameo Contracts and Terms of Service

Beyond copyright, the opinion provides critical instruction on the pleading standards required for contract and fraud claims involving third-party platforms like Cameo. The Second Circuit’s analysis of the state law claims highlights the strict requirements for establishing standing and liability under contract law. Santos attempted to assert breach of contract claims based on the Cameo Terms of Service. However, the Court pointed out that Santos was not a party to the Terms of Service agreement between the platform and the user (Kimmel). Under the applicable Illinois law, there is a strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties unless the contract expressly declares such intent. Because the Terms of Service lacked an express declaration allowing third parties like Santos to enforce them, the direct breach of contract claim failed.

The Court’s analysis of the implied-in-fact contract claim further illustrates the necessity of specific factual allegations in a complaint. To survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, a plaintiff must allege the essential terms of the alleged contract and a meeting of the minds. The Court found that Santos’s complaint did not plead these essential terms in nonconclusory language, nor did it plausibly allege that Kimmel agreed to adhere to the Terms of Service. Additionally, the dismissal of the fraudulent inducement claim underscores the specific elements required under New York law. The Court affirmed the District Court’s conclusion that Santos failed to allege any actual out-of-pocket loss resulting from the alleged fraud. This aspect of the opinion teaches students that in fraud actions, alleging deception is insufficient; the plaintiff must also plead the requisite damages, such as out-of-pocket loss, to state a valid claim.

## Final Disposition of the Appeal

The conclusion of the opinion formalizes the end of the litigation at the appellate level. After addressing the primary arguments regarding copyright and state law claims, the Second Circuit stated that it had considered Santos’s remaining arguments and found them without merit. The final order is an affirmation of the District Court’s judgment. In the context of federal appellate procedure, this disposition means the case is closed unless the parties seek further review from the Supreme Court of the United States, which is a discretionary process. For the purposes of this case, the dismissal of the complaint stands as the final judgment.

This final disposition serves as a practical example of how appellate courts resolve cases where the lower court’s legal analysis is sound. By affirming the dismissal, the Second Circuit confirmed that Santos could not proceed to discovery or trial on these claims. The opinion demonstrates that when a plaintiff fails to meet the pleading standards for both federal intellectual property claims and state law tort or contract claims, the case can be terminated efficiently at the motion to dismiss stage. For students, this reinforces the importance of understanding the specific elements required for each cause of action and the high bar for establishing fair use defenses in the context of transformative media.
