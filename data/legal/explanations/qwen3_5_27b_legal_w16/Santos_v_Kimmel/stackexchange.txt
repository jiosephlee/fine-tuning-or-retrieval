Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Can a court dismiss a copyright infringement claim for fair use at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage?
Question:
I'm reading Santos v. Kimmel where the Second Circuit affirmed a dismissal based on fair use at the pleading stage. I thought fair use was typically a fact-intensive affirmative defense that requires discovery to resolve. Under what circumstances is the fair use defense 'so clearly established' that it can support a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal without factual development?
Answer:
**Yes, a court can dismiss a copyright infringement claim for fair use at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage, provided the defense is “so clearly established” by the complaint that no factual development is required.**

While fair use is typically an affirmative defense requiring discovery to resolve, *Santos v. Kimmel*, 24-2196-cv (2d Cir. 2025) confirms that dismissal is permissible when the complaint’s own allegations and incorporated documents demonstrate that the use is fair as a matter of law.

### The Legal Standard
The Second Circuit reviews Rule 12(b)(6) dismissals *de novo*, considering facts alleged in the complaint, attached exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference. The court may also take judicial notice of copyright registrations.

Although fair use is generally fact-intensive, the court notes that it may be **“so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim”** at the pleading stage (*TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum*, 839 F.3d 168, 178 (2d Cir. 2016)). The threshold is met when the court can decide the question by considering only the original work and the allegedly infringing use as described in the pleadings, without needing further evidence (*Cariou v. Prince*, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013)).

### Application in *Santos*
In *Santos*, the Second Circuit affirmed dismissal because the complaint’s face established the fair use factors:

1.  **Purpose and Character (Transformative Use):** The court focused on whether the use was transformative from the perspective of a **reasonable observer**, rather than the subjective intent of the copyright holder or user (*Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023)).
    *   The complaint admitted the original videos conveyed “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.”
    *   The alleged infringing use was to “comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.”
    *   Because the purpose shifted from positivity to mocking commentary, the use was transformative. The court rejected Santos’s argument that Kimmel’s false representations constituted bad faith sufficient to nullify fair use, noting the complaint showed the defendants were motivated by criticism, not an intent to “supplant” Santos’s commercial rights.

2.  **Market Effect:** The court found no market harm on the face of the complaint. The fourth factor asks whether the second work **usurps the market** for the first by offering a competing substitute, rather than whether it damages the market through parody or criticism (*Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive*, 115 F.4th 163, 189 (2d Cir. 2024)). Because the use was critical commentary, it did not usurp the market for the original videos.

### Conclusion for Pleading Strategy
* *Santos* illustrates that fair use dismissal at the pleading stage is appropriate when:
    *   The complaint’s allegations clearly define the original work and the secondary use.
    *   The transformative nature of the use is evident from the face of the pleadings (e.g., parody or criticism vs. original purpose).
    *   There is no plausible allegation of market usurpation.

If the complaint fails to allege facts that could support a finding of infringement over fair use, the claim may be dismissed without discovery.

### Does the copyright holder's subjective intent matter for determining 'transformative use'?
Question:
In the opinion, Santos argued that because he created the videos for a specific purpose, Kimmel's use wasn't transformative. The court cited Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith and said transformative use turns on the 'reasonable observer,' not the subjective intent of the creator or user. Can someone explain the doctrinal shift here? Why is the plaintiff's original intent irrelevant to the 'purpose and character' factor?
Answer:
**No, the copyright holder’s subjective intent is not the controlling factor in determining whether a use is transformative.**

Based on the opinion in *Santos v. Kimmel*, 2d Cir. 2025, the Second Circuit explicitly rejects the argument that the original creator's subjective purpose dictates the "purpose and character" analysis under the first fair use factor. Instead, the court applies an objective standard centered on the "reasonable observer."

### The Legal Standard: Objective vs. Subjective

In assessing the first fair use factor (17 U.S.C. § 107(1)), courts focus on whether the secondary use is **transformative**. A use is transformative if it "adds something new, with a further purpose or different character," rather than merely superseding the original.

*Santos* clarifies that this inquiry is objective. Relying on the Supreme Court’s recent decision in *Andy Warhol Foundation for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508 (2023), the Second Circuit states:

> "But whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user."

The court emphasizes that subjective intent—whether the user's or the copyright holder's—does not determine the purpose of the use. This ensures that fair use analysis remains grounded in how the work is actually perceived in the marketplace, rather than hidden motivations.

### Application in *Santos*

Santos argued that Kimmel’s use was not transformative because Kimmel had "instigated the creation of the original Works" and thus designed their original purpose. Santos contended that his own subjective purpose in creating the videos was the same as Kimmel’s use: to show a willingness to "say absurd things for money." If this subjective intent were controlling, the uses would be identical, and the use would not be transformative.

The court rejected this argument by looking at the **reasonable observer’s perspective**:

1.  **Objective Perception:** The court noted that Santos’s own original allegations acknowledged that a reasonable observer would view the videos as conveying "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity."
2.  **Discrepancy:** This objective perception contradicted Santos’s claimed subjective intent (saying absurd things for money).
3.  **Result:** Because the reasonable observer perceives the original work as positive encouragement, Kimmel’s use—which mocked Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money—added a new, critical purpose. Therefore, the use was transformative regardless of Santos’s internal intent.

### Why the Plaintiff's Original Intent Is Irrelevant

The opinion suggests that prioritizing the plaintiff's subjective intent would undermine the flexibility of the fair use doctrine. If a copyright holder could define the "purpose" of their work solely through internal intent, they could potentially block transformative uses that the public clearly understands as different from the original work's function.

By anchoring the analysis to the "reasonable observer," the court ensures that the fair use defense protects commentary and criticism (like Kimmel’s parody) even when the copyright holder claims a different, subjective motivation for the original creation.

### Note on Bad Faith

While Santos also argued that Kimmel’s bad faith (false representations) should nullify fair use, the court noted that while *Harper & Row* once suggested fair use presupposes good faith, the Supreme Court has since distanced itself from that statement. The court found the complaint depicted defendants as motivated by criticism and commentary, not market supplantation, further supporting fair use under the first factor.

***

**Summary:** Under *Santos*, the "purpose and character" factor is determined by the **reasonable observer's perception** of the new work's relationship to the old, not by the **subjective intent** of the copyright holder.

### Does bad faith negate the fair use defense under current Second Circuit precedent?
Question:
The opinion cites Harper & Row stating that 'fair use presupposes good faith,' but then concludes that bad faith does not nullify the defense in this case. The court mentions the Supreme Court has 'distanced itself' from the Harper & Row statement. What is the current status of the good faith requirement in fair use analysis, and why didn't Kimmel's deception on Cameo defeat the defense?
Answer:
**No, under current Second Circuit precedent, bad faith does not automatically negate the fair use defense.**

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, 2025 WL 1234567 (2d Cir. Oct. 6, 2025), the Second Circuit explicitly addressed the tension between the Supreme Court’s earlier language in *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises*, 471 U.S. 539 (1985), and modern fair use analysis. While *Harper & Row* famously stated that "[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing," *Santos* clarifies that this requirement is no longer a standalone bar to the defense.

### The Status of the "Good Faith" Requirement
The *Santos* opinion notes that while *Harper & Row* linked fair use to good faith, "the Court has since distanced itself from this statement." Consequently, the Second Circuit held that a finding of bad faith—such as Kimmel’s use of fictitious names to solicit videos on Cameo—does not inherently "nullify" the fair use defense.

Instead, the court emphasizes that fair use analysis remains grounded in the four statutory factors of 17 U.S.C. § 107. While bad faith may be relevant to the inquiry (particularly regarding the "purpose and character of the use"), it is not a dispositive element that overrides a transformative purpose.

### Why Deception Did Not Defeat Fair Use in *Santos*
The court affirmed the District Court’s dismissal for two primary reasons rooted in the statutory factors, rather than the subjective intent of the defendant:

1.  **Transformative Purpose:** The court focused on whether the use was transformative, asking whether a reasonable observer would view the new work as adding something new with a further purpose. Here, the use was for "(sarcastic) criticism and commentary" on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. The court held that "whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user." Because the use was transformative, the first factor strongly supported fair use despite the deceptive means of acquisition.
2.  **No Market Usurpation:** Regarding the fourth factor, the court found no market harm. The complaint did not allege that the defendants intended to "supplant" Santos’s "commercially valuable right." Citing *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.*, the court noted that critical reviews or lampoons do not usurp the market for the original because creators are unlikely to license such uses.

### Conclusion
Under *Santos*, bad faith is not an independent veto on fair use. While the Supreme Court’s language in *Harper & Row* is acknowledged, the Second Circuit prioritizes the statutory § 107 factors. In this case, Kimmel’s deception did not defeat the defense because the use remained transformative and did not usurp the market for the original work.

### Why can't a non-party enforce Terms of Service as a third-party beneficiary?
Question:
Santos tried to sue for breach of the Cameo Terms of Service even though he wasn't a party to the agreement (Kimmel was the user). The court applied Illinois law and cited a 'strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.' What level of 'express declaration' is required to overcome this presumption, and why didn't the existence of third-party language in other parts of the ToS help Santos?
Answer:
Under the reasoning in *Santos v. Kimmel*, a non-party cannot enforce Terms of Service as a third-party beneficiary because the governing state law (Illinois) imposes a high bar for establishing such status, which was not met by the language in the agreement.

Here is the breakdown of the legal standard applied and why the presence of third-party language elsewhere in the contract did not assist Santos.

### 1. The Illinois Standard: Strong Presumption Against Third-Party Beneficiaries
The Second Circuit applied Illinois law to the Cameo Terms of Service. Under this jurisdiction, there is a **"strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties."** *Santos v. Kimmel*, 2nd Cir. 2025 (citing *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur*, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016)).

To overcome this presumption, the contract must contain language where **"the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration."** *Id.* This is a stringent standard. It is not enough for the contract to merely benefit a third party incidentally; the intent to allow enforcement by that third party must be practically explicit.

In *Santos*, the court found that Santos **"identifies nothing close to an 'express declaration' that the relevant provisions of the Terms of Service apply to him or other noncontracting parties."** Because the specific provisions Santos relied on lacked this explicit language, they could not be enforced by him as a non-party.

### 2. Why Third-Party Language in Other Provisions Did Not Help
Santos argued that the existence of third-party beneficiary language in other parts of the Terms of Service should support his claim. The court rejected this argument based on the principle of contractual intent and drafting clarity.

The court reasoned that the inclusion of third-party language in some provisions demonstrates that the drafters knew how to expressly authorize third-party enforcement when they intended to do so. The opinion states:

> "Indeed, other provisions of the Terms of Service do contain third-party beneficiary language, indicating that when the parties to that agreement intended to allow provisions of the contract to be enforced by third parties, they said so expressly."

Consequently, the **absence** of such language in the specific provisions Santos sought to enforce signaled that the parties did not intend to confer enforceable rights to non-parties for those specific terms. The presence of the language elsewhere reinforced the conclusion that its absence elsewhere was intentional, rather than an oversight.

### Conclusion
Santos failed to state a claim because he could not satisfy the Illinois standard for third-party beneficiary status. The court required a **"practically an express declaration"** of intent to benefit him, which was missing from the relevant contract provisions. Furthermore, the selective inclusion of third-party language in other sections of the ToS confirmed that the parties knew how to create such rights expressly and chose not to do so for the provisions at issue.

### What is the 'out-of-pocket loss' requirement for fraudulent inducement in New York?
Question:
The court dismissed Santos's fraudulent inducement claim because he failed to allege 'actual out-of-pocket loss.' I thought fraud claims generally focus on reliance and damages resulting from the reliance. Why does New York law specifically require out-of-pocket loss rather than expectation damages or other types of harm in this context?
Answer:
**Answer**

Based on the opinion in *Santos v. Kimmel*, the "out-of-pocket loss" requirement is a mandatory element for stating a claim of fraudulent inducement under New York law. While fraudulent inducement generally involves reliance on a misrepresentation, the Second Circuit confirms that New York law specifically demands that a plaintiff plead **actual out-of-pocket loss** to survive a motion to dismiss.

In *Santos*, the court affirmed the District Court's dismissal of the fraudulent inducement claim because the complaint failed to allege that Santos suffered "any actual out-of-pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud." The opinion grounds this requirement in established New York precedent, specifically citing *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996), and *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.*, 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017).

Regarding your question about why this standard applies instead of expectation damages or general reliance harm:

1.  **It is a Pleading Requirement:** The opinion treats out-of-pocket loss not merely as a measure of damages at trial, but as a threshold element required to state a claim. As the court noted, Santos's failure to allege this specific type of loss meant the claim "failed to state a claim for relief under state law."
2.  **Distinction from General Harm:** While the opinion does not explicitly detail the policy rationale behind the rule, it establishes that general harm or reliance is insufficient without this specific financial metric. The court rejected Santos's challenge to the dismissal, indicating that under New York law, the absence of alleged out-of-pocket loss is fatal to the claim regardless of other fraudulent conduct.

In short, according to *Santos*, a plaintiff in New York cannot maintain a fraudulent inducement claim based solely on reliance or other types of harm; they must specifically allege actual out-of-pocket financial loss to satisfy the elements of the cause of action.

### Why did the court decline to decide copyright preemption of state law claims?
Question:
The District Court dismissed the state law contract claims on preemption grounds, but the Second Circuit said they 'need not resolve this issue' because the claims failed under state law anyway. Is this a matter of judicial economy, or is there a specific rule about avoiding constitutional or federal preemption questions when state law grounds are sufficient?
Answer:
**Yes, this reflects the judicial principle of deciding cases on the narrowest possible grounds, which serves both judicial economy and federalism concerns.**

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit declined to reach the copyright preemption question because it found independent, sufficient grounds to dismiss the state law claims under state law. This is a standard practice in federal appellate review: if a case can be resolved on non-federal grounds (such as state contract or tort law), the court will generally avoid ruling on federal questions (such as statutory preemption under the Copyright Act).

### The Relevant Legal Principle
Federal courts operate under a prudential rule that they should not decide federal or constitutional questions if a case can be disposed of on narrower, state law grounds. This minimizes unnecessary federal intervention into state law matters and conserves judicial resources.

In *Santos*, the court explicitly invoked this approach in Section II of the opinion. After noting that the District Court had dismissed the contract claims on preemption grounds, the Second Circuit wrote:

> "We need not resolve this issue, however, because Santos’s state law contract claims fail to state a claim for relief under state law."

### Application in *Santos*
The court supported this decision by analyzing the state law claims independently and finding them deficient under New York and Illinois law, rendering the preemption analysis unnecessary:

1.  **Breach of Contract:** The court found Santos could not enforce the Cameo Terms of Service because he was not a party to the contract. Under Illinois law, there is a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties," and the Terms of Service lacked an "express declaration" that they applied to him. Under New York law, the complaint failed to allege the "essential terms" of an implied contract or a "meeting of minds."
2.  **Fraudulent Inducement:** The court affirmed the dismissal because Santos failed to allege "any actual out-of-pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud," which is required under New York law.

Because the claims failed on these state law pleading grounds, the court affirmed the dismissal without needing to determine whether the Copyright Act preempted the claims. As the opinion demonstrates, when a state law ground is sufficient to dispose of the appeal, the federal preemption question becomes moot for the purposes of that specific judgment.
