Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# When Does a Computer 'Receive' a Notice? 5th Circuit Modernizes Louisiana Insurance Statute

## The Tension Between Strict Statutes and Automated Systems

In the high-stakes world of insurance regulation, the difference between a valid and invalid policy cancellation often hinges on procedural minutiae. For consumers, a lapse in coverage can mean financial ruin after an accident; for insurers, strict compliance with state statutes is a shield against bad faith litigation. In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, decided October 3, 2025, the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit addressed a growing friction point in modern litigation: how legacy statutory language applies to automated digital processes. The court held that an automated email received by an insurer’s computer system satisfies the statutory requirement for "receipt" of a cancellation notice, even if human possession of the physical documents occurs later. This decision signals a willingness by federal courts to interpret statutory terms functionally rather than formally when technology has displaced traditional manual processes.

## The Statutory Framework and Strict Adherence

The case arose under a Louisiana statute governing the cancellation of insurance policies financed through premium assistance companies. Under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3), a finance company must send a notice of cancellation to the insurer along with a statement certifying specific facts, such as the existence of a power of attorney and the default status of the insured. Louisiana courts have historically required strict adherence to these requirements, ruling that any defect in the process results in an ineffective cancellation. This strictness serves two purposes: fixing the exact time of cancellation and granting a minimum cure period for the insured.

Plaintiffs Kimberly Williams and others argued that GoAuto Insurance’s procedures violated this strict adherence standard. Their challenge focused on two procedural gaps. First, they contended that the finance company’s certification lacked a signature at the moment of cancellation, as a signature stamp was applied to printed documents only after the policy had already been automatically cancelled. Second, they argued that GoAuto did not legally "receive" the cancellation request until a human employee physically took possession of the documents, whereas GoAuto’s computer system had processed the cancellation immediately upon receiving an automated email.

## Interpreting 'Receipt' in the Digital Age

The Fifth Circuit’s most significant contribution lies in its interpretation of the term "receipt." The statute mandates that the insurer may proceed to cancel "Upon receipt of such notice." Plaintiffs argued that "receipt" implies physical possession by a human agent, a view supported by traditional definitions of the word. However, Judge Southwick’s opinion rejects this formalistic approach in favor of a functional one. Citing Black’s Law Dictionary, the court defined receipt as the act of taking physical possession, delivery, or custody. The court reasoned that in the context of a modern statute, company computers perform the functions individuals once did.

By concluding that GoAuto’s web service "listening" for and processing an email from the finance company constituted receipt, the court effectively decoupled the legal concept of receipt from human intervention. This reasoning acknowledges that automated systems are the new standard for business operations. If a statute requires an insurer to act upon receipt, and the insurer’s system is designed to act immediately upon electronic delivery, forcing a delay until a human files a paper copy would render the statute’s efficiency goals obsolete. The court noted that the district court’s alternative theory—that the APAC employee acted as a "private carrier"—was imaginative and unnecessary given the email finding, suggesting the Fifth Circuit preferred a straightforward technological interpretation over a strained analogy to common carrier law.

## The Certification Requirement and Corporate Agency

The court also addressed the plaintiffs’ argument regarding the lack of a signature on the certification statement. Plaintiffs relied on *Benitez v. Elsayed*, a Louisiana state court opinion noting that a notice lacking a signature failed to certify statements. However, the Fifth Circuit distinguished *Benitez* by noting that the notices in *Williams* did explicitly "certify" the required information, even without a named employee or signature at the precise moment of transmission. The opinion emphasizes that the statute identifies the "insurance premium finance company" as the actor effecting cancellation, not a specific individual.

This distinction is doctrinally significant. It reinforces the principle that strict adherence to a statute is not an invitation for courts to add procedural requirements absent from the text. While Louisiana law demands strict compliance, the Fifth Circuit suggests that compliance is measured against the statutory text itself, not against a judicially constructed checklist of formalities like signatures. This aligns with a textualist approach where the absence of an explicit signature requirement in the statute is dispositive. By allowing a corporate entity to certify without an individual signature, the court accommodates the reality of automated corporate governance where digital assertions of fact are standard practice.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The broader implication of *Williams* extends beyond Louisiana insurance law. The decision provides a roadmap for federal courts interpreting older statutes in the context of modern automation. As businesses increasingly rely on APIs, web services, and automated email triggers to satisfy regulatory obligations, courts will face similar questions regarding when a notice is "sent," "received," or "filed." By anchoring the definition of "receipt" in the functionality of the receiving system rather than the physical actions of a human employee, the Fifth Circuit reduces the risk that technological efficiency will be penalized by archaic statutory interpretation.

However, this approach may raise concerns regarding consumer protection. The strict adherence doctrine in Louisiana was designed to ensure insureds have clear notice and time to cure defaults. If "receipt" is satisfied by an automated server ping, the window for human review or potential error correction may be narrowed. The court dismissed these concerns by noting the statute’s purpose is to fix the time of cancellation, which the automated system does with precision. Nevertheless, downstream litigation may focus on whether the automated system’s accuracy meets the "good faith" standards implied by the statute, even if the procedural "receipt" requirement is met.

## Conclusion

Ultimately, the Fifth Circuit declined to certify the questions of statutory interpretation to the Louisiana Supreme Court, citing a general reluctance to certify absent compelling reasons. This leaves the federal interpretation standing as the final word on these specific procedural mechanics within the Fifth Circuit. By affirming the district court, *Williams* validates the use of automated systems to satisfy statutory notice requirements. It suggests that while the legislature may write statutes in traditional terms, the judiciary will interpret them in light of the technological reality in which they operate. For insurers and regulators alike, the case serves as a reminder that in the digital age, a server’s inbox is as legally potent as a human’s filing cabinet.



# Strict Adherence or Strict Textualism? The 5th Circuit on Signature Requirements in Louisiana Insurance Law

In the realm of insurance regulation, few doctrines are as rigid as Louisiana’s requirement of “strict adherence” to statutory cancellation procedures. Designed to prevent policyholders from losing coverage due to administrative technicalities, this standard typically demands that insurers and finance companies follow the letter of the law with precision. However, in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, decided October 3, 2025, the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit confronted a modern twist on this traditional doctrine. Writing for the panel, Circuit Judge Leslie H. Southwick upheld a dismissal of claims against an insurer, ruling that automated electronic processes satisfy Louisiana’s statutory requirements for cancellation notice, even in the absence of a human signature or physical receipt prior to the automated cancellation. The decision highlights a growing tension between consumer protection statutes rooted in physical paper trails and the realities of automated digital administration.

The case arose from a class action lawsuit involving insureds who financed their premiums through American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (APAC). When the insureds defaulted on payments, APAC exercised its power of attorney to cancel the policies by sending an automated email to GoAuto Insurance. GoAuto’s computer system processed the request and cancelled the policies automatically; human employees only reviewed and stamped the documents afterward. The plaintiffs argued that this process violated Louisiana Revised Statutes § 9:3550, which governs the cancellation of financed insurance policies. Specifically, they contended that the statute’s requirement for a finance company to send a statement “certifying” compliance necessitated a signature before cancellation, and that the insurer’s “receipt” of the notice required physical possession by a human agent before the policy could be terminated.

The Fifth Circuit’s reasoning rests heavily on textualism rather than the broader protective spirit of Louisiana’s “strict adherence” jurisprudence. Acknowledging that Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these procedures to fix the exact time of cancellation and grant a cure period, the court nonetheless looked to the statutory text for the definition of compliance. Judge Southwick noted that the statute explicitly requires a “statement certifying” certain facts but makes no explicit reference to a signature. The court surveyed Louisiana case law, including *Benitez v. Elsayed*, which noted a lack of signature as a shortcoming but did not hold it disqualifying on its own. Consequently, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the statute does not require a signature, reasoning that “strict adherence” is not an invitation for courts to add procedural requirements not found in the text. This approach prioritizes the plain language of the statute over the potential administrative safeguards that a signature might provide.

Furthermore, the court addressed the definition of “receipt” under the statute, which states that an insurer may proceed to cancel “upon receipt” of the notice. The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto did not truly “receive” the notice until a human employee stamped the physical document, which occurred after the automated cancellation. The Fifth Circuit rejected this argument, relying on Black’s Law Dictionary to define receipt as taking possession or custody. The court held that GoAuto’s computer system taking custody of the electronic notice satisfied the statutory requirement. This interpretation effectively equates automated data ingestion with human receipt, a significant shift for industries relying on legacy statutes drafted before the advent of algorithmic processing.

From an analytical perspective, this ruling represents a notable federal intervention in state consumer protection law. While the Fifth Circuit is applying Louisiana law, its refusal to read implied requirements into the statute—such as a signature—may dilute the practical effect of the “strict adherence” doctrine. The Louisiana Supreme Court has previously emphasized that strict adherence serves to provide a clear method of fixing the exact time of cancellation and granting a minimum cure period. By allowing automated cancellation to precede human verification, the court risks undermining the certainty that the doctrine seeks to establish. If the insurer’s system cancels the policy the moment an email hits a server, the insured may have less practical time to cure a default than if a human had to process the request. The court’s reliance on dictionary definitions over state judicial intent suggests a preference for textual clarity over the protective policy goals underlying the statute.

The decision also carries significant procedural implications regarding the relationship between federal courts and state supreme courts. The plaintiffs requested that the Fifth Circuit certify the question of statutory interpretation to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that the issue was important to the state’s insurance industry. The court declined, citing *Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n* to note that certification is reserved for compelling reasons and that the absence of a state supreme court definition is not sufficient grounds. By resolving the issue itself, the Fifth Circuit effectively sets a precedent for how federal courts will interpret Louisiana’s insurance statutes in diversity cases. This limits the Louisiana Supreme Court’s ability to clarify its own law in this specific context, potentially creating a divergence where federal courts apply a more textualist reading of state statutes than state courts might prefer.

Looking downstream, *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* signals a green light for the automation of insurance cancellations in the Fifth Circuit. Insurers and premium finance companies operating in Louisiana can now rely on fully automated systems to effectuate cancellations without fear of liability for lacking a signature or human receipt. While this promotes efficiency and reduces administrative costs, it raises questions about whether the statutory safeguards intended for vulnerable consumers are being eroded by technological evolution. As insurance administration becomes increasingly digitized, the “strict adherence” doctrine may require legislative updates to ensure that electronic compliance matches the protective rigor of its paper-based origins. Until then, the Fifth Circuit’s textualist approach suggests that if the statute does not explicitly say “signature,” the law does not require one.



# Federal Courts and State Law: Why the 5th Circuit Declined Certification in Williams v. GoAuto

## The Tension Between Federal Interpretation and State Authority

When federal courts sit in diversity, they are tasked with applying state substantive law while adhering to the procedural constraints of the federal system. This dynamic creates a recurring tension: how should a federal court proceed when the state law governing a dispute is unsettled? In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, decided by the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit on October 3, 2025, the court navigated this tension by affirming a district court’s summary judgment against former insureds challenging policy cancellations. While the substantive holding clarifies Louisiana insurance cancellation procedures, the decision is equally significant for its procedural posture. The Fifth Circuit declined the plaintiffs’ request to certify questions of Louisiana law to the state’s highest court, reaffirming a high bar for certification that prioritizes federal judicial independence over comity in the absence of a compelling reason to defer.

## The Underlying Dispute and Statutory Ambiguity

The case arose from a class action involving insureds who financed their premiums through American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (APAC). When the insureds defaulted, APAC utilized a power of attorney to request cancellation from GoAuto Insurance. The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto’s cancellation procedures violated Louisiana Revised Statutes § 9:3550, specifically regarding the delivery of notice and the certification of default. The core factual dispute centered on whether GoAuto’s automated email system constituted valid "receipt" of the cancellation request and whether the statute required a human signature on the certification notice prior to the policy being cancelled.

The Fifth Circuit, writing through Judge Leslie H. Southwick, held that the insurer’s automated procedures complied with the statute. The court reasoned that the statute requires the "insurance premium finance company" to certify information, not necessarily a specific named employee, meaning a signature was not strictly required. Furthermore, the court determined that "receipt" by the insurer was satisfied when GoAuto’s computer system received the automated email request, even though a human employee did not physically take possession of the document until after the cancellation occurred. While the substantive ruling provides clarity for the insurance industry regarding automated compliance, the procedural decision to interpret these ambiguous state statutory terms without seeking guidance from the Louisiana Supreme Court is where the doctrinal weight of the opinion lies.

## The High Bar for Certification

Plaintiffs requested that the Fifth Circuit certify two questions of statutory interpretation to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that the proper interpretation was vital to the state’s insurance scheme. In declining this request, the Fifth Circuit invoked the standard set forth in *Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n*, 106 F.3d 1245 (5th Cir. 1997). The court reiterated that it is "chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so." The opinion explicitly states that the mere fact that a term has not yet been defined by the state supreme court is insufficient to warrant certification.

This approach underscores the Fifth Circuit’s preference for resolving state law questions internally rather than deferring to state courts, unless the federal court identifies a specific, compelling necessity. By treating the lack of state supreme court precedent as a gap to be filled rather than a signal to pause, the court signaled that federal judges are willing to engage in statutory construction of state laws even when those laws lack a definitive judicial gloss. This decision aligns with a broader trend in some federal circuits where certification is viewed as an exceptional remedy rather than a routine step in diversity cases involving unsettled state law.

## Analytical Perspective on Federal-State Judicial Comity

The decision to decline certification invites scrutiny regarding the risks of *Erie* errors. Under *Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins*, federal courts must apply state law as the state courts would. When a state supreme court has not spoken, federal courts must predict how that court would rule. In *Williams*, the Fifth Circuit engaged in this prediction by analyzing Louisiana appellate opinions and statutory text to conclude that "strict adherence" to the statute does not invite courts to add procedural requirements like signatures. While the court’s textualist reading is logical, it carries the risk that the Louisiana Supreme Court might interpret the legislative intent differently, particularly regarding consumer protections in insurance cancellations.

The court’s reliance on the definition of "receipt" from Black’s Law Dictionary further illustrates this federal interpretive role. By accepting a dictionary definition over a specific state judicial definition, the Fifth Circuit effectively set the standard for what constitutes receipt in this context. While the court acknowledged that state law requires strict adherence, it simultaneously asserted that federal courts are not barred from interpreting the mechanics of that adherence. This balancing act suggests that the Fifth Circuit views the certification mechanism as a tool to be used sparingly, reserving it for situations where the federal court lacks the capacity to make a reasonable prediction of state law, rather than whenever state law is merely unclear.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The implications of *Williams* extend beyond the immediate insurance dispute. For litigants in the Fifth Circuit, the decision reinforces that a request for certification must demonstrate a compelling reason beyond the simple absence of state supreme court precedent. This may discourage parties from seeking certification in diversity cases involving novel statutory questions, potentially leading to more federal court rulings on state law that could later be contradicted by state supreme court decisions. For the insurance industry, the ruling validates the use of automated systems for compliance with state statutes, provided the underlying data transmission satisfies the statutory definition of receipt.

Ultimately, *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* serves as a reminder of the procedural autonomy federal courts maintain when interpreting state law. By affirming the district court and declining certification, the Fifth Circuit prioritized judicial efficiency and the resolution of the case at hand over the potential benefit of a definitive ruling from the Louisiana Supreme Court. While this approach ensures timely dispositions, it places the burden on federal judges to accurately predict state judicial trends, a task that remains fraught with uncertainty in the absence of clear state guidance. As state statutes increasingly intersect with modern technology, the tension between federal interpretation and state authority will likely continue to surface in diversity cases across the circuit.
