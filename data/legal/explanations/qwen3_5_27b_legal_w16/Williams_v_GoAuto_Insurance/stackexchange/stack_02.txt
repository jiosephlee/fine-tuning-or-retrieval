Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Can an automated server's receipt of an email satisfy the statutory requirement of 'receipt' and 'possession'?
Question:
In *Williams*, the court held that GoAuto's computer system receiving an email constituted 'receipt' under the statute, even though a human didn't physically take possession until later. The court cited Black's Law Dictionary definitions of 'receipt' and 'receive.' I'm struggling with the concept of a computer system 'taking possession.' If the statute requires the insurer to 'consider that cancellation... has been requested' upon receipt, does an automated trigger satisfy the intent of the legislature to ensure human oversight before a policy is terminated?
Answer:
**Yes, under *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, an automated server's receipt of an email satisfies the statutory requirements of "receipt" and "possession."**

The Fifth Circuit held that an insurer's computer system receiving an email cancellation request constitutes "receipt" under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i), even if human involvement occurs only after the automated cancellation takes place.

### The Statutory Requirement
The Louisiana statute governing the cancellation of financed insurance policies states that "[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested." The Plaintiffs argued that "receipt" required human possession of the physical notice before the policy could be terminated.

### The Court's Interpretation of "Receipt"
The court looked to the statutory text and standard legal definitions to resolve the ambiguity. Because the Louisiana Supreme Court had not defined "receipt" in this context, the Fifth Circuit relied on *Black's Law Dictionary* (12th ed. 2024), which defines "receipt" as "[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession," and "receive" as "[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source."

Applying these definitions, the court concluded:
> "We accept these definitions and conclude that GoAuto may cancel a policy once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation."

The court determined that when GoAuto’s computer system received the emailed notice, the insurer had effectively taken "custody" of the request. The fact that a human employee did not physically handle the document until later in the morning did not negate the fact that the insurer’s system had already "received" and acted upon the notice.

### Addressing Legislative Intent and Human Oversight
You asked whether an automated trigger satisfies the legislative intent to ensure human oversight. The court addressed this by contextualizing the statute within modern technology.

The opinion explicitly notes that "This is a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did." The court reasoned that strict adherence to the statute does not require adding procedural steps (like manual human review prior to cancellation) that the legislature did not mandate. Instead, the court found that:
> "GoAuto’s method of receiving the notice and reacting to it satisfies the statute."

### Conclusion
In *Williams*, the court affirmed that "receipt" is satisfied when the insurer's automated system receives the electronic notice, regardless of whether a human takes physical possession of the documents until after the cancellation is effectuated. The statutory requirement is met by the computer system's possession and processing of the email, rendering the subsequent human stamping of documents a post-cancellation formality.
