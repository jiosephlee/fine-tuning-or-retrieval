Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Does statutory compliance with cancellation procedures automatically preclude a breach of good faith claim?
Question:
The Plaintiffs alleged both statutory violations and a breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing. The district court dismissed *all* claims with prejudice after finding no statutory violation. The Fifth Circuit affirmed this. I don't understand the link between the two. If the statutory procedure was followed, does that automatically mean there was no bad faith? Can a company follow the letter of the statute but still act in bad faith, or does statutory compliance act as a complete defense to the tort claim in Louisiana insurance law?
Answer:
**Short Answer:**
Based on *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Fifth Circuit did not explicitly articulate a general rule that statutory compliance automatically precludes a breach of good faith claim. However, in this specific case, the court affirmed the dismissal of **all** claims—including the bad faith claim—solely because the insurer complied with the statutory cancellation procedures. The opinion treats statutory compliance as the threshold determination that validated the cancellation, thereby supporting the dismissal of the related tort claims.

**Detailed Analysis**

**1. The Statutory Standard: "Strict Adherence"**
The opinion grounds its analysis in Louisiana law regarding insurance-premium finance cancellations. The court cites *Delatte v. Lemotte* and *Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co.* to establish that Louisiana courts require "strict adherence" to the statutory requirements in La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3). The opinion notes that "any defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy."

The court emphasizes that this strict adherence serves two functions: fixing the exact time of cancellation and granting a minimum cure period. Consequently, the validity of the cancellation turns entirely on whether the finance company followed the statutory mechanics (notice, certification, and receipt).

**2. Application to GoAuto’s Procedures**
The Plaintiffs argued that GoAuto’s procedures violated the statute because the certification lacked a signature and because GoAuto did not physically "receive" the cancellation request before the policy was cancelled electronically.

The Fifth Circuit rejected these arguments, holding that:
*   **Signatures:** The statute requires a "statement certifying" compliance but does not explicitly require a signature. The court concluded that "Louisiana law does not require a signature on the notice sent by the premium finance company to the insurer."
*   **Receipt:** The court interpreted "receipt" under the statute to include electronic receipt. Because GoAuto’s computer system received the e-mail notice and cancelled the policy, the court held that GoAuto satisfied the statutory requirement of taking "possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation."

**3. The Link to the Bad Faith Claim**
The opinion does not contain a separate analysis of the breach of good faith and fair dealing claim. Instead, the procedural history notes that the district court "dismissed all claims with prejudice" after finding no statutory violation. The Fifth Circuit affirmed this disposition.

The logical link established by the opinion is that the statutory requirements govern the *validity* of the cancellation. Because the court found GoAuto complied with the statute, the cancellation was effective. Once the cancellation was deemed effective under the statute, the basis for the Plaintiffs' claims (including the allegation that the cancellation was improper or in bad faith) was removed.

**Conclusion**
While *Williams* does not explicitly rule on whether statutory compliance is a complete defense to bad faith in all contexts, it demonstrates that in disputes over financed insurance cancellations, proving statutory compliance is dispositive. The court affirmed the dismissal of the bad faith claim because the statutory procedures were followed, effectively treating the statutory compliance as the controlling factor for the case's outcome.
