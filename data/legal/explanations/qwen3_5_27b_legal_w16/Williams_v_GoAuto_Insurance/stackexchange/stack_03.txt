Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### What is the Fifth Circuit's standard for certifying questions of state law to the state supreme court?
Question:
The Plaintiffs in *Williams* requested certification of the statutory interpretation issues to the Louisiana Supreme Court, but the Fifth Circuit denied it. The opinion cites *Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass'n* regarding the need for a 'compelling reason.' Can someone explain the specific threshold for certification in the Fifth Circuit? Why is the fact that a term has not been defined by the state supreme court insufficient to warrant certification, especially when it involves a significant insurance industry practice?
Answer:
Based on the opinion in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Fifth Circuit applies a **"compelling reason"** standard when deciding whether to certify questions of state law to a state supreme court. The mere fact that a statutory term has not been defined by the state's highest court is **insufficient** to meet this threshold.

### The *Jefferson* Standard
In *Williams*, the Fifth Circuit explicitly relied on its prior decision in *Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass'n*, 106 F.3d 1245 (5th Cir. 1997), to establish the governing rule. The opinion quotes *Jefferson* as follows:

> "As a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often."

### Why Undefined Terms Are Insufficient
The court in *Williams* reiterates a specific limitation from *Jefferson* regarding undefined terms. Even if a significant insurance industry practice or statutory scheme involves a term the state supreme court has not yet defined, this gap in case law does not automatically trigger certification. The opinion states:

> "That a term has not yet been defined by a state supreme court 'is not sufficient to warrant certification.'"

The rationale provided is one of resource conservation. Certification is treated as a limited resource; using it too frequently risks the state court's willingness to accept future certifications. Therefore, the federal court must exercise caution ("chary") and only seek guidance when absolutely necessary.

### Application in *Williams*
In this case, the Plaintiffs argued that the interpretation of terms like "receipt" and "private carrier" was important to the state's insurance industry. However, the Fifth Circuit declined to certify the question because it found the statutory text and dictionary definitions provided sufficient clarity to decide the case.

The court concluded that GoAuto’s procedures complied with the statute based on its own analysis of the text (e.g., accepting Black’s Law Dictionary definitions for "receipt"). Because the court could resolve the statutory interpretation without state guidance, no "compelling reason" existed to justify the use of the certification mechanism.
