Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### How did the court distinguish *Benitez v. Elsayed* regarding the lack of employee identification on the notice?
Question:
The opinion discusses *Benitez v. Elsayed*, where a court noted a notice was not signed and did not identify a specific employee. However, the Fifth Circuit distinguished *Benitez* because the notices in *Williams* 'certified' the relevant information even without a named employee. I'm confused about the legal distinction here. If *Benitez* found the lack of identification problematic, why did the Fifth Circuit decide that the absence of a named employee didn't invalidate the certification in *Williams*? Does the word 'certify' carry more weight than the lack of a name?
Answer:
**The court distinguished *Benitez* based on the presence of explicit certification language in the notice, not the presence of a signature or employee name.**

In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Fifth Circuit clarified that the statutory requirement for a "statement certifying" specific information does not mandate a signature or a named individual, provided the document itself explicitly purports to certify the facts.

### The Statutory Standard
Under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a), a premium finance company must send a notice of cancellation together with "a statement certifying" four specific conditions (e.g., valid power of attorney, default status). Louisiana law requires "strict adherence" to these statutory procedures for a cancellation to be effective.

### The Distinction from *Benitez*
The Plaintiffs in *Williams* relied on *Benitez v. Elsayed*, where a Louisiana court found a notice defective. However, the Fifth Circuit noted a critical factual difference between the two cases:

1.  **In *Benitez*:** The court noted the notice "does not identify any particular employee... is not signed by any individual, **and does not purport to 'certify' any of the statements contained therein.**"
2.  **In *Williams*:** While the notices also lacked a specific employee name or signature at the time of sending, the document explicitly stated: "**APAC certifies that:**" followed by the required statutory points.

### Why the Word "Certify" Matters
The Fifth Circuit reasoned that the statute imposes the duty on the **company**, not a specific human actor. The opinion states:

> "The statutory section itself states it is 'the insurance premium finance company' who effects cancellation by 'certifying' certain information in a notice."

Because the *Williams* notices explicitly used the word "certifies" to affirm the statutory conditions, they satisfied the requirement of a "statement certifying." The court concluded that without clearer authority, "a company cannot certify information without a named employee being identified" is not a valid requirement.

### Conclusion
The absence of a named employee did not invalidate the certification in *Williams* because the document itself fulfilled the statutory command by explicitly stating the company was certifying the information. *Benitez* was distinguishable because its notice failed on the substantive level of not purporting to certify the information at all, whereas *Williams* satisfied the text of the statute despite the lack of a signature or name.
