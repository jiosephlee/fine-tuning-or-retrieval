Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Does Louisiana law require a physical signature on a premium finance company's cancellation certification?
Question:
I'm reading *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* and I'm confused about the court's interpretation of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The statute requires the finance company to send a 'statement certifying' certain facts, but it doesn't explicitly mention a signature. The Plaintiffs argued that 'certifying' implies a signature, but the Fifth Circuit said no. Given Louisiana's 'strict adherence' doctrine for insurance cancellations, why is a signature not considered an implicit requirement of 'certification'? Does the text of the statute override the general expectation of a signature in legal certifications?
Answer:
**No, under *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, Louisiana law does not require a physical signature on a premium finance company’s cancellation certification.**

While Louisiana courts generally require "strict adherence" to statutory cancellation procedures, the Fifth Circuit held that this doctrine does not authorize courts to add requirements—such as a signature—that are not explicitly contained in the text of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a).

Here is the breakdown of the court’s reasoning regarding your question:

### 1. Textualism Overrides Implicit Expectations
The statute requires the finance company to send a "statement certifying" specific facts regarding the power of attorney, default status, and notice sent to the insured. However, the text makes "no explicit reference to a signature."

The court reasoned that if a signature were required, the word "certifying" would have to create that requirement implicitly. The Fifth Circuit declined to read such a requirement into the statute, noting that "strict adherence" to the procedures "is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures." Instead, the court focused on the statutory language stating it is "the insurance premium finance company" who effects cancellation by certifying information, rather than a specific named employee.

### 2. Distinguishing Prior Case Law
The Plaintiffs relied on *Benitez v. Elsayed*, where a court criticized a notice for not identifying an employee or being signed. However, the *Williams* court distinguished this precedent. In *Benitez*, the court did not hold that the absence of a signature *by itself* disqualified the cancellation if other elements were present. Furthermore, unlike *Benitez*, the notices in *Williams* explicitly "certified" the relevant information, even without a named employee or signature at the moment of transmission.

### 3. Timing and Electronic Receipt
The court also addressed the timing of the signature. In GoAuto’s process, the computer system received the electronic certification and cancelled the policy *before* an APAC employee affixed a signature stamp to the printed documents later that morning.

The court concluded that the statute requires the insurer to have "receipt" of the notice, which is satisfied when the insurer’s computer system receives the electronic request. Because the certification was received electronically prior to cancellation, the subsequent physical signature did not invalidate the process.

### Conclusion
Your confusion stems from the tension between the "strict adherence" doctrine and statutory text. *Williams* clarifies that while the procedure for cancellation must be followed strictly, the requirements are limited to what the legislature wrote. Since § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) does not mandate a signature, a physical signature is not an implicit requirement for a valid certification under Louisiana law.
