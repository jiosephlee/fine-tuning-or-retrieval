Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Why did the Ninth Circuit apply different likelihood-of-success conclusions for the admissions suspension versus the funding defunding?
Question:
In *Pacito v. Trump*, the court applies the *Nken* stay factors. They state the government is 'likely to prevail' on the challenge to Executive Order 14163 (suspending refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)), but 'not likely to prevail' on the APA challenge regarding the defunding of resettlement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. I'm confused why the court treats these two components of the same executive action so differently under the same 'likelihood of success' factor. Is there a doctrinal distinction between suspending 'entry' versus defunding 'services' that triggers different standards of review?
Answer:
The Ninth Circuit applies different likelihood-of-success conclusions because the two components of the executive action rely on **distinct statutory authorities with different constraints on executive discretion**. While the *Nken* stay standard is the same for both, the underlying merits analysis differs based on whether the President is acting under the plenary power of **8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)** (entry) or the specific mandates of **8 U.S.C. § 1522** (resettlement services).

Here is the breakdown of the court’s reasoning based on the text of the opinion:

### 1. Admissions Suspension: High Deference Under § 1182(f)
For the suspension of refugee admissions (Executive Order 14163), the court finds the government **likely to prevail**. This conclusion rests on the broad deference afforded to the President under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA).

The opinion cites *Trump v. Hawaii*, noting that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" and vests the President with "ample power to impose entry restrictions." The court emphasizes that the statutory text sets a low bar:
> "The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President 'find[]' that the entry of the covered aliens 'would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.' The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here."

Because the statute allows the President to suspend entry based on a finding of detriment without further judicial inquiry into the persuasiveness of justifications, the government satisfies the "likelihood of success" factor for the admissions suspension.

### 2. Funding/Services: Statutory Mandate Under § 1522
For the defunding of resettlement services, the court finds the government **not likely to prevail**. This is not a challenge to the President’s immigration power, but an Administrative Procedure Act (APA) challenge regarding the administration of existing statutory duties.

The court distinguishes this based on the mandatory language in **8 U.S.C. § 1522**. Unlike § 1182(f), which grants discretion, § 1522 imposes specific obligations on the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement. The opinion highlights:
*   **Unaccompanied minors:** The Director "shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child."
*   **Interim support:** The Secretary "shall develop and implement alternative projects" for cash and medical assistance.

The court concludes that because Congress appropriated funds ($3.178 billion) and the statute mandates services ("shall"), the government cannot simply terminate cooperative agreements without a plan to replace them. The opinion notes the government was "unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency," suggesting a failure to meet the statutory duty.

### 3. The Doctrinal Distinction
There is no difference in the *standard of review* applied to the *Nken* factors; rather, the difference lies in the **statutory interpretation** of the merits.

*   **Entry (§ 1182(f)):** The court applies the *Hawaii* deference standard, where the President's finding is virtually conclusive.
*   **Services (§ 1522):** The court applies standard statutory interpretation to determine if the Executive Branch has violated a mandatory duty. As Judge Lee’s concurrence/dissent notes, this is the point of contention—he argues § 1522 is an "authorization" (permissive), while the majority reads it as a "mandate" (mandatory).

### Conclusion
The court treats the components differently because they implicate different legal questions. The suspension of entry is an exercise of **plenary immigration authority** protected by § 1182(f) and *Hawaii* deference. The defunding of services is an exercise of **administrative power** constrained by the specific mandates of § 1522. Under *Nken*, the likelihood of success is assessed on the merits of each claim separately, leading to the split outcome in the stay order.

### Does the deference granted under Trump v. Hawaii extend to defunding statutory refugee resettlement programs?
Question:
The majority cites *Trump v. Hawaii* to support the President's authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend refugee entry, noting the statute 'exudes deference.' However, the court also finds the government unlikely to prevail on the challenge to the defunding of services under the Refugee Act of 1980. If the President has broad discretion under § 1182(f) to suspend entry, does that authority implicitly allow him to defund the statutory mechanisms (like 8 U.S.C. § 1522) that facilitate that entry, or does the Refugee Act create a statutory limit that § 1182(f) cannot override?
Answer:
**No, based on the *Pacito v. Trump* panel order, the deference granted under *Trump v. Hawaii* regarding entry suspension does not extend to defunding statutory resettlement programs.**

The Ninth Circuit distinguishes between the President's authority to suspend entry under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and the statutory obligations to provide services under the Refugee Act of 1980 (specifically 8 U.S.C. § 1522). While the court applies *Trump v. Hawaii* deference to the former, it finds the government unlikely to prevail regarding the latter.

### 1. Authority to Suspend Entry (§ 1182(f))
The court agrees with the government that the President possesses broad discretion to suspend refugee admissions. In analyzing the likelihood of success on the merits, the panel explicitly relies on *Trump v. Hawaii*, noting that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President.

The opinion states:
> "The government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions... The language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority."

Under this standard, the President’s finding that entry is "detrimental to the interests of the United States" is sufficient to sustain the suspension of admissions.

### 2. Obligation to Fund Resettlement (§ 1522)
However, the court rejects the argument that this entry authority implicitly overrides the statutory duty to fund resettlement services for admitted refugees. The panel finds the government "not likely to prevail" on the challenge to the defunding of services.

The court interprets 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing mandatory obligations rather than discretionary ones. Specifically, the opinion highlights that the statute requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement "to the extent of available appropriations" to provide specific services, including:
*   Employment training and placement.
*   English language training.
*   Cash assistance.
*   Legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied minors.

The court emphasizes the statutory use of "shall," noting:
> "Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government must provide certain reception and placement services to refugees after their admission into the United States."

### 3. The Distinction
The opinion treats the two statutory provisions as separate legal regimes. The deference under *Hawaii* applies to the *power to exclude* (entry), but it does not negate the *duty to support* those who have been admitted. Because Congress appropriated funds for these specific services (noting the FY 2025 appropriation), and the statute mandates their provision, the President cannot use § 1182(f) authority to nullify the statutory mechanisms of § 1522.

Consequently, the court directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide these services, stating:
> "In light of the government’s uncertainty regarding its ability to provide the reception and placement services statutorily mandated under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements."

### Dissenting View
It is important to note that Judge Lee, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part, argued that § 1522 should be read as "authorization—not a mandate," suggesting the executive branch has discretion over funding. However, the majority's stay order rests on the interpretation that § 1522 creates a statutory limit that the President cannot override via defunding, even if the President retains broad authority to suspend entry.

### Conclusion
In *Pacito*, the 9th Circuit indicates that while the President may suspend entry under § 1182(f) with high deference, that authority does not implicitly allow the defunding of statutory resettlement programs under § 1522. The Refugee Act creates a separate statutory limit requiring the government to provide reception and placement services to admitted refugees, regardless of the suspension of new admissions.

### How should courts interpret 'Authorization' versus 'Shall' in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 when determining mandatory funding duties?
Question:
Judge Lee's dissent argues that 8 U.S.C. § 1522 is permissive because the title says 'Authorization' and subsections use 'authorized.' The majority, however, relies on subsections using 'shall' (e.g., § 1522(a) and (d)(2)(B)) to find a mandatory duty. As a student, I'm struggling with the canon of construction here. When a statute mixes permissive language ('authorized') with mandatory language ('shall') within the same section, how do courts determine if the overall provision creates a mandatory spending obligation or merely discretionary authority?
Answer:
**Answer:**

Based on the reasoning in *Pacito v. Trump*, when a statute mixes permissive language (e.g., "authorized," "Authorization") with mandatory language (e.g., "shall"), courts generally prioritize the **specific operative text** of the subsections creating the duty over the **section title** or general permissive phrasing.

In *Pacito*, the Ninth Circuit panel (majority) and Judge Lee (dissent) presented two competing canons of construction regarding 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The majority’s approach offers the controlling interpretation for the stay order, while the dissent illustrates the counter-argument.

### 1. The Majority Approach: Specific "Shall" Controls
The majority resolved the tension by looking to the specific verbs used in the subsections that define the government’s obligations, rather than the section title.

*   **Operative Text vs. Title:** Although the section title is "Authorization for programs..." (which Judge Lee argues implies permission), the majority focused on subsections that explicitly use "shall." Specifically, § 1522(a)(1)(A) states the Director **"shall"** make available resources for employment training and English language training. Similarly, § 1522(d)(2)(B) states the Director **"shall assume legal responsibility"** for unaccompanied minors, and § 1522(e)(7)(A) states the Secretary **"shall develop and implement"** alternative projects.
*   **The Appropriations Clause:** The majority rejected the argument that the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" grants discretion to defund. Instead, they interpreted this as a condition on the *source* of funds, not a grant of discretion to ignore the duty if funds exist. Since Congress appropriated funds (noted as $3.178 billion, reduced by $800 million), the mandatory duty attached to those funds.

**Takeaway:** If a specific subsection uses "shall" to define a duty, that language typically creates a mandatory obligation, even if the section title or other subsections use permissive language.

### 2. The Dissent Approach: Title and Context Signal Discretion
Judge Lee’s dissent argues for a broader contextual reading where the Title and the funding subsections govern the nature of the statute.

*   **Title as Guide:** Lee emphasizes that the Title ("Authorization") defines the section's nature as permissive ("permission to do something").
*   **Conditional "Shall":** Lee argues that the "shall" in § 1522(a) is conditional on the government’s choice to fund. He reads the phrase "In providing assistance under this section... the Director shall..." to mean: *if* the government chooses to provide assistance, *then* it must follow these conditions.
*   **Qualifying Language:** Lee points out that even the "shall" provisions in (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A) are qualified by phrases like "if necessary" or "as needed," which he argues preserves executive discretion over the scope of the mandate.

**Takeaway:** A dissenting view might argue that permissive titles and qualifying phrases ("if necessary") can render "shall" language conditional rather than absolute.

### 3. Application to Your Question
As a law student analyzing mixed statutory language, *Pacito* demonstrates the following interpretive hierarchy:

1.  **Specific Operative Verbs:** Look for the specific command in the subsection creating the duty. "Shall" in the operative clause usually trumps "Authorization" in the title.
2.  **Contextual Qualifiers:** Examine whether "shall" is modified by conditions (e.g., "if necessary," "to the extent of available appropriations"). The majority viewed the appropriations clause as a funding limit, while the dissent viewed it as a discretion trigger.
3.  **Statutory Structure:** Consider whether permissive language in one subsection (e.g., § 1522(b)-(e) using "authorized") negates mandatory language in another (e.g., § 1522(a) using "shall"). The majority treated these as distinct: the authority to contract is permissive, but the duty to provide services once funded is mandatory.

**Conclusion:** Under the *Pacito* panel order, courts determine mandatory spending obligations by enforcing the specific "shall" commands in the statute, provided appropriations exist, rather than deferring to the permissive tone of the section title.

### Can a federal court order the Executive Branch to reinstate private cooperative agreements as equitable relief?
Question:
The panel order directs the government to 'reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services.' These agreements are contracts between the State Department and private nonprofit organizations. I thought courts generally avoid specific performance or mandamus regarding private contracts entered into by the Executive. What is the legal basis for this court ordering the reinstatement of third-party contracts rather than just ordering the release of funds?
Answer:
**Yes, but the court is enforcing a statutory mandate, not the private contract itself.**

While you are correct that courts generally avoid ordering specific performance of private contracts entered into by the Executive Branch, the Ninth Circuit panel in *Pacito v. Trump* (Sept. 12, 2025) grounded its directive in a distinct legal basis: the government’s statutory obligation to provide refugee resettlement services.

### 1. The Statutory Mandate (8 U.S.C. § 1522)
The panel distinguished this case from general contract disputes by pointing to **8 U.S.C. § 1522**. The court interpreted this statute as imposing a mandatory duty on the federal government to provide "reception and placement services" to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States.

The opinion notes that § 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to make resources available for employment training, English language training, and cash assistance. Furthermore, § 1522(d)(2)(B) states the Director "shall assume legal responsibility... for the child" regarding unaccompanied minors. The majority viewed these provisions as creating a non-discretionary duty to provide services, distinct from the discretionary authority to admit refugees in the first place.

### 2. Practical Necessity and Government Admission
The court did not order reinstatement of the contracts merely because the contracts existed, but because the government admitted it could not fulfill the statutory duty without them.

During oral argument, the government advised the panel that it was "unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency or agencies that could provide reception and placement services." Because the government had terminated the existing cooperative agreements and could not demonstrate an alternative method to satisfy the statutory mandate in § 1522, the panel directed the government to "reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services."

In essence, the order treats the reinstatement of the agreements as the specific means required to comply with the statutory injunction against defunding services, rather than an enforcement of the private contract’s terms.

### 3. Distinction from Contract Enforcement
Your intuition about avoiding specific performance regarding private contracts is generally sound under administrative law principles. However, this order avoids that pitfall by focusing on the **Executive’s statutory duty** rather than the **private party’s contractual rights**.

The court is not ordering the State Department to honor the contract because the nonprofit is owed performance; it is ordering the State Department to reinstate the mechanism (the cooperative agreement) because Congress mandated the *service* be provided, and the Executive lacks an alternative mechanism.

### 4. The Dissenting View
It is important to note that this reasoning is not unanimous. Judge Lee’s concurrence/dissent argues that 8 U.S.C. § 1522 is permissive ("Authorization") rather than mandatory. Judge Lee contends that because the statute allows the Executive to craft programs rather than requiring them, the court is "in no position to mandate them." If the dissent’s statutory interpretation prevails in the full opinion, the legal basis for ordering the reinstatement of the agreements would be significantly undermined.

### Summary
The panel order rests on the intersection of **statutory duty** (§ 1522) and **administrative necessity** (the government’s admitted inability to perform otherwise). The court is enforcing the statute’s requirement that services be provided, using the reinstatement of cooperative agreements as the necessary equitable relief to ensure compliance.

### What is the legal effect of vacating a prior stay pending appeal while issuing a new stay in the same order?
Question:
The opinion states: 'We hereby vacate our administrative stay issued July 18... We vacate our prior stay issued March 25... The district court’s... preliminary injunctions are stayed in their entirety except as specified.' I am confused about the procedural mechanics here. If the court vacates the prior stays, does that mean the injunctions were briefly enforceable before the new stay took effect? How does vacating a stay differ from simply modifying it, and what impact does this have on the parties during the interim?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** No, vacating the prior stays in the same order that issues a new stay does not create a period where the district court’s injunctions are enforceable. The new stay takes effect immediately upon the entry of the order, superseding the prior stays seamlessly. The vacatur serves to clear the record of superseded directives rather than to lift the stay temporarily.

### Procedural Mechanics: Continuity of the Stay

The confusion often arises from the word "vacate," which implies termination. However, in this context, the Ninth Circuit is using vacatur to replace an old procedural posture with a new one without a lapse in coverage.

The opinion establishes continuity explicitly in the opening paragraph:
> "In the interim, this Order shall constitute a stay pending appeal and further order of this court."

Immediately following this declaration, the court states:
> "We hereby vacate our administrative stay issued July 18, 2025. We vacate our prior stay issued March 25, 2025... The district court’s... preliminary injunctions are stayed in their entirety except as specified."

Because the command to stay the injunctions and the command to vacate the prior stays appear in the **same operative order**, the new stay becomes effective the moment the order is issued (September 12, 2025). There is no temporal gap where the injunctions are "un-stayed." The vacatur merely removes the legal authority of the previous orders (March 25 and July 18) so that the September 12 order is the sole governing directive.

### Vacatur vs. Modification

You asked how vacating differs from modifying. In appellate practice, this distinction is often about clarity and the record:

1.  **Modifying** would imply the March 25 stay is still technically in place but altered. This can lead to ambiguity if the new terms conflict with the old text.
2.  **Vacating** wipes the slate clean. The court determined that after briefing and oral argument (held September 3, 2025), the prior stay framework was no longer appropriate. By vacating, the court ensures that the only valid terms are those in the September 12 order.

The opinion notes that the government filed an emergency motion on July 17 to "enforce, clarify or amend the stay order." The court resolved this by denying that motion "as moot" and instead issuing this comprehensive order. Vacating the old stays ensures that the "clarification" is not a patch on an old order, but a new, definitive ruling based on the updated briefing.

### Substantive Effect on the Parties

While the procedural stay is continuous, the **scope** of the stay has changed. This is the practical impact on the parties during the interim:

*   **What is Stayed:** The district court’s preliminary injunctions (Feb 28 and March 24) are "stayed in their entirety" regarding the suspension of refugee admissions (Executive Order 14163). The government can enforce the suspension of entry pending the final opinion.
*   **What is Not Stayed (The Carveout):** Unlike the March 25 stay, which had a "limited carveout" for individuals conditionally approved before January 20, 2025, the September 12 order creates a new carveout based on statutory mandates.
    > "The government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States."

Therefore, the "impact on the parties" is not a gap in enforcement, but a shift in obligations. The government must continue providing resettlement services (under 8 U.S.C. § 1522) despite the stay on the broader admission suspension.

### Summary

The court applied the *Nken v. Holder* factors to issue the new stay. By vacating the prior orders within the same document, the court prevented any ambiguity about which terms controlled. The injunctions remained stayed throughout the transition; only the specific terms of the stay were updated to reflect the court's assessment of the APA challenges versus the immigration authority challenges.

**Key Takeaway:** When an appellate court vacates a prior stay and issues a new one in the same order, the new stay is effective immediately. The vacatur eliminates the prior order's authority but does not create a window where the underlying judgment becomes enforceable.

### What happened to the 'limited carveout' for refugees approved before January 20, 2025?
Question:
The background section mentions a March 25, 2025 stay that included a 'limited carveout from the stay' for individuals conditionally approved before January 20, 2025. However, the September 12 order vacates the March 25 stay and issues a new stay. The new order does not explicitly mention preserving this carveout, only directing the reinstatement of cooperative agreements. Does the vacatur of the March order eliminate the protection for those pre-existing applicants, or does the new stay implicitly cover them under the 'except as specified' clause?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** The "limited carveout" for individuals conditionally approved before January 20, 2025, is **eliminated** by the September 12, 2025 order. The new stay applies to the district court’s preliminary injunctions "in their entirety," and the only exception preserved in the new order concerns post-admission services for refugees already inside the United States, not entry rights for conditionally approved applicants abroad.

### Analysis of the September 12 Order

To understand why the carveout is no longer in effect, you must compare the scope of the vacated March 25 stay against the new September 12 stay.

**1. The March 25 Stay Included the Carveout**
As noted in the Background section, the March 25, 2025 order granted the government's motion for a stay of the district court's preliminary injunction *except* for a specific group:
> "We denied the motion for a stay, however, 'to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025.'"

Under that order, the injunction prohibiting the suspension of refugee entry remained in force for those pre-approved individuals.

**2. The September 12 Order Vacates the Prior Stay**
The September 12 order explicitly states:
> "We hereby vacate our administrative stay issued July 18, 2025. We vacate our prior stay issued March 25, 2025, as amended on April 21 and May 9, 2025."

By vacating the March 25 order, the specific carveout contained within it is nullified. The court then issues a *new* stay pending appeal with its own terms.

**3. The New Stay's Scope is Broader Regarding Entry**
The September 12 order mandates:
> "The district court’s orders of February 28 and March 24, 2025 are stayed in their entirety, except as noted below."

Unlike the March 25 order, the September 12 order does not include a carveout for conditionally approved applicants. Instead, it stays the injunctions "in their entirety." This means the government's suspension of refugee entry under Executive Order No. 14163 is now stayed *in favor of the government* for all individuals, including those conditionally approved before January 20, 2025.

**4. The "Exception" in the New Order Applies Only to Admitted Refugees**
The September 12 order does contain an exception ("except as noted below"), but it is legally distinct from the March 25 carveout. The exception directs the government to:
> "reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States."

This exception preserves the district court's order regarding **funding and services** (8 U.S.C. § 1522) for refugees who are **already admitted** to the U.S. It does not address the **entry suspension** (8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)) for refugees who are **conditionally approved but remain outside the country**.

### Conclusion

The vacatur of the March 25 stay removes the specific protection for pre-January 20, 2025, conditional approvals. The new stay reinstates the suspension of entry for all applicants under the Executive Order, preserving only the mandate to provide reception and placement services to those already admitted to the United States. Consequently, conditionally approved refugees outside the U.S. are no longer shielded from the entry suspension by the Ninth Circuit's stay.
