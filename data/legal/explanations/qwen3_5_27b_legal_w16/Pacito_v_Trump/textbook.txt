Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Factual Background and Procedural History

## Issuance of Executive Order 14163 suspending the USRAP

The dispute in *Pacito v. Trump* originates from a series of executive actions taken by the President in January 2025. On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” This order invoked the President’s authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and 8 U.S.C. § 1185(a). Under these statutes, the President is granted broad discretion to suspend the entry of aliens into the United States whenever he finds that such entry would be detrimental to the interests of the country. In Executive Order 14163, the President determined that the entry of refugees under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) met this standard of detriment. Consequently, § 3(a) of the order directed that the entry of refugees under the USRAP be suspended pending further findings. This executive action effectively halted the admission of new refugees into the United States and formed the primary factual predicate for the plaintiffs’ challenge regarding the suspension of admissions.

## Suspension of USRAP funding under Executive Order 14169

Parallel to the suspension of refugee admissions, the Executive Branch took steps to suspend funding associated with the program. On January 24, 2025, the Department of State suspended all funding for the USRAP. This action was purportedly taken pursuant to a separate Executive Order, No. 14169, entitled “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid,” which was also issued on January 20, 2025. The suspension of funding was significant because it extended beyond the entry of refugees; it included funding for domestic resettlement services for refugees who had already been admitted to the United States. The government’s decision to cut off these financial resources created a second distinct legal issue for the plaintiffs, involving the statutory obligations of the federal government to support refugees already within the country. This bifurcation of the executive action—suspending both future entry and current support services—shaped the scope of the litigation that followed.

## Composition of plaintiffs: Refugees and resettlement organizations

The plaintiffs in this case consist of two distinct groups with different interests in the outcome. The first group comprises individual refugees. Some of these individuals had already been admitted to the United States prior to the executive orders, while others had been approved for resettlement but remained outside the country awaiting entry. The second group consists of three organizations that held cooperative agreements with the State Department. These organizations were responsible for providing overseas processing for persons applying for refugee status and for delivering resettlement services to refugees admitted to the United States. The organizational plaintiffs alleged that the government’s actions violated their contractual and statutory relationships with the Department of State. Understanding the composition of the plaintiffs is essential for the procedural history, as the district court’s orders and the appellate court’s stays were tailored to address the specific harms faced by these different categories of litigants, particularly regarding those already admitted versus those awaiting entry.

## District Court preliminary injunctions of February and March 2025

In February 2025, the plaintiffs filed suit in the Western District of Washington, alleging that Executive Order 14163 violated the Refugee Act of 1980 and that the defunding of USRAP violated the Administrative Procedure Act. The district court moved quickly to address the urgency of the situation. On February 25, 2025, the district court orally issued a preliminary injunction prohibiting the enforcement or implementation of specific sections of Executive Order No. 14163. A preliminary injunction is a provisional remedy designed to preserve the status quo and prevent irreparable harm to the parties while the case proceeds to trial. However, the government reacted swiftly to this order. On February 26, the day after the oral injunction, the government terminated every cooperative agreement for reception and placement services in the United States and all but one agreement for processing support abroad.

In response to the government’s termination of agreements, the district court clarified and expanded its relief. On February 28, 2025, the court issued a written order defining the scope of the February 25 injunction, specifying that it also prohibited the suspension of USRAP funding. Subsequently, on March 24, 2025, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction. This order explicitly commanded the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements that had been terminated after the February 25 ruling. These district court orders created the specific legal landscape that the Ninth Circuit had to address, as the government sought to pause the effect of these injunctions while the appeal was pending.

## Procedural history of stays granted by the Ninth Circuit motions panel

Following the district court’s orders, the government filed notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025. Simultaneously, the government sought a stay pending appeal of the February 28 district court order. A stay pending appeal is a procedural mechanism that halts the enforcement of a lower court’s judgment while the appellate court reviews the case, preventing the lower court’s order from becoming a moot fact on the ground. On March 25, 2025, a motions panel of the Ninth Circuit granted the stay in part. In doing so, the panel referenced *Trump v. Hawaii*, noting the significant deference afforded to the President under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). However, the panel denied the stay to the extent it applied to individuals who had been conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025. This created a "limited carveout" from the stay. The motions panel subsequently issued two additional orders on April 21 and May 9, 2025, to clarify the scope of this carveout. These procedural steps illustrate the appellate court’s effort to balance the government’s interest in enforcing the executive order with the plaintiffs’ interest in avoiding irreparable harm during the litigation.

## The government's emergency motions and the expedited oral argument

As the case moved toward argument, further procedural developments occurred in the district court and the Ninth Circuit. On July 14, 2025, the district court issued an order establishing a framework for enforcing the limited carveout and appointed a magistrate judge to oversee it. The government responded on July 17, 2025, by filing an emergency motion to enforce, clarify, or amend the stay order, which the plaintiffs opposed. In response to this emergency motion, the Ninth Circuit issued an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order on July 18, 2025. Due to the urgency of the matters involving refugee admissions and resettlement, the Ninth Circuit scheduled oral argument on an expedited basis, which took place on September 3, 2025. This expedited timeline highlights the time-sensitive nature of immigration cases where individual rights and government policy intersect. Following the argument, the court issued the current order on September 12, 2025, vacating prior stays and issuing a new stay pending the final resolution of the appeal, while noting that a full opinion on the merits would issue in due course.

# The Standard for Stays Pending Appeal

## The Nken v. Holder four-factor test for stays

When a party seeks to halt the enforcement of a district court’s order while an appeal is pending, the appellate court must apply a specific legal framework to determine whether a stay is appropriate. In *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit explicitly relied on the Supreme Court’s precedent in *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009), to guide its decision. The opinion outlines a four-factor test that the court must consider: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing that he is likely to succeed on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure the other parties interested in the proceeding; and (4) where the public interest lies. This test serves as the procedural mechanism for appellate courts to balance the competing interests of maintaining the status quo against the potential harm of delaying the lower court’s ruling. In *Pacito*, the government, as the party seeking the stay, was required to satisfy this four-part inquiry to vacate the district court’s preliminary injunctions regarding the suspension of refugee admissions and the defunding of resettlement services.

## The critical nature of likelihood of success and irreparable injury

While all four factors in the *Nken* test are relevant, the Ninth Circuit emphasized that the first two factors are not equal in weight to the others. As the opinion states, the showing of success on the merits and the demonstration of irreparable injury are “the most critical.” This prioritization means that even if a stay applicant can show that the public interest favors a stay or that other parties will not be harmed, the court may still deny the stay if the applicant cannot demonstrate a strong likelihood of prevailing on the underlying legal claims or suffering harm that cannot be remedied later. In *Pacito*, the court applied this criticality by analyzing the government’s likelihood of success differently for the two main components of the district court’s injunction. Regarding the Executive Order suspending refugee admissions, the court found the government was likely to prevail based on deference to presidential authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). However, regarding the challenge to the defunding of resettlement services under the Administrative Procedure Act and 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the court determined the government was *not* likely to prevail on at least one challenge. Consequently, the stay was granted for the admission suspension but denied for the resettlement services, illustrating how the likelihood of success factor can drive a partial stay order.

## The consideration of harm to other parties and the public interest

The third and fourth factors of the *Nken* test require the court to look beyond the immediate parties to the broader consequences of issuing a stay. The third factor examines whether the stay will substantially injure the other parties interested in the proceeding, while the fourth factor assesses where the public interest lies. In *Pacito*, the court noted that the remaining factors, touching upon both domestic and foreign interests of the United States, favored the government in the context of the Executive Order. The court referenced the “broad discretion” conferred on the President in immigration matters, suggesting that the public interest aligned with allowing the government to implement its policy on entry restrictions pending final review. However, this public interest analysis was tempered by the statutory mandates regarding resettlement services. The court found that the uncertainty regarding the government’s ability to provide statutorily mandated reception and placement services created a situation where the public interest and the injury to the plaintiffs (refugees and service organizations) weighed against a full stay. This demonstrates that the public interest factor is not a monolithic concept but must be evaluated in the context of specific statutory obligations and the practical effects of the stay on the parties.

## The interim nature of the order versus a final merits decision

It is essential for law students to distinguish between a stay order and a final opinion on the merits. The document in *Pacito* is explicitly labeled a “Panel order” and the court states that “An opinion on the merits will issue in due course.” A stay pending appeal is an interim remedy designed to preserve the status quo and prevent irreparable harm while the appellate court reviews the case; it is not a final adjudication of the legal rights at stake. The court in *Pacito* acknowledged this distinction by noting that the case was “submitted for decision” after oral argument, yet the stay order was issued “in the interim.” This procedural posture allows the court to make preliminary assessments—such as determining that the government is “likely to prevail” on certain claims—without issuing a binding judgment that resolves the entire case. Students should note that the court’s language regarding the likelihood of success in the stay order informs the stay decision but does not preclude the possibility that the final opinion might reach a different conclusion on the merits, although the stay analysis provides a strong indicator of the court’s current view.

## The burden of proof on the government as the stay applicant

The party seeking the stay bears the burden of proof to satisfy the *Nken* factors. In *Pacito*, the government was the stay applicant, having filed an emergency motion to stay the district court’s preliminary injunctions. The opinion clarifies that the applicant must make a “strong showing” that he is likely to succeed on the merits. This is a significant burden, particularly when challenging a district court’s preliminary injunction, which itself implies a finding of likelihood of success by the lower court. The government in *Pacito* met this burden regarding the Executive Order by relying on Supreme Court precedent (*Trump v. Hawaii*) that establishes a high degree of deference to the President’s findings under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). However, the government failed to meet this burden regarding the defunding of resettlement services. The court found that statutory text in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 created a mandate for services that the government could not easily justify suspending. This highlights that the burden of proof is fact-specific; the government must demonstrate a strong case for each distinct legal issue raised in the underlying litigation, and a failure to do so on even one issue can result in a partial denial of the stay.

# Presidential Authority Over Refugee Admissions

In *Pacito v. Trump*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit addressed a challenge to Executive Order 14163, which suspended the United States Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP). While the court issued a full opinion on the merits at a later date, this panel order provides critical insight into the court’s preliminary assessment of the President’s authority to restrict refugee entry. For law students learning to read judicial opinions, this case illustrates how appellate courts analyze statutory authority and executive deference when evaluating a stay pending appeal. The court’s reasoning relies heavily on the text of the Immigration and Nationality Act and binding Supreme Court precedent to determine whether the government was likely to succeed on the merits of its challenge to a district court injunction. Understanding this reasoning requires a close reading of the statutory grant of power, the specific factual findings required by that statute, and the level of judicial deference applied to executive decisions in immigration matters.

## Statutory authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

The foundation of the President’s power to suspend refugee admissions in this case is found in 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), a provision of the Immigration and Nationality Act. The Ninth Circuit explicitly cites this statute as the authority under which President Trump issued Executive Order 14163. The opinion quotes the relevant portion of the statute, which states that whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interests of the United States, he may suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens. The statute further empowers the President to impose restrictions on the entry of aliens as he deems appropriate. In the context of this opinion, the court treats this statutory language as the primary legal source for the Executive Order’s suspension of the USRAP. When analyzing the case, it is essential to recognize that the court views this statute as vesting the President with specific, textually grounded authority to manage the entry of noncitizens, distinct from the legislative appropriations or administrative agreements discussed elsewhere in the case.

## The requirement of a finding that entry is 'detrimental to the interests of the United States'

Under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the President’s power to suspend entry is not absolute; it is conditioned on a specific factual determination. The opinion highlights that the "sole prerequisite" set forth in the statute is that the President must find that the entry of the covered aliens would be detrimental to the interests of the United States. In Executive Order 14163, the President made this explicit determination, stating that entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP would be detrimental to the interests of the United States. The Ninth Circuit’s analysis accepts this finding as sufficient to satisfy the statutory requirement. The court notes that the President has "undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here." For the law student, this illustrates a key aspect of statutory interpretation in administrative law: if the statute requires a finding and the executive branch makes that finding on the record, the court often treats the statutory condition as met without independently verifying the underlying facts, provided the finding falls within the scope of the statutory language.

## Application of Trump v. Hawaii precedent regarding deference

The court’s analysis of the President’s authority is heavily influenced by the Supreme Court’s decision in *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018). The Ninth Circuit quotes *Hawaii* extensively to establish the standard of review applicable to the President’s exercise of power under § 1182(f). The opinion observes that the authority under which the President issued the Executive Order "exudes deference" to the President. This language signals to the reader that the judiciary will not conduct a rigorous review of the policy choices made by the Executive Branch in this context. The court emphasizes that it cannot engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications." This precedent teaches students that in areas involving national security and immigration entry, courts often apply a highly deferential standard, looking primarily to whether the action falls within the text of the statute rather than evaluating the wisdom or necessity of the policy itself.

## The court's conclusion on the likelihood of government success regarding admissions

In the context of a stay pending appeal, the court must assess whether the applicant has made a strong showing that it is likely to succeed on the merits. Applying the deferential standard derived from *Trump v. Hawaii* and the statutory analysis of § 1182(f), the Ninth Circuit concludes that the government is likely to prevail on the plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions. This conclusion is a critical component of the stay analysis, as the likelihood of success on the merits is one of the most critical factors in granting a stay. The court distinguishes this issue from the challenge regarding the defunding of resettlement services, noting that while the government is likely to succeed on the admissions suspension, it is not likely to prevail on all challenges under the Administrative Procedure Act. This distinction is vital for understanding the disposition of the order: the court stays the district court’s injunction regarding the suspension of admissions but leaves other aspects of the injunction in place.

## The scope of the President's power to impose entry restrictions

Finally, the opinion clarifies the breadth of the President’s authority under § 1182(f). Relying on *Trump v. Hawaii*, the court states that the statute vests the President with "ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act]." The court finds that the language of § 1182(f) is clear and that the Executive Order does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority to suspend the entry of any class of aliens. This reasoning underscores the expansive nature of executive power in this specific domain. Furthermore, the court notes that the remaining stay factors, including the public interest, favor the United States because of the "broad discretion" conferred on the President. Even Judge Lee, in his partial concurrence and dissent, agrees with the majority that the President has the authority to impose a moratorium on refugee admissions, reinforcing the strength of this legal conclusion within the panel. This scope of power allows the President to act unilaterally to suspend entry, provided the statutory prerequisite of a detrimental finding is met.

# Statutory Mandates for Refugee Resettlement Services

## Statutory requirements under 8 U.S.C. § 1522

In analyzing the government's obligation to provide services to refugees already admitted to the United States, the Ninth Circuit panel relied heavily on the text of the Refugee Act of 1980, specifically 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The court identified specific statutory provisions that create duties for federal agencies regarding the treatment of refugees post-admission. Under § 1522(a)(1)(A), the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement within the Department of Health and Human Services is required to make available sufficient resources for employment training, English language training, and cash assistance. The opinion highlights that these requirements are designed to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible. Furthermore, the court pointed to § 1522(d)(2)(B), which mandates that the Director assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied minor refugees during interim periods before placement. Finally, the court noted § 1522(e)(7)(A), which requires the Secretary of Health and Human Services to develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States for less than thirty-six months, providing interim support and medical services. By citing these specific subsections, the majority opinion establishes a statutory framework that appears to impose affirmative duties on the executive branch to facilitate the resettlement of refugees who have already entered the country, distinct from the President's authority to suspend future entry under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f).

## Distinction between authorization and mandatory language ('shall')

A central point of legal disagreement in this opinion concerns the interpretation of statutory language, specifically whether the Refugee Act creates a mandatory obligation or merely grants discretionary authority. The majority opinion treats the use of the word "shall" in § 1522 as creating a binding mandate. For instance, the court emphasizes that the Director "shall" provide resources and "shall" assume responsibility for unaccompanied minors. This textual reading supports the conclusion that the government has a legal duty to provide reception and placement services. Conversely, Circuit Judge Lee, in his partial concurrence and dissent, argues that the statute should be read as permissive. He points to the title of § 1522, which is "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees," arguing that "authorization" implies permission rather than requirement. Judge Lee contends that the operative text, which repeatedly states the Director is "authorized" to provide assistance, signals executive discretion over funding. He further interprets the "shall" language in § 1522(a) as conditional, applying only if the government chooses to fund the programs. This divergence illustrates a critical lesson in statutory interpretation for law students: the tension between a statute's title and its operative text, and the weight given to mandatory verbs like "shall" versus permissive verbs like "authorized."

## The government's termination of cooperative agreements

The factual background of the case reveals a significant conflict between the statutory duties identified by the court and the actions taken by the executive branch. The opinion details that in February 2025, the Department of State terminated every cooperative agreement used to provide reception and placement services for refugees in the United States. These agreements were with public or private nonprofit agencies that historically provided the services mandated by § 1522. The plaintiffs, which included three organizations that held these cooperative agreements, alleged that the defunding violated the Administrative Procedure Act and the Refugee Act. The district court had previously issued preliminary injunctions ordering the State Department to reinstate these agreements. The appellate court's stay order acknowledges this history, noting that the statutory scheme allows services to be provided through contracts or cooperative agreements with nonprofit agencies. The termination of these agreements by the State Department, occurring shortly after the plaintiffs filed suit, formed the factual basis for the claim that the government was failing to comply with its statutory obligations under the Refugee Act.

## The government's failure to secure replacement agencies

A critical factor in the court's analysis of the stay was the government's ability to comply with the statutory mandate through alternative means. The opinion highlights a discrepancy in the government's representations regarding its plan to replace the terminated agreements. On March 10, 2025, the government advised the district court that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency." However, during oral argument before the Ninth Circuit, the government informed the panel that it was unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency or agencies to provide reception and placement services. This uncertainty was pivotal to the court's reasoning. Because the statute requires the provision of services, and the government had terminated the existing mechanisms for providing them without a confirmed replacement, the court found a risk that the statutory mandate would go unfulfilled. This factual development undermined the government's position that it could maintain compliance with the law while the appeal proceeded, influencing the court's decision to carve out an exception to the stay regarding these specific services.

## The court's conclusion on the likelihood of government success regarding services

The court's disposition of the stay hinged on the application of the *Nken v. Holder* factors, particularly the likelihood of success on the merits. The panel distinguished between the government's challenge to the suspension of refugee admissions and its challenge regarding resettlement services. On the issue of suspending entry under Executive Order 14163, the court found the government likely to prevail, citing *Trump v. Hawaii* and the broad deference owed to the President under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). However, regarding the challenge to the defunding of services under the APA and § 1522, the court concluded the government was not likely to prevail. The opinion states, "For reasons to be explained in full in an opinion to follow, however, the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA." This split finding led to a partial stay. The district court's orders were stayed in their entirety regarding the suspension of entry, but the stay did not apply to the requirement to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted. This outcome demonstrates how a court can apply different standards of review to different components of a single case, granting deference on immigration authority while enforcing statutory mandates on domestic resettlement.

## The role of congressional appropriations in statutory mandates

The interplay between statutory mandates and congressional funding was a focal point of the statutory analysis. The majority opinion notes that § 1522 requires the Director to provide resources "to the extent of available appropriations." The government's position, as reflected in the dissent, suggests that this language limits the mandate, implying that if appropriations are rescinded or unavailable, the duty to provide services ceases. The opinion records that for FY 2025, Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, though this was reduced by $800 million in the Rescissions Act of 2025. By highlighting the existence of these appropriations, the majority implicitly counters the argument that there are no funds available to support the mandate. The court directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide services, suggesting that the existence of appropriated funds triggers the statutory duty. This section of the opinion teaches students to examine the funding context of a statute; a mandate conditioned on "available appropriations" does not necessarily grant the executive branch the power to unilaterally decide that no appropriations are available when Congress has allocated specific funds for the purpose.

# The Stay Order and Judicial Dissent

## The final stay order: Vacating prior stays and staying district court injunctions

This chapter examines the procedural mechanics of a stay pending appeal, using the Ninth Circuit’s order in *Pacito v. Trump* as a case study. While the court noted that a full opinion on the merits would issue later, this interim order resolved the immediate need for a stay. The procedural posture is critical: the government appealed preliminary injunctions issued by the district court, which had blocked the enforcement of an executive order suspending refugee admissions and the termination of funding for refugee services. Throughout the litigation, the Ninth Circuit had issued multiple administrative stays and modified them several times between March and July 2025. In this final order, the panel vacated all prior stays, including the administrative stay of July 18 and the stay issued on March 25.

To understand this disposition, students must recognize the standard for granting a stay pending appeal. The court cited *Nken v. Holder*, identifying four factors: (1) whether the applicant made a strong showing of likelihood to succeed on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will suffer irreparable injury without a stay; (3) whether the stay will substantially injure other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. The court noted that the first two factors are the most critical. Applying this framework, the majority concluded that the government was likely to succeed on the challenge regarding the suspension of refugee admissions. Consequently, the court stayed the district court’s preliminary injunctions of February 28 and March 24, 2025, in their entirety. This means that, generally, the executive branch was permitted to enforce the suspension of the refugee program while the appeal proceeded.

## The specific exception requiring reinstatement of cooperative agreements

Despite staying the injunctions broadly, the majority order contained a significant exception, or "carveout." The court directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who had already been admitted to the United States. This exception was grounded in the court's analysis of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which the majority interpreted as imposing a statutory mandate to provide certain services, such as employment training, English language training, and cash assistance, to admitted refugees. The court reasoned that the government was not likely to prevail on the plaintiffs' challenges regarding these statutory duties.

The rationale for this exception relied heavily on the government's own admissions during oral argument. Although the government had previously told the district court it was preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency, it informed the appellate panel that it was unaware if any progress had been made. Because the government could not demonstrate an alternative mechanism to fulfill the statutory obligations under § 1522, the court required the reinstatement of the existing cooperative agreements with private nonprofit agencies. This illustrates how a stay order can be tailored to address specific statutory conflicts while allowing other executive actions to proceed. The order specified that these services must include the "usual and customary services" afforded under the prior agreements, ensuring continuity for admitted refugees during the pendency of the appeal.

## Judge Lee's concurrence on presidential authority

Circuit Judge Lee filed a partial concurrence and partial dissent, providing a valuable lesson in how judges may agree on certain legal principles while diverging on others. Judge Lee concurred with the majority regarding the President's authority to suspend refugee admissions. He agreed that the district court incorrectly enjoined the executive branch from implementing the policy decision to limit admitting refugees. This alignment reinforces the majority’s reliance on *Trump v. Hawaii*, which established that the President enjoys vast discretion under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend the entry of aliens deemed detrimental to the United States.

Judge Lee’s concurrence confirms that the stay on the admission suspension was not controversial among the panel. Both the majority and the concurrence recognized that the statutory language of § 1182(f) vests ample power in the President to impose entry restrictions. By agreeing to stay the injunction regarding the suspension of admissions, Judge Lee signaled that the judicial branch should not engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications for national security or policy decisions regarding entry. This section of the opinion serves as a reminder that even in divided opinions, there may be consensus on foundational issues of executive power, even if the judges disagree on the scope of statutory mandates regarding domestic implementation.

## Judge Lee's dissent on the interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522

The primary divergence in this opinion concerns the interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, specifically whether the statute mandates the government to fund and provide resettlement services. Judge Lee dissented in part, arguing that the federal government does not have a legal duty to provide services to refugees who were recently admitted before the suspension went into effect. He believed the district court was incorrect to enjoin the termination of funding and cooperative agreements. For a law student, this dissent highlights the importance of statutory construction in administrative law. While the majority viewed the statute as creating an enforceable right to services, Judge Lee viewed the statute as granting the executive branch discretion to craft programs.

This disagreement fundamentally alters the outcome for the organizational plaintiffs and the refugees. If Judge Lee’s view had prevailed, the stay would have applied to the injunctions in their entirety, allowing the government to terminate all cooperative agreements and cease funding for resettlement services. The dissent underscores the tension between congressional intent to support refugees and executive discretion over funding and program administration. It also illustrates the procedural reality that a stay pending appeal often acts as a preliminary ruling on the merits, as the court must assess the likelihood of success to determine whether to issue the stay.

## Textualist arguments regarding 'authorization' versus 'mandate'

Judge Lee’s dissent offers a detailed textualist argument that is instructive for reading statutory language. He focused on the title of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which reads "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." Lee argued that the word "Authorization" is permissive rather than mandatory, citing Black's Law Dictionary to define it as "permission to do something." He further pointed to the specific provisions within the statute, noting that subsections (b) through (e) state that the Director is "authorized" to provide assistance. To Lee, these were clear statutory signals that the executive branch retains discretion over funding.

The majority, conversely, focused on the word "shall" found in § 1522(a) and specific subsections regarding unaccompanied minors and cash assistance. Judge Lee countered this by arguing that the "shall" language was qualified by the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" and conditioned on the government choosing to fund the programs. He interpreted phrases like "if necessary" and "as needed" as invoking deference to executive discretion rather than imposing a firm mandate. This debate between "authorization" and "mandate" is a classic example of how statutory interpretation can drive litigation outcomes. Students should note how both sides relied on the same text but prioritized different words and structural cues to reach opposite conclusions regarding the government's obligations.

## Practical implications for refugees and resettlement organizations

The final disposition of this stay order has immediate practical consequences for the parties involved. For refugees who were admitted to the United States prior to the suspension, the majority’s carveout ensures that reception and placement services continue. They will retain access to employment training, English language classes, and cash assistance through the reinstated cooperative agreements. However, for refugees outside the country approved for resettlement, the stay allows the suspension of the admissions program to proceed, meaning their entry is delayed pending the final resolution of the appeal.

For the organizational plaintiffs, the order creates a split result. They are relieved of the immediate threat of total defunding for services related to admitted refugees, as the government must reinstate the agreements necessary to comply with § 1522. However, the stay prevents them from challenging the broader suspension of the refugee program at this stage. This order demonstrates how appellate courts manage complex immigration litigation by balancing the government's interest in enforcing policy with the statutory protections afforded to individuals already within the jurisdiction. It also highlights the role of private nonprofit agencies as essential partners in the federal refugee resettlement system, a relationship the court sought to preserve through the specific exception in the stay.
