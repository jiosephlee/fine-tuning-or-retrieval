Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

## The Border Line: 9th Circuit Upholds Entry Ban but Blocks Defunding of Resettlement

The Ninth Circuit’s recent stay order in *Pacito v. Trump* offers a stark illustration of the limits of presidential immigration power. While the panel deferred heavily to the Executive Branch regarding the suspension of new refugee admissions, it drew a hard line at the defunding of services for refugees already admitted to the United States. This split decision highlights a critical doctrinal tension in immigration law: the distinction between the President’s plenary authority to exclude aliens and Congress’s statutory mandate to support those who are permitted to enter. By granting the stay on the entry ban but denying it on the defunding of resettlement services, the court signals that while the Executive may close the door, it cannot necessarily strip the floor from those already inside.

The court’s reasoning on the suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) relies heavily on the precedent set in *Trump v. Hawaii*. Citing 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the panel noted that the statute “exudes deference” to the President and vests him with ample power to impose entry restrictions. The court found that the President satisfied the sole statutory prerequisite by determining that refugee entry would be detrimental to the United States. In line with *Hawaii*, the Ninth Circuit declined to engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications. Consequently, the government is likely to succeed on the merits regarding the entry ban, leading the court to stay the district court’s injunctions on that specific front. This reaffirms the high bar challengers face when contesting exclusionary measures grounded in national security or foreign policy interests under § 1182(f).

However, the analysis shifts dramatically when the court turns to the defunding of reception and placement services under the Refugee Act of 1980. Here, the panel concluded that the government was not likely to prevail on the plaintiffs’ Administrative Procedure Act challenges. The court focused on 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which outlines specific requirements for the Office of Refugee Resettlement to provide employment training, English language training, and cash assistance. The court emphasized that Congress had appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance for Fiscal Year 2025. Because the statute directs the Director to provide these services “to the extent of available appropriations,” and because the government had terminated cooperative agreements without a viable replacement plan, the court found a statutory duty remained. The order directs the government to reinstate agreements necessary to provide these services to refugees who have already been admitted, effectively carving out a protected zone for post-admission treatment even while admissions are suspended.

Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and dissent provide the strongest counter-argument to the majority’s reading of the Refugee Act. He argues that § 1522 is best read as an authorization rather than a mandate, pointing to the section title and the use of permissive language throughout the statute. In his view, the “shall” language in subsection (a) applies only if the government chooses to fund the programs, and subsections regarding unaccompanied minors and cash assistance are qualified by executive discretion. This statutory interpretation battle is significant because it touches on the power of the Executive to effectively nullify congressional programs through administrative inaction or termination of contracts. If Judge Lee’s reading were to prevail, the President could arguably suspend a program’s operations even while Congress appropriates funds for it, provided the statute contains discretionary language.

The downstream implications of *Pacito* extend beyond the refugee program. The decision reinforces a bifurcated approach to immigration litigation: exclusionary policies will likely receive significant deference under § 1182(f), while policies affecting the rights and benefits of those already within U.S. jurisdiction will face stricter statutory scrutiny. For future litigation, this suggests that plaintiffs may find more traction challenging the internal mechanics of immigration enforcement and benefit distribution than the initial decision to exclude. Furthermore, the court’s reliance on the existence of congressional appropriations as a trigger for statutory duties underscores the role of the power of the purse in checking executive overreach. As the merits opinion is expected to follow, the Ninth Circuit’s stay order provides a roadmap for how courts may balance the President’s broad immigration authority against specific congressional mandates regarding the treatment of vulnerable populations.
