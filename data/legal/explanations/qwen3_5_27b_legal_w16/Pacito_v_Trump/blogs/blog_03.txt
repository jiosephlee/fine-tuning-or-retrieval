Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Emergency Stays in Immigration: The 9th Circuit's Evolving Carveouts

## The High-Stakes Procedural Dance

In the realm of immigration litigation, the period between a district court’s preliminary injunction and a final appellate ruling is often where the most critical human consequences play out. The Ninth Circuit’s panel order in *Pacito v. Trump*, issued on September 12, 2025, offers a compelling case study in how appellate courts manage these interim periods when executive authority clashes with statutory mandates. While the substantive merits of the refugee admissions suspension remain pending, the procedural history of this case—from March to September 2025—reveals a nuanced judicial approach to emergency stays. The court’s willingness to craft specific exceptions, or "carveouts," within a broader stay demonstrates a sophisticated balancing act between deferring to presidential power and protecting statutory rights during the pendency of an appeal.

The litigation began in earnest when President Trump issued Executive Order 14163 in January 2025, suspending the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) under the authority of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). Following a district court preliminary injunction in February, the government sought a stay pending appeal. The Ninth Circuit’s initial response on March 25, 2025, was pivotal. The motions panel granted a stay of the district court’s injunction but included a significant exception: the stay did not apply to individuals who had been conditionally approved for refugee status before January 20, 2025. This "conditionally approved" carveout allowed a specific cohort of refugees to proceed with resettlement even as the broader program was frozen. This decision signaled early on that while the court recognized the President’s broad discretion over entry, it was unwilling to let that discretion swallow existing statutory commitments to those already in the pipeline.

## From Procedural Carveouts to Statutory Mandates

The procedural posture evolved significantly over the summer of 2025, culminating in the September 12 order. In that order, the panel vacated the March 25 stay and the subsequent administrative stays, replacing them with a new framework that shifted the focus from procedural status to statutory obligation. While the court reaffirmed that the government was likely to succeed on the challenge to the suspension of admissions itself—citing *Trump v. Hawaii* and the broad deference owed to the President under § 1182(f)—the panel drew a sharp line at the funding and provision of resettlement services.

The court found that the government was not likely to prevail on the plaintiffs’ Administrative Procedure Act challenges regarding the defunding of reception and placement services. Relying on 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the panel noted that the statute uses mandatory language, requiring the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to provide specific services to refugees already admitted to the United States. Consequently, the September 12 order directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide these services, even as the broader stay on admissions remained in effect. This evolution from the March "conditionally approved" exception to the September "admitted refugee services" mandate illustrates how the court’s reasoning matured from a focus on individual procedural status to a broader interpretation of congressional intent regarding domestic resettlement obligations.

## Strategic Implications for Emergency Litigation

The trajectory of *Pacito* offers valuable lessons for litigators navigating emergency immigration enforcement cases. The initial "conditionally approved" carveout highlights the efficacy of seeking tailored relief that isolates specific classes of plaintiffs most vulnerable to immediate harm. By carving out those already conditionally approved, the court preserved the district court’s injunction for a discrete group without immediately resolving the broader constitutional question of presidential suspension power. This strategy allows the judiciary to mitigate irreparable harm while the legal merits are hashed out, a critical consideration given the *Nken* factors governing stays.

However, the shift in the September order suggests that successful emergency litigation may increasingly require grounding arguments in specific statutory mandates rather than relying solely on equitable discretion. The court’s reliance on § 1522 to mandate the reinstatement of funding agreements indicates that when Congress has explicitly authorized and structured a program with mandatory language ("shall"), executive attempts to defund or terminate it face a higher bar for staying injunctions. For future challengers of immigration policies, this implies that identifying specific statutory duties that survive a general suspension of entry could be a potent litigation strategy. It suggests that even when the President wins the battle on the border, Congress may have already won the war on domestic support, provided the statutory language is sufficiently clear.

## Doctrinal Tensions and the Dissent

The split in the panel underscores the doctrinal tension at the heart of this case. Judge Lee, concurring in part and dissenting in part, argued that § 1522 should be read as an authorization rather than a mandate. He pointed to the title of the section and the use of "authorized" in various subsections to argue that the executive branch retains discretion over whether to fund these programs, provided appropriations exist. This view aligns with a broader textualist approach that emphasizes executive flexibility in immigration administration.

However, the majority’s interpretation of the "shall" language in § 1522(a) and related subsections regarding unaccompanied minors and interim support suggests a more rigid view of congressional intent. This disagreement highlights a recurring theme in immigration law: the extent to which statutory funding authorizations create enforceable rights for beneficiaries versus discretionary tools for the executive. The majority’s decision to order the reinstatement of agreements despite the government’s uncertainty about its ability to secure new agencies indicates that the court views the statutory duty as non-delegable in this context.

## Looking Ahead

As the Ninth Circuit prepares to issue a full opinion on the merits, the stay orders in *Pacito* have already set a significant precedent for how courts manage the intersection of executive immigration power and statutory resettlement obligations. The procedural history demonstrates that appellate courts are willing to engage in granular stay orders that balance deference to the President with the protection of statutory rights. For the parties involved, the distinction between the suspension of entry and the provision of services for those already admitted has become the defining feature of the litigation. For observers, the case serves as a reminder that in high-stakes immigration enforcement, the procedural mechanisms of stays and injunctions are often just as consequential as the final judgment.
