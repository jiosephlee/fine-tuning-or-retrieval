Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

## The Border Line: 9th Circuit Upholds Entry Ban but Blocks Defunding of Resettlement

The Ninth Circuit’s recent stay order in *Pacito v. Trump* offers a stark illustration of the limits of presidential immigration power. While the panel deferred heavily to the Executive Branch regarding the suspension of new refugee admissions, it drew a hard line at the defunding of services for refugees already admitted to the United States. This split decision highlights a critical doctrinal tension in immigration law: the distinction between the President’s plenary authority to exclude aliens and Congress’s statutory mandate to support those who are permitted to enter. By granting the stay on the entry ban but denying it on the defunding of resettlement services, the court signals that while the Executive may close the door, it cannot necessarily strip the floor from those already inside.

The court’s reasoning on the suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) relies heavily on the precedent set in *Trump v. Hawaii*. Citing 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the panel noted that the statute “exudes deference” to the President and vests him with ample power to impose entry restrictions. The court found that the President satisfied the sole statutory prerequisite by determining that refugee entry would be detrimental to the United States. In line with *Hawaii*, the Ninth Circuit declined to engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications. Consequently, the government is likely to succeed on the merits regarding the entry ban, leading the court to stay the district court’s injunctions on that specific front. This reaffirms the high bar challengers face when contesting exclusionary measures grounded in national security or foreign policy interests under § 1182(f).

However, the analysis shifts dramatically when the court turns to the defunding of reception and placement services under the Refugee Act of 1980. Here, the panel concluded that the government was not likely to prevail on the plaintiffs’ Administrative Procedure Act challenges. The court focused on 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which outlines specific requirements for the Office of Refugee Resettlement to provide employment training, English language training, and cash assistance. The court emphasized that Congress had appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance for Fiscal Year 2025. Because the statute directs the Director to provide these services “to the extent of available appropriations,” and because the government had terminated cooperative agreements without a viable replacement plan, the court found a statutory duty remained. The order directs the government to reinstate agreements necessary to provide these services to refugees who have already been admitted, effectively carving out a protected zone for post-admission treatment even while admissions are suspended.

Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and dissent provide the strongest counter-argument to the majority’s reading of the Refugee Act. He argues that § 1522 is best read as an authorization rather than a mandate, pointing to the section title and the use of permissive language throughout the statute. In his view, the “shall” language in subsection (a) applies only if the government chooses to fund the programs, and subsections regarding unaccompanied minors and cash assistance are qualified by executive discretion. This statutory interpretation battle is significant because it touches on the power of the Executive to effectively nullify congressional programs through administrative inaction or termination of contracts. If Judge Lee’s reading were to prevail, the President could arguably suspend a program’s operations even while Congress appropriates funds for it, provided the statute contains discretionary language.

The downstream implications of *Pacito* extend beyond the refugee program. The decision reinforces a bifurcated approach to immigration litigation: exclusionary policies will likely receive significant deference under § 1182(f), while policies affecting the rights and benefits of those already within U.S. jurisdiction will face stricter statutory scrutiny. For future litigation, this suggests that plaintiffs may find more traction challenging the internal mechanics of immigration enforcement and benefit distribution than the initial decision to exclude. Furthermore, the court’s reliance on the existence of congressional appropriations as a trigger for statutory duties underscores the role of the power of the purse in checking executive overreach. As the merits opinion is expected to follow, the Ninth Circuit’s stay order provides a roadmap for how courts may balance the President’s broad immigration authority against specific congressional mandates regarding the treatment of vulnerable populations.



# Textualism in the Refugee Act: Judge Lee's Dissent on Statutory Discretion

In the high-stakes arena of immigration litigation, the tension between presidential deference and statutory constraints rarely plays out more sharply than in the Ninth Circuit’s recent order in *Pacito v. Trump*. While the panel largely deferred to the Executive Branch’s authority to suspend the refugee admissions program under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), a significant fissure emerged regarding the government’s duty to fund resettlement services for refugees already admitted to the United States. The majority held that the Refugee Act of 1980 imposes a mandatory obligation on the government to provide reception and placement services, while Judge Lee, in a partial dissent, argued that the statute’s text grants the President broad discretion to defund those programs. This split highlights a critical textualist debate over how courts should interpret statutory titles and permissive language when they conflict with mandatory-sounding verbs.

The procedural posture of *Pacito* is as complex as it is consequential. Following President Trump’s January 2025 executive order suspending the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP), the district court issued preliminary injunctions blocking both the suspension of admissions and the termination of funding for resettlement services. On appeal, the Ninth Circuit panel granted the government’s request to stay the injunction regarding the suspension of new admissions, citing the Supreme Court’s ruling in *Trump v. Hawaii* that § 1182(f) vests the President with ample power to impose entry restrictions. However, the panel refused to stay the injunction requiring the government to reinstate cooperative agreements for services provided to refugees already in the country. This carveout rests on the majority’s assessment that the government is unlikely to succeed on the merits of its challenge to the funding termination under the Administrative Procedure Act.

The majority’s reasoning turns on a reading of 8 U.S.C. § 1522 that views the statute as creating a statutory floor for humanitarian aid. The panel noted that Congress appropriated billions for migration and refugee assistance and that § 1522 contains specific directives for the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement. Specifically, the court pointed to language requiring the Director to "make available sufficient resources for employment training" and to "assume legal responsibility... for the child" in the case of unaccompanied minors. The majority interpreted these "shall" provisions as creating non-discretionary duties that the Executive cannot unilaterally void by terminating cooperative agreements with private nonprofit agencies. In the court’s view, the government’s admission that it was unaware whether it could secure new agencies to provide these statutorily mandated services underscored the irreparable injury to the plaintiffs if the stay were fully granted.

Judge Lee’s dissent offers a rigorous textualist counter-argument that challenges the majority’s characterization of the statute as mandatory. He begins with the statute’s title, "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees," arguing that "Authorization" is permissive by definition and signals congressional intent to grant permission rather than impose a command. Lee further scrutinizes the operative text of subsections (b) through (e), noting that they repeatedly state the Director is "authorized" to provide assistance rather than "required" to do so. He contends that the majority’s reliance on the "shall" language in subsection (a) is misplaced because that provision is explicitly conditional, applying only "In providing assistance under this section." To Lee, this structure suggests that § 1522(a) sets conditions for how the Executive must act *if* it chooses to fund the programs, not a mandate that it must fund them at all.

The dissent also tackles the specific subsections the majority cited as evidence of mandatory duties. Lee argues that even where the statute uses "shall," it is qualified by terms like "if necessary" or "as needed," which preserve executive discretion over the scope and existence of the programs. For instance, regarding unaccompanied minors, the statute requires the Director to assume responsibility only "if necessary." Lee posits that this language drips with deference to the Executive Branch and fails to establish the firm mandate the majority perceives. By framing the statute as an authorization scheme rather than a spending mandate, Lee concludes that the judiciary is in no position to compel the Executive to maintain specific funding arrangements when Congress has left the operational details to administrative discretion.

The doctrinal implications of this split extend well beyond the immediate facts of *Pacito*. If the majority’s interpretation prevails, it establishes that Congress can effectively limit the President’s immigration enforcement powers through the specific spending mandates of the Refugee Act, even in the face of broad plenary power under § 1182(f). This would suggest that once the Executive admits a class of aliens, it may be statutorily locked into providing specific support services, regardless of policy shifts. Conversely, if Judge Lee’s textualist approach gains traction in future litigation, it could empower the Executive to reshape or dismantle existing refugee infrastructure by characterizing statutory "authorizations" as discretionary grants of power. This would align with a broader trend of judicial deference to the Executive in immigration matters, potentially insulating funding decisions from APA review unless Congress uses unmistakably mandatory language.

Ultimately, *Pacito* serves as a microcosm of the modern administrative state’s struggle over the meaning of statutory commands. The majority prioritized the statutory text’s specific directives to ensure continuity of care for vulnerable populations, while the dissent prioritized the structural implications of the statute’s title and permissive framing to preserve executive flexibility. As the Ninth Circuit prepares to issue its full opinion on the merits, this textualist divide will likely attract attention from immigration advocates and scholars alike, who are watching to see whether the courts will treat the Refugee Act as a binding social contract or a discretionary policy tool.



# Emergency Stays in Immigration: The 9th Circuit's Evolving Carveouts

## The High-Stakes Procedural Dance

In the realm of immigration litigation, the period between a district court’s preliminary injunction and a final appellate ruling is often where the most critical human consequences play out. The Ninth Circuit’s panel order in *Pacito v. Trump*, issued on September 12, 2025, offers a compelling case study in how appellate courts manage these interim periods when executive authority clashes with statutory mandates. While the substantive merits of the refugee admissions suspension remain pending, the procedural history of this case—from March to September 2025—reveals a nuanced judicial approach to emergency stays. The court’s willingness to craft specific exceptions, or "carveouts," within a broader stay demonstrates a sophisticated balancing act between deferring to presidential power and protecting statutory rights during the pendency of an appeal.

The litigation began in earnest when President Trump issued Executive Order 14163 in January 2025, suspending the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) under the authority of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). Following a district court preliminary injunction in February, the government sought a stay pending appeal. The Ninth Circuit’s initial response on March 25, 2025, was pivotal. The motions panel granted a stay of the district court’s injunction but included a significant exception: the stay did not apply to individuals who had been conditionally approved for refugee status before January 20, 2025. This "conditionally approved" carveout allowed a specific cohort of refugees to proceed with resettlement even as the broader program was frozen. This decision signaled early on that while the court recognized the President’s broad discretion over entry, it was unwilling to let that discretion swallow existing statutory commitments to those already in the pipeline.

## From Procedural Carveouts to Statutory Mandates

The procedural posture evolved significantly over the summer of 2025, culminating in the September 12 order. In that order, the panel vacated the March 25 stay and the subsequent administrative stays, replacing them with a new framework that shifted the focus from procedural status to statutory obligation. While the court reaffirmed that the government was likely to succeed on the challenge to the suspension of admissions itself—citing *Trump v. Hawaii* and the broad deference owed to the President under § 1182(f)—the panel drew a sharp line at the funding and provision of resettlement services.

The court found that the government was not likely to prevail on the plaintiffs’ Administrative Procedure Act challenges regarding the defunding of reception and placement services. Relying on 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the panel noted that the statute uses mandatory language, requiring the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to provide specific services to refugees already admitted to the United States. Consequently, the September 12 order directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide these services, even as the broader stay on admissions remained in effect. This evolution from the March "conditionally approved" exception to the September "admitted refugee services" mandate illustrates how the court’s reasoning matured from a focus on individual procedural status to a broader interpretation of congressional intent regarding domestic resettlement obligations.

## Strategic Implications for Emergency Litigation

The trajectory of *Pacito* offers valuable lessons for litigators navigating emergency immigration enforcement cases. The initial "conditionally approved" carveout highlights the efficacy of seeking tailored relief that isolates specific classes of plaintiffs most vulnerable to immediate harm. By carving out those already conditionally approved, the court preserved the district court’s injunction for a discrete group without immediately resolving the broader constitutional question of presidential suspension power. This strategy allows the judiciary to mitigate irreparable harm while the legal merits are hashed out, a critical consideration given the *Nken* factors governing stays.

However, the shift in the September order suggests that successful emergency litigation may increasingly require grounding arguments in specific statutory mandates rather than relying solely on equitable discretion. The court’s reliance on § 1522 to mandate the reinstatement of funding agreements indicates that when Congress has explicitly authorized and structured a program with mandatory language ("shall"), executive attempts to defund or terminate it face a higher bar for staying injunctions. For future challengers of immigration policies, this implies that identifying specific statutory duties that survive a general suspension of entry could be a potent litigation strategy. It suggests that even when the President wins the battle on the border, Congress may have already won the war on domestic support, provided the statutory language is sufficiently clear.

## Doctrinal Tensions and the Dissent

The split in the panel underscores the doctrinal tension at the heart of this case. Judge Lee, concurring in part and dissenting in part, argued that § 1522 should be read as an authorization rather than a mandate. He pointed to the title of the section and the use of "authorized" in various subsections to argue that the executive branch retains discretion over whether to fund these programs, provided appropriations exist. This view aligns with a broader textualist approach that emphasizes executive flexibility in immigration administration.

However, the majority’s interpretation of the "shall" language in § 1522(a) and related subsections regarding unaccompanied minors and interim support suggests a more rigid view of congressional intent. This disagreement highlights a recurring theme in immigration law: the extent to which statutory funding authorizations create enforceable rights for beneficiaries versus discretionary tools for the executive. The majority’s decision to order the reinstatement of agreements despite the government’s uncertainty about its ability to secure new agencies indicates that the court views the statutory duty as non-delegable in this context.

## Looking Ahead

As the Ninth Circuit prepares to issue a full opinion on the merits, the stay orders in *Pacito* have already set a significant precedent for how courts manage the intersection of executive immigration power and statutory resettlement obligations. The procedural history demonstrates that appellate courts are willing to engage in granular stay orders that balance deference to the President with the protection of statutory rights. For the parties involved, the distinction between the suspension of entry and the provision of services for those already admitted has become the defining feature of the litigation. For observers, the case serves as a reminder that in high-stakes immigration enforcement, the procedural mechanisms of stays and injunctions are often just as consequential as the final judgment.
