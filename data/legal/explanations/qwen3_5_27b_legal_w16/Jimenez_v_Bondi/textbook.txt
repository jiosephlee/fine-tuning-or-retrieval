Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Factual and Procedural Background

This chapter establishes the factual and procedural foundation for *Jimenez v. Bondi*, a decision by the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit. To understand the court’s legal analysis regarding asylum eligibility, a law student must first grasp the specific circumstances that led the Petitioners to flee Colombia, the administrative process they underwent in the United States, and the statutory basis for the appellate court’s jurisdiction. The following sections detail the Petitioners’ identities, the escalating threats they faced, the immigration court proceedings, and the jurisdictional framework governing this appeal.

## Petitioner Profile: Colombian Citizens and Liberal Party Members

The Petitioners in this case are Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons. They are natives and citizens of Colombia who originally resided in Bogota. Before relocating to the United States in December 2022, the family owned and operated a furniture factory in Bogota. A central component of their personal history relevant to their asylum claim is their political affiliation. Collectively, the family has been members of the Colombian Liberal Party since 2012. During the 2022 Colombian presidential election, they actively supported Federico Gutierrez, a candidate who ran against the eventual winner, President Gustavo Petro.

Jimenez’s professional background is also significant to the factual record. At the time of the relevant events in 2022, Jimenez was a law student holding a provisional law license. In addition to his work at the family factory, he worked as an intern for the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, assisting victims of domestic abuse, extortion, and other crimes. This background is crucial because it informs the Petitioners’ argument regarding the motives behind the threats they received. The combination of their political activity and Jimenez’s legal work creates the factual matrix the court must evaluate to determine if the harm they suffered was connected to a protected ground under immigration law.

## The Conflict: Property Dispute with 'Don Rafa' and Escalating Threats

The factual record outlines a timeline of escalating threats against the Petitioners, beginning in May 2022 and intensifying through September 2022. Initially, the threats appeared politically motivated. Beginning in late May 2022, Jimenez received threatening phone calls once or twice a week regarding his involvement with the Liberal Party. Other party leaders received similar threats, and in early June 2022, a colleague was kidnapped and murdered despite reporting threats. The Petitioners continued their campaign activities despite these dangers.

The nature of the threats shifted in June 2022 when Jimenez became involved in a property dispute on behalf of a client. The client sought help recovering a property occupied by her ex-husband, Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, known as "Don Rafa." Jimenez discovered that Don Rafa was a leader of narco-trafficking guerrillas politically aligned with President Petro and that the property was used for criminal activities including prostitution and kidnapping. During a visit to negotiate the property's return, Jimenez was warned by a local police officer to stay away. Following this intervention, the threats against the Petitioners escalated significantly.

In late June 2022, Jimenez received a threatening brochure at his factory stating, "death to snitches from Liberal Party." In July 2022, two men held Jimenez at gunpoint at the factory, accusing him of being a "snitch" for the prosecutor’s office and blaming him for police searches at Don Rafa’s property. Approximately two months later, the same men assaulted a factory employee and demanded to know the Petitioners' whereabouts, explicitly stating the order came from Don Rafa. The threats extended to the family’s children; Jimenez witnessed a man grab his younger son at school and tell him to "run to your dad." After reporting these incidents to a police patrol officer, who stated nothing could be done and advised the family to leave, the Petitioners moved to the United States. These facts are essential for the legal analysis because the court must distinguish whether the harm was inflicted due to political opinion or due to Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s criminal enterprises.

## Immigration Proceedings: IJ Denial and BIA Affirmation

Upon arriving in the United States without being admitted or paroled, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) served the Petitioners with individual Notices to Appear three months after their arrival. These notices charged each Petitioner as removable pursuant to the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). In August 2023, during immigration court proceedings, the Petitioners admitted the truth of the DHS allegations. The Immigration Judge (IJ) charged them as inadmissible and directed removal to Colombia. In response, the Petitioners applied for asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children proceeding as derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s application.

The IJ conducted a merits hearing based on the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte. While the IJ found the Petitioners to be credible, the judge denied their applications for relief. The IJ concluded that the Petitioners failed to establish that the harm they suffered amounted to past persecution, that the harm had a nexus to a statutorily protected ground, that internal relocation within Colombia was not reasonably available, or that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them. The IJ also denied protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT). The Petitioners appealed this decision to the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA). The BIA affirmed the IJ’s decision and dismissed the appeal, also rejecting the Petitioners' due process arguments regarding their right to counsel. This administrative history establishes the procedural posture of the case: the Petitioners are seeking judicial review of a final order of removal affirmed by the BIA.

## Appellate Jurisdiction Under 8 U.S.C. § 1252

The United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit exercised jurisdiction over this petition for review under 8 U.S.C. § 1252. This statute grants federal courts of appeals jurisdiction to review final orders of removal, which can include a consolidated review of asylum claims denied by the BIA. The court noted that its review is limited to the administrative record on which the order of removal is based. Because a single member of the BIA affirmed the IJ’s decision, the appellate court reviewed the BIA’s decision but was permitted to consult the IJ’s more complete explanation of the grounds for denial.

Understanding this jurisdictional framework is vital for interpreting the scope of the court’s opinion. The court reviews the BIA’s findings of fact under a "substantial-evidence standard." Under this standard, the BIA’s findings of fact are conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary. This deferential standard of review means the appellate court does not reweigh the evidence or substitute its own judgment for that of the immigration judges. Instead, the court examines whether the administrative record contains substantial evidence to support the IJ’s and BIA’s conclusions regarding the Petitioners' eligibility for asylum and withholding of removal. This procedural constraint shapes the court's analysis, requiring the Petitioners to show that the administrative findings were unsupported by the record rather than simply incorrect.

# Legal Standards for Asylum and Withholding

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit reviews a final order of removal issued by the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA). The petitioners, Colombian nationals, sought asylum and withholding of removal, claiming they faced persecution in their home country. To understand the court’s analysis, a law student must first grasp the statutory framework governing these forms of relief. The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) establishes the eligibility requirements for asylum, which serves as the foundation for the court’s review. Asylum is a discretionary form of relief available to eligible noncitizens who can prove they qualify as refugees. The court in *Jimenez* relies on this statutory definition to evaluate whether the petitioners met their burden of proof. Furthermore, because the appellate court is reviewing an administrative decision, it applies a specific standard of review to the factual findings made by the immigration judge and the BIA. This chapter outlines these critical legal standards as applied in the opinion.

## INA Definition of a Refugee and Protected Grounds

The core of any asylum claim is the statutory definition of a refugee found in the INA. As cited by the Tenth Circuit in *Jimenez*, a refugee is defined as any person who is outside their country of nationality and is unable or unwilling to return to, or avail themselves of the protection of, that country. Crucially, this inability or unwillingness must be because of persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution. The statute requires that this persecution be on account of specific protected grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. In *Jimenez*, the petitioners argued that their political opinion was the basis for the harm they suffered, specifically their support for a rival political candidate in Colombia.

The court emphasizes that the protected ground must be the reason for the persecution, not merely incidental to it. The opinion notes that to show persecution on account of a political opinion, the applicant must establish that the political opinion was “at least one central reason” for the persecution. This means the political opinion cannot be tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for the harm. In mixed-motive cases where a persecutor may have multiple reasons for targeting an individual, the protected ground must still play a central role. If the harm was primarily due to a personal dispute or professional conflict, rather than the applicant’s political views, the nexus requirement is not met. The court in *Jimenez* applies this definition to determine if the threats and violence the petitioners faced were driven by their political activities or by other factors, such as a property dispute.

## Burden of Proof: Preponderance of the Evidence

In asylum proceedings, the burden of proof rests squarely on the applicant. The Tenth Circuit clarifies in *Jimenez* that an applicant bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that they are a refugee. This standard requires the applicant to show that it is more likely than not that they meet the statutory definition of a refugee. This burden applies to the various elements of the claim, including the occurrence of persecution, the nexus to a protected ground, and the government’s inability or unwillingness to protect.

The opinion highlights that this burden is significant. The court notes that the petitioners must demonstrate that they suffered persecution, that the persecution had a nexus to a statutorily protected ground, and that the Colombian government is unwilling or unable to help them. If the applicant fails to meet this burden on any essential element, the claim for asylum must fail. Additionally, the court notes that withholding of removal has a higher standard than asylum. Consequently, if petitioners fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution under the asylum standard, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard required for withholding of removal. This hierarchy of standards underscores the importance of the preponderance of the evidence threshold in establishing eligibility for discretionary relief.

## Distinction Between Past Persecution and Well-Founded Fear

A critical legal distinction in asylum law is between a showing of past persecution and a showing of a well-founded fear of future persecution. The opinion explains that an applicant may obtain refugee status through a showing of past persecution, which gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution. To establish past persecution, the applicant must prove three elements: an incident or incidents that rise to the level of persecution, that the persecution was on account of a statutorily protected ground, and that it was committed by the government or forces the government is unable or unwilling to control.

The court in *Jimenez* provides specific guidance on what constitutes persecution. Persecution is undefined in the INA, but Tenth Circuit precedent requires the infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ in a way regarded as offensive. This harm must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty. The court reviews incidents collectively, as the cumulative effects of multiple incidents may constitute persecution. However, the opinion makes clear that threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution. While the court considers threats in assessing the cumulative impact, the harm suffered must be offensive and more than mere sporadic threats. In *Jimenez*, the petitioners conceded they were not physically harmed, and the court found that their emotional and psychological distress, stemming from threats, was insufficient to rise to the level of persecution under the law. If past persecution is not established, the applicant must independently prove a well-founded fear of future persecution, which requires showing that the government is unable or unwilling to protect them from that future harm.

## Substantial Evidence Standard for Factual Findings

When the Tenth Circuit reviews the BIA’s decision, it does not conduct a de novo trial. Instead, the court reviews the BIA’s findings of fact under a substantial-evidence standard. This standard is deferential to the administrative agency. Under this standard, the BIA’s findings of fact are conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary. The court emphasizes that it is not its prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the BIA’s decision.

This standard of review limits the appellate court’s ability to overturn factual determinations made by the immigration judge and the BIA. The court notes that it reviews the administrative record on which the order of removal is based. While the BIA may affirm an IJ’s decision, the appellate court is not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of the grounds for denial. In *Jimenez*, the court applies this standard to the petitioners’ claims regarding the level of harm, the nexus to political opinion, and the availability of government protection. Because the record contained substantial evidence supporting the BIA’s findings that the harm did not amount to persecution and that the government was not unwilling to protect, the court denies the petition for review. This standard ensures that judicial review focuses on whether the agency’s decision was reasonable based on the record, rather than whether the appellate judges would have decided the facts differently.

# Analysis of Persecution and Nexus

## Chapter Description

This chapter examines the court's reasoning regarding whether the Petitioners suffered harm rising to the level of persecution and whether that harm was motivated by a statutorily protected ground.

## Threshold for Persecution: Threats Versus Physical Harm

To obtain asylum, an applicant must demonstrate they are a refugee, which requires showing persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on account of a protected ground. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit addressed the threshold question of whether the Petitioners' experiences constituted "persecution." The court clarified that while the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) does not define persecution, the circuit has established that it requires the infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ in a way regarded as offensive. Crucially, this harm must entail more than mere restrictions or threats to life and liberty.

The court applied this standard to the Petitioners' specific circumstances, noting that they conceded at their merits hearing that they were not physically harmed. Although physical harm is not strictly necessary to satisfy the persecution prong, the court emphasized that threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution. The Petitioners argued that they suffered lasting emotional and psychological harm, citing the wife’s inability to sleep and the son’s panic attacks. However, the court relied on precedent to reject this argument, noting that fears stemming from threats are generally insufficient to rise to the level of persecution. The opinion highlights that the court has rarely, if ever, granted asylum based solely on threats and emotional or psychological harm, even when the events were physically stressful. By grounding this analysis in the Petitioners' own concessions and binding circuit case law, the court established a high bar for non-physical harm, requiring it to be more than the distress naturally resulting from threats.

## Cumulative Impact Analysis of Multiple Incidents

A critical pedagogical point in *Jimenez* is how courts evaluate multiple incidents of mistreatment. The court explicitly stated that it does not look at each incident in isolation. Instead, it considers them collectively because the cumulative effects of multiple incidents may constitute persecution. This cumulative impact analysis allows an applicant to aggregate various forms of harm—such as threats, intimidation, and property damage—to meet the threshold for persecution.

In this case, the Petitioners attempted to aggregate several incidents: threatening phone calls, a threatening brochure, an armed confrontation at the factory, an assault on an employee, and a threat involving their son. Despite applying the cumulative rule, the court found that the aggregate of these events still did not amount to past persecution. The reasoning illustrates that the cumulative standard does not guarantee a finding of persecution simply because there were multiple negative events. The court reiterated that the collective harm must still be offensive and more than sporadic threats. Because the underlying incidents were primarily threats or intimidation without the requisite level of inflicted suffering, adding them together did not transform the nature of the harm into legal persecution. This distinction teaches students that the cumulative rule is a method of evaluation, not a lower standard of proof for the severity of harm required.

## Nexus Requirement: Political Opinion as a Central Reason

Once the threshold for persecution is established, an applicant must prove a "nexus" between the harm and a statutorily protected ground, such as political opinion. In *Jimenez*, the Petitioners argued that the harm was on account of their political opinion, specifically their support for the Colombian Liberal Party and a presidential candidate rival to the current administration. The court outlined the statutory requirement that the political opinion must be "at least one central reason" for the persecution. This standard is derived from 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i) and requires that the protected ground cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for the harm.

The court’s analysis of the nexus requirement demonstrates the burden of proof resting on the applicant. It is not enough to show that the applicant holds a political opinion and that the persecutor knows about it. The applicant must demonstrate that the persecutor was motivated by that opinion as a central reason for the inflicted harm. The opinion emphasizes that while a persecutor can have multiple motives, the protected ground must play a significant role in the decision to target the victim. If the political opinion is merely a minor factor compared to other motivations, the nexus requirement is not met. This section of the opinion serves as a guide for distinguishing between harm that coincidentally involves a protected characteristic and harm that is causally linked to it.

## Mixed-Motive Cases: Distinguishing Professional Conflict from Political Opinion

The court’s reasoning in *Jimenez* provides a clear example of how to analyze mixed-motive cases, where a persecutor may have both protected and non-protected reasons for targeting an individual. The Petitioners claimed a nexus to political opinion, but the court found substantial evidence supporting the Immigration Judge’s conclusion that the predominant motive was professional conflict rather than political ideology. The court noted that the Petitioners had continued their political campaigning despite earlier threats, suggesting the initial threats were not the primary driver of the escalated violence.

The escalation of threats coincided with Jimenez’s professional involvement in a property dispute with "Don Rafa," a narco-trafficking leader. The court highlighted specific evidence: the men accused Jimenez of being a "snitch" for the prosecutor’s office and responsible for searches and seizures at the property, rather than focusing solely on his political affiliation. Furthermore, the threats explicitly stated that the orders came from Don Rafa regarding the property issue. By distinguishing between the timing and content of the threats, the court determined that the Petitioners' political opinion was tangential and subordinate to the main reason for the harm: Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s revenue and security. This analysis teaches students to scrutinize the specific accusations and timing of threats to determine the true motive behind the persecution, ensuring that political opinions are not treated as a central reason when professional or criminal disputes are the actual catalyst.

# State Protection and Internal Relocation

In asylum and withholding of removal claims, proving past persecution or a well-founded fear of future persecution is not sufficient on its own. An applicant must also demonstrate that their home government is unable or unwilling to protect them from that harm. Furthermore, even if the government cannot protect the applicant in their current location, relief may still be denied if the applicant can safely relocate to another part of their home country. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit addressed both of these critical requirements. The court analyzed whether the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect the Petitioners from narco-traffickers and whether the Petitioners could reasonably relocate within Colombia to avoid future harm. The following sections break down the legal standards applied by the court and how those standards were applied to the specific facts of the Petitioners’ case.

## Government Protection Inquiry: Notice and Response

To establish eligibility for asylum, an applicant must show that the harm they suffered or fear is inflicted by the government or by forces the government is unable or unwilling to control. In *Jimenez*, the court clarified that this "unable or unwilling" analysis depends heavily on the government’s actual response to the persecution. The court cited *Singh v. Bondi* to explain that two types of information weigh heavily in this inquiry: whether the government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors, and whether the government offered protection to the applicant, such as by placing them in protective custody.

Crucially, the court emphasized that this analysis is intertwined with whether the government had notice of the alleged persecution and an opportunity to respond. Without notice, there is no way to determine how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped. Therefore, a key fact in the analysis is whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government. In the Petitioners’ case, the record showed that their efforts to reach out to the government for help were minimal. Although Jimenez and his father worked for the government in Colombia, neither reported wrongdoing or corruption related to the threats they faced. The Petitioners testified that they spoke to two individual police officers who advised them not to seek trouble, but the court noted that this interaction did not constitute a formal report of the threats or a request for protection. Because the Petitioners did not report the persecution, the court could not conclude that the government was unwilling to protect them, as the government never had the opportunity to act on the specific threats against the family.

## Futility of Reporting: Evidence of Systemic Failure

The legal standard acknowledges that a failure to report persecution does not automatically undercut a claim that the government is unable or unwilling to protect. An applicant may satisfy the government protection requirement by demonstrating that filing a police report would have been futile or dangerous. However, the burden remains on the applicant to provide evidence supporting this claim. In *Jimenez*, the Petitioners argued that contacting the government would have been futile because their persecutors were closely aligned and associated with the Colombian government. They suggested that the police were contributing to the problem.

The court rejected this argument because it was unsupported by the record. The opinion noted that the Colombian government was actually aware of the property at issue, which was being used for narco-trafficking and prostitution, and had attempted several searches and seizures to address the ongoing criminal conduct there. This evidence countered the Petitioners’ assertion that the government was unwilling or unable to stop the persecutors. Furthermore, the court distinguished between the actions of individual officers and systemic government failure. Citing *Osorio-Morales v. Garland*, the court explained that while police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is unable or unwilling to protect a victim. Because the Petitioners could not show systemic failure or that reporting would have been dangerous given the government’s existing actions against the property, they failed to establish that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them.

## Internal Relocation Feasibility and Reasonableness

Even if an applicant establishes past persecution and that the government cannot protect them in their current location, asylum may still be denied if the applicant can avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of their country. Under the regulations cited in the opinion, the government may rebut an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution by showing that the applicant could reasonably relocate. In *Jimenez*, the court addressed this argument briefly but definitively. The court found that the record supported the finding that the Petitioners could reasonably relocate to another part of Colombia.

The court relied on several factual findings to determine feasibility. First, the Petitioners’ family and relatives continued to live in Colombia unharmed, and Jimenez’s father still worked for the criminal justice system, suggesting that the threat was not nationwide. Second, the Petitioners had safely lived with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before deciding to leave for the United States, demonstrating that they could survive outside their immediate neighborhood. Finally, the court noted the specific scope of the threats received. The men who threatened the Petitioners told them to move out of the neighborhood, but they did not threaten the family to move out of the country. This distinction was critical; because the threat was geographically limited, the Petitioners could mitigate the potential for future persecution by moving to a different area within Colombia where the persecutors did not have reach.

## Rebuttal of Well-Founded Fear Through Relocation

The finding that internal relocation is feasible has a direct procedural impact on the asylum claim. As the court explained, the government may rebut an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution if it shows by a preponderance of the evidence that the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating. By determining that the Petitioners could safely and reasonably relocate within Colombia, the court concluded that they could not establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum. This determination was sufficient to deny the petition for review regarding asylum.

The court also addressed the implications of this finding for the Petitioners’ request for withholding of removal. Withholding of removal requires a higher standard of proof than asylum; an applicant must show that it is more likely than not that they would be persecuted upon return. Because the Petitioners failed to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution under the lower asylum standard—largely due to the availability of internal relocation—they necessarily failed to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal. The court cited *Carias-Mejia v. Garland* to support the proposition that failure on asylum grounds precludes withholding of removal. Consequently, the Tenth Circuit denied the petition for review, affirming the BIA’s decision that the Petitioners did not qualify for protection because they could not show the government was unable to protect them and because safe relocation within Colombia was a viable option.

# Holding, Disposition, and Significance

## Denial of Petition for Review

The Tenth Circuit concluded its opinion by denying the Petitioners' request for review of the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) decision. Exercising jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, the court affirmed the lower administrative rulings, holding that the Petitioners failed to satisfy the statutory requirements for asylum or withholding of removal. This disposition reflects the court's deferential standard of review, which requires substantial evidence to support the BIA's findings of fact. In *Jimenez*, the court found that the record contained sufficient evidence to support the Immigration Judge's and BIA's determinations on four critical fronts: the Petitioners did not suffer past persecution, their alleged harm lacked the necessary nexus to a protected ground, the Colombian government was willing and able to protect them, and internal relocation within Colombia was reasonably available. For law students, this disposition underscores the practical reality of appellate review in immigration cases; the court does not reweigh the evidence or substitute its judgment for that of the administrative agency. Instead, the court asks whether a reasonable adjudicator could have reached the same conclusion based on the record presented. Because the Petitioners could not demonstrate that their claims met the statutory definition of a refugee, the petition for review was denied, leaving the order of removal in effect.

## Higher Standard for Withholding of Removal

In the conclusion of the opinion, the court briefly addresses the Petitioners' claim for withholding of removal, a form of relief distinct from asylum. While the Petitioners appealed the denial of both forms of relief, the court reasoned that a failure to qualify for asylum automatically precludes eligibility for withholding of removal. This legal principle rests on the differing statutory standards governing each form of protection. Asylum is discretionary and requires a showing of a "well-founded fear" of persecution, whereas withholding of removal is mandatory but requires a higher showing of a "clear probability" that the applicant's life or freedom would be threatened. As the court stated, "because Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal." This reasoning teaches students that in immigration litigation, the asylum standard often serves as a threshold inquiry. If an applicant cannot meet the lower bar of a well-founded fear, the more stringent requirement for withholding of removal cannot be satisfied. Consequently, the court did not need to conduct a separate, exhaustive analysis of the withholding claim once the asylum claim was rejected on the merits.

## Implications for Private Persecution Claims

*Jimenez* provides significant guidance for understanding how the Tenth Circuit evaluates claims involving private persecutors, such as the narco-trafficking group led by "Don Rafa" in this case. Under the Immigration and Nationality Act, persecution by non-state actors is actionable only if the government is unable or unwilling to control those actors. The court's analysis in Section II.E highlights the rigorous evidentiary burden placed on applicants in these scenarios. Specifically, the court emphasized that an applicant must demonstrate that they sought government protection and that such efforts were futile. The opinion notes that while a failure to report is not necessarily fatal, it "can undercut a claim that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors." In *Jimenez*, the Petitioners' minimal efforts to report threats to police, coupled with evidence that law enforcement had previously attempted searches and seizures at the property in question, undermined their argument that the government was complicit or helpless. This reinforces the doctrinal requirement that applicants cannot assume government futility without evidence; they must show that the specific persecutors are beyond the reach of state authority or that the state is actively colluding with them.

## Reliance on Recent Circuit Precedent (e.g., *Singh v. Bondi*)

The opinion in *Jimenez* is heavily anchored in recent Tenth Circuit precedent, most notably *Singh v. Bondi*, 130 F.4th 848 (10th Cir. 2025). The court cites *Singh* repeatedly to establish the jurisdictional framework, the standard of review, and the legal tests for government protection and persecution. For example, the court relies on *Singh* to define the scope of its jurisdiction over final orders of removal and to articulate the "unable-or-unwilling" analysis regarding government protection. By grounding its decision in *Singh*, the *Jimenez* court signals that it is applying settled circuit law rather than creating new doctrine. This reliance is pedagogically important for students to recognize how appellate courts maintain consistency within a circuit. When reading *Jimenez*, one should observe how the court applies the rules from *Singh* to the specific facts of the Petitioners' case. For instance, just as *Singh* requires evidence of a government's response to past persecution, *Jimenez* applies that same rule to reject the Petitioners' claim that the Colombian government was unable to protect them. This demonstrates the hierarchical nature of case law, where newer opinions like *Jimenez* operationalize the broader principles established in controlling precedents like *Singh*.
