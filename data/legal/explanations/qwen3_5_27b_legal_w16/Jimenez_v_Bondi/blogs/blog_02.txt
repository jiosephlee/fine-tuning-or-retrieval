Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Beyond Threats: The 10th Circuit's Rigid Definition of Persecution in Jimenez

## The High Bar for Asylum in the Tenth Circuit

In immigration law, the definition of "persecution" often serves as the gatekeeper for asylum relief. While the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) does not explicitly define the term, circuit courts have developed distinct standards for what constitutes sufficient harm to warrant protection. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, decided October 7, 2025, the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit reaffirmed its stringent approach to this threshold. The panel denied review of a Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) decision rejecting the asylum claims of a Colombian family, holding that a combination of death threats, psychological distress, and economic interference did not rise to the level of past persecution. For practitioners and asylum seekers in the Tenth Circuit, *Jimenez* serves as a stark reminder that threats and emotional trauma, absent physical injury, face an exceptionally high bar to qualify as persecution.

## Factual Background and the Nature of the Harm

The petitioners, a Colombian family residing in the United States, sought asylum based on their involvement with the Colombian Liberal Party and a subsequent conflict with a local narco-trafficking leader known as "Don Rafa." The record indicates that the father, Jimenez, received threatening phone calls, a threatening brochure, and two in-person armed confrontations at his factory. While the threats were severe—including accusations that Jimenez was a "snitch" and explicit warnings to leave the neighborhood—the petitioners conceded at the merits hearing that they suffered no physical harm.

The family did, however, allege significant psychological tolls. The mother testified to insomnia, and their son reportedly suffered panic attacks and stress. Despite these claims, the Tenth Circuit found substantial evidence supporting the BIA’s conclusion that these incidents, even viewed cumulatively, did not constitute persecution. The court emphasized that while physical harm is not strictly necessary to satisfy the persecution prong, the harm inflicted must be "offensive and more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats." By citing precedents such as *Karki v. Holder* and *Chhetri v. Rosen*, the court signaled that the Tenth Circuit rarely, if ever, grants asylum based solely on threats and emotional or psychological harm, even when those threats involve weapons or the potential for death.

## The Cumulative Effects Doctrine and Its Limits

A central tension in asylum jurisprudence is the "cumulative effects" doctrine, which allows adjudicators to consider multiple incidents of mistreatment collectively rather than in isolation. The Tenth Circuit acknowledged this doctrine in *Jimenez*, noting that "the cumulative effects of multiple incidents may constitute persecution." However, the application of this doctrine in the decision highlights its limitations within the circuit.

The court reasoned that while the petitioners endured "unfortunate circumstances," the aggregate of their experiences—threats, loss of sleep, and anxiety—did not cross the threshold into persecution. The opinion distinguishes between being "physically stressful or psychologically unsettling" and meeting the statutory definition of persecution. This distinction is critical for legal strategy. It suggests that in the Tenth Circuit, the cumulative effects doctrine functions more as a tool to weigh the severity of non-physical incidents rather than a mechanism to elevate psychological distress to the level of persecution without some accompanying physical or severe liberty-restricting harm. The court pointed to *Brandy v. Holder*, where even a 10-hour arrest and detention combined with threats was insufficient, to illustrate the high magnitude of harm required.

## Doctrinal Implications for Future Litigation

The *Jimenez* decision has significant downstream effects for asylum litigation in the Tenth Circuit. First, it reinforces the "threats alone" rule established in *Karki*. Attorneys arguing asylum cases involving non-lethal threats must be prepared to demonstrate that the threats were accompanied by some form of tangible harm or restriction on liberty that goes beyond the fear of future violence. Second, the opinion underscores the importance of the nexus requirement, even when persecution is established. While the court primarily focused on the persecution element, it noted that the political opinion was "tangential and subordinate" to the property dispute motive. This mixed-motive analysis requires petitioners to prove that their political opinion was "at least one central reason" for the harm, a standard that can be difficult to meet when criminal actors are involved.

Furthermore, *Jimenez* highlights the evidentiary burden regarding government protection. The court noted that the petitioners' efforts to seek help were minimal and relied on anecdotal conversations with unhelpful officers rather than systemic evidence of government unwillingness. The fact that law enforcement had previously attempted searches of the trafficker's property weighed against the claim that the government was unable to control the persecutors. This suggests that future applicants must provide concrete evidence of government inaction or complicity, rather than relying on the general reputation of corruption or isolated interactions with police.

## Conclusion

*Jimenez v. Bondi* does not break new ground doctrinally, but it solidifies the Tenth Circuit's position on the spectrum of asylum jurisprudence. By denying relief to a family facing credible death threats and significant psychological trauma without physical injury, the court maintains a rigid definition of persecution that prioritizes tangible harm over fear and distress. For asylum seekers in the circuit, the message is clear: unless the record demonstrates physical harm or severe restrictions on liberty, claims based on threats and psychological impact are likely to fall short of the statutory requirement. As the circuit continues to apply precedents like *Chhetri* and *Karki*, the path to asylum remains narrow for those fleeing non-physical but life-threatening environments.
