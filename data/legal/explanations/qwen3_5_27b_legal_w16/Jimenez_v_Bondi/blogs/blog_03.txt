Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# The Duty to Report and Internal Relocation: High Hurdles for Asylum Seekers in Jimenez

In the complex landscape of immigration law, the difference between safety and removal often hinges on evidentiary technicalities as much as factual realities. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, decided October 7, 2025, the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit issued a decision that underscores the rigorous procedural burdens placed on asylum seekers. While the case involved a family fleeing Colombia after threats from narco-traffickers linked to the political establishment, the court denied relief primarily on the grounds that the petitioners failed to prove the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them and that internal relocation was a viable option. *Jimenez* serves as a stark reminder that even credible accounts of danger can fall short if the applicant cannot bridge the gap between private harm and state responsibility.

## The Burden of Proving State Complicity

The core of the *Jimenez* decision rests on the "unable or unwilling" prong of the asylum standard. To establish eligibility, an applicant must show that the government is either unable or unwilling to control the forces threatening them. The Tenth Circuit, relying on its recent precedent in *Singh v. Bondi*, emphasized that this analysis is fact-intensive and heavily dependent on whether the applicant sought government protection in the first place.

The court found that the petitioners' efforts to engage the state were insufficient to prove systemic failure. Although the petitioners testified that a police officer advised them to leave because "nothing could be done," the court held that a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily demonstrate that the entire government is unwilling to protect its citizens. The opinion distinguishes between individual apathy and systemic inability, citing *Osorio-Morales* to note that courts generally require evidence of systemic failure rather than isolated incidents of police inaction.

This reasoning places a significant evidentiary burden on applicants in countries with high levels of corruption or criminal infiltration. The court noted that the Colombian government had actually attempted several searches and seizures at the property in question, suggesting the state was not entirely aligned with the persecutors. Consequently, the petitioners' failure to file formal reports or utilize Jimenez’s contacts within the law enforcement system undercut their claim that seeking help would have been futile. The court acknowledged that an applicant might satisfy the requirement by showing that reporting would have been dangerous, but it found the record insufficient to support that conclusion. This creates a catch-22 for many asylum seekers: they must report crimes to prove the government is ineffective, yet in environments where reporting is dangerous, the failure to report can be used against them to prove the government *was* effective or that the danger was not state-sponsored.

## The Reasonableness of Internal Relocation

Even if the petitioners had established a well-founded fear of persecution, the Tenth Circuit held that they would still fail because internal relocation was reasonably available. The internal relocation doctrine allows the government to rebut an asylum claim by showing the applicant could avoid persecution by moving to another part of their home country. In *Jimenez*, the court looked closely at the geographic scope of the threats.

The persecutors had explicitly told the family to leave the neighborhood, not the country. The court weighed this instruction against the family's ability to survive elsewhere, noting that they had safely lived with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before fleeing to the United States. Furthermore, the court pointed to the continued safety of the petitioners' relatives and Jimenez’s father, who still works for the criminal justice system in Colombia. The court rejected the argument that the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety, stating it could not grant asylum based on hypothetical hardships regarding those relatives' potential political opinions.

This analysis highlights the court's preference for concrete evidence of danger over generalized fears of country conditions. By focusing on the specific nature of the threats and the family's prior ability to relocate within Colombia, the court narrowed the window for asylum based on internal relocation. It suggests that unless a persecutor has the capacity to track an applicant across the entire nation, or unless the country is so unstable that no safe zone exists, relocation remains a viable bar to relief.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The *Jimenez* opinion reinforces a trend in the Tenth Circuit toward a strict application of the "unable or unwilling" and internal relocation standards. The decision signals that courts will not easily accept generalized assertions of state complicity without specific evidence of reporting attempts or systemic corruption that directly impedes the applicant. For practitioners, this means the record must be meticulously developed to show not just that the police were unhelpful, but that the system itself was broken or that reporting was objectively futile.

The downstream effect of this reasoning may be to shift the burden of proof even further onto the applicant regarding the futility of reporting. While the law recognizes that reporting can be dangerous, *Jimenez* suggests that subjective fear of retaliation is not enough without corroborating evidence that the state is aligned with the persecutor. In nations where corruption is endemic but not total, this distinction may become a primary battleground in appellate litigation. Ultimately, *Jimenez* illustrates that in asylum law, the path to protection is as much about documenting the failure of the state as it is about documenting the violence of the persecutor.
