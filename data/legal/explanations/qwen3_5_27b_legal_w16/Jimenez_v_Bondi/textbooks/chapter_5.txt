Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 5: # Holding, Disposition, and Significance

## Denial of Petition for Review

The Tenth Circuit concluded its opinion by denying the Petitioners' request for review of the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) decision. Exercising jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, the court affirmed the lower administrative rulings, holding that the Petitioners failed to satisfy the statutory requirements for asylum or withholding of removal. This disposition reflects the court's deferential standard of review, which requires substantial evidence to support the BIA's findings of fact. In *Jimenez*, the court found that the record contained sufficient evidence to support the Immigration Judge's and BIA's determinations on four critical fronts: the Petitioners did not suffer past persecution, their alleged harm lacked the necessary nexus to a protected ground, the Colombian government was willing and able to protect them, and internal relocation within Colombia was reasonably available. For law students, this disposition underscores the practical reality of appellate review in immigration cases; the court does not reweigh the evidence or substitute its judgment for that of the administrative agency. Instead, the court asks whether a reasonable adjudicator could have reached the same conclusion based on the record presented. Because the Petitioners could not demonstrate that their claims met the statutory definition of a refugee, the petition for review was denied, leaving the order of removal in effect.

## Higher Standard for Withholding of Removal

In the conclusion of the opinion, the court briefly addresses the Petitioners' claim for withholding of removal, a form of relief distinct from asylum. While the Petitioners appealed the denial of both forms of relief, the court reasoned that a failure to qualify for asylum automatically precludes eligibility for withholding of removal. This legal principle rests on the differing statutory standards governing each form of protection. Asylum is discretionary and requires a showing of a "well-founded fear" of persecution, whereas withholding of removal is mandatory but requires a higher showing of a "clear probability" that the applicant's life or freedom would be threatened. As the court stated, "because Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal." This reasoning teaches students that in immigration litigation, the asylum standard often serves as a threshold inquiry. If an applicant cannot meet the lower bar of a well-founded fear, the more stringent requirement for withholding of removal cannot be satisfied. Consequently, the court did not need to conduct a separate, exhaustive analysis of the withholding claim once the asylum claim was rejected on the merits.

## Implications for Private Persecution Claims

*Jimenez* provides significant guidance for understanding how the Tenth Circuit evaluates claims involving private persecutors, such as the narco-trafficking group led by "Don Rafa" in this case. Under the Immigration and Nationality Act, persecution by non-state actors is actionable only if the government is unable or unwilling to control those actors. The court's analysis in Section II.E highlights the rigorous evidentiary burden placed on applicants in these scenarios. Specifically, the court emphasized that an applicant must demonstrate that they sought government protection and that such efforts were futile. The opinion notes that while a failure to report is not necessarily fatal, it "can undercut a claim that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors." In *Jimenez*, the Petitioners' minimal efforts to report threats to police, coupled with evidence that law enforcement had previously attempted searches and seizures at the property in question, undermined their argument that the government was complicit or helpless. This reinforces the doctrinal requirement that applicants cannot assume government futility without evidence; they must show that the specific persecutors are beyond the reach of state authority or that the state is actively colluding with them.

## Reliance on Recent Circuit Precedent (e.g., *Singh v. Bondi*)

The opinion in *Jimenez* is heavily anchored in recent Tenth Circuit precedent, most notably *Singh v. Bondi*, 130 F.4th 848 (10th Cir. 2025). The court cites *Singh* repeatedly to establish the jurisdictional framework, the standard of review, and the legal tests for government protection and persecution. For example, the court relies on *Singh* to define the scope of its jurisdiction over final orders of removal and to articulate the "unable-or-unwilling" analysis regarding government protection. By grounding its decision in *Singh*, the *Jimenez* court signals that it is applying settled circuit law rather than creating new doctrine. This reliance is pedagogically important for students to recognize how appellate courts maintain consistency within a circuit. When reading *Jimenez*, one should observe how the court applies the rules from *Singh* to the specific facts of the Petitioners' case. For instance, just as *Singh* requires evidence of a government's response to past persecution, *Jimenez* applies that same rule to reject the Petitioners' claim that the Colombian government was unable to protect them. This demonstrates the hierarchical nature of case law, where newer opinions like *Jimenez* operationalize the broader principles established in controlling precedents like *Singh*.
