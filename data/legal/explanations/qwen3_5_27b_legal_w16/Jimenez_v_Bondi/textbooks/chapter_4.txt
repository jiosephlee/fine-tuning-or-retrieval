Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 4: # State Protection and Internal Relocation

In asylum and withholding of removal claims, proving past persecution or a well-founded fear of future persecution is not sufficient on its own. An applicant must also demonstrate that their home government is unable or unwilling to protect them from that harm. Furthermore, even if the government cannot protect the applicant in their current location, relief may still be denied if the applicant can safely relocate to another part of their home country. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit addressed both of these critical requirements. The court analyzed whether the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect the Petitioners from narco-traffickers and whether the Petitioners could reasonably relocate within Colombia to avoid future harm. The following sections break down the legal standards applied by the court and how those standards were applied to the specific facts of the Petitioners’ case.

## Government Protection Inquiry: Notice and Response

To establish eligibility for asylum, an applicant must show that the harm they suffered or fear is inflicted by the government or by forces the government is unable or unwilling to control. In *Jimenez*, the court clarified that this "unable or unwilling" analysis depends heavily on the government’s actual response to the persecution. The court cited *Singh v. Bondi* to explain that two types of information weigh heavily in this inquiry: whether the government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors, and whether the government offered protection to the applicant, such as by placing them in protective custody.

Crucially, the court emphasized that this analysis is intertwined with whether the government had notice of the alleged persecution and an opportunity to respond. Without notice, there is no way to determine how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped. Therefore, a key fact in the analysis is whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government. In the Petitioners’ case, the record showed that their efforts to reach out to the government for help were minimal. Although Jimenez and his father worked for the government in Colombia, neither reported wrongdoing or corruption related to the threats they faced. The Petitioners testified that they spoke to two individual police officers who advised them not to seek trouble, but the court noted that this interaction did not constitute a formal report of the threats or a request for protection. Because the Petitioners did not report the persecution, the court could not conclude that the government was unwilling to protect them, as the government never had the opportunity to act on the specific threats against the family.

## Futility of Reporting: Evidence of Systemic Failure

The legal standard acknowledges that a failure to report persecution does not automatically undercut a claim that the government is unable or unwilling to protect. An applicant may satisfy the government protection requirement by demonstrating that filing a police report would have been futile or dangerous. However, the burden remains on the applicant to provide evidence supporting this claim. In *Jimenez*, the Petitioners argued that contacting the government would have been futile because their persecutors were closely aligned and associated with the Colombian government. They suggested that the police were contributing to the problem.

The court rejected this argument because it was unsupported by the record. The opinion noted that the Colombian government was actually aware of the property at issue, which was being used for narco-trafficking and prostitution, and had attempted several searches and seizures to address the ongoing criminal conduct there. This evidence countered the Petitioners’ assertion that the government was unwilling or unable to stop the persecutors. Furthermore, the court distinguished between the actions of individual officers and systemic government failure. Citing *Osorio-Morales v. Garland*, the court explained that while police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is unable or unwilling to protect a victim. Because the Petitioners could not show systemic failure or that reporting would have been dangerous given the government’s existing actions against the property, they failed to establish that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them.

## Internal Relocation Feasibility and Reasonableness

Even if an applicant establishes past persecution and that the government cannot protect them in their current location, asylum may still be denied if the applicant can avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of their country. Under the regulations cited in the opinion, the government may rebut an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution by showing that the applicant could reasonably relocate. In *Jimenez*, the court addressed this argument briefly but definitively. The court found that the record supported the finding that the Petitioners could reasonably relocate to another part of Colombia.

The court relied on several factual findings to determine feasibility. First, the Petitioners’ family and relatives continued to live in Colombia unharmed, and Jimenez’s father still worked for the criminal justice system, suggesting that the threat was not nationwide. Second, the Petitioners had safely lived with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before deciding to leave for the United States, demonstrating that they could survive outside their immediate neighborhood. Finally, the court noted the specific scope of the threats received. The men who threatened the Petitioners told them to move out of the neighborhood, but they did not threaten the family to move out of the country. This distinction was critical; because the threat was geographically limited, the Petitioners could mitigate the potential for future persecution by moving to a different area within Colombia where the persecutors did not have reach.

## Rebuttal of Well-Founded Fear Through Relocation

The finding that internal relocation is feasible has a direct procedural impact on the asylum claim. As the court explained, the government may rebut an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution if it shows by a preponderance of the evidence that the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating. By determining that the Petitioners could safely and reasonably relocate within Colombia, the court concluded that they could not establish a well-founded fear of future persecution for the purposes of asylum. This determination was sufficient to deny the petition for review regarding asylum.

The court also addressed the implications of this finding for the Petitioners’ request for withholding of removal. Withholding of removal requires a higher standard of proof than asylum; an applicant must show that it is more likely than not that they would be persecuted upon return. Because the Petitioners failed to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution under the lower asylum standard—largely due to the availability of internal relocation—they necessarily failed to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal. The court cited *Carias-Mejia v. Garland* to support the proposition that failure on asylum grounds precludes withholding of removal. Consequently, the Tenth Circuit denied the petition for review, affirming the BIA’s decision that the Petitioners did not qualify for protection because they could not show the government was unable to protect them and because safe relocation within Colombia was a viable option.
