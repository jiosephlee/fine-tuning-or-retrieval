Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 2: # Legal Standards for Asylum and Withholding

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit reviews a final order of removal issued by the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA). The petitioners, Colombian nationals, sought asylum and withholding of removal, claiming they faced persecution in their home country. To understand the court’s analysis, a law student must first grasp the statutory framework governing these forms of relief. The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) establishes the eligibility requirements for asylum, which serves as the foundation for the court’s review. Asylum is a discretionary form of relief available to eligible noncitizens who can prove they qualify as refugees. The court in *Jimenez* relies on this statutory definition to evaluate whether the petitioners met their burden of proof. Furthermore, because the appellate court is reviewing an administrative decision, it applies a specific standard of review to the factual findings made by the immigration judge and the BIA. This chapter outlines these critical legal standards as applied in the opinion.

## INA Definition of a Refugee and Protected Grounds

The core of any asylum claim is the statutory definition of a refugee found in the INA. As cited by the Tenth Circuit in *Jimenez*, a refugee is defined as any person who is outside their country of nationality and is unable or unwilling to return to, or avail themselves of the protection of, that country. Crucially, this inability or unwillingness must be because of persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution. The statute requires that this persecution be on account of specific protected grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. In *Jimenez*, the petitioners argued that their political opinion was the basis for the harm they suffered, specifically their support for a rival political candidate in Colombia.

The court emphasizes that the protected ground must be the reason for the persecution, not merely incidental to it. The opinion notes that to show persecution on account of a political opinion, the applicant must establish that the political opinion was “at least one central reason” for the persecution. This means the political opinion cannot be tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for the harm. In mixed-motive cases where a persecutor may have multiple reasons for targeting an individual, the protected ground must still play a central role. If the harm was primarily due to a personal dispute or professional conflict, rather than the applicant’s political views, the nexus requirement is not met. The court in *Jimenez* applies this definition to determine if the threats and violence the petitioners faced were driven by their political activities or by other factors, such as a property dispute.

## Burden of Proof: Preponderance of the Evidence

In asylum proceedings, the burden of proof rests squarely on the applicant. The Tenth Circuit clarifies in *Jimenez* that an applicant bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that they are a refugee. This standard requires the applicant to show that it is more likely than not that they meet the statutory definition of a refugee. This burden applies to the various elements of the claim, including the occurrence of persecution, the nexus to a protected ground, and the government’s inability or unwillingness to protect.

The opinion highlights that this burden is significant. The court notes that the petitioners must demonstrate that they suffered persecution, that the persecution had a nexus to a statutorily protected ground, and that the Colombian government is unwilling or unable to help them. If the applicant fails to meet this burden on any essential element, the claim for asylum must fail. Additionally, the court notes that withholding of removal has a higher standard than asylum. Consequently, if petitioners fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution under the asylum standard, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard required for withholding of removal. This hierarchy of standards underscores the importance of the preponderance of the evidence threshold in establishing eligibility for discretionary relief.

## Distinction Between Past Persecution and Well-Founded Fear

A critical legal distinction in asylum law is between a showing of past persecution and a showing of a well-founded fear of future persecution. The opinion explains that an applicant may obtain refugee status through a showing of past persecution, which gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution. To establish past persecution, the applicant must prove three elements: an incident or incidents that rise to the level of persecution, that the persecution was on account of a statutorily protected ground, and that it was committed by the government or forces the government is unable or unwilling to control.

The court in *Jimenez* provides specific guidance on what constitutes persecution. Persecution is undefined in the INA, but Tenth Circuit precedent requires the infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ in a way regarded as offensive. This harm must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty. The court reviews incidents collectively, as the cumulative effects of multiple incidents may constitute persecution. However, the opinion makes clear that threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution. While the court considers threats in assessing the cumulative impact, the harm suffered must be offensive and more than mere sporadic threats. In *Jimenez*, the petitioners conceded they were not physically harmed, and the court found that their emotional and psychological distress, stemming from threats, was insufficient to rise to the level of persecution under the law. If past persecution is not established, the applicant must independently prove a well-founded fear of future persecution, which requires showing that the government is unable or unwilling to protect them from that future harm.

## Substantial Evidence Standard for Factual Findings

When the Tenth Circuit reviews the BIA’s decision, it does not conduct a de novo trial. Instead, the court reviews the BIA’s findings of fact under a substantial-evidence standard. This standard is deferential to the administrative agency. Under this standard, the BIA’s findings of fact are conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary. The court emphasizes that it is not its prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the BIA’s decision.

This standard of review limits the appellate court’s ability to overturn factual determinations made by the immigration judge and the BIA. The court notes that it reviews the administrative record on which the order of removal is based. While the BIA may affirm an IJ’s decision, the appellate court is not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of the grounds for denial. In *Jimenez*, the court applies this standard to the petitioners’ claims regarding the level of harm, the nexus to political opinion, and the availability of government protection. Because the record contained substantial evidence supporting the BIA’s findings that the harm did not amount to persecution and that the government was not unwilling to protect, the court denies the petition for review. This standard ensures that judicial review focuses on whether the agency’s decision was reasonable based on the record, rather than whether the appellate judges would have decided the facts differently.
