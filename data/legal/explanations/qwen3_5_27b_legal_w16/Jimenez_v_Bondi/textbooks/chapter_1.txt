Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 1: # Factual and Procedural Background

This chapter establishes the factual and procedural foundation for *Jimenez v. Bondi*, a decision by the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit. To understand the court’s legal analysis regarding asylum eligibility, a law student must first grasp the specific circumstances that led the Petitioners to flee Colombia, the administrative process they underwent in the United States, and the statutory basis for the appellate court’s jurisdiction. The following sections detail the Petitioners’ identities, the escalating threats they faced, the immigration court proceedings, and the jurisdictional framework governing this appeal.

## Petitioner Profile: Colombian Citizens and Liberal Party Members

The Petitioners in this case are Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons. They are natives and citizens of Colombia who originally resided in Bogota. Before relocating to the United States in December 2022, the family owned and operated a furniture factory in Bogota. A central component of their personal history relevant to their asylum claim is their political affiliation. Collectively, the family has been members of the Colombian Liberal Party since 2012. During the 2022 Colombian presidential election, they actively supported Federico Gutierrez, a candidate who ran against the eventual winner, President Gustavo Petro.

Jimenez’s professional background is also significant to the factual record. At the time of the relevant events in 2022, Jimenez was a law student holding a provisional law license. In addition to his work at the family factory, he worked as an intern for the National Prosecutor’s office in Colombia, assisting victims of domestic abuse, extortion, and other crimes. This background is crucial because it informs the Petitioners’ argument regarding the motives behind the threats they received. The combination of their political activity and Jimenez’s legal work creates the factual matrix the court must evaluate to determine if the harm they suffered was connected to a protected ground under immigration law.

## The Conflict: Property Dispute with 'Don Rafa' and Escalating Threats

The factual record outlines a timeline of escalating threats against the Petitioners, beginning in May 2022 and intensifying through September 2022. Initially, the threats appeared politically motivated. Beginning in late May 2022, Jimenez received threatening phone calls once or twice a week regarding his involvement with the Liberal Party. Other party leaders received similar threats, and in early June 2022, a colleague was kidnapped and murdered despite reporting threats. The Petitioners continued their campaign activities despite these dangers.

The nature of the threats shifted in June 2022 when Jimenez became involved in a property dispute on behalf of a client. The client sought help recovering a property occupied by her ex-husband, Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, known as "Don Rafa." Jimenez discovered that Don Rafa was a leader of narco-trafficking guerrillas politically aligned with President Petro and that the property was used for criminal activities including prostitution and kidnapping. During a visit to negotiate the property's return, Jimenez was warned by a local police officer to stay away. Following this intervention, the threats against the Petitioners escalated significantly.

In late June 2022, Jimenez received a threatening brochure at his factory stating, "death to snitches from Liberal Party." In July 2022, two men held Jimenez at gunpoint at the factory, accusing him of being a "snitch" for the prosecutor’s office and blaming him for police searches at Don Rafa’s property. Approximately two months later, the same men assaulted a factory employee and demanded to know the Petitioners' whereabouts, explicitly stating the order came from Don Rafa. The threats extended to the family’s children; Jimenez witnessed a man grab his younger son at school and tell him to "run to your dad." After reporting these incidents to a police patrol officer, who stated nothing could be done and advised the family to leave, the Petitioners moved to the United States. These facts are essential for the legal analysis because the court must distinguish whether the harm was inflicted due to political opinion or due to Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s criminal enterprises.

## Immigration Proceedings: IJ Denial and BIA Affirmation

Upon arriving in the United States without being admitted or paroled, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) served the Petitioners with individual Notices to Appear three months after their arrival. These notices charged each Petitioner as removable pursuant to the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). In August 2023, during immigration court proceedings, the Petitioners admitted the truth of the DHS allegations. The Immigration Judge (IJ) charged them as inadmissible and directed removal to Colombia. In response, the Petitioners applied for asylum and withholding of removal, with the minor children proceeding as derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s application.

The IJ conducted a merits hearing based on the testimonies of Jimenez and Duarte. While the IJ found the Petitioners to be credible, the judge denied their applications for relief. The IJ concluded that the Petitioners failed to establish that the harm they suffered amounted to past persecution, that the harm had a nexus to a statutorily protected ground, that internal relocation within Colombia was not reasonably available, or that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them. The IJ also denied protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT). The Petitioners appealed this decision to the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA). The BIA affirmed the IJ’s decision and dismissed the appeal, also rejecting the Petitioners' due process arguments regarding their right to counsel. This administrative history establishes the procedural posture of the case: the Petitioners are seeking judicial review of a final order of removal affirmed by the BIA.

## Appellate Jurisdiction Under 8 U.S.C. § 1252

The United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit exercised jurisdiction over this petition for review under 8 U.S.C. § 1252. This statute grants federal courts of appeals jurisdiction to review final orders of removal, which can include a consolidated review of asylum claims denied by the BIA. The court noted that its review is limited to the administrative record on which the order of removal is based. Because a single member of the BIA affirmed the IJ’s decision, the appellate court reviewed the BIA’s decision but was permitted to consult the IJ’s more complete explanation of the grounds for denial.

Understanding this jurisdictional framework is vital for interpreting the scope of the court’s opinion. The court reviews the BIA’s findings of fact under a "substantial-evidence standard." Under this standard, the BIA’s findings of fact are conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary. This deferential standard of review means the appellate court does not reweigh the evidence or substitute its own judgment for that of the immigration judges. Instead, the court examines whether the administrative record contains substantial evidence to support the IJ’s and BIA’s conclusions regarding the Petitioners' eligibility for asylum and withholding of removal. This procedural constraint shapes the court's analysis, requiring the Petitioners to show that the administrative findings were unsupported by the record rather than simply incorrect.
