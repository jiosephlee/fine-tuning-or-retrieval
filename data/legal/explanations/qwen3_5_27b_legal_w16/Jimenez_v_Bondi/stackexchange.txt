Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### What is the specific threshold for 'persecution' when only threats and psychological harm are alleged?
Question:
In Section II.C of *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the court states that 'threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution' and that even cumulative threats and psychological harm (like panic attacks or inability to sleep) rarely qualify. However, the opinion also notes that courts consider incidents 'collectively.' Given that the petitioners faced escalating threats, a kidnapping of a colleague, and a direct confrontation at their factory, at what point does the 'cumulative effect' cross the line from harassment to persecution? Is there a specific type of harm (e.g., physical detention vs. verbal threat) that acts as a hard threshold in the 10th Circuit?
Answer:
**There is no bright-line "hard threshold" requiring physical detention, but the 10th Circuit sets a high bar for threats and psychological harm alone.**

Based on *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 10th Cir. 2025, the court clarifies that while physical harm is not strictly necessary to establish persecution, allegations consisting solely of threats and psychological distress rarely meet the statutory standard. The opinion establishes that the threshold is qualitative rather than quantitative, focusing on whether the harm is "offensive" and "more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty."

**The Legal Standard for Persecution**
Under 10th Circuit precedent cited in *Jimenez*, persecution requires the "infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ... in a way regarded as offensive." The court emphasizes that this standard "must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty." While the court acknowledges that "physical harm is not necessary to satisfy the persecution prong," it explicitly notes that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution."

**The Cumulative Effect Doctrine**
You asked at what point the "cumulative effect" crosses the line. *Jimenez* confirms that courts "do not look at each incident in isolation, but instead consider them collectively." However, the opinion cautions that even when incidents are aggregated, the nature of the harm matters significantly. The court states it has "rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone."

**Illustrative Precedents from the Opinion**
To illustrate the high threshold, *Jimenez* references several 10th Circuit cases where harm failed to qualify as persecution despite being severe:
*   In *Brandy*, a 10-hour arrest and detention combined with threats was deemed insufficient.
*   In *Chhetri*, four telephone threats and one vague in-person threat did not constitute persecution.
*   In *Carias-Mejia*, threats with machetes and being robbed six times were insufficient because the threats were contingent on compliance.

**Application to the Threshold Question**
Consequently, there is no specific type of harm, such as physical detention, that acts as an absolute "hard threshold" where anything less is automatically excluded. However, in practice, the absence of physical harm places the burden on the petitioner to show that the threats and psychological impact were severe enough to be "offensive" in a legal sense. In *Jimenez*, the petitioners conceded they were not physically harmed and experienced no lasting physical harm; the court found their psychological harm (panic attacks, inability to sleep) and the escalating threats (including a colleague's kidnapping and a confrontation at their factory) did not rise to the level of persecution.

**Conclusion**
The threshold is not defined by a specific act like detention, but by the severity of the suffering. As *Jimenez* holds, the harm must be more than "mere restrictions or sporadic threats." While the court considers incidents collectively, the 10th Circuit maintains that without physical harm, the cumulative effect of threats and psychological distress must be exceptionally severe to cross the line from harassment to persecution.

### How do courts apply the 'central reason' test in mixed-motive asylum cases?
Question:
Section II.D discusses the nexus requirement where the persecutor may have multiple motives. The court found the petitioners' political opinion was 'tangential and subordinate' to the property dispute with the narcotrafficker. In mixed-motive cases, how does an adjudicator distinguish between a protected ground being a 'central reason' versus merely being present? If the persecutor acknowledges both political affiliation and a property dispute as reasons for harm, what evidence tips the scale toward the protected ground being the primary driver?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit clarifies that while a persecutor may have multiple motives, an asylum applicant must demonstrate that the protected ground (e.g., political opinion) was **"at least one central reason"** for the persecution, rather than merely incidental or subordinate.

Based on the court's analysis in Section II.D of the opinion, here is how adjudicators distinguish between a protected ground being "central" versus "present," and what evidence tips the scale.

### The Legal Standard: "At Least One Central Reason"
Under the standard applied in *Jimenez*, a protected ground cannot play a "minor role" in the persecutor’s motivation. The court cites 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i) and precedent (*Rivera-Barrientos*, *Dallakoti*) to establish that the nexus is not satisfied if the political opinion is **"incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm."**

Crucially, the court acknowledges that mixed-motive cases are permissible: "a persecutor can have multiple motives for targeting someone." However, the protected ground must remain a **central driver** of the inflicted harm.

### Distinguishing "Central" from "Subordinate"
In *Jimenez*, the court found the petitioners' political opinion was "tangential and subordinate" to a property dispute. To make this distinction, the adjudicator must look at the **predominant motive** behind the threats.

The court identified three key evidentiary factors that tipped the scale against the petitioners, effectively showing the property dispute was the central reason rather than the political opinion:

1.  **Timing and Escalation:** The petitioners had engaged in political activity and received general threats *before* the severe persecution began, yet they were "undeterred." The threats only escalated significantly **after** Jimenez became involved in a property dispute with the persecutor, Don Rafa. This temporal link suggested the property dispute, not the politics, triggered the heightened danger.
2.  **Specific Content of Threats:** The persecutors explicitly linked the harm to Jimenez's professional role regarding the property, not his political affiliation. The men accused Jimenez of being a **"snitch for the prosecutor’s office"** and being responsible for **"searches and seizures at the property."** While they noted the petitioners were "in politics," the primary accusation focused on the interference with Don Rafa’s revenue and security.
3.  **Causation and Awareness:** The record showed Don Rafa became aware of Jimenez specifically through the property investigation. The court noted that petitioners "do not point to any evidence in the record that suggests Don Rafa was behind those initial politically affiliated threats." The nexus was severed because the specific persecutor (Don Rafa) targeted them for the legal intervention regarding the property, not the political campaign.

### Practical Takeaway for Mixed-Motive Cases
To satisfy the "central reason" test in a mixed-motive scenario, an adjudicator must look for evidence that the protected ground is not merely a background characteristic but a primary catalyst for the harm.

In *Jimenez*, the scale tipped toward the non-protected ground (property dispute) because the **specific accusations** made by the persecutors focused on the legal/property interference, and the **severity of the harm** coincided with that dispute rather than the political activity. If the persecutor had explicitly stated the political opinion was the reason for the property dispute, or if the threats had remained consistent regardless of the property investigation, the protected ground might have been found central. As it stood, the political opinion was present, but legally subordinate to the economic and security threat posed by the property dispute.

### Is reporting to police a mandatory prerequisite for proving government unwillingness to protect?
Question:
In Section II.E, the court emphasizes that 'a key fact to the analysis is whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government.' The petitioners spoke to an officer who told them to leave, but the court seemed to weigh heavily against them because they didn't report more formally or through Jimenez's law enforcement contacts. Does this create a strict duty to report? If an applicant reasonably believes reporting would be futile or dangerous (as the petitioners argued regarding government ties to the narcos), does the burden shift entirely to the applicant to prove that futility, or is the failure to report itself dispositive?
Answer:
**No, reporting to the police is not a strict mandatory prerequisite, but failure to report is a significant evidentiary hurdle that the applicant must overcome by proving futility or danger.**

Based on *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit clarifies the role of police reporting in the "unable or unwilling" government protection analysis. While reporting is a "key fact," the court explicitly states that a failure to report is **"not necessarily fatal"** to an asylum claim. However, the burden remains on the applicant to justify why they did not seek protection.

### The Legal Standard: Notice and Response
The court explains that the government protection analysis hinges on whether the government had **"notice of the alleged persecution and an opportunity to respond."** Without notice (typically via a report), adjudicators cannot determine how the police would have reacted. Consequently, the failure to report can **"undercut a claim that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors."**

### The Futility or Danger Exception
If an applicant did not report, they may still satisfy the government protection requirement by demonstrating that **"filing a police report would have been futile or dangerous."**

In *Jimenez*, the petitioners argued that reporting was futile because the persecutors were closely aligned with the Colombian government. However, the court found this argument unsupported by the record. The opinion notes that the government had actually attempted **"several searches and seizures"** at the property in question. This evidence countered the petitioners' claim that the government was unwilling or unable to act, rendering their failure to report dispositive in *this specific case* because they could not prove the futility they alleged.

### Practical Implications for Applicants
The opinion highlights two practical takeaways for the burden of proof:

1.  **Individual vs. Systemic Failure:** Speaking to an individual officer who offers no help is insufficient to prove government unwillingness. Citing *Osorio-Morales v. Garland*, the court notes that **"a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is 'unable or unwilling' to protect a victim."** Applicants must show systemic failure, not just individual apathy.
2.  **Utilizing Available Resources:** The court weighed against the petitioners because they did not seek help through Jimenez’s law enforcement contacts or outside their immediate neighborhood, despite his professional ties. The opinion emphasizes that **"Petitioners' efforts to reach out to the government for help were minimal, if any."**

### Conclusion
To answer your specific questions:
*   **Strict Duty?** No, there is no strict duty, but reporting is heavily weighted.
*   **Burden Shift?** The burden does not shift; the applicant always bears the burden of proving the government is unable or unwilling. If they did not report, they must affirmatively prove that reporting would have been futile or dangerous.
*   **Dispositive?** Failure to report is not automatically dispositive, but if the applicant cannot substantiate the futility of reporting (as the petitioners failed to do regarding the government's actual search efforts), the claim will likely fail.

### How does short-term safety with family impact the 'reasonable internal relocation' analysis?
Question:
Section II.F addresses internal relocation. The court noted the petitioners lived safely with Jimenez's mother in the countryside for three weeks before leaving. The petitioners argued that family presence doesn't guarantee safety. How much weight does temporary safety carry in the relocation analysis? If a persecutor threatens to kill a family if they are seen in a specific neighborhood, does the fact that they survived three weeks in a different area automatically make relocation 'reasonable,' or should the court consider the potential for the persecutors to track them down eventually?
Answer:
**Short-term safety is a significant evidentiary factor in the internal relocation analysis, but it is not automatically dispositive.** In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit treated the petitioners’ three-week stay with family as part of a broader factual record demonstrating that relocation was feasible, particularly because the threats were location-specific rather than country-wide.

### The Legal Standard
Under 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i), the government may rebut an applicant’s presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution by showing the applicant could avoid persecution by relocating to another part of their country. As the court notes in *Jimenez*, the inquiry focuses on whether the applicant could "reasonably relocate to another part of the applicant’s country."

### How the Court Applied the Standard
In **Section II.F**, the court addressed the petitioners' argument that internal relocation was not reasonable. The court identified three key factors supporting the BIA’s finding that relocation was available:

1.  **Evidence of Feasibility:** The court highlighted that "Petitioners safely lived with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before deciding to relocate to the United States." This factual finding served as concrete evidence that the family could physically exist safely in a different location within Colombia.
2.  **Scope of the Threat:** Crucially, the court distinguished between threats to leave a neighborhood versus threats to leave the country. The opinion notes: "recall that the men who threatened Petitioners told them to move out of the neighborhood; they did not threaten the family to move out of the country." This distinction suggests the risk was tied to the specific location (Bogota/neighborhood) rather than a country-wide pursuit that would render any relocation futile.
3.  **Rejection of Hypothetical Hardships:** Regarding your question about whether family presence guarantees safety, the petitioners argued that relatives might also be at risk due to political opinions. The court rejected this, stating: "But we cannot grant asylum based on such hypothetical hardships." The court required evidence of actual inability to relocate, not speculative risks regarding family safety.

### Conclusion
Based on *Jimenez*, surviving three weeks in a different area does not **automatically** make relocation reasonable in every case, but it is strong evidence of feasibility when combined with the nature of the threat.

If the threats are specific to a neighborhood (as in *Jimenez*), short-term safety elsewhere supports a finding that the risk is geographically limited. However, if the persecutors have the intent and ability to track the applicant anywhere in the country, the "three-week safety" factor would likely carry less weight. In *Jimenez*, the court relied on the specific instruction to "move out of the neighborhood" to conclude that relocation could mitigate the persecution, rather than relying solely on the duration of the stay.

### What is the scope of review when the BIA affirms an IJ decision without a separate opinion?
Question:
In Section II.A, the court states that because a single BIA member affirmed the IJ's decision, they review the BIA's decision but are 'not precluded from consulting the IJ's more complete explanation.' Procedurally, how does the appellate court weigh the BIA's brief affirmation against the IJ's detailed findings? If the BIA affirms without explanation, does the IJ's reasoning become the primary basis for the substantial evidence review, or does the lack of BIA reasoning limit the scope of what can be challenged?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** When the BIA affirms an IJ decision without a separate opinion, the appellate court reviews the BIA's decision but is permitted to consult the IJ's opinion for the detailed factual reasoning. The lack of a separate BIA opinion does not limit the scope of review; rather, the IJ's findings are treated as the explanation for the BIA's affirmation and are subject to the same substantial evidence standard.

### The Standard of Review

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit explicitly addresses this procedural posture in **Section II.A (Standard of Review)**. The court states:

> "Because a single member of the BIA affirmed the IJ’s decision, we review the BIA’s decision, but 'we are not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds.'"

This establishes two key principles for your question:
1.  **The Final Order is the BIA's:** Technically, the court is reviewing the Board's decision, not the IJ's.
2.  **The IJ's Reasoning is Consulted:** The court is not restricted to the BIA's bare affirmation. It looks to the IJ's opinion to understand the "grounds" for the decision.

### How the Court Weighs the Findings

Regarding your question on how the court weighs the BIA's brief affirmation against the IJ's detailed findings, *Jimenez* demonstrates that the court treats the IJ's reasoning as the substantive basis for the BIA's affirmation.

Throughout the discussion, the court does not isolate the BIA's decision from the IJ's findings. Instead, it applies the **substantial evidence standard** to the record supporting both. For example:

*   **In Section II.D (Nexus):** The court writes, "there is substantial evidence supporting the **BIA and IJ’s findings** that Petitioners were primarily targeted because Jimenez threatened Don Rafa’s revenue and security."
*   **In Section II.E (Government Protection):** The court notes, "Again, there is substantial evidence in the record supporting the **BIA’s and IJ’s findings**."

This indicates that when the BIA affirms without explanation, the IJ's detailed findings effectively become the agency's reasoned decision for appellate review purposes. The court does not view the lack of BIA reasoning as a limitation on what can be challenged; rather, it reviews the administrative record to ensure that the IJ's findings (which the BIA adopted) are supported by substantial evidence.

### Conclusion

To answer your specific procedural question: The IJ's reasoning becomes the primary basis for the factual review because the BIA has adopted it by affirmation. As *Jimenez* confirms, the appellate court is "not precluded from consulting the IJ's more complete explanation." Therefore, the scope of review is not limited by the BIA's silence; the court reviews the IJ's detailed findings under the substantial evidence standard as if they were the BIA's own.

### Why does failing the asylum standard automatically result in failing the withholding of removal standard?
Question:
In the Conclusion, the court states: 'because Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal.' I understand withholding has a higher burden of proof ('clear probability' vs. 'well-founded fear'), but does this mean the factual findings for asylum are always dispositive for withholding? Are there scenarios where an applicant might fail the discretionary asylum claim but still succeed on the mandatory withholding claim based on the same facts?
Answer:
**Yes, under the logic applied in *Jimenez*, failing the evidentiary standard for asylum precludes meeting the standard for withholding of removal based on the same facts.**

The Tenth Circuit's reasoning in *Jimenez v. Bondi* rests on the hierarchical relationship between the burdens of proof for the two forms of relief.

### The Legal Standards
As outlined in **Section II.B**, asylum is a discretionary form of relief where the applicant must prove they are a refugee by a preponderance of the evidence. A key component of this is demonstrating a **"well-founded fear of persecution."**

While the opinion does not explicitly define the withholding standard in the body of the text, the **Conclusion** explicitly characterizes withholding of removal as having a **"higher standard"** than asylum.

### The Logical Necessity
The court treats the factual findings regarding the fear of persecution as the baseline for both claims. In the **Conclusion**, the court states:

> "because Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal."

This phrasing indicates a logical dependency: if the evidence is insufficient to satisfy the lower threshold (asylum's "well-founded fear"), it is inherently insufficient to satisfy the higher threshold (withholding). The court cites *Carias-Mejia* and *Uanreroro* to support this proposition that failing the lower standard necessitates failing the higher one.

### Application to Your Question
You asked if there are scenarios where an applicant might fail asylum but succeed on withholding based on the same facts. Based strictly on the reasoning in *Jimenez*, the answer is **no regarding the merits of the persecution claim**.

The court found that the Petitioners failed to establish the core elements required for asylum: they did not demonstrate past persecution, a nexus to a protected ground, or that the government was unable or unwilling to protect them. Because the record did not support the "well-founded fear" required for asylum, the court concluded it could not support the "higher standard" required for withholding. The opinion treats the inability to meet the asylum burden of proof as dispositive for the withholding claim when the factual basis is identical.

In short, *Jimenez* confirms that the "well-founded fear" standard acts as a floor; if an applicant cannot clear that floor, they cannot reach the higher ceiling required for withholding of removal.
