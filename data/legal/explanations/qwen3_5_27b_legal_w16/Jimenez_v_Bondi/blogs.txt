Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Mixed Motives and the 'Central Reason' Standard: Jimenez v. Bondi and the Nexus Requirement

The intersection of political dissent and criminal enterprise often creates a murky landscape for asylum seekers, particularly when a persecutor has multiple motivations for inflicting harm. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals addressed this complexity, reinforcing a rigorous application of the "at least one central reason" nexus standard in mixed-motive asylum claims. The decision matters because it clarifies the threshold for establishing that a protected ground—such as political opinion—is not merely incidental to the harm suffered. For practitioners and scholars, the opinion serves as a stark reminder that even in countries with significant political violence, applicants must demonstrate that their political identity was a primary driver of the persecution, rather than a secondary factor obscured by personal disputes or criminal activity.

The case involves a Colombian family who fled to the United States after facing threats from a narco-trafficker known as "Don Rafa." The petitioners were active members of the Colombian Liberal Party and had campaigned for a presidential candidate opposed to the current administration. Initially, they received threatening phone calls linked to their political activities. However, the violence escalated significantly after the petitioner, Jimenez, acted as a provisional lawyer to assist a client in a property dispute involving Don Rafa’s rental property. The trafficker’s associates subsequently threatened the family at their furniture factory and near their children’s school, accusing Jimenez of being a "snitch" for the prosecutor’s office and threatening their lives. While the petitioners argued these events constituted persecution on account of their political opinion, the Tenth Circuit denied their petition for review, finding substantial evidence supported the Board of Immigration Appeals’ conclusion that the political opinion was tangential to the primary motive.

At the heart of the court’s reasoning is the statutory requirement that persecution be on account of a protected ground. The court applied the standard that the applicant’s political opinion must be "at least one central reason" for the persecution, citing 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i). The court acknowledged that a persecutor can have multiple motives, referencing recent Tenth Circuit precedent such as *O.C.V. v. Bondi*. However, the court emphasized that the protected ground cannot play a "minor role" or be "incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate" to another reason for harm. In distinguishing the timeline of events, the court noted that the petitioners had continued their political activities undeterred by earlier threats. It was only after Jimenez intervened in the property dispute that the threats became physical and lethal. The court found that the persecutors explicitly linked the violence to Jimenez’s professional investigation and his perceived role in police searches, rather than his Liberal Party affiliation. Consequently, the political opinion was deemed subordinate to the motive of silencing a threat to the trafficker’s revenue and security.

Beyond the nexus analysis, the court’s treatment of the government protection and internal relocation prongs further illustrates the high bar for asylum eligibility in the Tenth Circuit. The petitioners argued that the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect them, suggesting the trafficker was aligned with the government. The court rejected this, pointing to evidence that law enforcement had attempted several searches and seizures at the trafficker’s property. This factual finding undermined the claim that the government was complicit or incapable of acting. Furthermore, regarding internal relocation, the court observed that the threats specifically instructed the family to leave the neighborhood, not the country. Given that the petitioners had relatives living safely in Colombia and had previously stayed with family in the countryside, the court found relocation to be a reasonable alternative to fleeing the country. These findings collectively support the conclusion that the harm did not meet the statutory definition of persecution warranting asylum.

The doctrinal implications of *Jimenez* are significant for mixed-motive cases where political activity intersects with criminal disputes. The decision signals that courts will scrutinize the specific trigger of the harm to determine the persecutor’s primary intent. If the escalation of violence correlates more closely with a personal or professional conflict than with the expression of political opinion, the nexus requirement may fail even if the applicant holds a protected political view. This approach prevents the asylum system from becoming a remedy for ordinary criminal victimization, even in jurisdictions where crime is politically charged. For future petitioners, the opinion underscores the necessity of providing concrete evidence linking the persecutor’s motive directly to the protected ground, rather than relying on the general context of political instability.

Ultimately, *Jimenez v. Bondi* reaffirms the substantial evidence standard in reviewing asylum denials, particularly where the record contains competing explanations for the harm. While the court acknowledged the credibility of the petitioners and the severity of the threats they faced, it held that the legal requirements for refugee status were not met. The ruling serves as a cautionary precedent for cases involving narco-political violence, where the line between political persecution and criminal retaliation is often blurred. By insisting that the protected ground be a central reason for the harm, the Tenth Circuit maintains a strict boundary around the definition of a refugee, ensuring that the nexus requirement remains a meaningful gatekeeper in the asylum process.



# Beyond Threats: The 10th Circuit's Rigid Definition of Persecution in Jimenez

## The High Bar for Asylum in the Tenth Circuit

In immigration law, the definition of "persecution" often serves as the gatekeeper for asylum relief. While the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) does not explicitly define the term, circuit courts have developed distinct standards for what constitutes sufficient harm to warrant protection. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, decided October 7, 2025, the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit reaffirmed its stringent approach to this threshold. The panel denied review of a Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) decision rejecting the asylum claims of a Colombian family, holding that a combination of death threats, psychological distress, and economic interference did not rise to the level of past persecution. For practitioners and asylum seekers in the Tenth Circuit, *Jimenez* serves as a stark reminder that threats and emotional trauma, absent physical injury, face an exceptionally high bar to qualify as persecution.

## Factual Background and the Nature of the Harm

The petitioners, a Colombian family residing in the United States, sought asylum based on their involvement with the Colombian Liberal Party and a subsequent conflict with a local narco-trafficking leader known as "Don Rafa." The record indicates that the father, Jimenez, received threatening phone calls, a threatening brochure, and two in-person armed confrontations at his factory. While the threats were severe—including accusations that Jimenez was a "snitch" and explicit warnings to leave the neighborhood—the petitioners conceded at the merits hearing that they suffered no physical harm.

The family did, however, allege significant psychological tolls. The mother testified to insomnia, and their son reportedly suffered panic attacks and stress. Despite these claims, the Tenth Circuit found substantial evidence supporting the BIA’s conclusion that these incidents, even viewed cumulatively, did not constitute persecution. The court emphasized that while physical harm is not strictly necessary to satisfy the persecution prong, the harm inflicted must be "offensive and more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats." By citing precedents such as *Karki v. Holder* and *Chhetri v. Rosen*, the court signaled that the Tenth Circuit rarely, if ever, grants asylum based solely on threats and emotional or psychological harm, even when those threats involve weapons or the potential for death.

## The Cumulative Effects Doctrine and Its Limits

A central tension in asylum jurisprudence is the "cumulative effects" doctrine, which allows adjudicators to consider multiple incidents of mistreatment collectively rather than in isolation. The Tenth Circuit acknowledged this doctrine in *Jimenez*, noting that "the cumulative effects of multiple incidents may constitute persecution." However, the application of this doctrine in the decision highlights its limitations within the circuit.

The court reasoned that while the petitioners endured "unfortunate circumstances," the aggregate of their experiences—threats, loss of sleep, and anxiety—did not cross the threshold into persecution. The opinion distinguishes between being "physically stressful or psychologically unsettling" and meeting the statutory definition of persecution. This distinction is critical for legal strategy. It suggests that in the Tenth Circuit, the cumulative effects doctrine functions more as a tool to weigh the severity of non-physical incidents rather than a mechanism to elevate psychological distress to the level of persecution without some accompanying physical or severe liberty-restricting harm. The court pointed to *Brandy v. Holder*, where even a 10-hour arrest and detention combined with threats was insufficient, to illustrate the high magnitude of harm required.

## Doctrinal Implications for Future Litigation

The *Jimenez* decision has significant downstream effects for asylum litigation in the Tenth Circuit. First, it reinforces the "threats alone" rule established in *Karki*. Attorneys arguing asylum cases involving non-lethal threats must be prepared to demonstrate that the threats were accompanied by some form of tangible harm or restriction on liberty that goes beyond the fear of future violence. Second, the opinion underscores the importance of the nexus requirement, even when persecution is established. While the court primarily focused on the persecution element, it noted that the political opinion was "tangential and subordinate" to the property dispute motive. This mixed-motive analysis requires petitioners to prove that their political opinion was "at least one central reason" for the harm, a standard that can be difficult to meet when criminal actors are involved.

Furthermore, *Jimenez* highlights the evidentiary burden regarding government protection. The court noted that the petitioners' efforts to seek help were minimal and relied on anecdotal conversations with unhelpful officers rather than systemic evidence of government unwillingness. The fact that law enforcement had previously attempted searches of the trafficker's property weighed against the claim that the government was unable to control the persecutors. This suggests that future applicants must provide concrete evidence of government inaction or complicity, rather than relying on the general reputation of corruption or isolated interactions with police.

## Conclusion

*Jimenez v. Bondi* does not break new ground doctrinally, but it solidifies the Tenth Circuit's position on the spectrum of asylum jurisprudence. By denying relief to a family facing credible death threats and significant psychological trauma without physical injury, the court maintains a rigid definition of persecution that prioritizes tangible harm over fear and distress. For asylum seekers in the circuit, the message is clear: unless the record demonstrates physical harm or severe restrictions on liberty, claims based on threats and psychological impact are likely to fall short of the statutory requirement. As the circuit continues to apply precedents like *Chhetri* and *Karki*, the path to asylum remains narrow for those fleeing non-physical but life-threatening environments.



# The Duty to Report and Internal Relocation: High Hurdles for Asylum Seekers in Jimenez

In the complex landscape of immigration law, the difference between safety and removal often hinges on evidentiary technicalities as much as factual realities. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, decided October 7, 2025, the United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit issued a decision that underscores the rigorous procedural burdens placed on asylum seekers. While the case involved a family fleeing Colombia after threats from narco-traffickers linked to the political establishment, the court denied relief primarily on the grounds that the petitioners failed to prove the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them and that internal relocation was a viable option. *Jimenez* serves as a stark reminder that even credible accounts of danger can fall short if the applicant cannot bridge the gap between private harm and state responsibility.

## The Burden of Proving State Complicity

The core of the *Jimenez* decision rests on the "unable or unwilling" prong of the asylum standard. To establish eligibility, an applicant must show that the government is either unable or unwilling to control the forces threatening them. The Tenth Circuit, relying on its recent precedent in *Singh v. Bondi*, emphasized that this analysis is fact-intensive and heavily dependent on whether the applicant sought government protection in the first place.

The court found that the petitioners' efforts to engage the state were insufficient to prove systemic failure. Although the petitioners testified that a police officer advised them to leave because "nothing could be done," the court held that a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily demonstrate that the entire government is unwilling to protect its citizens. The opinion distinguishes between individual apathy and systemic inability, citing *Osorio-Morales* to note that courts generally require evidence of systemic failure rather than isolated incidents of police inaction.

This reasoning places a significant evidentiary burden on applicants in countries with high levels of corruption or criminal infiltration. The court noted that the Colombian government had actually attempted several searches and seizures at the property in question, suggesting the state was not entirely aligned with the persecutors. Consequently, the petitioners' failure to file formal reports or utilize Jimenez’s contacts within the law enforcement system undercut their claim that seeking help would have been futile. The court acknowledged that an applicant might satisfy the requirement by showing that reporting would have been dangerous, but it found the record insufficient to support that conclusion. This creates a catch-22 for many asylum seekers: they must report crimes to prove the government is ineffective, yet in environments where reporting is dangerous, the failure to report can be used against them to prove the government *was* effective or that the danger was not state-sponsored.

## The Reasonableness of Internal Relocation

Even if the petitioners had established a well-founded fear of persecution, the Tenth Circuit held that they would still fail because internal relocation was reasonably available. The internal relocation doctrine allows the government to rebut an asylum claim by showing the applicant could avoid persecution by moving to another part of their home country. In *Jimenez*, the court looked closely at the geographic scope of the threats.

The persecutors had explicitly told the family to leave the neighborhood, not the country. The court weighed this instruction against the family's ability to survive elsewhere, noting that they had safely lived with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before fleeing to the United States. Furthermore, the court pointed to the continued safety of the petitioners' relatives and Jimenez’s father, who still works for the criminal justice system in Colombia. The court rejected the argument that the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety, stating it could not grant asylum based on hypothetical hardships regarding those relatives' potential political opinions.

This analysis highlights the court's preference for concrete evidence of danger over generalized fears of country conditions. By focusing on the specific nature of the threats and the family's prior ability to relocate within Colombia, the court narrowed the window for asylum based on internal relocation. It suggests that unless a persecutor has the capacity to track an applicant across the entire nation, or unless the country is so unstable that no safe zone exists, relocation remains a viable bar to relief.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The *Jimenez* opinion reinforces a trend in the Tenth Circuit toward a strict application of the "unable or unwilling" and internal relocation standards. The decision signals that courts will not easily accept generalized assertions of state complicity without specific evidence of reporting attempts or systemic corruption that directly impedes the applicant. For practitioners, this means the record must be meticulously developed to show not just that the police were unhelpful, but that the system itself was broken or that reporting was objectively futile.

The downstream effect of this reasoning may be to shift the burden of proof even further onto the applicant regarding the futility of reporting. While the law recognizes that reporting can be dangerous, *Jimenez* suggests that subjective fear of retaliation is not enough without corroborating evidence that the state is aligned with the persecutor. In nations where corruption is endemic but not total, this distinction may become a primary battleground in appellate litigation. Ultimately, *Jimenez* illustrates that in asylum law, the path to protection is as much about documenting the failure of the state as it is about documenting the violence of the persecutor.
