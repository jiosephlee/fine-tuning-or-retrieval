Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Is reporting to police a mandatory prerequisite for proving government unwillingness to protect?
Question:
In Section II.E, the court emphasizes that 'a key fact to the analysis is whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government.' The petitioners spoke to an officer who told them to leave, but the court seemed to weigh heavily against them because they didn't report more formally or through Jimenez's law enforcement contacts. Does this create a strict duty to report? If an applicant reasonably believes reporting would be futile or dangerous (as the petitioners argued regarding government ties to the narcos), does the burden shift entirely to the applicant to prove that futility, or is the failure to report itself dispositive?
Answer:
**No, reporting to the police is not a strict mandatory prerequisite, but failure to report is a significant evidentiary hurdle that the applicant must overcome by proving futility or danger.**

Based on *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit clarifies the role of police reporting in the "unable or unwilling" government protection analysis. While reporting is a "key fact," the court explicitly states that a failure to report is **"not necessarily fatal"** to an asylum claim. However, the burden remains on the applicant to justify why they did not seek protection.

### The Legal Standard: Notice and Response
The court explains that the government protection analysis hinges on whether the government had **"notice of the alleged persecution and an opportunity to respond."** Without notice (typically via a report), adjudicators cannot determine how the police would have reacted. Consequently, the failure to report can **"undercut a claim that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors."**

### The Futility or Danger Exception
If an applicant did not report, they may still satisfy the government protection requirement by demonstrating that **"filing a police report would have been futile or dangerous."**

In *Jimenez*, the petitioners argued that reporting was futile because the persecutors were closely aligned with the Colombian government. However, the court found this argument unsupported by the record. The opinion notes that the government had actually attempted **"several searches and seizures"** at the property in question. This evidence countered the petitioners' claim that the government was unwilling or unable to act, rendering their failure to report dispositive in *this specific case* because they could not prove the futility they alleged.

### Practical Implications for Applicants
The opinion highlights two practical takeaways for the burden of proof:

1.  **Individual vs. Systemic Failure:** Speaking to an individual officer who offers no help is insufficient to prove government unwillingness. Citing *Osorio-Morales v. Garland*, the court notes that **"a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is 'unable or unwilling' to protect a victim."** Applicants must show systemic failure, not just individual apathy.
2.  **Utilizing Available Resources:** The court weighed against the petitioners because they did not seek help through Jimenez’s law enforcement contacts or outside their immediate neighborhood, despite his professional ties. The opinion emphasizes that **"Petitioners' efforts to reach out to the government for help were minimal, if any."**

### Conclusion
To answer your specific questions:
*   **Strict Duty?** No, there is no strict duty, but reporting is heavily weighted.
*   **Burden Shift?** The burden does not shift; the applicant always bears the burden of proving the government is unable or unwilling. If they did not report, they must affirmatively prove that reporting would have been futile or dangerous.
*   **Dispositive?** Failure to report is not automatically dispositive, but if the applicant cannot substantiate the futility of reporting (as the petitioners failed to do regarding the government's actual search efforts), the claim will likely fail.
