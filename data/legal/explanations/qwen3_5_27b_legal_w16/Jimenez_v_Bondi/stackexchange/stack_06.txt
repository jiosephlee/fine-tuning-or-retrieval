Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Why does failing the asylum standard automatically result in failing the withholding of removal standard?
Question:
In the Conclusion, the court states: 'because Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal.' I understand withholding has a higher burden of proof ('clear probability' vs. 'well-founded fear'), but does this mean the factual findings for asylum are always dispositive for withholding? Are there scenarios where an applicant might fail the discretionary asylum claim but still succeed on the mandatory withholding claim based on the same facts?
Answer:
**Yes, under the logic applied in *Jimenez*, failing the evidentiary standard for asylum precludes meeting the standard for withholding of removal based on the same facts.**

The Tenth Circuit's reasoning in *Jimenez v. Bondi* rests on the hierarchical relationship between the burdens of proof for the two forms of relief.

### The Legal Standards
As outlined in **Section II.B**, asylum is a discretionary form of relief where the applicant must prove they are a refugee by a preponderance of the evidence. A key component of this is demonstrating a **"well-founded fear of persecution."**

While the opinion does not explicitly define the withholding standard in the body of the text, the **Conclusion** explicitly characterizes withholding of removal as having a **"higher standard"** than asylum.

### The Logical Necessity
The court treats the factual findings regarding the fear of persecution as the baseline for both claims. In the **Conclusion**, the court states:

> "because Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal."

This phrasing indicates a logical dependency: if the evidence is insufficient to satisfy the lower threshold (asylum's "well-founded fear"), it is inherently insufficient to satisfy the higher threshold (withholding). The court cites *Carias-Mejia* and *Uanreroro* to support this proposition that failing the lower standard necessitates failing the higher one.

### Application to Your Question
You asked if there are scenarios where an applicant might fail asylum but succeed on withholding based on the same facts. Based strictly on the reasoning in *Jimenez*, the answer is **no regarding the merits of the persecution claim**.

The court found that the Petitioners failed to establish the core elements required for asylum: they did not demonstrate past persecution, a nexus to a protected ground, or that the government was unable or unwilling to protect them. Because the record did not support the "well-founded fear" required for asylum, the court concluded it could not support the "higher standard" required for withholding. The opinion treats the inability to meet the asylum burden of proof as dispositive for the withholding claim when the factual basis is identical.

In short, *Jimenez* confirms that the "well-founded fear" standard acts as a floor; if an applicant cannot clear that floor, they cannot reach the higher ceiling required for withholding of removal.
