Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Why is a statutory right to an investigation insufficient for Article III standing under Summers?
Question:
I'm reading *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* and the court cites *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.* to say a 'procedural right in vacuo' isn't enough for standing. AFL argued OSC violated its procedural right to an investigation under 5 U.S.C. § 1216. I thought if a statute says an agency 'shall' investigate, that creates a legally protected interest. Why does the court require an additional 'concrete interest' beyond the procedural right itself, and what qualifies as that concrete interest in this context?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** Under *Summers*, a statutory mandate that an agency "shall" investigate creates a procedural right, but that right alone does not constitute an Article III injury. To establish standing, the plaintiff must show that the violation of that procedure deprived them of a separate, **concrete interest** (such as specific information or a tangible benefit) that the procedure was designed to protect. In *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer*, the court held that AFL failed because it could not identify a concrete interest that was actually harmed by OSC's refusal to investigate.

### The *Summers* Doctrine: Procedural Right *In Vacuo*

As the court explains in Section II, Article III standing requires an "invasion of a legally protected interest" that is concrete and particularized. While statutes often grant procedural rights (e.g., a right to notice or a right to an investigation), *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.* establishes that the deprivation of such a right *in vacuo*—without more—is insufficient for standing.

The court quotes *Summers* directly:
> "the 'deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient' to confer Article III standing."

This means that even if a statute uses mandatory language like "shall conduct an investigation" (as 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) does), the plaintiff cannot sue solely on the basis that the agency failed to follow the procedure. They must demonstrate that the failure to follow the procedure caused a concrete injury to a specific interest they hold.

### Why the "Shall" Mandate Was Insufficient Here

You noted that 5 U.S.C. § 1216 states OSC "shall … conduct an investigation." While this creates a duty for OSC, it does not automatically create a concrete interest for AFL. The court analyzed two potential concrete interests AFL claimed were affected by OSC's inaction, and rejected both:

**1. Interest in FOIA Records (Causation/Redressability Failure)**
AFL argued its concrete interest was receiving the records it requested from DOJ under FOIA. However, the court found the causal link between OSC’s investigation and the release of records too attenuated.
*   **The Chain:** OSC investigates → OSC seeks corrective/disciplinary action → MSPB sanctions employees → DOJ produces records.
*   **The Problem:** This relies on speculation about the choices of independent actors (MSPB and DOJ) not before the court. As the opinion notes, "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors." Therefore, the procedural violation (no investigation) did not concretely cause the injury (no records).

**2. Interest in Investigation Notices (No Entitlement)**
AFL argued its concrete interest was the informational notices OSC would provide during an investigation under 5 U.S.C. § 1214.
*   **The Problem:** The court found that § 1216 does not entitle AFL to receive information. While § 1216 investigations *may* follow § 1214 procedures, OSC has discretion whether to do so.
*   **The Holding:** "Absent any alleged entitlement to receive information under section 1214, AFL lacks a concrete injury sufficient to support its second standing theory." Because AFL was not legally entitled to the notices, the failure to provide them was not an injury in fact.

### What Qualifies as a "Concrete Interest"?

Based on *Greer*, a "concrete interest" in the context of a procedural violation must be:
1.  **Legally Protected:** The plaintiff must have a statutory entitlement to the specific outcome or information, not just a hope that the procedure might yield it.
2.  **Directly Affected:** The violation of the procedure must directly cause the loss of that interest, without relying on speculative actions by third parties.

In this case, because the "shall investigate" mandate did not guarantee AFL would receive specific records or notices, the procedural right existed without a corresponding concrete interest. Consequently, AFL lacked standing to enforce the statute.

### How does the 'independent actor' doctrine affect causation in standing analysis here?
Question:
In the standing analysis, the court rejects AFL's argument that OSC's failure to investigate caused the denial of FOIA records. The court lists a chain: OSC investigates -> MSPB sanctions -> DOJ produces records. I'm confused about why this chain is 'too speculative.' If OSC is mandated to investigate, isn't it reasonable to assume they would find a violation and the MSPB would act? How does the 'independent actor' doctrine (citing *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*) specifically break the causal link between OSC's inaction and the injury?
Answer:
The "independent actor" doctrine breaks the causal chain in this case because the relief AFL seeks depends on the discretionary choices of government agencies that are **not parties to the lawsuit**.

According to the opinion, Article III standing requires that the plaintiff’s injury be fairly traceable to the defendant’s conduct. While AFL argues that OSC’s failure to investigate caused the denial of FOIA records, the Court rejects this because the causal chain relies on speculation about the actions of the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) and the Department of Justice (DOJ).

### 1. The Independent Actor Doctrine
The Court cites *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* for the principle that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts."

In this context, the "independent actors" are:
1.  **OSC:** Even if OSC investigates, the opinion notes that OSC has discretion over whether to pursue corrective or disciplinary action (*Heckler v. Chaney*).
2.  **MSPB:** An independent agency not named in the suit. Even if OSC refers a case, MSPB must decide whether to sanction employees.
3.  **DOJ:** The agency holding the records. Even if sanctions are threatened, DOJ must decide whether to produce the records.

### 2. Why the Chain is "Too Speculative"
You asked why it is not reasonable to assume OSC would find a violation and MSPB would act. The Court explains that standing cannot rest on a "might" or "could" regarding these third parties. The opinion outlines the speculative chain explicitly:

1.  **OSC Investigation:** If OSC investigates, it *might* decide to pursue corrective action.
2.  **MSPB Sanction:** Then, MSPB *might* decide to sanction the employees.
3.  **DOJ Production:** Next, DOJ *might* decide to produce the records.

The Court emphasizes that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." Because MSPB and DOJ are not defendants in this case, the Court cannot assume they would act in a specific way to redress AFL’s injury.

### 3. Redressability and the "Mandate"
Your confusion regarding the "mandate" to investigate is addressed by the distinction between the **investigation** and the **outcome**.

Even assuming OSC is mandated to investigate under § 1216, a judicial order compelling OSC to investigate does not guarantee the production of records. The opinion states that redressability "generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale 'might persuade actors who are not before the court.'"

Because a judgment against OSC binds only OSC, it cannot compel MSPB to sanction or DOJ to produce records. Therefore, a favorable judgment would not "very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress" the informational injury.

### Summary
The independent actor doctrine prevents AFL from establishing standing because the link between OSC’s inaction and the lack of records is severed by the discretionary decisions of non-parties (MSPB and DOJ). The Court cannot presume these independent actors would act in a way that provides the relief AFL seeks, rendering the causal connection too attenuated for Article III purposes.

### Does 5 U.S.C. § 1216 create an entitlement to information for informational standing purposes?
Question:
The court discusses 'informational standing' and says AFL lacks an entitlement to receive information under § 1216. AFL argued that if OSC investigates, they might provide notices required under § 1214. The court says § 1214 notice requirements are discretionary in § 1216 investigations. I'm struggling with the statutory interpretation here: § 1216(c) says OSC acts 'in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.' Doesn't that language import the mandatory notice provisions of § 1214, or is there a distinction between the *investigation* method and the *notice* obligations?
Answer:
No. Based on the opinion in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, 5 U.S.C. § 1216 does not create a statutory entitlement to information sufficient to support informational standing.

### Informational Standing Standard
To establish informational standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate that a statute "entitled [them] to receive" specific information. As the D.C. Circuit explains, the deprivation of a procedural right without a concrete interest affected by that deprivation is insufficient for Article III standing. *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009) (cited in opinion). Therefore, the threshold question is whether § 1216 grants AFL a right to receive information from the Office of Special Counsel (OSC).

### Statutory Interpretation of § 1216(c) vs. § 1214
Your confusion regarding § 1216(c)'s "in the same way" language is central to the court's analysis. While § 1216(c) states that OSC may investigate and seek corrective action "in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved," the court distinguishes between the *method* of enforcement and the *obligation* to provide notice.

1.  **Discretionary Notice:** The opinion notes that while § 1214 sets forth procedures for prohibited personnel practices (including notice requirements), OSC’s decision to apply § 1214’s procedural requirements to a § 1216 investigation is "discretionary." The court cites *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.* for the proposition that OSC is not required to "engraft section 1214 notice requirements onto its section 1216 investigations."
2.  **No Entitlement Created:** Because OSC is not statutorily required to provide § 1214 notices during a § 1216 investigation, § 1216 does not create an entitlement to receive that information. The court writes: "Absent any alleged entitlement to receive information under section 1214, AFL lacks a concrete injury sufficient to support its second standing theory."

### Conclusion
The "in the same way" language in § 1216(c) governs the mechanisms for seeking corrective or disciplinary action, not the mandatory notice obligations owed to the complainant. Since the notice provisions of § 1214 are discretionary in the context of a § 1216 investigation, there is no statutory right to information. Consequently, AFL cannot satisfy the injury-in-fact requirement for informational standing.

### How does Heckler v. Chaney influence the redressability prong of standing in this case?
Question:
The court mentions *Heckler v. Chaney* regarding agency enforcement discretion when discussing redressability. AFL wants the court to order OSC to investigate. Even if the court orders an investigation, OSC might still decide not to take disciplinary action. How does the presumption against judicial review of non-enforcement decisions affect the 'likely to be redressed' requirement? Does it mean a plaintiff can never get standing to compel an investigation unless the outcome is guaranteed?
Answer:
**Short Answer**
*Heckler v. Chaney* does not categorically bar standing to compel an investigation, but in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, it reinforces that **redressability fails when the remedy depends on speculative future exercises of agency discretion.** The court held that because OSC has discretion to decide whether to pursue enforcement after an investigation, a court order mandating the investigation is not "likely to redress" the plaintiff's injury.

**Detailed Explanation**

### 1. The Redressability Standard
To establish Article III standing, a plaintiff must show that a favorable judicial decision is "likely to be redressed" by the court's judgment. In this case, AFL argued that ordering OSC to investigate would lead to DOJ producing the requested FOIA records. The court rejected this because the causal chain was too attenuated.

### 2. How *Heckler v. Chaney* Influences the Analysis
The court cites *Heckler v. Chaney* to highlight the **speculative nature of agency enforcement discretion**.

*   **The Speculative Chain:** The court outlined the chain of events AFL relied upon:
    1.  Court orders OSC to investigate.
    2.  OSC *might* decide to pursue corrective or disciplinary action.
    3.  MSPB *might* sanction employees.
    4.  DOJ *might* then produce records.
*   **The *Chaney* Bar:** The opinion notes that step 2 requires "speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel."
*   **The Result:** Because *Chaney* establishes a presumption that courts will not compel agency enforcement decisions, the court cannot assume OSC will move from investigation to discipline. Therefore, the judgment would not "very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress" the injury.

### 3. Addressing the "Guarantee" Question
You asked if this means a plaintiff can **never** get standing unless the outcome is guaranteed. The opinion clarifies two important points:

*   **Likelihood, Not Guarantee:** Standing requires a judgment to be "likely to redress" the injury, not guaranteed. However, when the redress depends on discretionary acts by the agency (which *Chaney* protects from judicial compulsion), the likelihood drops below the constitutional threshold.
*   **Binding Effect:** The court emphasizes that redressability "generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale 'might persuade actors who are not before the court.'" If the court orders an investigation but cannot bind OSC to take disciplinary action, the judgment does not bind the outcome necessary to fix the injury.

**Conclusion**
*Heckler v. Chaney* influences the redressability prong by reminding the court that it cannot presume an agency will exercise its discretion to enforce the law after an investigation. Consequently, a plaintiff cannot establish standing if the only way to redress their injury is through a series of discretionary steps that the court cannot compel.

### Why did the D.C. Circuit vacate and remand for jurisdictional dismissal instead of affirming the merits dismissal?
Question:
The district court dismissed AFL's case for 'failure to state a claim' (a merits-based dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6)). However, the D.C. Circuit concluded AFL lacks Article III standing (a jurisdictional issue). The opinion states they 'vacate the district court's judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.' Why is this procedural distinction important? Why can't the appellate court just affirm the dismissal on alternative grounds?
Answer:
The D.C. Circuit vacated the judgment rather than affirming on the merits because **Article III standing is a jurisdictional prerequisite, not a merits issue.** Affirming a merits dismissal would imply the district court possessed the constitutional authority to hear the case, whereas the Circuit concluded it did not.

### The Legal Distinction: Jurisdiction vs. Merits
As the opinion explains in Section II, the appellate court must address standing "Before reaching the merits." The opinion cites *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998), for the proposition that standing is "a question bearing on whether the district court had subjectmatter jurisdiction."

This creates a hierarchy in federal litigation:
1.  **Jurisdiction:** The court must first determine if it has the Article III power to adjudicate the dispute.
2.  **Merits:** Only if jurisdiction exists may the court decide the substantive claims.

The district court dismissed AFL’s case under Rule 12(b)(6) for "failure to state a claim." This is a dismissal on the **merits**, which presupposes that the court had the authority to evaluate the claim. However, the D.C. Circuit found that AFL lacked Article III standing, meaning the district court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction entirely.

### Why Vacate and Remand?
If the D.C. Circuit had simply affirmed the dismissal on alternative grounds (i.e., standing), it would have left standing as a secondary rationale for a judgment that was fundamentally flawed. A judgment based on the merits is procedurally improper when the court lacks jurisdiction.

By **vacating** the judgment and **remanding with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction**, the appellate court ensures the record reflects the correct legal basis:
*   **Corrects the Basis:** It changes the dismissal from a merits-based ruling to a jurisdictional one.
*   **Preserves Article III Limits:** It acknowledges that the federal "judicial Power" is restricted to "Cases" and "Controversies." As the opinion states, "Article III of the Constitution restricts the federal 'judicial Power' to resolving certain 'Cases' and 'Controversies.'"

In short, the distinction is vital because a court cannot validly rule on the merits of a case it lacks the constitutional power to hear. The vacatur corrects the district court’s error in reaching the merits before establishing jurisdiction.

### Why did the court decline to resolve the conflict between 5 U.S.C. § 1216 and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)?
Question:
The district court ruled that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC's power under § 1216, but the D.C. Circuit says 'we need not decide' this issue because of the standing failure. I'm confused about the relationship between these two statutes. If the court determined there was no standing, does it mean the statutory conflict is irrelevant, or is this an example of the constitutional avoidance canon? Shouldn't the court clarify the statutory scheme for future cases even if this plaintiff lacks standing?
Answer:
# The Court Declined to Resolve the Statutory Conflict Because It Lacked Article III Jurisdiction

The D.C. Circuit declined to resolve the conflict between 5 U.S.C. § 1216 and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) because the plaintiff lacked **Article III standing**, which is a jurisdictional prerequisite that must be resolved before a court can reach the merits of a statutory dispute.

### 1. Jurisdiction Precedes Merits
As the opinion explains, federal courts are restricted by Article III to resolving actual "Cases" and "Controversies." Standing doctrine is derived from this constitutional limitation. The court explicitly prioritized this threshold inquiry over the statutory interpretation question:

> "Before reaching the merits, we must consider whether AFL satisfied the requirements for Article III standing, a question bearing on whether the district court had subject-matter jurisdiction. *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998)."

Because the plaintiff failed to demonstrate an injury that was concrete, particularized, and redressable, the court concluded it lacked the power to hear the case. Consequently, any ruling on the statutory conflict would be beyond the court's authority. The opinion concludes:

> "Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction."

### 2. This Is Not Constitutional Avoidance
You asked if this is an example of the constitutional avoidance canon. It is not. The constitutional avoidance canon applies when a court *has* jurisdiction but chooses a narrower statutory interpretation to avoid a constitutional question.

Here, the court did not choose to avoid the statutory conflict; it was **prohibited** from reaching it. Without standing, there is no "case or controversy," and any judgment on the statutory scheme would amount to an advisory opinion, which federal courts are constitutionally barred from issuing. The court noted that because the plaintiff could not satisfy standing requirements:

> "we need not decide whether these injuries are procedural or substantive."

This logic extends to the underlying statutory interpretation: if the plaintiff has no standing to complain about the injury, the court need not determine the scope of the statute that allegedly caused it.

### 3. Why the Court Did Not Clarify the Scheme for Future Cases
While it might seem beneficial to clarify the statutory relationship for future litigants, federal courts generally cannot do so absent a proper case or controversy. The opinion emphasizes that standing requires an injury "likely to be redressed by a favorable decision."

The court found that any judgment ordering an investigation would not redress the plaintiff's injury because the causal chain was too speculative (involving the discretion of OSC, the MSPB, and DOJ). Since the plaintiff could not show that a favorable ruling would actually fix their problem, the court could not issue a ruling that would bind the parties or clarify the law for them.

In summary, the statutory conflict is legally irrelevant in this specific case because the plaintiff failed to clear the jurisdictional hurdle of standing. The court cannot rule on the merits of the law if it lacks the authority to rule on the case.
