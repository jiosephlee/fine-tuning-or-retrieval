Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

## Procedural Rights in Vacuo: D.C. Circuit Reaffirms *Summers* Standing Requirements in FOIA Context

In a decision released on October 3, 2025, the D.C. Circuit delivered a significant reminder to litigants seeking to enforce statutory procedural mandates through the federal courts: a statutory right to a procedure is not, in itself, a concrete injury sufficient to confer Article III standing. Writing for the panel, Judge Katsas dismissed *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, holding that the plaintiff could not demonstrate that the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) refusal to investigate a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) violation caused a redressable injury to the foundation. While the case arose in the specific context of FOIA enforcement and OSC’s investigative powers, its reasoning reinforces the continuing vitality of *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* and tightens the doctrinal noose around procedural standing claims.

The case presents a complex statutory interplay between the Civil Service Reform Act and FOIA. The America First Legal Foundation (AFL) sought records from the Department of Justice regarding judicial recusals. When DOJ allegedly denied the request based on a policy of not searching employee emails without consent, AFL asked OSC to investigate the withholding under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which states OSC "shall" investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings. OSC declined, arguing that FOIA’s own enforcement provision, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), required a prior court order finding arbitrary withholding before OSC was mandated to act. The district court initially agreed with AFL that § 1216 was not limited by FOIA’s enforcement provision but ultimately dismissed the case because OSC’s decision to investigate remained discretionary. On appeal, the D.C. Circuit bypassed the statutory interpretation question entirely, concluding that AFL lacked standing to pursue the claim in the first place.

Judge Katsas’s opinion rigorously applies the Supreme Court’s procedural injury framework established in *Summers*. The core holding is that the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest affected by that deprivation is insufficient for standing. AFL attempted to overcome this by asserting two distinct concrete injuries. First, it argued that OSC’s failure to investigate prevented it from receiving the underlying DOJ records. Second, it claimed an informational injury based on the notices and updates it would have received had OSC conducted the investigation. The court rejected both theories, offering a masterclass in the causation and redressability requirements that often trip up plaintiffs in administrative enforcement contexts.

The court’s rejection of the first theory highlights the persistent difficulty of establishing causation when the plaintiff seeks to use one agency to compel action by another. AFL’s theory required a speculative chain of events: OSC would investigate, potentially seek corrective action, the Merit Systems Protection Board would sanction employees, and only then would DOJ produce the records. Judge Katsas noted that this chain relies on the "unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts," citing recent Supreme Court precedent such as *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.* and *Haaland v. Brackeen*. The opinion emphasizes that redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties before the court, not on whether the rationale might persuade third parties like DOJ or the MSPB. This reasoning signals that plaintiffs cannot bootstrap standing by suing an enforcement agency to pressure a non-party agency into compliance, particularly when the enforcement agency retains significant discretion in how it prosecutes violations.

The second theory regarding informational standing failed because the statute did not create an entitlement to the specific information AFL sought. While § 1216 mandates an investigation, it does not mandate the notice procedures found in § 1214, which apply to prohibited personnel practices. Because OSC’s decision to follow § 1214 notice requirements in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary, AFL had no statutory right to receive that information. Consequently, there was no concrete injury in fact. This distinction is critical for future litigation involving agency investigations. It clarifies that a statutory mandate to investigate does not automatically generate a private right to the fruits of that investigation, especially when the statute leaves the scope and procedure of the investigation to the agency’s discretion.

From a doctrinal perspective, this decision aligns with the D.C. Circuit’s recent trend of strictly policing the boundaries of Article III standing, particularly in the wake of Supreme Court decisions that have tightened redressability standards. The opinion suggests that even when a statute uses mandatory language like "shall," the judiciary may decline to intervene if the plaintiff cannot show a direct link between the agency’s inaction and a concrete harm. This has downstream effects for public interest litigation seeking to enforce agency oversight mechanisms. Litigants hoping to use OSC or similar oversight bodies as a lever to force transparency or compliance elsewhere in the executive branch will face a high bar. They must demonstrate not just that the oversight agency failed to act, but that the plaintiff’s specific injury is directly traceable to that failure and likely to be redressed by a court order compelling the investigation.

Ultimately, *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* serves as a cautionary tale for plaintiffs relying on procedural mandates to establish standing. While the court did not reach the merits of whether OSC was legally required to investigate the DOJ’s FOIA policy, the standing dismissal effectively ends the case. By grounding the decision in the fundamental requirements of injury, causation, and redressability, Judge Katsas reaffirmed that statutory procedure cannot substitute for the concrete injury necessary to invoke the judicial power. For litigators, the takeaway is clear: before arguing that an agency violated a procedural duty, one must first be able to articulate a concrete interest that the procedure was designed to protect, and prove that a court order compelling the procedure would actually fix the harm.
