Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# FOIA Enforcement Limbo: The Unresolved Conflict Between OSC Authority and FOIA Statutes

In a decision issued October 3, 2025, the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals dismissed *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* for lack of Article III standing, effectively closing the door on the plaintiff’s attempt to compel the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) violation. While the immediate outcome is a jurisdictional defeat for the plaintiff, the opinion by Circuit Judge Katsas leaves a significant statutory question hanging in the air. The court sidestepped the core tension between the Civil Service Reform Act and the FOIA statutes regarding whether OSC has a mandatory duty to investigate arbitrary withholdings absent a prior court order. This unresolved conflict creates a zone of legal uncertainty for future litigants seeking to use OSC as a enforcement mechanism for FOIA compliance.

The statutory friction at the heart of the dispute stems from two competing provisions. Under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), OSC is directed to “conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” On its face, this language appears mandatory and broad. However, FOIA itself contains a specific enforcement provision in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), which mandates OSC to initiate a proceeding only after a court has ordered production, awarded fees, and specifically questioned whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously. The District Court had previously suggested that the FOIA provision did not limit OSC’s broader authority under § 1216, but the D.C. Circuit, focusing on standing, declined to resolve whether these statutes conflict or how they should be harmonized. By vacating the District Court’s judgment and remanding with instructions to dismiss, the D.C. Circuit ensures that the statutory hierarchy remains unclarified by appellate precedent.

Judge Katsas’s opinion rigorously applies Article III standing doctrine to foreclose the case, offering a roadmap of why plaintiffs struggle to challenge agency inaction in this context. The court rejected two primary theories of injury. First, AFL argued that OSC’s failure to investigate prevented them from receiving the records they sought from the Department of Justice. The court found this theory fatally flawed on causation and redressability grounds, noting that any informational injury was traceable to DOJ, not OSC. The causal chain required speculation that an OSC investigation would lead to MSPB sanctions, which in turn would pressure DOJ to release records. Citing recent Supreme Court precedent regarding independent actors, the court held that such speculation is insufficient to establish that a judgment against OSC would likely redress the injury.

Second, the court rejected AFL’s claim of an informational injury regarding the OSC investigation itself. While plaintiffs sometimes establish standing when denied information they are statutorily entitled to receive, the court found no such entitlement here. Even if § 1216 mandates an investigation, it does not mandate that OSC share the fruits of that investigation with the complainant. The notice requirements under § 1214 apply to prohibited personnel practices, and existing precedent suggests OSC retains discretion in applying those procedures to § 1216 investigations. Consequently, the court determined that ordering an investigation would not necessarily result in the information AFL sought, failing the redressability prong of standing.

The doctrinal implications of this avoidance are substantial for public interest litigation and FOIA enforcement strategy. By dismissing on standing, the D.C. Circuit signals that plaintiffs cannot use the procedural right to an investigation as a standalone basis for standing without a concrete, particularized interest that is directly redressable by the court’s order. This aligns with the Supreme Court’s tightening of procedural standing requirements, as seen in cases like *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*. For future litigants, this suggests that simply alleging a violation of a “shall investigate” statute is insufficient. They must demonstrate a direct link between the agency’s inaction and a specific harm that a court order can fix, which is notoriously difficult when the remedy involves discretionary enforcement actions by a third party like the MSPB.

Furthermore, the unresolved statutory tension invites future litigation once a plaintiff with clearer standing emerges. The question of whether § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) limits the broader mandate of § 1216 remains open. If a plaintiff could show a concrete injury unrelated to the release of records—perhaps a specific regulatory harm or a distinct statutory right to notice—the court might eventually be forced to confront the textual conflict. Until then, OSC retains significant leeway to interpret its own investigative priorities under the guise of discretion, effectively insulating its decisions from judicial review in most FOIA contexts. The *Greer* opinion thus serves as a reminder that while statutory text may promise investigation, the constitutional constraints on judicial power often keep those promises out of reach for private litigants.
