Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Factual Background and Statutory Framework

## Parties involved: America First Legal Foundation, Office of Special Counsel, and Department of Justice

To understand this opinion, a student must first identify the litigants and their relationships. The plaintiff is America First Legal Foundation (AFL), a non-governmental organization. The defendant is the Office of Special Counsel (OSC), specifically represented by its head, Jamieson Greer. While the lawsuit is technically brought against OSC, the underlying dispute concerns the conduct of a third party, the Department of Justice (DOJ). AFL sought to compel OSC to investigate the DOJ for allegedly violating the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). Procedurally, this case arrived at the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals following a dismissal by the United States District Court for the District of Columbia. The district court initially ruled on the merits of AFL’s statutory claims but ultimately dismissed the case for failure to state a claim, leading AFL to appeal. The appellate court’s review is de novo, meaning it examines the legal questions anew without deference to the lower court’s conclusions.

## The Civil Service Reform Act and OSC's investigative powers under 5 U.S.C. § 1216

The statutory foundation for OSC’s authority lies in the Civil Service Reform Act. The opinion explains that OSC is an investigative and prosecutorial agency tasked with ensuring a "fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service." Its primary power involves investigating "prohibited personnel practices." Under 5 U.S.C. § 1214, OSC can seek corrective action for these practices, which includes notifying complaining parties about investigation progress. However, the specific provision driving this litigation is 5 U.S.C. § 1216. This section empowers OSC to investigate forms of misconduct beyond standard prohibited personnel practices.

Section 1216(a)(3) is particularly critical to the factual background. It mandates that OSC "shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552." Section 552 is the statutory citation for FOIA. Thus, the statute creates a mandatory duty for OSC to investigate allegations that an agency arbitrarily withheld information in violation of FOIA. If OSC finds such misconduct, § 1216(c) allows it to seek corrective or disciplinary action under the same procedures used for prohibited personnel practices (§§ 1214 and 1215). The text of § 1216 uses the word "shall," which AFL argues creates a non-discretionary obligation to investigate upon receiving an allegation.

## FOIA enforcement provisions and OSC's role under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)

While the Civil Service Reform Act grants OSC broad investigative power, FOIA itself contains a specific enforcement mechanism involving OSC. The opinion highlights 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), which establishes a separate pathway for OSC involvement in FOIA cases. This provision creates a specific trigger for OSC action. Under this statute, OSC is required to "promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted" only if three specific predicates are met. First, a court must order the production of records that were improperly withheld. Second, the court must award attorneys’ fees against the agency. Third, the court must specifically question whether the agency "acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding."

This statutory provision creates a potential conflict or ambiguity in the statutory framework. Section 1216 of the Civil Service Reform Act appears to mandate investigation upon an allegation of arbitrary withholding, whereas § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) of FOIA appears to limit OSC’s disciplinary investigation to situations where a court has already ruled on the case and met specific criteria. Understanding the interplay between these two statutes is essential to grasping the legal dispute, as OSC relied on the FOIA provision to deny AFL’s request, while AFL relied on the Civil Service Reform Act to compel it.

## The underlying FOIA request: DOJ's policy on searching employee emails

The factual dispute originated with AFL’s effort to investigate judicial recusals. To further this goal, AFL submitted a FOIA request to the DOJ seeking certain emails exchanged between an outside attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. The DOJ denied the request based on a specific internal policy. According to AFL, the Civil Division refused to search employee emails for responsive records without the employee’s consent. AFL characterized this ground for denial as "dubious."

Following the denial, AFL initiated a lawsuit against the DOJ under FOIA to compel production. The opinion notes that in this separate lawsuit, the DOJ has produced and continues to produce records responsive to the request. Crucially for the purposes of this appeal, the sufficiency of that production is not at issue. The focus of this opinion is not whether the DOJ violated FOIA, but rather whether OSC was obligated to investigate the DOJ’s policy of not searching emails without consent. The existence of the parallel FOIA litigation against DOJ is a key factual element, as it impacts the procedural posture and the status of the records at the time OSC declined to act.

## OSC's refusal to investigate based on unmet statutory predicates

AFL’s immediate grievance arose when it asked OSC to investigate the DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy. AFL invoked § 1216(a)(3), arguing that the statute mandated an investigation of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. OSC declined to investigate, asserting that it lacked authority because the three predicates for triggering an investigation under the FOIA enforcement provision, § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), were not satisfied. Specifically, because no court had yet ordered production, awarded fees, or questioned the agency’s arbitrariness in the context of a final judgment, OSC disclaimed authority to proceed.

AFL disagreed with this interpretation, arguing that § 1216 mandated an investigation even if the specific FOIA enforcement predicates were not met. The district court initially grappled with this statutory conflict. It concluded that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) did not limit OSC’s power under § 1216, meaning OSC’s refusal was based on a legal error. However, the district court also held that § 1216(c) committed the decision to investigate to OSC’s discretion, so it could not order the investigation. After remand, OSC again declined to investigate, and the district court dismissed AFL’s remaining claims. This sequence establishes the procedural posture for the appeal: AFL is challenging the dismissal, but the appellate court must first determine if it has jurisdiction to hear the case at all.

# Procedural History and Jurisdictional Thresholds

## District court's ruling: Authority under § 1216 vs. Discretion under § 1216(c)

The litigation began in the District Court for the District of Columbia, where America First Legal Foundation (AFL) sought to compel the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate the Department of Justice’s alleged policy of withholding emails from FOIA requests. The district court’s analysis focused on the interplay between two statutory provisions governing OSC’s powers. Under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), OSC is directed to investigate allegations concerning arbitrary withholding of information prohibited by FOIA. The district court agreed with AFL that this provision granted OSC the authority to investigate the specific conduct at issue. However, the court simultaneously held that 5 U.S.C. § 1216(c), which allows OSC to seek corrective action “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved,” committed the decision to investigate to OSC’s discretion. Consequently, while the district court found that OSC’s initial refusal to investigate was based on a legal error regarding its authority, it declined to order the investigation because the statute left the ultimate decision to OSC’s discretion. This mixed ruling established that OSC had the power to act but was not legally compelled to do so in this specific context.

## Remand to OSC and subsequent dismissal by the district court

Following the initial ruling, the district court remanded the case to OSC for further consideration, presumably to allow the agency to reconsider its decision in light of the court’s interpretation of its statutory authority. Upon remand, OSC again declined to investigate AFL’s allegations regarding the DOJ’s FOIA policy. With the agency having reaffirmed its refusal, AFL moved for entry of final judgment. The district court granted this motion by dismissing AFL’s remaining claims. This dismissal provided the final, appealable order necessary to bring the case before the Court of Appeals. The procedural posture at this stage was critical because it solidified the administrative record: the agency had been given notice of the legal error, reconsidered its position, and maintained its refusal to investigate. This sequence ensured that the appellate court was reviewing a fully developed administrative decision rather than an interlocutory or premature ruling.

## AFL's appeal and the D.C. Circuit's de novo review

AFL appealed the district court’s dismissal to the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit. Because the appeal concerned the interpretation of federal statutes and the scope of agency authority, the appellate court applied a de novo standard of review. This standard means the court examined the legal questions anew, without deference to the district court’s conclusions. The court cited *N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf* to support this approach, emphasizing that legal determinations regarding statutory authority and standing are reviewed independently. This standard of review is significant for law students to recognize because it signals that the appellate court is not merely checking for abuse of discretion but is making its own independent judgment on the legal framework governing the case. It allows the appellate court to correct legal errors regarding jurisdiction and statutory interpretation that may have been overlooked or misapplied below.

## The court's sua sponte inquiry into Article III standing

A pivotal moment in the appellate process occurred when the D.C. Circuit raised the issue of Article III standing on its own initiative, known as a *sua sponte* inquiry. Neither AFL nor the government had initially addressed whether AFL possessed the constitutional authority to bring the lawsuit in federal court. Recognizing that standing is a fundamental requirement for federal judicial power, the court ordered supplemental briefing specifically on this question. This procedural step highlights an important lesson for law students: federal courts have an affirmative duty to ensure they have jurisdiction, regardless of whether the parties waive or overlook the issue. By ordering briefing rather than dismissing immediately, the court allowed the parties to articulate their arguments regarding standing, ensuring a fully developed record before making a final jurisdictional determination. This approach prevents the court from deciding merits-based questions when it lacks the constitutional power to hear the case at all.

## Standing as a prerequisite for subject-matter jurisdiction

The court’s decision to prioritize standing rests on the principle that Article III standing is a prerequisite for subject-matter jurisdiction. Under Article III of the Constitution, federal courts are restricted to resolving actual “Cases” and “Controversies.” The court explained that standing doctrine flows directly from this case-or-controversy requirement. To satisfy this threshold, a plaintiff must demonstrate an injury that is concrete, particularized, and actual or imminent, caused by the defendant, and likely to be redressed by a favorable decision. Because standing is jurisdictional, the court must address it before reaching the merits of the statutory claims. In this case, the court concluded that AFL failed to establish standing because it could not show that a judicial order compelling OSC to investigate would likely redress AFL’s alleged informational injuries. Since the court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction due to this standing deficiency, it was required to vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss. This outcome underscores that even if a plaintiff has a plausible legal claim regarding agency authority, the case cannot proceed without a valid constitutional basis for the plaintiff to appear in federal court.

# Article III Standing Doctrine and Procedural Injuries

## The three elements of standing: Injury in fact, causation, and redressability

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit addressed whether the plaintiff, America First Legal Foundation (AFL), possessed the constitutional authority to maintain its lawsuit. Before reaching the merits of the dispute regarding the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) investigative duties, the court determined it must first consider Article III standing. This doctrine stems from the Constitution’s restriction of federal judicial power to resolving actual “Cases” and “Controversies.” The court emphasized that standing is a question bearing on subject-matter jurisdiction, meaning a court lacks the power to hear the case if the plaintiff cannot satisfy these requirements. In this instance, because neither party initially addressed standing, the court ordered supplemental briefing to ensure jurisdiction was properly established.

To satisfy Article III standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate three core elements: injury in fact, causation, and redressability. The court relied on the Supreme Court’s framework in *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, which requires the plaintiff to have suffered an injury caused by the defendant that is likely to be redressed by a favorable decision. At the motion-to-dismiss stage, the plaintiff bears the burden of alleging facts that plausibly support each of these elements. In *Greer*, AFL failed to meet this burden. The court analyzed AFL’s claims through this three-part lens, ultimately finding that the organization could not establish a sufficient causal link between OSC’s conduct and its alleged injuries, nor could it show that a court order would likely remedy those injuries.

## The requirement for concrete and particularized injuries (Lujan standard)

A fundamental prerequisite for standing is the existence of an injury in fact. The opinion clarifies that this injury must be “an invasion of a legally protected interest” that is both “concrete and particularized” and “actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.” This standard, derived from *Lujan*, ensures that federal courts do not issue advisory opinions on abstract grievances. In the context of *Greer*, the court examined whether AFL’s grievance met this threshold. AFL argued that OSC violated an alleged procedural right to an investigation under 5 U.S.C. § 1216. However, the court noted that a mere deprivation of a procedural right, without a concrete interest affected by that deprivation, is insufficient to confer standing. This principle prevents plaintiffs from claiming standing based solely on a statutory right that exists in the abstract, or *in vacuo*. The injury must be tangible in nature, not merely a legal technicality.

## Standing for procedural right violations (Summers v. Earth Island Inst.)

The opinion specifically addresses the complexities of standing when a plaintiff alleges a violation of a procedural right granted by statute. Citing *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*, the court reiterated that the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation is insufficient to confer Article III standing. In *Greer*, AFL claimed that OSC’s refusal to investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy violated its rights under § 1216(a)(3), which states that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of arbitrary FOIA withholdings. While AFL argued this statutory language created a mandatory duty, the court looked beyond the text to determine if the procedural right protected a concrete interest. The court distinguished between a right to a process and a right to a specific outcome or information. Without a showing that the procedural right was designed to protect a specific, concrete interest of the plaintiff, the violation of that procedure alone does not create an injury in fact sufficient for federal jurisdiction.

## The relaxed standard for imminence and redressability in procedural injury cases

When a plaintiff alleges injury resulting from the violation of a procedural right afforded by statute, courts may apply a modified standing analysis. The opinion explains that in such cases, courts relax the requirements regarding imminence and redressability, but they do not eliminate the requirements of injury in fact or causation. This relaxed standard acknowledges that procedural violations often threaten future harm that is difficult to predict with certainty. However, the court in *Greer* stressed that this relaxation does not lower the bar for establishing a concrete injury. AFL attempted to invoke this relaxed standard, arguing that OSC’s failure to investigate caused it informational injuries. The court, however, concluded that AFL satisfied neither the requirements for injury in fact nor even the relaxed standard of redressability. Because AFL could not demonstrate a concrete interest affected by the procedural deprivation, the court determined it did not need to decide whether the injuries were procedural or substantive, as the threshold requirement of a concrete injury was not met.

## The necessity of a threatened concrete interest affected by the procedural deprivation

The crux of the court’s analysis in *Greer* focused on whether AFL could link the alleged procedural violation to a threatened concrete interest. The court evaluated two distinct standing theories advanced by AFL. First, AFL argued that OSC’s failure to investigate prevented it from receiving records requested under FOIA. The court rejected this theory on causation and redressability grounds, noting that any informational injury from DOJ’s refusal was traceable to DOJ, not OSC. The causal chain required speculation about how OSC would exercise enforcement discretion, how the Merit Systems Protection Board would act, and how DOJ would respond, which the court found too attenuated to support standing.

Second, AFL argued it had an informational interest in the fruits of the OSC investigation itself, specifically the notices and information required under 5 U.S.C. § 1214. The court found that even if § 1216 required an investigation, it created no entitlement to receive information. AFL acknowledged that OSC’s decision to follow § 1214’s procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary. Absent an alleged entitlement to receive information under § 1214, AFL lacked a concrete injury sufficient to support this theory. Consequently, a judicial order mandating an investigation would not be reasonably likely to redress the injury. Because AFL lacked Article III standing, the court vacated the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

# Application of Standing Principles to AFL's Claims

## Theory One: Injury from DOJ's failure to produce FOIA records

Before addressing the merits of the statutory interpretation dispute, the D.C. Circuit was required to determine whether America First Legal Foundation (AFL) possessed Article III standing to bring the suit. Because neither party initially raised the issue, the court ordered supplemental briefing to resolve this threshold jurisdictional question. AFL advanced two theories to establish standing, the first of which relied on an informational injury stemming from the Department of Justice’s (DOJ) failure to produce records requested under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). AFL argued that the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) refusal to investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy prevented AFL from receiving the records it sought. To succeed, AFL needed to satisfy the three elements of Article III standing: injury in fact, causation, and redressability. The court acknowledged that AFL suffered a concrete injury by not receiving the requested emails, but it found that the remaining elements—specifically causation and redressability—were not met. The court emphasized that while procedural injuries can sometimes relax the requirements for imminence and redressability, they do not eliminate the need for a concrete injury or a causal link between the defendant’s conduct and that injury.

## Causation analysis: The speculative chain involving OSC, MSPB, and DOJ

The court rejected AFL’s first theory primarily because the causal connection between OSC’s inaction and the denial of FOIA records was too attenuated to support standing. The court outlined a speculative chain of events that would need to occur for AFL’s desired result to materialize. First, OSC would need to conduct the investigation AFL demanded. Second, OSC would need to decide to pursue corrective or disciplinary action against DOJ employees. Third, the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) would need to decide to sanction those employees. Finally, DOJ would need to decide to produce the records in response to those sanctions. The court found this chain insufficient because it relied on the unfettered choices of independent actors not before the court, specifically the MSPB and DOJ. Citing *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.*, the court noted that causation generally cannot rest on speculation about the decisions of third parties. Furthermore, the chain required speculation about how OSC would exercise its own enforcement discretion, which courts generally cannot compel under *Heckler v. Chaney*. Because AFL could not show that a favorable judgment would very likely spur the necessary steps to redress the injury, the causal element of standing failed.

## Redressability analysis: Limitations of binding non-parties (DOJ) via OSC

Even if causation were established, the court held that the injury was not redressable by a judgment against OSC. Redressability requires that a favorable court decision be likely to remedy the plaintiff’s injury. In this context, a judicial order mandating OSC to investigate would bind only OSC, not the DOJ or the MSPB. The court explained that redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties before the court, not on whether the rationale of the judgment might persuade non-parties to act. Citing *Haaland v. Brackeen*, the court reinforced that a judgment cannot be expected to redress an injury if it does not legally compel the actor responsible for the injury to change its behavior. Since the DOJ, not OSC, was the agency withholding the records, a court order directed solely at OSC would not guarantee that DOJ would produce the requested emails. Consequently, AFL could not establish standing based on its interest in the information it requested from DOJ, as the relief sought would not necessarily fix the underlying informational injury.

## Theory Two: Injury from lack of OSC investigation notices

Having dismissed the first theory, the court turned to AFL’s second theory of standing, which focused on an informational interest in the fruits of the OSC investigation itself. AFL argued that OSC’s refusal to investigate deprived it of the notices and information required under 5 U.S.C. § 1214, which governs corrective actions for prohibited personnel practices. AFL contended that this deprivation constituted a concrete injury sufficient for standing. The court analyzed this claim under the framework for informational standing. To establish standing based on the denial of information, a plaintiff must show that a statute entitled them to receive that specific information. The court noted that while AFL claimed OSC violated a procedural right to an investigation under § 1216, the deprivation of a procedural right in a vacuum, without a concrete interest affected by the deprivation, is insufficient to confer standing. Therefore, the inquiry hinged on whether AFL had a statutory entitlement to the information it claimed OSC failed to provide.

## Absence of statutory entitlement to information under § 1216

The court found that even if § 1216(a)(3) mandated OSC to investigate FOIA violations, that provision did not create an entitlement for AFL to receive information resulting from that investigation. Section 1216 empowers OSC to investigate and seek corrective action, but it does not explicitly grant the complaining party a right to receive the data or notices generated during the process. The court distinguished between the authority to investigate and the obligation to disclose information to the requester. Because § 1216 does not entitle AFL to receive information, the failure to investigate did not result in a concrete injury of the type required for informational standing. The court reasoned that without a statutory right to the information, AFL could not claim a concrete injury from OSC’s refusal to initiate the process that might have produced it. This lack of entitlement meant that AFL’s second standing theory could not satisfy the injury-in-fact requirement, regardless of whether the investigation itself was mandatory.

## Discretionary nature of § 1214 notice requirements in § 1216 investigations

Finally, the court addressed AFL’s argument that OSC should be required to follow the notice requirements of § 1214 during a § 1216 investigation. AFL suggested that if OSC conducted the investigation, it would be required to provide notices under § 1214, thereby creating the concrete injury AFL claimed. However, the court noted that AFL acknowledged existing case law, specifically *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*, which holds that OSC’s decision whether to follow § 1214’s procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary. Because OSC is not legally required to engraft § 1214 notice requirements onto its § 1216 investigations, AFL had no legal right to expect those notices. Consequently, a judicial order mandating an investigation would not be reasonably likely to redress AFL’s alleged injury, as OSC could still choose not to provide the notices. With no statutory entitlement to the information and no guarantee that the investigation would yield the specific procedural protections AFL desired, the court concluded that AFL lacked standing on this theory as well, leading to the dismissal of the case for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

# Disposition and Legal Significance

## Vacatur of the district court's judgment

The D.C. Circuit’s disposition of the case begins with a critical procedural correction regarding the district court’s earlier ruling. Although the district court had dismissed America First Legal Foundation’s (AFL) claims on the merits for failure to state a claim, the appellate court determined that it could not reach the merits at all. This is because Article III standing is a threshold requirement that bears on the federal courts’ subject-matter jurisdiction. As the court noted, citing *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, federal judicial power is restricted to resolving actual “Cases” and “Controversies.” When a plaintiff lacks standing, the court lacks the constitutional authority to adjudicate the dispute, regardless of the strength of the underlying legal claims. Because the district court proceeded to rule on the merits without first establishing that AFL satisfied the standing requirements, its judgment was legally flawed. Consequently, the D.C. Circuit vacated the district court’s judgment. This vacatur serves as a procedural safeguard, ensuring that lower courts do not issue advisory opinions or rulings on substantive law when they lack jurisdiction to hear the case in the first place.

## Remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction

Following the vacatur, the appellate court issued specific instructions to the district court on how to proceed. Rather than remanding the case for further fact-finding or legal analysis on the merits, the D.C. Circuit directed the district court to dismiss AFL’s claims for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. This instruction reflects the finality of the standing determination. Because standing depends on the plaintiff’s ability to allege a concrete injury, causation, and redressability at the outset, a failure to meet these requirements is generally fatal to the lawsuit. The court indicated that AFL could not satisfy even the relaxed standing requirements applicable to procedural injuries. Since the defect lies in the jurisdiction of the court itself, there is no further factual development that could cure the problem. By ordering a dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, the appellate court ensures that the case is terminated without prejudice to the substantive legal arguments, but with the clear message that the federal judiciary cannot entertain this specific claim against the Office of Special Counsel (OSC).

## Implications for standing in FOIA-related OSC enforcement actions

This opinion establishes significant limitations for litigants seeking to compel the Office of Special Counsel to investigate alleged violations of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). AFL attempted to rely on a procedural injury theory, arguing that the statutory requirement for OSC to investigate under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) created a right to an investigation that, when denied, constituted an injury. However, the court clarified that the deprivation of a procedural right in a vacuum is insufficient for Article III standing. Citing *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, the court emphasized that a plaintiff must still demonstrate a concrete interest affected by the deprivation. In this context, AFL failed to show that OSC’s refusal to investigate caused a concrete injury to AFL’s own interests. The opinion signals that future plaintiffs cannot simply point to a statutory mandate for an agency investigation and claim standing; they must demonstrate that the failure to investigate directly harms a legally protected interest of their own, distinct from the general public interest in law enforcement.

## The role of agency discretion in investigative proceedings (Heckler v. Chaney)

The court’s analysis reinforces the principle that federal courts generally lack the power to compel agencies to exercise their enforcement discretion. In evaluating AFL’s redressability argument, the opinion invoked *Heckler v. Chaney*, which established a presumption against judicial review of agency decisions not to take enforcement action. The court reasoned that even if OSC were ordered to investigate, it retains discretion over whether to pursue corrective or disciplinary action. Because the judiciary cannot force OSC to exercise its enforcement authority in a specific way, a court order mandating an investigation would not guarantee the outcome AFL sought. This reliance on *Heckler* underscores the separation of powers concerns inherent in mandamus actions against investigative agencies. It teaches that litigants cannot use the courts as a vehicle to override an agency’s prosecutorial judgment, even when statutes use mandatory language like “shall” in the context of internal agency processes that remain discretionary in execution.

## The boundary between judicial oversight and agency enforcement choices

Finally, the opinion delineates the boundary between judicial oversight and the independent choices of other government actors. AFL’s standing theory relied on a speculative chain of causation: that an OSC investigation would lead to MSPB sanctions, which would then pressure the Department of Justice to produce records. The D.C. Circuit rejected this chain, citing *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* and *Haaland v. Brackeen*. The court explained that causation cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices of independent actors not before the court. Redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties before the court, not on whether the court’s rationale might persuade other agencies to change their behavior. This reasoning clarifies that for a plaintiff to have standing, the relief sought must be likely to redress the injury directly through the defendant’s compliance, rather than through a series of contingent actions by third parties. This principle protects the integrity of the judicial process by preventing courts from issuing orders based on hypothetical future actions by non-parties.
