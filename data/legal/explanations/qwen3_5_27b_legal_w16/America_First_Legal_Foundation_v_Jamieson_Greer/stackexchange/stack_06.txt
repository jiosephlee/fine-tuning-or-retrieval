Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Why did the court decline to resolve the conflict between 5 U.S.C. § 1216 and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)?
Question:
The district court ruled that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC's power under § 1216, but the D.C. Circuit says 'we need not decide' this issue because of the standing failure. I'm confused about the relationship between these two statutes. If the court determined there was no standing, does it mean the statutory conflict is irrelevant, or is this an example of the constitutional avoidance canon? Shouldn't the court clarify the statutory scheme for future cases even if this plaintiff lacks standing?
Answer:
# The Court Declined to Resolve the Statutory Conflict Because It Lacked Article III Jurisdiction

The D.C. Circuit declined to resolve the conflict between 5 U.S.C. § 1216 and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) because the plaintiff lacked **Article III standing**, which is a jurisdictional prerequisite that must be resolved before a court can reach the merits of a statutory dispute.

### 1. Jurisdiction Precedes Merits
As the opinion explains, federal courts are restricted by Article III to resolving actual "Cases" and "Controversies." Standing doctrine is derived from this constitutional limitation. The court explicitly prioritized this threshold inquiry over the statutory interpretation question:

> "Before reaching the merits, we must consider whether AFL satisfied the requirements for Article III standing, a question bearing on whether the district court had subject-matter jurisdiction. *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998)."

Because the plaintiff failed to demonstrate an injury that was concrete, particularized, and redressable, the court concluded it lacked the power to hear the case. Consequently, any ruling on the statutory conflict would be beyond the court's authority. The opinion concludes:

> "Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction."

### 2. This Is Not Constitutional Avoidance
You asked if this is an example of the constitutional avoidance canon. It is not. The constitutional avoidance canon applies when a court *has* jurisdiction but chooses a narrower statutory interpretation to avoid a constitutional question.

Here, the court did not choose to avoid the statutory conflict; it was **prohibited** from reaching it. Without standing, there is no "case or controversy," and any judgment on the statutory scheme would amount to an advisory opinion, which federal courts are constitutionally barred from issuing. The court noted that because the plaintiff could not satisfy standing requirements:

> "we need not decide whether these injuries are procedural or substantive."

This logic extends to the underlying statutory interpretation: if the plaintiff has no standing to complain about the injury, the court need not determine the scope of the statute that allegedly caused it.

### 3. Why the Court Did Not Clarify the Scheme for Future Cases
While it might seem beneficial to clarify the statutory relationship for future litigants, federal courts generally cannot do so absent a proper case or controversy. The opinion emphasizes that standing requires an injury "likely to be redressed by a favorable decision."

The court found that any judgment ordering an investigation would not redress the plaintiff's injury because the causal chain was too speculative (involving the discretion of OSC, the MSPB, and DOJ). Since the plaintiff could not show that a favorable ruling would actually fix their problem, the court could not issue a ruling that would bind the parties or clarify the law for them.

In summary, the statutory conflict is legally irrelevant in this specific case because the plaintiff failed to clear the jurisdictional hurdle of standing. The court cannot rule on the merits of the law if it lacks the authority to rule on the case.
