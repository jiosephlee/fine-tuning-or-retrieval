Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### How does Heckler v. Chaney influence the redressability prong of standing in this case?
Question:
The court mentions *Heckler v. Chaney* regarding agency enforcement discretion when discussing redressability. AFL wants the court to order OSC to investigate. Even if the court orders an investigation, OSC might still decide not to take disciplinary action. How does the presumption against judicial review of non-enforcement decisions affect the 'likely to be redressed' requirement? Does it mean a plaintiff can never get standing to compel an investigation unless the outcome is guaranteed?
Answer:
**Short Answer**
*Heckler v. Chaney* does not categorically bar standing to compel an investigation, but in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, it reinforces that **redressability fails when the remedy depends on speculative future exercises of agency discretion.** The court held that because OSC has discretion to decide whether to pursue enforcement after an investigation, a court order mandating the investigation is not "likely to redress" the plaintiff's injury.

**Detailed Explanation**

### 1. The Redressability Standard
To establish Article III standing, a plaintiff must show that a favorable judicial decision is "likely to be redressed" by the court's judgment. In this case, AFL argued that ordering OSC to investigate would lead to DOJ producing the requested FOIA records. The court rejected this because the causal chain was too attenuated.

### 2. How *Heckler v. Chaney* Influences the Analysis
The court cites *Heckler v. Chaney* to highlight the **speculative nature of agency enforcement discretion**.

*   **The Speculative Chain:** The court outlined the chain of events AFL relied upon:
    1.  Court orders OSC to investigate.
    2.  OSC *might* decide to pursue corrective or disciplinary action.
    3.  MSPB *might* sanction employees.
    4.  DOJ *might* then produce records.
*   **The *Chaney* Bar:** The opinion notes that step 2 requires "speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel."
*   **The Result:** Because *Chaney* establishes a presumption that courts will not compel agency enforcement decisions, the court cannot assume OSC will move from investigation to discipline. Therefore, the judgment would not "very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress" the injury.

### 3. Addressing the "Guarantee" Question
You asked if this means a plaintiff can **never** get standing unless the outcome is guaranteed. The opinion clarifies two important points:

*   **Likelihood, Not Guarantee:** Standing requires a judgment to be "likely to redress" the injury, not guaranteed. However, when the redress depends on discretionary acts by the agency (which *Chaney* protects from judicial compulsion), the likelihood drops below the constitutional threshold.
*   **Binding Effect:** The court emphasizes that redressability "generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale 'might persuade actors who are not before the court.'" If the court orders an investigation but cannot bind OSC to take disciplinary action, the judgment does not bind the outcome necessary to fix the injury.

**Conclusion**
*Heckler v. Chaney* influences the redressability prong by reminding the court that it cannot presume an agency will exercise its discretion to enforce the law after an investigation. Consequently, a plaintiff cannot establish standing if the only way to redress their injury is through a series of discretionary steps that the court cannot compel.
