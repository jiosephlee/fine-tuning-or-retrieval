Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

## Procedural Rights in Vacuo: D.C. Circuit Reaffirms *Summers* Standing Requirements in FOIA Context

In a decision released on October 3, 2025, the D.C. Circuit delivered a significant reminder to litigants seeking to enforce statutory procedural mandates through the federal courts: a statutory right to a procedure is not, in itself, a concrete injury sufficient to confer Article III standing. Writing for the panel, Judge Katsas dismissed *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, holding that the plaintiff could not demonstrate that the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) refusal to investigate a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) violation caused a redressable injury to the foundation. While the case arose in the specific context of FOIA enforcement and OSC’s investigative powers, its reasoning reinforces the continuing vitality of *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* and tightens the doctrinal noose around procedural standing claims.

The case presents a complex statutory interplay between the Civil Service Reform Act and FOIA. The America First Legal Foundation (AFL) sought records from the Department of Justice regarding judicial recusals. When DOJ allegedly denied the request based on a policy of not searching employee emails without consent, AFL asked OSC to investigate the withholding under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), which states OSC "shall" investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings. OSC declined, arguing that FOIA’s own enforcement provision, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), required a prior court order finding arbitrary withholding before OSC was mandated to act. The district court initially agreed with AFL that § 1216 was not limited by FOIA’s enforcement provision but ultimately dismissed the case because OSC’s decision to investigate remained discretionary. On appeal, the D.C. Circuit bypassed the statutory interpretation question entirely, concluding that AFL lacked standing to pursue the claim in the first place.

Judge Katsas’s opinion rigorously applies the Supreme Court’s procedural injury framework established in *Summers*. The core holding is that the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest affected by that deprivation is insufficient for standing. AFL attempted to overcome this by asserting two distinct concrete injuries. First, it argued that OSC’s failure to investigate prevented it from receiving the underlying DOJ records. Second, it claimed an informational injury based on the notices and updates it would have received had OSC conducted the investigation. The court rejected both theories, offering a masterclass in the causation and redressability requirements that often trip up plaintiffs in administrative enforcement contexts.

The court’s rejection of the first theory highlights the persistent difficulty of establishing causation when the plaintiff seeks to use one agency to compel action by another. AFL’s theory required a speculative chain of events: OSC would investigate, potentially seek corrective action, the Merit Systems Protection Board would sanction employees, and only then would DOJ produce the records. Judge Katsas noted that this chain relies on the "unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts," citing recent Supreme Court precedent such as *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.* and *Haaland v. Brackeen*. The opinion emphasizes that redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties before the court, not on whether the rationale might persuade third parties like DOJ or the MSPB. This reasoning signals that plaintiffs cannot bootstrap standing by suing an enforcement agency to pressure a non-party agency into compliance, particularly when the enforcement agency retains significant discretion in how it prosecutes violations.

The second theory regarding informational standing failed because the statute did not create an entitlement to the specific information AFL sought. While § 1216 mandates an investigation, it does not mandate the notice procedures found in § 1214, which apply to prohibited personnel practices. Because OSC’s decision to follow § 1214 notice requirements in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary, AFL had no statutory right to receive that information. Consequently, there was no concrete injury in fact. This distinction is critical for future litigation involving agency investigations. It clarifies that a statutory mandate to investigate does not automatically generate a private right to the fruits of that investigation, especially when the statute leaves the scope and procedure of the investigation to the agency’s discretion.

From a doctrinal perspective, this decision aligns with the D.C. Circuit’s recent trend of strictly policing the boundaries of Article III standing, particularly in the wake of Supreme Court decisions that have tightened redressability standards. The opinion suggests that even when a statute uses mandatory language like "shall," the judiciary may decline to intervene if the plaintiff cannot show a direct link between the agency’s inaction and a concrete harm. This has downstream effects for public interest litigation seeking to enforce agency oversight mechanisms. Litigants hoping to use OSC or similar oversight bodies as a lever to force transparency or compliance elsewhere in the executive branch will face a high bar. They must demonstrate not just that the oversight agency failed to act, but that the plaintiff’s specific injury is directly traceable to that failure and likely to be redressed by a court order compelling the investigation.

Ultimately, *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* serves as a cautionary tale for plaintiffs relying on procedural mandates to establish standing. While the court did not reach the merits of whether OSC was legally required to investigate the DOJ’s FOIA policy, the standing dismissal effectively ends the case. By grounding the decision in the fundamental requirements of injury, causation, and redressability, Judge Katsas reaffirmed that statutory procedure cannot substitute for the concrete injury necessary to invoke the judicial power. For litigators, the takeaway is clear: before arguing that an agency violated a procedural duty, one must first be able to articulate a concrete interest that the procedure was designed to protect, and prove that a court order compelling the procedure would actually fix the harm.



# The 'Independent Actor' Barrier: Causation Challenges in Multi-Agency Enforcement Schemes

In a decision that underscores the tightening constraints on Article III standing, the D.C. Circuit recently dismissed a challenge brought by the America First Legal Foundation (AFL) against the Office of Special Counsel (OSC). While the case ostensibly concerned the scope of OSC’s investigative authority under the Civil Service Reform Act and the Freedom of Information Act, the court’s ruling turned entirely on jurisdictional grounds. In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, Judge Katsas wrote for the court to hold that AFL lacked standing because it could not demonstrate that a judicial order compelling OSC to investigate would likely redress its alleged injury. This decision highlights a growing doctrinal hurdle for plaintiffs attempting to leverage multi-agency enforcement mechanisms: the "independent actor" barrier. When a plaintiff’s desired remedy depends on a chain of events involving multiple government entities with independent discretion, the causal link required for standing often proves too attenuated to survive.

To understand the significance of this ruling, one must appreciate the statutory tangle at play. AFL sought to compel OSC to investigate the Department of Justice’s alleged policy of not searching employee emails without consent when processing FOIA requests. The Civil Service Reform Act, specifically 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), states that OSC "shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552." AFL argued this language created a mandatory duty for OSC to investigate, regardless of whether the specific predicates for enforcement under FOIA itself were met. The district court had previously acknowledged that OSC likely erred in law by refusing to investigate but ultimately dismissed the case because the decision to pursue corrective or disciplinary action remained within OSC’s discretion. On appeal, the D.C. Circuit bypassed the statutory interpretation question entirely, concluding that the district court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction because AFL failed to establish standing.

The court’s analysis of standing provides a rigorous illustration of how causation and redressability function in complex administrative contexts. AFL advanced two theories of injury. The first, and more significant for doctrinal purposes, posited that OSC’s failure to investigate prevented AFL from receiving the requested records from DOJ. The D.C. Circuit rejected this theory by dissecting the speculative chain of events required to connect OSC’s inaction to the production of records. The court noted that any informational injury regarding the records is traceable to DOJ, not OSC. For a favorable judgment against OSC to redress the injury, one would have to assume that OSC would conduct an investigation, decide to seek corrective or disciplinary action, the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) would agree to sanction the offending employees, and DOJ would subsequently produce the records.

This is where the court leaned heavily on recent Supreme Court precedent regarding independent actors. Relying on *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, the court emphasized that causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts. The chain of causation here required guessing at the decisions of two other government agencies—the MSPB and DOJ—neither of which is a party to the lawsuit. Furthermore, the court noted that even OSC’s own enforcement choices are generally committed to discretion under *Heckler v. Chaney*, meaning a court order mandating an investigation does not guarantee the investigation will yield the specific outcome AFL desires. By invoking *Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, the D.C. Circuit reinforced the principle that redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties before the court, not on whether the court’s rationale might persuade other actors to change their behavior.

The second standing theory, which relied on an informational interest in the OSC investigation itself, also faltered on the lack of a concrete entitlement. AFL argued it was entitled to notices and information that OSC would provide if it were investigating a prohibited personnel practice under 5 U.S.C. § 1214. However, the court pointed out that § 1216 investigations do not carry the same mandatory notice requirements, and existing precedent holds that OSC’s decision to follow § 1214 procedures in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary. Without a statutory entitlement to specific information, the deprivation of a procedural right in a vacuum is insufficient to confer standing under *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*. This reinforces the requirement that procedural injuries must still affect a concrete, particularized interest, a standard that proves difficult to meet when the statute grants the agency broad latitude in how it conducts its internal inquiries.

The doctrinal implications of *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* extend beyond the narrow context of FOIA and civil service enforcement. The decision signals that courts are increasingly skeptical of standing claims that attempt to bypass the direct source of an injury by targeting a supervisory or enforcement agency. In multi-agency enforcement schemes, where one agency investigates and another adjudicates or implements sanctions, plaintiffs face a high bar to prove that compelling the first agency will actually result in the relief sought. The reliance on *Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* suggests that the "independent actor" doctrine is becoming a potent tool for dismissing cases where the causal chain involves multiple layers of administrative discretion.

Downstream, this ruling may chill efforts to use OSC as a proxy for enforcing FOIA compliance when direct litigation against the withholding agency is not viable or has not yet reached the necessary judicial findings. While the Civil Service Reform Act uses mandatory language like "shall" regarding investigations, the court’s focus on standing effectively neutralizes that mandate in the context of private enforcement. Plaintiffs seeking to trigger these enforcement mechanisms will need to demonstrate a much tighter causal link between the agency’s action and their specific injury, likely requiring that the agency in question has the direct power to provide the relief sought without relying on the cooperation of other independent bodies. As administrative law continues to evolve, cases like this will serve as a reminder that Article III standing remains a formidable jurisdictional gatekeeper, particularly when the path to redress winds through a maze of federal agencies.



# FOIA Enforcement Limbo: The Unresolved Conflict Between OSC Authority and FOIA Statutes

In a decision issued October 3, 2025, the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals dismissed *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* for lack of Article III standing, effectively closing the door on the plaintiff’s attempt to compel the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) violation. While the immediate outcome is a jurisdictional defeat for the plaintiff, the opinion by Circuit Judge Katsas leaves a significant statutory question hanging in the air. The court sidestepped the core tension between the Civil Service Reform Act and the FOIA statutes regarding whether OSC has a mandatory duty to investigate arbitrary withholdings absent a prior court order. This unresolved conflict creates a zone of legal uncertainty for future litigants seeking to use OSC as a enforcement mechanism for FOIA compliance.

The statutory friction at the heart of the dispute stems from two competing provisions. Under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), OSC is directed to “conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” On its face, this language appears mandatory and broad. However, FOIA itself contains a specific enforcement provision in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), which mandates OSC to initiate a proceeding only after a court has ordered production, awarded fees, and specifically questioned whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously. The District Court had previously suggested that the FOIA provision did not limit OSC’s broader authority under § 1216, but the D.C. Circuit, focusing on standing, declined to resolve whether these statutes conflict or how they should be harmonized. By vacating the District Court’s judgment and remanding with instructions to dismiss, the D.C. Circuit ensures that the statutory hierarchy remains unclarified by appellate precedent.

Judge Katsas’s opinion rigorously applies Article III standing doctrine to foreclose the case, offering a roadmap of why plaintiffs struggle to challenge agency inaction in this context. The court rejected two primary theories of injury. First, AFL argued that OSC’s failure to investigate prevented them from receiving the records they sought from the Department of Justice. The court found this theory fatally flawed on causation and redressability grounds, noting that any informational injury was traceable to DOJ, not OSC. The causal chain required speculation that an OSC investigation would lead to MSPB sanctions, which in turn would pressure DOJ to release records. Citing recent Supreme Court precedent regarding independent actors, the court held that such speculation is insufficient to establish that a judgment against OSC would likely redress the injury.

Second, the court rejected AFL’s claim of an informational injury regarding the OSC investigation itself. While plaintiffs sometimes establish standing when denied information they are statutorily entitled to receive, the court found no such entitlement here. Even if § 1216 mandates an investigation, it does not mandate that OSC share the fruits of that investigation with the complainant. The notice requirements under § 1214 apply to prohibited personnel practices, and existing precedent suggests OSC retains discretion in applying those procedures to § 1216 investigations. Consequently, the court determined that ordering an investigation would not necessarily result in the information AFL sought, failing the redressability prong of standing.

The doctrinal implications of this avoidance are substantial for public interest litigation and FOIA enforcement strategy. By dismissing on standing, the D.C. Circuit signals that plaintiffs cannot use the procedural right to an investigation as a standalone basis for standing without a concrete, particularized interest that is directly redressable by the court’s order. This aligns with the Supreme Court’s tightening of procedural standing requirements, as seen in cases like *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*. For future litigants, this suggests that simply alleging a violation of a “shall investigate” statute is insufficient. They must demonstrate a direct link between the agency’s inaction and a specific harm that a court order can fix, which is notoriously difficult when the remedy involves discretionary enforcement actions by a third party like the MSPB.

Furthermore, the unresolved statutory tension invites future litigation once a plaintiff with clearer standing emerges. The question of whether § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) limits the broader mandate of § 1216 remains open. If a plaintiff could show a concrete injury unrelated to the release of records—perhaps a specific regulatory harm or a distinct statutory right to notice—the court might eventually be forced to confront the textual conflict. Until then, OSC retains significant leeway to interpret its own investigative priorities under the guise of discretion, effectively insulating its decisions from judicial review in most FOIA contexts. The *Greer* opinion thus serves as a reminder that while statutory text may promise investigation, the constitutional constraints on judicial power often keep those promises out of reach for private litigants.
