Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

Chapter 4: # Analysis of the Scope of Consent

Having established that Lisa Coleman voluntarily consented to the officers' entry, the Seventh Circuit turned to the second critical Fourth Amendment inquiry: whether the officers exceeded the scope of that consent once inside the home. The court emphasized that even when consent is valid, the subsequent search must respect the boundaries of the permission granted. This chapter examines the court's reasoning regarding the permissible actions of law enforcement during a consent-based entry, specifically focusing on welfare checks, the verification of safety, and the objective reasonableness standard used to evaluate the officers' conduct.

## Defining the Purpose of Entry: Welfare Check

The first step in analyzing the scope of consent is to identify the specific purpose for which entry was authorized. In *United States v. Coleman*, the court looked to the dialogue between Officer Kizer and Lisa Coleman to determine the intent behind the consent. The officers arrived in response to a 911 call reporting domestic violence and threats to life. When Officer Kizer requested entry, he framed the request around ensuring the safety of the children and the occupants. The court noted that the "stated purpose" of the entry was to "check the welfare of her children inside, including by confirming that Coleman presented no danger to them or anyone else."

This distinction is vital for law students reading the opinion. Consent is not a blanket authorization to search; it is limited by the purpose understood by the person giving consent. Because Lisa Coleman agreed to entry based on the officers' expressed concern for the children's safety, the scope of that consent was tied to that specific welfare check. The court reasoned that any actions taken to fulfill this safety purpose fell within the boundaries of the permission granted. This contrasts with a general search for evidence, which would require a warrant or a different exception to the Fourth Amendment. By anchoring the scope to the welfare check, the court established a narrow lane within which the officers' actions were permissible.

## Reasonable Actions to Verify Safety of Children

Once inside, the officers took specific steps to verify the safety of the children, which the court analyzed to determine if they remained within the scope of consent. The opinion details that Lisa Coleman informed the officers that the children were in her bedroom and that the dogs were friendly. The officers then "led the officers to one of the back bedrooms and pointed out where the kids were." The court found these actions reasonable because they were directly related to the stated purpose of ensuring the children were safe.

The court highlighted that the officers' interaction with the children and the environment was consistent with a welfare check. For instance, Officer Kizer asked about the dogs to ensure no physical harm would come to the officers during the safety check, and Lisa confirmed they would not bite. These exchanges were not investigative in the sense of looking for contraband; rather, they were logistical steps necessary to safely enter the space where the children were located. The court concluded that asking basic questions and physically locating the children were necessary components of fulfilling the consented-upon purpose. This teaches students that actions incidental to the primary purpose of the consent—such as navigating the home to find the subject of the welfare check—are generally considered within the scope.

## Scanning the Home and Locating Occupants

A significant portion of the scope analysis involved the officers' movement through the home to locate Jaison Coleman. After finding the children, the officers did not see Coleman immediately. Officer Kizer asked where he was, and after Lisa responded that she did not know, the officers conducted a "quick canvas of the home" and found him in a separate bedroom. The court determined that this movement did not exceed the scope of consent.

The reasoning here relies on the connection between locating the husband and ensuring safety. Since the 911 call involved threats made by Coleman, and the consent was given to ensure the safety of the household, locating Coleman was integral to the welfare check. The court stated that doing so "necessarily required the police to scan the home, locate everyone, and ask some basic questions." By framing the search for Coleman as a safety measure rather than an evidence-gathering operation, the court found the "quick canvas" permissible. This illustrates to students that the scope of consent can extend to areas of the home necessary to achieve the consented purpose, even if that means entering multiple rooms, provided the officers do not use that access to search for evidence unrelated to the safety purpose.

## Distinguishing Protective Sweep from General Search

While the opinion does not explicitly invoke the doctrine of a protective sweep under *Maryland v. Buie*, the court's reasoning distinguishes between actions taken for safety and a general search for evidence. The court noted that the officers' purpose included "confirming that Coleman presented no danger." This language aligns with the concept of a protective sweep, but the court analyzed it strictly within the framework of the consent scope. The officers were not searching for weapons or contraband during the initial entry; they were looking for people to assess risk.

The distinction is crucial for understanding the Fourth Amendment implications. A general search for evidence, such as looking under mattresses for drugs, would exceed the scope of a consent given for a welfare check. However, actions taken to ensure the immediate safety of the occupants and officers, such as locating an individual who had made threats, were deemed consistent with the consent. The court observed that the officers "respected the consent that Lisa conferred in the first instance." By limiting the analysis to whether the safety measures were within the scope of the welfare check, the court avoided the need to rely on the exigent circumstances or protective sweep doctrines. This teaches students that when consent is broad enough to cover safety concerns, the court may not need to resort to other exceptions to justify the officers' presence in different parts of the home.

## Connection Between Consent and Subsequent Warrant

The opinion clarifies the procedural relationship between the initial consent entry and the subsequent warrant-based search. The firearms were not seized during the initial consent entry; rather, the information gathered during that entry "led to their securing a warrant to search the home." The children told the officers about a gun threat, which provided the probable cause for the warrant. The defendant argued that the initial entry was invalid, which would have tainted the evidence found later.

The court's affirmation of the scope of consent validates the chain of events leading to the warrant. Because the initial entry was lawful and the officers did not exceed the scope of consent, the information they gathered (the children's statements about the gun) was admissible to support the warrant application. The court noted that their conclusions regarding the scope of consent "eliminate any need to consider the government's alternative contention that the doctrines of exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery also supported the police's entry." This highlights a key lesson for students: if the initial entry is valid under consent, the evidence derived from that entry can lawfully support a warrant, avoiding the need for the government to prove alternative Fourth Amendment exceptions.

## Objective Reasonableness of the Officers' Conduct

Finally, the court applied the legal standard of "objective reasonableness" to evaluate whether the officers' conduct stayed within the scope of consent. Citing *Florida v. Jimeno*, the Seventh Circuit explained that the proper inquiry is "what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer and the person giving consent?" This standard requires the court to look at the totality of the circumstances surrounding the consent exchange.

In applying this standard, the court found no clear error in the district court's determination that the officers acted reasonably. The court observed that the officers' actions were consistent with what a reasonable person would understand as a welfare check. The officers did not rummage through drawers or open closed containers; they located people and asked safety-related questions. The court emphasized that the review of these factual findings is for "clear error," meaning the appellate court gives deference to the district court's assessment of the interaction. By grounding the analysis in objective reasonableness, the court ensures that the scope of consent is not defined by the officers' subjective intent but by how a reasonable person would interpret the permission granted. This reinforces the principle that consent searches are valid only to the extent that a reasonable person would understand the permission to extend.
