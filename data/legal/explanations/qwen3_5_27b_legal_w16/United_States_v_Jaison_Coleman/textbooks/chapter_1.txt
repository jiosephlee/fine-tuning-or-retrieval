Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

Chapter 1: # Factual Background and Procedural History

## The 911 Call and Initial Police Response
The factual foundation of *United States v. Jaison Coleman* begins with a domestic violence emergency. Just after 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, a caller contacted 911 to report that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and remained inside the home. Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department responded to this call. This initial response sets the stage for the Fourth Amendment inquiry that follows, as the circumstances surrounding the officer's arrival often inform the reasonableness of subsequent police conduct. Upon reaching the Coleman residence, Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced his presence. Lisa Coleman responded by stepping onto the porch. At this initial stage, the interaction was characterized by concern for the safety of the household members, specifically the children. Officer Kizer asked Lisa if her children were safe, but she hesitated to answer directly, stating only that they were in her bedroom. This hesitation, documented in the record, is a critical factual detail because it underscores the tense environment in which the officer sought to secure consent for entry.

## The Interaction at the Door: Hesitation, Refusal, and Consent
The core factual dispute in this case revolves around a specific dialogue between Officer Kizer and Lisa Coleman at the front door. The record, derived from body camera footage, shows a progression from refusal to consent. After initial hesitation regarding the children's safety, Lisa told Officer Kizer she did not know where her husband was. When Officer Kizer asked if he could enter at that moment, Lisa paused and stated, “um … I’d prefer you not.” This explicit refusal is legally significant; it demonstrates that Lisa understood she had the authority to deny entry. Following this refusal, Lisa stepped inside to retrieve her shoes. During her absence, Officer Kizer, concerned for her safety, cracked the door and called into the house. When Lisa returned, Officer Kizer asked again about the situation and specifically identified Jaison Coleman as the husband who had made threats.

The pivotal exchange occurred when Officer Kizer asked if Coleman was calm and if the children were okay. Lisa responded tentatively, saying they were okay “right this second.” Officer Kizer then stated, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa responded, “Okay.” She subsequently provided information about the location of the children and the behavior of the family dogs. This sequence is essential for understanding the district court’s findings. The facts show that Lisa initially refused entry but later consented after a brief interval and a renewed request focused on child welfare. For a law student, this timeline is crucial: it establishes that consent was not immediate and followed an initial denial, a fact that the appellant would later argue rendered the consent involuntary.

## Entry, Welfare Check, and Discovery of Firearms
Once Lisa opened the door, she facilitated the officers' entry by brushing the dogs aside and leading them to the bedroom where the children were located. The officers’ stated purpose upon entry was to verify the welfare of the children and ensure no danger was present. Not seeing Coleman in the children’s room, Officer Kizer asked Lisa where he was, and she replied that she did not know. A quick canvas of the home allowed Officer Kizer to locate Coleman in a separate bedroom. Coleman permitted a protective pat down, and during conversations with the children, the officers learned that Coleman had been loud and aggressive. The children confirmed they had seen their father point a gun at their mother and threaten to shoot her and burn the house down. Although the children did not know the gun’s location at that moment, this information was instrumental in the next procedural step. Based on the information gained during this welfare check, the officers sought and secured a search warrant. The subsequent search of the home resulted in the discovery of several firearms, which became the basis for the criminal prosecution.

## Criminal Charges and the Motion to Suppress
The discovery of the firearms led to a federal indictment charging Coleman with possessing a firearm as a convicted felon in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). In response to the indictment, Coleman filed a motion to suppress the recovery of the firearms. His legal argument focused on the initial entry into the home; he contended that his wife, Lisa, never validly consented to the officers entering in the first instance. If the entry was unlawful, the subsequent discovery of the weapons would be fruit of the poisonous tree. The district court held a hearing to resolve this factual dispute. A magistrate judge issued a report finding that Lisa did consent to the entry and recommending the denial of the motion. The district court adopted this finding, agreeing that Lisa consented and that the officers did not exceed the scope of that consent once inside. Consequently, the district court denied Coleman’s motion to suppress, allowing the firearms to be admitted as evidence at trial.

## Guilty Plea with Reserved Right to Appeal
Following the denial of his motion to suppress, Coleman chose to plead guilty to the charge. However, he did so while reserving his right to appeal the suppression ruling. This procedural mechanism is significant for students to understand because, generally, a guilty plea waives non-jurisdictional defects, including Fourth Amendment claims. By explicitly reserving the right to appeal the suppression issue, Coleman preserved his ability to challenge the district court’s ruling on consent despite entering a guilty plea. This procedural posture brought the case before the Seventh Circuit Court of Appeals. The appeal was limited to the validity of the consent and the scope of the search, rather than the merits of the underlying conviction itself. This distinction clarifies that the appellate court’s review was focused strictly on the constitutional question regarding the police entry, not on Coleman’s guilt or innocence regarding the firearm possession.

## The Role of Body Camera Footage in Resolving Factual Disputes
A critical component of the factual background in *Coleman* is the reliance on body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues. The Seventh Circuit explicitly noted that this video record helped resolve potential factual disputes regarding the interaction at the door. Citing *United States v. Norville* and *Scott v. Harris*, the court observed that a video record of events can “evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist.” For a law student, this highlights the evidentiary weight given to objective video records in appellate review. The footage allowed the appellate court to independently assess the tone of the officer and the demeanor of Lisa Coleman, rather than relying solely on conflicting witness testimony. This visual evidence supported the district court’s finding that the consent was voluntary and not coerced, as the video showed Officer Kizer speaking calmly and Lisa responding in a manner that demonstrated her understanding of the situation. This reliance on video evidence influenced the standard of review applied, as the appellate court reviewed the district court’s factual findings for clear error, a standard that is difficult to overcome when the record is visually clear.
