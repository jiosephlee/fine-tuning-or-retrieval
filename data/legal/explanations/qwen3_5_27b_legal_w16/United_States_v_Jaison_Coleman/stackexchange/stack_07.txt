Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

### Does an initial refusal to consent vitiate a subsequent grant of consent during the same encounter?
Question:
The facts show that Lisa initially told Officer Kizer, 'I'd prefer you not' when asked to enter. After a break in conversation and further questioning, she later said 'Okay' and opened the door. The court found valid consent based on the second exchange. Does the initial refusal act as a withdrawal of consent that cannot be easily re-granted during the same continuous encounter? Or does the passage of time and the officer's new line of questioning reset the consent inquiry?
Answer:
**No, an initial refusal to consent does not automatically vitiate a subsequent grant of consent during the same encounter.**

Based on *United States v. Jaison Coleman*, 24-3051 (7th Cir. 2025), a prior refusal does not permanently bar a valid consent later in the interaction, provided the subsequent consent is voluntary under the totality of the circumstances.

### The Legal Standard
Under the Fourth Amendment, consent must be "freely and voluntarily" given. Courts evaluate this using a **totality of the circumstances** test. Relevant factors include whether the person was advised of their rights, the duration of detention, and whether physical coercion was used (*United States v. Figueroa-Espana*, cited in *Coleman*).

Crucially, the Seventh Circuit in *Coleman* clarified that the capacity to refuse consent is a factor that can support a finding of voluntariness for a later grant.

### Application in *Coleman*
In *Coleman*, Lisa Coleman initially told Officer Kizer, "I’d prefer you not" when asked to enter the home. However, the court found valid consent when she later said "Okay" and opened the door. The court’s reasoning directly addresses your question regarding whether the initial refusal "resets" the inquiry:

1.  **Distinct Interactions:** The opinion notes a "break in the conversation" occurred between the refusal and the grant. Lisa went inside to get shoes, and the officer checked on her before she returned to the doorway. This pause allowed for a "new line of questioning," effectively resetting the consent inquiry.
2.  **Demonstration of Capacity:** The court explicitly stated: "Lisa showed that she had the ability to deny consent to entry, as she did just that in response to Officer Kizer’s initial request." Rather than vitiating the later consent, the initial refusal demonstrated that Lisa understood she was not compelled to answer, which supported the finding that her subsequent "Okay" was voluntary.
3.  **Absence of Coercion:** The court emphasized that the second request was "unaccompanied by any coercion." Officer Kizer’s tone was described as "concerned, not someone coercing," and he asked permission again without escalating pressure despite the initial refusal.

### Conclusion
An initial refusal does not act as a permanent withdrawal of consent that cannot be re-granted. In *Coleman*, the Seventh Circuit affirmed that the passage of time, the break in conversation, and the officer's non-coercive demeanor allowed the subsequent grant to stand as valid consent. The initial refusal, in this context, actually bolstered the voluntariness of the later consent by proving the individual understood they had the right to say no.
