Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

### What defines the scope of consent for a welfare check entry?
Question:
The court held that the officers acted within the scope of Lisa's consent, which was given to check on the children's welfare. However, once inside, the officers located Coleman and conducted a protective pat-down, which eventually led to a warrant for the guns. The opinion states the scope inquiry is 'objective reasonableness' (*Florida v. Jimeno*). At what point does a welfare check entry exceed its scope? If the officers were only there for the kids, did scanning the entire home to locate the husband exceed the consent granted for a child welfare check?
Answer:
Based on the Seventh Circuit's opinion in *United States v. Jaison Coleman*, 7th Cir. (Oct. 7, 2025), scanning the home to locate the husband did **not** exceed the scope of consent granted for a child welfare check.

### The Legal Standard: Objective Reasonableness
The opinion confirms that the scope of consent is governed by the **objective reasonableness** standard established in *Florida v. Jimeno*. As the court notes, the inquiry asks:

> "what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer and the person giving consent?"

This means the scope is not defined by the officer's subjective intent, but by what a reasonable person in the consenter's position would understand the permission to cover.

### Application to Welfare Checks
In *Coleman*, the court rejected the argument that consent limited to checking on the children precluded the officers from looking for the husband. The court reasoned that the purpose of the welfare check was not merely to visually confirm the children's presence, but to ensure their safety in light of the reported domestic violence.

The opinion explicitly links the welfare of the children to the location of the alleged threat:

> Their stated purpose was to check the welfare of her children inside, including by confirming that Coleman presented no danger to them or anyone else. Doing so necessarily required the police to scan the home, locate everyone, and ask some basic questions.

### When Does the Entry Exceed Scope?
While the opinion does not provide an exhaustive list of prohibited actions, it establishes that the entry exceeds its scope only when the officers' actions go beyond what a reasonable person would understand the consent to authorize.

In this case, the court found that a "quick canvas of the home" was **necessary** to fulfill the specific purpose of the consent (verifying safety from the husband). Therefore, the boundary of the scope is defined by the **necessity of the actions to achieve the consenter's understood purpose**. If the officers had searched for evidence of a crime unrelated to the immediate safety of the children or the location of the threat, they likely would have exceeded the scope. However, locating the husband to ensure he did not pose a danger was within the objective understanding of the consent Lisa gave.
