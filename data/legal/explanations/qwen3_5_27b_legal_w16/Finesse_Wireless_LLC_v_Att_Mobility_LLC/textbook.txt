Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Case Background and Technological Context

## Identification of Parties: Finesse Wireless LLC versus AT&T Mobility and Nokia

The dispute in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* involves a patent infringement suit brought by Finesse Wireless LLC against AT&T Mobility LLC. Finesse is the owner of the patents-in-suit and initiated the litigation alleging that AT&T infringed its intellectual property rights. AT&T Mobility, a major wireless carrier, was the primary defendant accused of using infringing technology in its network. Nokia of America Corporation, the manufacturer of the specific radio equipment used by AT&T, intervened in the district court proceedings. Because Nokia manufactures the accused products, its interests were directly tied to the outcome of the infringement allegations, leading to its status as a co-appellant alongside AT&T. Understanding the relationship between these parties is essential for tracking the procedural posture: while Finesse sued AT&T for using the radios, Nokia’s involvement ensures that the manufacturer’s technical specifications and defense strategies are part of the record.

## Overview of Asserted Patents: U.S. Patent Nos. 7,346,134 and 9,548,775

The legal claims in this case center on two specific patents: U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 (the ’134 patent) and U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 (the ’775 patent). Both patents generally relate to methods for mitigating intermodulation product interference in radios. In the district court, Finesse asserted specific claims from each patent against the accused products. For the ’134 patent, the suit focused on claims 1–3. For the ’775 patent, the suit focused on claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36. These patents do not cover the radios themselves in a broad sense but rather specific methods for processing signals to reduce interference. The ’134 patent claims a method involving isolating signals of interest from interference-generating signals and canceling the interference using a computed estimate. The ’775 patent claims a method involving digitally multiplying specific signals to generate cancellation signals. Distinguishing between these two patents is critical, as the Federal Circuit analyzed the infringement evidence for each patent separately in its discussion.

## Technical Background: Passive Intermodulation (PIM) and Intermodulation Products (IMPs)

To understand the infringement allegations, a law student must grasp the technical problem the patents address: signal interference in radio communications. Radios typically avoid signal interference by operating on different frequencies. However, transmit signals can combine when they encounter physical obstacles, such as metal fences, loose cable connections, or rusted connectors. This combination creates new frequencies known as “intermodulation products” or IMPs. When these IMPs are caused by passive obstacles rather than active electronic components, they are referred to as “passive intermodulation products” or PIM. The opinion distinguishes between internal PIM, caused by mechanical issues inside the radio, and external PIM, caused by sources outside the radio. The patents-in-suit are directed toward methods to mitigate this interference, specifically by identifying and canceling out these unwanted intermodulation products so that the desired signals can be received clearly.

## The Accused Product: Nokia Radios and PIM Cancellation Features

The specific products accused of infringing the ’134 and ’775 patents are Nokia radios utilized by AT&T. Finesse alleged that AT&T infringed the patents by using the Passive Intermodulation Cancellation (PIM-C) feature embedded within these Nokia radios. The technical operation of this feature is the core of the factual dispute. During trial, Finesse’s expert relied on Nokia technical documents to demonstrate how the accused radios operate. These documents depicted the internal signal paths, including analog-to-digital converters and specific mathematical operations performed by the radio’s processing units. The infringement analysis hinges on whether the specific way the Nokia radios sample signals, isolate interference, and perform mathematical multiplications matches the limitations described in the patent claims. Because the accused product is a complex electronic device, the court’s review of the evidence involves closely examining technical diagrams and expert testimony regarding signal paths and processing steps.

## Trial Outcome: Jury Verdict of Infringement and $166 Million Damages Award

The case proceeded to a jury trial in the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas. The jury returned a verdict finding all asserted claims of both the ’134 and ’775 patents to be valid and infringed by the accused Nokia radios. Based on this finding of infringement, the jury awarded Finesse a lump-sum damages award of $166,303,391 for the remaining life of the patents. Following the verdict, the Appellants (AT&T and Nokia) filed post-trial motions, including a motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement and a motion for a new trial on damages. The district court denied all these motions. This denial of JMOL and the new trial motion constitutes the procedural posture for the current appeal. The Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction allows it to review these denials, specifically examining whether the evidence presented at trial was sufficient to support the jury’s findings or whether the law required a different result as a matter of law.

# Procedural Posture and Standards of Review

## District Court Proceedings: Denial of JMOL and New Trial Motions

To understand the Federal Circuit’s decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, one must first grasp the procedural history that brought the case to the appellate level. The litigation began when Finesse sued AT&T for patent infringement regarding the ’134 and ’775 patents, with Nokia intervening as a party. The case proceeded to a jury trial in the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas. The jury returned a verdict in favor of Finesse, finding all asserted claims valid and infringed, and awarded a lump-sum damages figure of approximately $166 million. Following this verdict, the Appellants (AT&T and Nokia) filed post-trial motions seeking relief from the jury’s findings. Specifically, they moved for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, JMOL on damages, and a new trial on damages.

The district court denied all of these motions. This denial is the critical procedural trigger for the appeal. In patent litigation, a party dissatisfied with a jury verdict often seeks JMOL under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 50, arguing that no reasonable jury could have reached the verdict based on the evidence presented. When the district court denies such a motion, the losing party may appeal that denial. In *Finesse*, the Appellants appealed the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement and the refusal to grant a new trial on damages. The Federal Circuit’s opinion addresses these specific denials, ultimately reversing the denial of JMOL and vacating the damages award because the underlying infringement finding was reversed.

## Jurisdiction and Appellate Review Standards under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1)

Before analyzing the merits of the appeal, the Federal Circuit establishes its authority to hear the case. The opinion explicitly states that the court has jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1). This statute grants the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit exclusive jurisdiction over appeals from district court decisions in civil actions arising under the patent laws of the United States. Because *Finesse* involves allegations of patent infringement and validity, it falls squarely within this statutory grant of authority.

Establishing jurisdiction is a threshold requirement for any appellate opinion. By citing § 1295(a)(1), the court confirms that it is the proper venue to review the district court’s decisions regarding the patents in question. This jurisdictional basis allows the Federal Circuit to apply its specific precedents regarding patent law while simultaneously adhering to the procedural rules of the regional circuit where the trial took place. In this case, the trial occurred in the Eastern District of Texas, which falls within the Fifth Circuit. Consequently, while the Federal Circuit applies federal patent law, it must look to Fifth Circuit law for certain procedural standards, such as the standard for reviewing a denial of JMOL.

## Standard of Review for JMOL Denials: Fifth Circuit Law Applied De Novo

A central procedural concept in this opinion is the standard of review applied to the district court’s denial of JMOL. The Federal Circuit clarifies that it reviews a district court’s denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit, which in this instance is the Fifth Circuit. The opinion cites *Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.* to establish that the Fifth Circuit reviews denials of JMOL *de novo*. This means the appellate court reviews the legal question anew, without deference to the district court’s conclusion.

However, the *de novo* review is constrained by the evidentiary standard of the regional circuit. The opinion explains that the Fifth Circuit will reverse a denial of JMOL “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” This is a highly deferential standard to the jury verdict. It requires the appellate court to assume the jury believed the evidence supporting the verdict and disbelieved evidence contradicting it. The Federal Circuit applies this rigorous standard to determine if the district court erred in letting the case go to the jury. In *Finesse*, the court uses this standard to evaluate whether the expert testimony provided sufficient evidence to support the infringement finding, ultimately concluding that the evidence did not meet this high bar.

## Substantial Evidence Standard for Jury Findings of Infringement

Distinct from the review of the district court’s JMOL denial, the Federal Circuit also reviews the jury’s factual findings directly. The opinion states that the court reviews a jury’s finding of infringement for “substantial evidence.” This standard is slightly different from the JMOL standard but is closely related. A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial. The opinion cites *Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc.* to define this standard.

In the context of *Finesse*, this standard governs the sufficiency of the evidence regarding the technical elements of the patents. The court examines whether the expert testimony, such as that provided by Dr. Wells, was sufficient for a reasonable jury to conclude that the accused radios met the claim limitations. The Federal Circuit emphasizes that when the party with the burden of proof rests its case on self-contradictory testimony, the evidence may be insufficient to satisfy the substantial evidence standard. By applying this standard, the court ensures that the jury’s verdict is grounded in reliable evidence rather than confusion or speculation. The reversal in *Finesse* was driven by the conclusion that no reasonable jury could have found infringement based on the testimony presented, thus failing the substantial evidence test.

## Claim Construction Review Standards: De Novo and Clear Error

Although the reversal in *Finesse* was based on the sufficiency of evidence regarding infringement rather than an error in claim construction, the opinion outlines the standard of review for claim construction to provide a complete procedural framework. The Federal Circuit reviews claim construction *de novo*, meaning it gives no deference to the district court’s interpretation of the patent claims. However, if the claim construction relies on underlying factual findings, those specific findings are reviewed for clear error.

This distinction is vital for law students reading patent opinions. Claim construction is a question of law, but it often depends on factual determinations, such as the understanding of a person of ordinary skill in the art or the meaning of technical terms. In *Finesse*, the court notes this standard in the introductory section of the Discussion. While the specific dispute in this case turned on whether the evidence supported the infringement finding under the claim construction already established, understanding this standard helps students recognize that if the case had turned on the meaning of a term like “three signals,” the appellate court would have reviewed that interpretation independently. The clear error standard for underlying facts ensures that factual determinations supporting the construction are not lightly overturned, while the *de novo* standard ensures legal interpretations remain uniform across the Federal Circuit.

# Infringement Analysis of the '134 Patent

This chapter examines the Federal Circuit’s reasoning regarding the infringement analysis of U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 (the ’134 patent) in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*. The court’s decision turns on the sufficiency of the evidence presented to prove that the accused device met specific claim limitations. Specifically, the Federal Circuit analyzed whether the plaintiff, Finesse, provided substantial evidence to support the jury’s finding of infringement, focusing on the testimony of its expert witness and the technical feasibility of the accused device’s operation. Through this analysis, the chapter illustrates how appellate courts review factual findings of infringement and the critical role of clear, non-contradictory expert testimony in patent litigation.

## Claim Limitation 1[a]: Requirement to Sample 'Signals of Interest' and 'Interference Generating Signals'

To understand the Federal Circuit’s reversal, students must first grasp the specific claim limitation at issue. The ’134 patent claims a method for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference. Claim 1 is representative of the asserted claims and includes a limitation labeled 1[a], which requires "over-sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals." This limitation establishes a structural and functional requirement for the accused radio receiver. As the plaintiff, Finesse bore the burden of proving that the accused radios sampled a passband of received signals that included both the "signals of interest" and the "interference generating signals."

The legal significance of this limitation lies in the timing and source of the signals. The claim requires the receiver to capture both types of signals during the initial sampling process. If the "interference generating signals" are not present at the moment of sampling, the accused device cannot literally meet the claim limitation. In this case, the accused device utilized a Passive Intermodulation Cancellation (PIM-C) feature. The dispute centered on whether the radio’s receiver actually sampled the interference-generating signals as they existed in the passband, or if those signals were generated later in the processing chain. The Federal Circuit’s analysis confirms that for a finding of literal infringement, the accused device must perform the steps recited in the claim in the manner described, and the evidence must support that the device physically or logically meets those requirements.

## Expert Testimony Analysis: Dr. Wells' Mapping of Signals to RF ADC Inputs

In patent infringement cases involving complex technical devices, expert testimony is often the primary vehicle for connecting the claim language to the accused product’s operation. In *Finesse Wireless*, Finesse relied on the testimony of its infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells. Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios infringed the asserted claims by relying on a Nokia technical document that depicted the radios' operation. His testimony was crucial in mapping the abstract claim terms to the specific components within the accused radio.

Dr. Wells identified a radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC) as the component performing the sampling. He consistently testified that the "signals of interest" corresponded to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and that the "interference generating signals" corresponded to the "modeled PIM path." Both of these were depicted in a dashed red path in the technical document. Dr. Wells opined that the RF ADC sampled both the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path to create the bit stream required by Claim 1[a]. This mapping was the foundation of Finesse’s case at trial. The jury accepted this testimony, finding all asserted claims of the ’134 patent infringed. However, the Federal Circuit’s review highlights that the mere existence of expert testimony is insufficient; the testimony must be clear, consistent, and logically support the conclusion that the claim limitations are met.

## The Timing Contradiction: Sampling Signals Generated Downstream

The core of the Federal Circuit’s reasoning regarding the ’134 patent involves a technical contradiction exposed during cross-examination. Appellants’ counsel challenged Dr. Wells’ mapping by pointing out a fundamental timing issue within the accused radio’s architecture. The counsel argued that the RF ADC could not sample the "modeled PIM path" because that path is generated downstream of the RF ADC, after sampling has already occurred. Logically, signals cannot be sampled before they are generated.

During cross-examination, Dr. Wells attempted to address this contradiction by pivoting his testimony. He suggested there were "two signals on that red path" because of inputs labeled "x1" and "x2" in the diagram. However, Dr. Wells failed to clearly map these new inputs, x1 and x2, onto the specific claim terms of "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." Instead, he continued to testify that the red path represented the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path. He never clarified that x1 and x2 were the actual inputs being sampled by the RF ADC in a way that satisfied the claim limitation. This created a situation where the expert’s testimony contained a logical impossibility (sampling a downstream signal) without providing a coherent alternative explanation that aligned with the claim language. The Federal Circuit noted that Dr. Wells never testified that x1 and x2 corresponded to the claimed signals, leaving the jury without a clear technical basis to find that the sampling limitation was met.

## Evaluation of Conflicting Testimony and Lack of Substantial Evidence

The Federal Circuit applied the standard of review for a denial of Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) to evaluate the sufficiency of this testimony. Reviewing under the law of the Fifth Circuit, the court examines whether the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that reasonable jurors could not arrive at a contrary conclusion. Additionally, the court reviews the jury’s finding of infringement for substantial evidence, meaning a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party based on the evidence presented.

The court concluded that the evidence was insufficient because it rested on self-contradictory testimony. Finesse attempted to argue on appeal that Dr. Wells was laboring under a misimpression and that his references to the red path actually meant x1 and x2. The Federal Circuit rejected this, noting that Dr. Wells continued to testify that the modeled PIM path was the "interference generating signal" even after being confronted with the timing issue. The court cited *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, noting that when a party with the burden of proof rests its case on an expert’s self-contradictory testimony, the evidence may be insufficient to satisfy the substantial evidence standard. Because Dr. Wells never clarified his contradictory statements or provided a clear mapping of the inputs to the claim limitations, the Federal Circuit determined that no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios sampled the required interference generating signals.

## Reversal of JMOL Denial for the '134 Patent Claims

Based on the insufficiency of the evidence regarding Claim 1[a], the Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’134 patent. The court held that the jury’s infringement verdict was not supported by substantial evidence. This holding underscores the importance of precise claim construction and the necessity for expert testimony to directly and consistently support every element of the claim.

The reversal of the JMOL denial had significant procedural consequences for the case. Because the Federal Circuit reversed the finding of infringement for the ’134 patent, the basis for the damages award associated with that patent was eliminated. Consequently, the court vacated the damages award. This outcome serves as a reminder to law students that even if a jury returns a verdict, appellate courts will scrutinize the evidentiary record to ensure that the verdict is supported by more than speculation or confused testimony. In *Finesse Wireless*, the failure to provide clear evidence that the accused device met the sampling limitation resulted in a complete reversal of the infringement finding for the ’134 patent.

# Infringement Analysis of the '775 Patent

## Claim Construction: Interpretation of 'Three Signals S1, S2, and S3'

To understand the Federal Circuit’s analysis of the '775 patent, one must first grasp how the court evaluated the scope of the claim language regarding signal inputs. Claim 1 of the '775 patent requires a method for performing interference cancellation that involves generating intermodulation product cancellation signals based on three signals, designated S1, S2, and S3. During the trial, the district court construed the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean that the signals must be “separately identifiable,” but not necessarily “unique.” This construction was significant because it determined whether the accused radios, which appeared to utilize only two distinct input signals, could still infringe the patent. The district court reached this construction by examining intrinsic evidence, which did not limit the signals to unique inputs, and by crediting inventor testimony as extrinsic evidence. The inventor testified that S1, S2, and S3 represented signals generally and could be the same signal or different signals. For the law student, this illustrates the interplay between intrinsic and extrinsic evidence in claim construction. While the intrinsic record is primary, extrinsic evidence such as inventor testimony can support a broader interpretation if it clarifies the meaning without contradicting the specification. In this case, the construction allowed for the possibility that fewer than three unique physical signals could satisfy the claim limitation, provided they were separately identifiable within the process.

## Prosecution History Analysis: Rejection of Surrender of Claim Scope Argument

A critical legal concept in this section of the opinion is prosecution history estoppel, specifically regarding the surrender of claim scope during patent prosecution. The Appellants argued that Finesse had surrendered the right to assert infringement against devices using only two unique input signals. This argument was based on the prosecution history of the '775 patent. Originally, the claims recited digitally multiplying “two or three signals.” During prosecution, the Patent Office rejected these claims as obvious over a prior art reference called *Filipovic*, which taught generating cancellation signals using two inputs. To overcome this rejection, Finesse amended the claims to recite multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven multiplications. The Appellants contended that this amendment constituted a clear disavowal of claim scope over products using only two unique input signals.

The Federal Circuit rejected this argument, applying the standard that a surrender of claim scope requires a “clear and unambiguous” disavowal. The court reasoned that Finesse’s amendment was primarily intended to distinguish the claimed method from *Filipovic* by emphasizing that the invention mitigated passive intermodulation products (PIMs), whereas *Filipovic* was limited to active IMPs. While Finesse did add the limitation of three signals, the court found this did not rise to the level of disavowing products with two unique input signals. Instead, the court held that the amendment merely required three separately identifiable signals, consistent with the district court’s construction. This portion of the opinion teaches students that not every amendment made to overcome a rejection results in a surrender of scope; the intent and clarity of the disavowal must be evident from the record.

## Claim Limitation 1[d]: Requirement for Seven Specific Multiplications

While the court rejected the surrender argument regarding the number of input signals, it found a fatal flaw in the evidence regarding the mathematical operations required by the claims. Claim 1, limitation 1[d], specifically requires generating an n-th order intermodulation product cancellation signal by digitally multiplying and filtering the three signals in seven specific combinations. The claim lists these combinations explicitly: S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3. For a finding of infringement, the accused device must perform these seven specific multiplications. This limitation is a concrete, structural requirement of the patented method. In patent infringement cases, particularly those involving technical methods, the plaintiff must prove that the accused device performs each step or limitation of the claim. Here, the requirement was not merely to use three signals, but to process them through a specific set of seven mathematical operations. The court emphasized that the evidence presented at trial failed to demonstrate that the accused radios performed this specific sequence of seven multiplications, distinguishing this technical deficiency from the broader claim construction issues discussed previously.

## Evidence Deficiencies: Failure to Map Accused Device Operations to Claim Multiplications

The Federal Circuit’s reversal hinged on the insufficiency of the evidence presented to the jury regarding the seven multiplications. Finesse’s infringement expert, Dr. Wells, relied on a Nokia technical document to prove that the accused radios performed the claimed operations. However, the court found that this document disclosed modulus and complex conjugate operations rather than the seven specific multiplications listed in the claim. The document only listed three distinct multiplications involving the signals x1 and x2. There was no evidence in the record showing that these multiplications were repeated or arranged in a way that satisfied the requirement for seven distinct operations as recited in limitation 1[d]. Furthermore, Dr. Wells did not testify at trial how the three distinct multiplications shown in the document mapped onto the seven claimed multiplications.

On appeal, Finesse attempted to clarify this mapping, arguing that the Nokia documents showed ten multiplications and that a subset corresponded to the seven claimed ones. The court rejected this new explanation because it was not presented to the jury. In patent litigation, a party cannot cure evidentiary deficiencies at the appellate stage with new theories or mappings that were not available to the trier of fact. Additionally, Finesse’s proposed mapping was flawed because it required mapping claim signals onto complex conjugates and modulus values that did not align with the plain language of the claim. This section highlights the importance of presenting a clear, complete chain of evidence at trial. An expert opinion that relies on a document without explicitly mapping its contents to the claim limitations leaves a gap that cannot be filled on appeal.

## Reversal of JMOL Denial for the '775 Patent Claims

The final outcome of the '775 patent analysis was the reversal of the district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement. The Federal Circuit reviews the denial of JMOL de novo, but reviews the underlying jury finding of infringement for substantial evidence. A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence only if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented. Because the evidence failed to show that the accused radios performed the seven specific multiplications required by limitation 1[d], the court concluded that no reasonable jury could have found infringement. The confusion in the expert testimony and the lack of a clear mapping between the accused device’s operations and the claim language rendered the jury’s verdict unsupported. Consequently, the court reversed the denial of JMOL for the asserted claims of the '775 patent. This holding reinforces the principle that the burden of proof remains on the patentee to demonstrate that every claim limitation is met by the accused device, and ambiguous or contradictory expert testimony cannot sustain a verdict when the technical requirements are not clearly satisfied by the record.

# Damages Award and Final Disposition

## Standard of Review for Denial of New Trial on Damages

In the final section of the opinion, the Federal Circuit addresses the procedural posture regarding the damages award. The Appellants had moved for a judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on damages or, in the alternative, a new trial on damages, both of which the district court denied. When reviewing a district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial, the Federal Circuit does not apply a uniform national standard but instead looks to the law of the regional circuit. In this case, because the appeal originated from the Eastern District of Texas, the court applied Fifth Circuit law. Under Fifth Circuit precedent, the review of a denial of a new trial is conducted for an abuse of discretion. This standard is deferential, meaning the appellate court will not overturn the district court’s decision unless the lower court’s ruling was arbitrary, capricious, or clearly erroneous. The court cites *Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.* to establish this framework, noting that while the standard is set, the substantive analysis of the damages evidence often becomes secondary if the underlying liability finding is overturned.

## Logical Dependency: Impact of Infringement Reversal on Damages

A critical legal principle illustrated in this section is the dependency of damages on a valid finding of infringement. In patent litigation, a damages award is predicated on the conclusion that the defendant infringed the patent claims. If the court determines that there was no infringement, there is no legal basis for awarding monetary relief to the patent holder. In *Finesse Wireless*, the Federal Circuit had already concluded in the preceding section that the jury’s finding of infringement was not supported by substantial evidence for either the ’134 or ’775 patents. Consequently, the court reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL on noninfringement. Because the infringement verdict was reversed, the damages award became legally untenable. The court explicitly states, “Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we vacate the damages award.” This reasoning demonstrates that the appellate court did not need to reach the merits of the Appellants’ arguments regarding the calculation of damages or the district court’s discretion in denying the new trial motion. Without a finding of infringement, the damages question is moot.

## Decision to Vacate the Lump-Sum Damages Award

The jury in the district court had originally awarded Finesse a lump sum of $166,303,391 in damages for the remaining life of the asserted patents. This award was based on the jury’s finding that the accused radios infringed the claims. However, upon the Federal Circuit’s reversal of the infringement finding, the legal foundation for this specific monetary award collapsed. To “vacate” a judgment or award means to set it aside or annul it, rendering it void. By vacating the damages award, the Federal Circuit effectively erased the $166 million judgment from the record. This action ensures that the patent holder does not retain a windfall based on a liability finding that the appellate court has determined was unsupported by the evidence. The vacatur applies to the entire damages award because the infringement reversal covered all asserted claims of both patents, leaving no portion of the award that could stand independently.

## Final Judgment: Reversal and Vacation

The operative portion of the opinion, often found at the very end before the costs section, contains the court’s final disposition of the case. Here, the Federal Circuit issued a single, consolidated order: “REVERSED AND VACATED.” This directive communicates the ultimate outcome of the appeal to the lower court and the parties. “Reversed” indicates that the appellate court disagrees with the district court’s decision to deny the JMOL of noninfringement and has overturned that ruling. “Vacated” indicates that the damages award associated with the infringement finding is nullified. This disposition typically remands the case back to the district court for further proceedings consistent with the appellate opinion. In the context of this opinion, since the evidence was found insufficient to support infringement, the practical effect is likely that the case ends in favor of the Appellants regarding liability, as the patent holder cannot proceed with a damages claim without a valid infringement verdict.

## Allocation of Costs to Defendants-Appellants

The final element of the opinion addresses the allocation of litigation costs. At the conclusion of the decision, the court orders, “Costs to Defendants-Appellants.” In federal appellate practice, costs generally include expenses such as the preparation of the record, printing of briefs, and other necessary filing fees. The prevailing party on appeal is typically entitled to an award of costs. In this instance, because the Appellants (AT&T and Nokia) succeeded in reversing the district court’s judgment on noninfringement and vacating the damages award, they are considered the prevailing party for the purposes of this appeal. This allocation is a standard procedural conclusion that follows the substantive decision, ensuring that the party who successfully challenged the lower court’s judgment is reimbursed for certain appellate expenses. It serves as the final administrative step in closing the appellate record for this specific docket.
