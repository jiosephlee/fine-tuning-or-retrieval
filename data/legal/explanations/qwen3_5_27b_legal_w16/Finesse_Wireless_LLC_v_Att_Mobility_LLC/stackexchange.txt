Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Why does the Federal Circuit apply regional circuit law when reviewing a denial of JMOL in a patent case?
Question:
In *Finesse Wireless v. AT&T*, the court explicitly states it reviews the district court's denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit (here, the Fifth Circuit), yet it reviews claim construction de novo under Federal Circuit law. I am confused about the jurisdictional split here. Since patent law is supposed to be national to promote uniformity, why does the standard for reviewing a procedural motion like JMOL depend on the regional circuit, while substantive patent issues do not? Does this create a risk of forum shopping based on the regional circuit's JMOL standards?
Answer:
### Answer

Based on the opinion in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit applies regional circuit law to the denial of a Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) because it treats the standard of review for that procedural motion as governed by the law of the regional circuit from which the appeal originates, whereas it treats claim construction as a substantive question of patent law subject to *de novo* review.

The opinion explicitly distinguishes between these two standards in the **Discussion** section. Regarding the JMOL motion, the court states:

> "We review a district court’s denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit, here the Fifth Circuit. *Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016)."

In contrast, regarding the interpretation of the patent claims, the court states:

> "We review... claim construction *de novo* and any underlying factual findings supporting the district court’s construction for clear error. *Wi-LAN*, 811 F.3d at 461."

**The Doctrinal Split**
The opinion demonstrates that this split is a matter of established precedent (citing *Wi-LAN*) rather than an ad hoc decision. The Federal Circuit applies regional circuit law to the JMOL standard because it concerns the sufficiency of the evidence and the jury's role, which are procedural matters tied to the district court's jurisdiction. However, the Federal Circuit reserves *de novo* review for claim construction because it involves the interpretation of federal patent law, which the Federal Circuit oversees to ensure national uniformity.

**Regarding Forum Shopping**
While the opinion does not explicitly discuss the policy implications of forum shopping regarding JMOL standards, it applies the regional standard consistently regardless of the substantive patent outcome. The court notes that while the *standard* for reviewing the JMOL denial comes from the regional circuit (the Fifth Circuit's *de novo* standard), the Federal Circuit still applies its own test for the underlying factual finding:

> "We review a jury’s finding of infringement for substantial evidence. *Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc.*, 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022)."

Thus, while the procedural hurdle for overturning a JMOL denial varies by region, the Federal Circuit retains oversight over whether the jury's infringement finding is supported by substantial evidence under Federal Circuit law.

### When does contradictory expert testimony fail to support a jury's infringement finding under the substantial evidence standard?
Question:
The court reversed the JMOL denial for the '134 patent because the plaintiff's expert testimony regarding the 'interference generating signals' was self-contradictory. The expert initially mapped the signals one way, then pivoted when cross-examined, but never clearly clarified the mapping. I understand that appellate courts review jury findings for 'substantial evidence,' but at what point does contradictory testimony render the evidence insufficient? Is there a specific test for when an expert's confusion or change of course negates the jury's ability to rely on that testimony?
Answer:
**Short Answer:**
Under *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, contradictory expert testimony fails to support a jury’s infringement finding when the party with the burden of proof rests its case on that testimony without providing a clear, detailed explanation for the contradiction. Specifically, if the expert pivots on cross-examination but fails to clearly map the accused device’s features to the claim limitations, the evidence is insufficient to meet the **substantial evidence standard**.

***

### The Substantial Evidence Standard
The Federal Circuit reviews jury findings of infringement for **substantial evidence**. As stated in *Finesse*, this standard is met if:

> “A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.”

However, the court clarified that this deference does not apply when the evidence itself is fundamentally unreliable due to expert confusion.

### When Contradictory Testimony Becomes Insufficient
The opinion establishes that contradictory testimony negates a jury verdict when the expert’s change of course leaves the claim elements unproven. The court identified three specific factors that rendered Dr. Wells’ testimony insufficient in this case:

1.  **Failure to Clarify the Mapping:** Dr. Wells initially mapped the claimed “interference generating signals” to the “modeled PIM path.” When confronted that the modeled PIM path is generated *after* sampling (making infringement impossible), he pivoted to suggest the signals were inputs labeled “x1 and x2.” Crucially, **he never testified that x1 and x2 corresponded to the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.”**
2.  **Persistent Contradiction:** Even after the pivot, the expert continued to testify that the modeled PIM path *was* the interference generating signal. The court noted:
    > “Dr. Wells continued to testify the modeled PIM path is the ‘interference generating signals’ and never clarified he meant x1 and x2 when he was saying DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path.”
3.  **Reliance on Inference:** The plaintiff attempted to salvage the testimony by piecing together statements found ninety pages apart in the record. The court rejected this, stating:
    > “There is nothing clear about Dr. Wells’ testimony... Finesse makes that inference by piecing together parts of Dr. Wells’ testimony over ninety pages apart in the record.”

### The Governing Rule
The court relied on *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.* to articulate the rule for when this confusion defeats a verdict:

> “When the party with the burden of proof, such as Finesse, rests its case on an expert’s self-contradictory testimony, we may conclude the evidence is insufficient to satisfy that standard.”

The court concluded that a “confusing change of course” where the expert offers “no clear or detailed explanation” is not sufficient to support a jury verdict.

### Practical Takeaway for Law Students
Contradictory expert testimony does not automatically mandate a JMOL. However, under *Finesse*, it will support a reversal if:
*   The contradiction goes to the heart of a claim limitation (e.g., whether a signal is sampled before or after generation).
*   The expert fails to provide a **clear, explicit mapping** between the accused device and the claim terms after the contradiction is raised.
*   The plaintiff is forced to rely on inferences drawn from disjointed testimony rather than a coherent expert opinion.

In short, if the expert’s testimony is so confused that a reasonable jury cannot determine *which* feature of the accused device meets the claim limitation, the substantial evidence standard is not met.

### Does amending a claim from 'two or three signals' to 'three signals' constitute clear and unambiguous disavowal of scope?
Question:
Regarding the '775 patent, the court analyzed whether Finesse surrendered claim scope over two signals when it amended the claims from 'two or three signals' to 'three signals' to overcome an obviousness rejection. The court held this did not rise to the level of 'clear and unambiguous disavowal.' I'm struggling with the prosecution history estoppel standard here. If an amendment is made specifically to distinguish prior art that used two signals, doesn't that imply an intent to surrender the two-signal scope? What distinguishes this from a situation where an amendment clearly disavows scope?
Answer:
**No, based on *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, amending a claim from "two or three signals" to "three signals" does not automatically constitute a clear and unambiguous disavowal of two-signal scope.**

In the '775 patent dispute, the Federal Circuit explicitly rejected the argument that Finesse surrendered scope over two signals merely by narrowing the claim language to overcome an obviousness rejection.

### The Legal Standard: Clear and Unambiguous Disavowal

Under the doctrine of prosecution history estoppel (and claim construction), a patentee may be deemed to have surrendered claim scope only if the prosecution history contains a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal of that scope. As the court notes, citing *Cont'l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019), an amendment alone is insufficient to establish surrender unless the record clearly indicates the patentee intended to give up specific territory.

### Application in *Finesse*

In this case, the original claims recited "digitally multiplying two or three signals." The Examiner rejected this as obvious over the *Filipovic* reference, which taught generating cancellation signals using two inputs. Finesse amended the claims to recite "three signals S1, S2 and S3."

Appellants argued this amendment surrendered scope over products using only two signals. The Federal Circuit disagreed for two primary reasons grounded in the opinion:

1.  **Nature of the Limitation:** The court found the amendment required "three separately identifiable signals" but did not require "any specific number of unique input signals." Consequently, a system using two unique signals (where one is copied to create a third identifiable signal) could still fall within the scope.
2.  **Reason for Amendment:** The court observed that Finesse overcame the rejection not solely by narrowing the signal count, but by explaining that *Filipovic* was "limited to mitigating active IMPs" whereas the claimed method addressed "passive IMPs."

Because the record did not show a clear intent to exclude all two-signal embodiments—specifically where the signals are separately identifiable despite not being unique—the amendment did not rise to the level of disavowal.

### Distinguishing Intent from Disavowal

Your question highlights a common tension: *If an amendment is made specifically to distinguish prior art that used two signals, doesn't that imply an intent to surrender the two-signal scope?*

*Finesse* clarifies that while the amendment may have been made to distinguish prior art, the **record must explicitly show the surrender** to meet the "clear and unambiguous" standard. Here, the court noted that the patentee's explanation focused on the *type* of interference (active vs. passive) rather than an absolute exclusion of two-signal configurations.

### Key Takeaway for Prosecution History Estoppel

When analyzing prosecution history estoppel, do not assume an amendment implies surrender of all scope not explicitly recited. Instead, look for:
*   **Explicit Statements:** Did the patentee argue in the record that the prior art covers the surrendered scope and that the amendment is necessary to exclude it?
*   **Claim Construction:** Does the amended language (e.g., "separately identifiable" vs. "unique") inherently exclude the prior art without disavowing broader interpretations?

In *Finesse*, the court held that the amendment to "three signals" was not a clear disavowal of two-signal embodiments, even though the prior art taught two inputs. The reversal on infringement in this case was ultimately based on the failure to perform the required **seven multiplications**, not the signal count limitation.

### How does the Federal Circuit distinguish between 'separately identifiable' and 'unique' signals in claim construction?
Question:
The district court construed the limitation 'three signals S1, S2 and S3' as 'separately identifiable' but not 'unique,' relying partly on inventor testimony. The Federal Circuit agreed. I am confused about the semantic difference in this context. If the signals are 'separately identifiable,' doesn't that imply they are distinct entities? How can they be the same signal (non-unique) yet still be separately identifiable in the context of a mathematical multiplication limitation? How much weight does inventor testimony carry when it seems to contradict the plain language of listing three distinct variables?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit affirmed the district court’s construction that "three signals S1, S2 and S3" must be **"separately identifiable"** but need not be **"unique."** While this distinction can seem semantic, the opinion clarifies that it turns on the difference between the *inputs required for a mathematical operation* versus the *source identity of those signals*.

### 1. The Distinction: "Separately Identifiable" vs. "Unique"

The confusion often arises from assuming that listing three variables (S1, S2, S3) inherently demands three distinct physical signals. The Federal Circuit rejected this, agreeing with the district court that the claim requires three separate *instances* of signals for the calculation, but not three *unique* sources.

*   **Separately Identifiable:** This refers to the mathematical process. The claim requires the system to treat three distinct inputs for the multiplication operations (e.g., S1×S1×S2). As the district court found, "using two signals, plus a copy of one of those signals, was sufficient to meet its construction of three separately identifiable signals." Essentially, the system must be able to identify and process three operands, even if two of those operands are copies of the same underlying signal.
*   **Unique:** This would require the signals to originate from different sources or contain different data. The court held the claim does *not* require this. The Federal Circuit agreed that the limitation "does not require any specific number of unique input signals."

Therefore, a device can infringe by using two physical signals (e.g., x1 and x2) where one is duplicated to satisfy the "three signals" requirement, provided the three inputs are separately identifiable within the algorithm.

### 2. The Weight of Inventor Testimony

You asked how much weight inventor testimony carries when it seems to contradict the plain language of listing three variables. In this opinion, the inventor testimony was treated as **extrinsic evidence** used to resolve ambiguity in the claim language, and the Federal Circuit upheld its use.

*   **Extrinsic Evidence:** The district court "credited inventor testimony... that 'S1, S2 and S3' represent signals generally and can be the same signal or different signals."
*   **No Contradiction of Plain Language:** The Federal Circuit did not find the testimony contradicted the plain language because the plain language ("three signals") did not explicitly specify "unique." The court noted that while Finesse amended the claims to recite "three signals" instead of "two or three signals," this amendment "does not rise to the level of 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal of claim scope over products... with two unique input signals."
*   **Standard of Review:** The Federal Circuit reviews claim construction *de novo*. By stating "We agree" with the district court's reliance on the testimony to define the scope, the opinion signals that inventor testimony is permissible to clarify the scope of a term when the intrinsic record (specification and prosecution history) does not clearly limit the term to "unique" signals.

### 3. Important Context: Construction vs. Infringement

It is crucial to note that while the Federal Circuit agreed with the claim construction (allowing non-unique signals), the court **reversed the infringement verdict** on other grounds.

The court held that "no reasonable jury could have found the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications." Even though the signals could be non-unique, the accused device failed to perform the specific mathematical operations required by the claim. This highlights that a favorable claim construction (like "not unique") is necessary but not sufficient for infringement; the accused device must still perform every limitation, including the specific multiplications.

### Summary
*   **Separately Identifiable:** The algorithm must process three distinct inputs/operands.
*   **Not Unique:** Those operands can be copies of the same underlying signal source.
*   **Inventor Testimony:** Valid extrinsic evidence to clarify that "three signals" does not mean "three unique signals," provided it does not contradict clear disavowal in the prosecution history.

*Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, No. 2024-1039 (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025).

### Can a patentee cure a lack of evidence mapping claim limitations to the accused device through post-hoc clarification on appeal?
Question:
In the '775 patent analysis, the court noted that the expert did not testify at trial how the three distinct multiplications in the accused device mapped to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse attempted to clarify this mapping in their appellate brief. The court rejected this, stating the evidence was never presented to the jury. I want to understand the boundary here: If the evidence exists in the record (e.g., in documents) but wasn't explicitly mapped by the expert at trial, is it ever permissible for the appellate court to consider that mapping to find substantial evidence, or must the mapping be explicitly made by the expert during the trial?
Answer:
**No, a patentee cannot cure a lack of expert testimony mapping claim limitations to the accused device through post-hoc clarification on appeal.**

Based on *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, 2024-1039 (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025), the Federal Circuit held that if the necessary mapping evidence was not presented to the jury at trial, the appellate court cannot consider new mappings or clarifications submitted in appellate briefs to sustain a jury verdict.

### The Legal Standard: Substantial Evidence
In patent infringement cases, a jury’s finding of infringement is reviewed for **substantial evidence**. As stated in *Finesse*, citing *Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc.*:

> “A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.”

This standard requires that the evidence supporting the verdict must have been **presented at trial**. The appellate court reviews the record as it stood before the jury; it does not act as a fact-finder to construct new theories of infringement from existing documents if the expert failed to articulate them during trial.

### Application to the '775 Patent
The specific issue you raised regarding the '775 patent illustrates this boundary clearly. The claim required the accused device to perform **seven specific multiplications** of three signals.

*   **The Trial Evidence:** The infringement expert, Dr. Wells, testified that the accused device performed only **three distinct multiplications** based on a Nokia technical document. Crucially, Dr. Wells **did not testify** at trial how those three distinct multiplications mapped onto the seven claimed multiplications.
*   **The Appellate Attempt:** On appeal, Finesse attempted to clarify this mapping in its brief, arguing that the documents supported the seven multiplications.
*   **The Court’s Ruling:** The Federal Circuit rejected this argument. The court explicitly stated:
    > “Finesse attempts to clarify the mapping on appeal, but that evidence was never presented to the jury.”

Because the expert failed to make the explicit connection during trial, the court found that “no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications.” The court further noted that Finesse’s appellate mapping was flawed because it required mapping signals to complex conjugates and moduli that were not clearly established in the trial testimony.

### Practical Takeaway
For a law student, the boundary is strict: **The mapping must be made by the expert during trial.**

If the evidence exists in the record (e.g., in technical documents) but the expert did not explicitly map the claim limitations to the accused device’s features during testimony, that gap cannot be filled on appeal. As *Finesse* demonstrates, the appellate court will not piece together testimony from disparate parts of the record or accept new logical bridges constructed in appellate briefs to cure a lack of foundational expert testimony. The verdict must be supported by evidence actually presented to the fact-finder.

### Why did the Federal Circuit vacate the damages award rather than remand for a new damages trial after reversing the infringement finding?
Question:
The court reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims and subsequently vacated the damages award. I would have expected the court to remand for a new trial on damages, perhaps to see if there was infringement on non-asserted claims or if a different damages calculation was possible. Why is the procedural remedy simply vacatur when the underlying infringement finding is overturned? Does the vacatur imply that no damages are recoverable at all, or is it just clearing the slate for a potential retrial on infringement?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** The Federal Circuit vacated the damages award because the award was legally predicated on the jury's finding of infringement for *all* asserted claims. Since the Court reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for every single claim that supported the damages calculation, there was no remaining legal basis for the award. A remand for a new damages trial would be futile without a finding of liability.

**Detailed Explanation**

The procedural remedy of vacatur in this context flows directly from the Court's disposition of the infringement claims. As the opinion states in the **Damages** section:

> "Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we vacate the damages award."

This sentence establishes the causal link: the damages award cannot stand independently of the infringement findings. The jury awarded a lump sum for the "remaining life of the ’134 and ’775 patents" based on its finding that the accused radios infringed the specific claims asserted by Finesse.

**1. Why Vacatur Instead of Remand for Damages?**
A remand for a new damages trial is designed to recalculate compensation when liability exists but the amount is erroneous. Here, the Court found that the evidence was insufficient to support infringement on *any* of the asserted claims.
*   For the **'134 patent**, the Court held there was no substantial evidence that the accused radios sampled the required "interference generating signals."
*   For the **'775 patent**, the Court held no reasonable jury could find the accused radios performed the "seven claimed multiplications."

Because the Court reversed the infringement finding for **all** asserted claims, the predicate for the damages award was eliminated. There is no damages calculation to be performed if there is no infringement.

**2. Does Vacatur Imply No Damages Are Recoverable?**
For the claims at issue in this litigation, yes. The vacatur signifies that the $166,303,391 award is nullified because the liability supporting it has been overturned. The opinion does not discuss non-asserted claims or potential future litigation; it strictly addresses the claims asserted in this action. Since the Court reversed the JMOL denial for "all asserted claims," the damages award tied to those claims is extinguished.

**3. Is This Clearing the Slate for a Retrial on Infringement?**
Based on the standard applied in the opinion, a retrial on infringement is unlikely. The Court reviews the denial of JMOL under the Fifth Circuit standard, reversing only if "reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion."

By reversing the denial of JMOL, the Court concluded that the evidence was legally insufficient to support the jury's verdict ("the jury’s infringement verdict is not supported by substantial evidence"). Unlike a grant of a new trial, which implies the evidence was conflicting or the trial was flawed but a retrial might yield a different result, a JMOL reversal indicates that no reasonable jury could find infringement based on the evidence presented. Therefore, the vacatur effectively ends the case on these claims rather than resetting the slate for a second attempt at proving infringement.
