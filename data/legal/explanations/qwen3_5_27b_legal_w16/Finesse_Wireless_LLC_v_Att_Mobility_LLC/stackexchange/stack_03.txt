Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Does amending a claim from 'two or three signals' to 'three signals' constitute clear and unambiguous disavowal of scope?
Question:
Regarding the '775 patent, the court analyzed whether Finesse surrendered claim scope over two signals when it amended the claims from 'two or three signals' to 'three signals' to overcome an obviousness rejection. The court held this did not rise to the level of 'clear and unambiguous disavowal.' I'm struggling with the prosecution history estoppel standard here. If an amendment is made specifically to distinguish prior art that used two signals, doesn't that imply an intent to surrender the two-signal scope? What distinguishes this from a situation where an amendment clearly disavows scope?
Answer:
**No, based on *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, amending a claim from "two or three signals" to "three signals" does not automatically constitute a clear and unambiguous disavowal of two-signal scope.**

In the '775 patent dispute, the Federal Circuit explicitly rejected the argument that Finesse surrendered scope over two signals merely by narrowing the claim language to overcome an obviousness rejection.

### The Legal Standard: Clear and Unambiguous Disavowal

Under the doctrine of prosecution history estoppel (and claim construction), a patentee may be deemed to have surrendered claim scope only if the prosecution history contains a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal of that scope. As the court notes, citing *Cont'l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019), an amendment alone is insufficient to establish surrender unless the record clearly indicates the patentee intended to give up specific territory.

### Application in *Finesse*

In this case, the original claims recited "digitally multiplying two or three signals." The Examiner rejected this as obvious over the *Filipovic* reference, which taught generating cancellation signals using two inputs. Finesse amended the claims to recite "three signals S1, S2 and S3."

Appellants argued this amendment surrendered scope over products using only two signals. The Federal Circuit disagreed for two primary reasons grounded in the opinion:

1.  **Nature of the Limitation:** The court found the amendment required "three separately identifiable signals" but did not require "any specific number of unique input signals." Consequently, a system using two unique signals (where one is copied to create a third identifiable signal) could still fall within the scope.
2.  **Reason for Amendment:** The court observed that Finesse overcame the rejection not solely by narrowing the signal count, but by explaining that *Filipovic* was "limited to mitigating active IMPs" whereas the claimed method addressed "passive IMPs."

Because the record did not show a clear intent to exclude all two-signal embodiments—specifically where the signals are separately identifiable despite not being unique—the amendment did not rise to the level of disavowal.

### Distinguishing Intent from Disavowal

Your question highlights a common tension: *If an amendment is made specifically to distinguish prior art that used two signals, doesn't that imply an intent to surrender the two-signal scope?*

*Finesse* clarifies that while the amendment may have been made to distinguish prior art, the **record must explicitly show the surrender** to meet the "clear and unambiguous" standard. Here, the court noted that the patentee's explanation focused on the *type* of interference (active vs. passive) rather than an absolute exclusion of two-signal configurations.

### Key Takeaway for Prosecution History Estoppel

When analyzing prosecution history estoppel, do not assume an amendment implies surrender of all scope not explicitly recited. Instead, look for:
*   **Explicit Statements:** Did the patentee argue in the record that the prior art covers the surrendered scope and that the amendment is necessary to exclude it?
*   **Claim Construction:** Does the amended language (e.g., "separately identifiable" vs. "unique") inherently exclude the prior art without disavowing broader interpretations?

In *Finesse*, the court held that the amendment to "three signals" was not a clear disavowal of two-signal embodiments, even though the prior art taught two inputs. The reversal on infringement in this case was ultimately based on the failure to perform the required **seven multiplications**, not the signal count limitation.
