Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Can a patentee cure a lack of evidence mapping claim limitations to the accused device through post-hoc clarification on appeal?
Question:
In the '775 patent analysis, the court noted that the expert did not testify at trial how the three distinct multiplications in the accused device mapped to the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse attempted to clarify this mapping in their appellate brief. The court rejected this, stating the evidence was never presented to the jury. I want to understand the boundary here: If the evidence exists in the record (e.g., in documents) but wasn't explicitly mapped by the expert at trial, is it ever permissible for the appellate court to consider that mapping to find substantial evidence, or must the mapping be explicitly made by the expert during the trial?
Answer:
**No, a patentee cannot cure a lack of expert testimony mapping claim limitations to the accused device through post-hoc clarification on appeal.**

Based on *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, 2024-1039 (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025), the Federal Circuit held that if the necessary mapping evidence was not presented to the jury at trial, the appellate court cannot consider new mappings or clarifications submitted in appellate briefs to sustain a jury verdict.

### The Legal Standard: Substantial Evidence
In patent infringement cases, a jury’s finding of infringement is reviewed for **substantial evidence**. As stated in *Finesse*, citing *Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc.*:

> “A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.”

This standard requires that the evidence supporting the verdict must have been **presented at trial**. The appellate court reviews the record as it stood before the jury; it does not act as a fact-finder to construct new theories of infringement from existing documents if the expert failed to articulate them during trial.

### Application to the '775 Patent
The specific issue you raised regarding the '775 patent illustrates this boundary clearly. The claim required the accused device to perform **seven specific multiplications** of three signals.

*   **The Trial Evidence:** The infringement expert, Dr. Wells, testified that the accused device performed only **three distinct multiplications** based on a Nokia technical document. Crucially, Dr. Wells **did not testify** at trial how those three distinct multiplications mapped onto the seven claimed multiplications.
*   **The Appellate Attempt:** On appeal, Finesse attempted to clarify this mapping in its brief, arguing that the documents supported the seven multiplications.
*   **The Court’s Ruling:** The Federal Circuit rejected this argument. The court explicitly stated:
    > “Finesse attempts to clarify the mapping on appeal, but that evidence was never presented to the jury.”

Because the expert failed to make the explicit connection during trial, the court found that “no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications.” The court further noted that Finesse’s appellate mapping was flawed because it required mapping signals to complex conjugates and moduli that were not clearly established in the trial testimony.

### Practical Takeaway
For a law student, the boundary is strict: **The mapping must be made by the expert during trial.**

If the evidence exists in the record (e.g., in technical documents) but the expert did not explicitly map the claim limitations to the accused device’s features during testimony, that gap cannot be filled on appeal. As *Finesse* demonstrates, the appellate court will not piece together testimony from disparate parts of the record or accept new logical bridges constructed in appellate briefs to cure a lack of foundational expert testimony. The verdict must be supported by evidence actually presented to the fact-finder.
