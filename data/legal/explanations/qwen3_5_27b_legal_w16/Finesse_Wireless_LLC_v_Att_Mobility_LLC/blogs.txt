Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

## When Expert Testimony Defies Physics: The Substantial Evidence Standard in Patent Infringement

In a decision issued on September 24, 2025, the Federal Circuit delivered a stark reminder to patent litigators: a jury verdict cannot stand when the expert testimony supporting it relies on technical impossibilities. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, Chief Judge Moore, writing for the panel, reversed the denial of a judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) and vacated a $166 million damages award. The court’s reasoning underscores a critical boundary in patent infringement litigation—expert testimony must not only be credible but must also align with the physical realities of the accused device. When an expert maps claim limitations to signals that cannot physically exist at the time of sampling, the evidence fails the substantial evidence test, regardless of the jury’s verdict.

The case turned on two patents covering methods for mitigating intermodulation product interference in radios. The core of the Federal Circuit’s reversal regarding the ’134 patent hinged on a fundamental timeline error in the plaintiff’s expert testimony. Claim 1 required the accused device to sample a passband of received signals that included both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Finesse’s expert, Dr. Wells, testified that the radio’s analog-to-digital converter (ADC) sampled these signals. However, Dr. Wells mapped the “interference generating signals” to a “modeled PIM path” that was generated downstream of the ADC, after sampling had already occurred. As the court noted, signals cannot be sampled before they are generated. When confronted with this contradiction during cross-examination, Dr. Wells pivoted to suggest the ADC sampled two other signals, labeled x1 and x2, but he never clearly mapped x1 and x2 to the specific claim terms. The Federal Circuit held that this confusing change of course was insufficient to support the jury’s infringement finding.

This decision reinforces the rigor with which appellate courts review JMOL motions in patent cases, particularly when the law of the regional circuit—in this instance, the Fifth Circuit—requires a high bar for overturning a verdict. While the Federal Circuit reviews JMOL denials de novo and jury findings for substantial evidence, the court emphasized that substantial evidence cannot be built on contradictory testimony. Citing *Johns Hopkins University v. Datascope Corp.*, the panel noted that when a party with the burden of proof rests its case on an expert’s self-contradictory testimony, reasonable jurors cannot be expected to find infringement. The court refused to allow Finesse to piece together a coherent theory on appeal from testimony scattered across ninety pages of the trial record. If the expert’s mapping defies the architecture of the accused device, the evidence points so strongly toward noninfringement that no reasonable jury could conclude otherwise.

The panel applied similar scrutiny to the ’775 patent, where the claim required seven specific digital multiplications of three signals. The evidence presented at trial only demonstrated three distinct multiplications, and the plaintiff failed to provide a mapping showing how the accused device performed the remaining four. Although Finesse attempted to argue on appeal that the device performed ten multiplications, the court rejected this as new evidence that was never presented to the jury. This aspect of the opinion highlights the importance of ensuring that the evidence presented at trial fully bridges the gap between the claim language and the accused technology. Post-hoc rationalizations cannot cure deficiencies in the trial record, particularly when the mathematical or technical requirements of the claim are not met by the evidence before the jury.

For practitioners, *Finesse Wireless* offers a cautionary tale regarding the preparation of infringement experts. The opinion suggests that courts are increasingly unwilling to let juries infer infringement from ambiguous or physically inconsistent testimony. Experts must provide clear, consistent mappings that account for the actual signal flow and timing within the accused device. If a claim limitation requires a signal to exist at a specific point in a process, the expert must prove that the accused device generates that signal at that specific point. Furthermore, the reversal of the damages award serves as a reminder that the foundation of any damages calculation is a valid infringement finding. Without substantial evidence supporting infringement, the damages award collapses with it. Ultimately, this decision signals that the Federal Circuit will not hesitate to reverse verdicts where the technical evidence fails to meet the basic requirements of logic and physics.



# Prosecution History Disavowal: Did Amending 'Two or Three' to 'Three' Surrender Claim Scope?

In the high-stakes arena of telecommunications patent litigation, the difference between a $166 million verdict and a complete reversal often comes down to the precise interpretation of a single claim term. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, decided by the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit on September 24, 2025, the court overturned a massive damages award against AT&T and Nokia. While the final judgment turned on the substantial evidence supporting infringement, the court’s analysis of prosecution history disavowal offers a critical lesson for patent practitioners regarding the amendment of claim language. Specifically, the court addressed whether amending a claim from "two or three signals" to "three signals" constituted a clear and unambiguous surrender of claim scope over devices using only two unique input signals.

## The Claim Construction Battle

The dispute centered on U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775, which relates to methods for mitigating intermodulation product interference in radios. Claim 1 of the ’775 patent requires generating interference cancellation signals by digitally multiplying "three signals S1, S2 and S3" in seven specific multiplications. During prosecution, the original claims recited "digitally multiplying two or three signals." The Patent Office examiner rejected this language as obvious over a prior art reference known as *Filipovic*, which taught generating cancellation signals using two inputs. To overcome this rejection, Finesse amended the claims to require "three signals S1, S2 and S3" in seven multiplications.

On appeal, Appellants argued that this amendment amounted to a surrender of claim scope. Their position was that by changing "two or three" to "three," Finesse clearly disavowed any device that utilized only two unique input signals. If accepted, this argument would have narrowed the patent’s scope significantly, potentially excluding the accused Nokia radios. The district court had previously construed "three signals S1, S2 and S3" to be "separately identifiable" but not necessarily "unique," allowing for the possibility that two physical signals could satisfy the limitation if they were treated as three separate inputs in the mathematical process.

## The Federal Circuit’s Analysis

Writing for the court, Chief Judge Moore rejected the argument that the amendment constituted a disavowal of two-signal devices. The Federal Circuit applied the strict standard established in *Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, which requires that a disavowal of claim scope be "clear and unambiguous." The court reasoned that while Finesse did amend the claims to overcome the *Filipovic* rejection, the rationale for the amendment did not explicitly narrow the scope to exclude two unique input signals.

The court noted that Finesse overcame the rejection in part by distinguishing *Filipovic* based on the type of interference addressed—active versus passive intermodulation—rather than solely on the number of signals. Consequently, the amendment to "three signals" did not rise to the level of a clear disavowal regarding the number of unique input sources. Instead, the court affirmed the district court’s construction that the claims require three "separately identifiable" signals. This distinction is vital: it means a device using two physical signals could still infringe if the mathematical process treats them as three separate inputs, provided the other claim limitations are met.

It is important to note, however, that while Finesse prevailed on this specific claim construction point, the court still reversed the infringement verdict. The Federal Circuit found that no reasonable jury could conclude the accused radios performed the seven specific multiplications required by the claim, regardless of the signal count. Thus, the patentee won the battle over claim scope but lost the war on infringement due to a lack of evidence regarding the mathematical operations.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

This decision reinforces the Federal Circuit’s consistent reluctance to find prosecution history estoppel or disavowal based solely on claim amendments absent explicit statements of surrender. The ruling serves as a reminder that the motivation behind an amendment matters as much as the amendment itself. If an applicant amends a claim to distinguish prior art on a specific technical feature—such as the type of interference—without explicitly stating that they are abandoning a different feature—such as the number of input signals—the court will likely not infer a disavowal.

For patent practitioners, this underscores the importance of drafting arguments during prosecution that clearly delineate the scope of the amendment. If an applicant wishes to narrow the scope to exclude two-signal devices, they must articulate that surrender explicitly in the record. Relying on the mere fact of an amendment to create a presumption of disavowal is insufficient under current Federal Circuit precedent. Conversely, for accused infringers, this case highlights the difficulty of using prosecution history to limit claim scope when the patentee’s arguments were focused on other distinctions, such as the nature of the problem solved.

Ultimately, *Finesse Wireless* illustrates the high threshold required to invoke prosecution history disavowal. While the court clarified that the amendment did not strip the patentee of the ability to assert claims against two-signal devices, the reversal on infringement grounds demonstrates that claim construction victories do not guarantee success at trial. Litigants must still ensure that the evidence presented at trial maps precisely to every element of the claim, regardless of how broadly the terms are construed. As patent disputes continue to involve increasingly complex technical architectures, the interplay between claim language, prosecution history, and evidentiary support will remain a central battleground in federal patent litigation.



# The Burden of Proof for Algorithmic Claims: Mapping Specific Multiplications to Accused Devices

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit delivered a stark reminder to patent holders litigating complex technology: general functionality is not enough to prove infringement. On September 24, 2025, Chief Judge Moore led the court in reversing a district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) and vacating a staggering $166 million damages award. The decision underscores the rigorous evidentiary standards required when asserting infringement of algorithmic or method claims, particularly where the patentee must map specific mathematical operations to an accused device. While the case involved two patents, the court’s analysis of the ’775 patent offers a critical lesson for practitioners in the software and telecommunications sectors regarding the burden of proof for literal infringement.

## The Factual Landscape and the Verdict

The dispute centered on patents owned by Finesse Wireless, which relate to methods for mitigating passive intermodulation (PIM) interference in radios. Finesse sued AT&T and Nokia, alleging that Nokia radios used by AT&T infringed the patents by utilizing a PIM cancellation feature. A jury in the Eastern District of Texas found all asserted claims valid and infringed, resulting in a lump-sum damages award for the remaining life of the patents. However, on appeal, the Federal Circuit determined that the evidence presented at trial was insufficient to support the jury’s verdict on either patent. The court reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for both the ’134 and ’775 patents, effectively nullifying the damages award. This reversal highlights the appellate court’s willingness to scrutinize expert testimony when it fails to align with the specific limitations of the asserted claims.

## The Seven Multiplications Limitation

The core of the Federal Circuit’s reasoning regarding the ’775 patent focused on a specific claim limitation requiring the accused device to perform seven distinct third-order multiplications of three signals. Claim 1 of the ’775 patent recites a method for generating intermodulation product cancellation signals by digitally multiplying and filtering three signals, S1, S2, and S3, in a specific pattern involving seven combinations (such as S1×S1×S2 and S1×S2×S3). During trial, Finesse’s expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, relied on a Nokia technical document to argue that the accused radios performed these operations. However, the document disclosed only three distinct multiplications involving signals x1 and x2, utilizing modulus and complex conjugate operations rather than the specific multiplications recited in the claim.

The Federal Circuit found that no reasonable jury could have concluded the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications based on this evidence. Dr. Wells did not testify how the three distinct multiplications shown in the Nokia document mapped onto the seven claimed multiplications. While Finesse attempted to clarify this mapping on appeal, arguing that the device performed ten multiplications in total, the court noted that this evidence was never presented to the jury. Furthermore, Finesse failed to explain which subset of the ten multiplications corresponded to the seven claimed ones. The court emphasized that because the document did not disclose multiplying x1 and x2 directly, but rather their complex conjugates and modulus, the mapping was flawed. This gap in evidence was fatal to the infringement claim, as the patentee bore the burden of proving every limitation was met.

## Analytical Perspective on Expert Testimony

This decision illustrates the danger of relying on expert testimony that is either contradictory or insufficiently detailed. In the ’134 patent analysis, the court similarly found that Dr. Wells’ testimony was confusing and self-contradictory regarding whether the accused device sampled interference-generating signals before they were generated. The Federal Circuit cited *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, noting that when a party rests its case on an expert’s self-contradictory testimony, the evidence may be insufficient to satisfy the substantial evidence standard. In the ’775 patent context, the court reinforced that post-hoc explanations provided during briefing cannot cure the absence of evidence presented at trial. The jury’s verdict must be supported by evidence introduced during the trial phase, not by new theories advanced on appeal. This strict adherence to the trial record ensures that the opposing party has a fair opportunity to cross-examine and rebut the evidence, maintaining the integrity of the adversarial process.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The ruling in *Finesse Wireless* carries significant implications for patent drafting and litigation strategy. For patentees, the decision signals that claiming specific algorithmic steps requires equally specific evidence of implementation. Broad assertions that an accused device performs a similar function or achieves a similar result will not suffice if the claim recites precise mathematical operations. Litigators must ensure their experts provide a clear, step-by-step mapping of the accused device’s operations to the claim limitations, avoiding reliance on technical documents that require significant inference to connect to the patent language. Additionally, the court’s rejection of the argument that Finesse surrendered claim scope over two signals during prosecution clarifies that amendment history must be clear and unambiguous to disavow scope. However, even where prosecution history estoppel is not found, the intrinsic limitations of the claim combined with the extrinsic evidence of the accused device must align perfectly.

Ultimately, *Finesse Wireless* serves as a cautionary tale for patent holders in the wireless and software industries. The Federal Circuit’s reversal demonstrates that the substantial evidence standard is not a rubber stamp for jury verdicts, particularly in technically complex cases where the claim language demands precision. By vacating the damages award and granting JMOL of noninfringement, the court has reinforced the principle that the burden of proof remains firmly on the patentee to demonstrate that the accused device literally practices every element of the claimed invention. As technology continues to integrate complex algorithms into hardware, this decision will likely encourage more rigorous claim construction and more precise expert testimony in future infringement litigation.
