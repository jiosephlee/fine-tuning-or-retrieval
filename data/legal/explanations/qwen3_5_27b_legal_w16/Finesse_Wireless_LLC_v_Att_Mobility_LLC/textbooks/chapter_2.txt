Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 2: # Procedural Posture and Standards of Review

## District Court Proceedings: Denial of JMOL and New Trial Motions

To understand the Federal Circuit’s decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, one must first grasp the procedural history that brought the case to the appellate level. The litigation began when Finesse sued AT&T for patent infringement regarding the ’134 and ’775 patents, with Nokia intervening as a party. The case proceeded to a jury trial in the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas. The jury returned a verdict in favor of Finesse, finding all asserted claims valid and infringed, and awarded a lump-sum damages figure of approximately $166 million. Following this verdict, the Appellants (AT&T and Nokia) filed post-trial motions seeking relief from the jury’s findings. Specifically, they moved for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement, JMOL on damages, and a new trial on damages.

The district court denied all of these motions. This denial is the critical procedural trigger for the appeal. In patent litigation, a party dissatisfied with a jury verdict often seeks JMOL under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 50, arguing that no reasonable jury could have reached the verdict based on the evidence presented. When the district court denies such a motion, the losing party may appeal that denial. In *Finesse*, the Appellants appealed the district court’s refusal to grant JMOL of noninfringement and the refusal to grant a new trial on damages. The Federal Circuit’s opinion addresses these specific denials, ultimately reversing the denial of JMOL and vacating the damages award because the underlying infringement finding was reversed.

## Jurisdiction and Appellate Review Standards under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1)

Before analyzing the merits of the appeal, the Federal Circuit establishes its authority to hear the case. The opinion explicitly states that the court has jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1). This statute grants the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit exclusive jurisdiction over appeals from district court decisions in civil actions arising under the patent laws of the United States. Because *Finesse* involves allegations of patent infringement and validity, it falls squarely within this statutory grant of authority.

Establishing jurisdiction is a threshold requirement for any appellate opinion. By citing § 1295(a)(1), the court confirms that it is the proper venue to review the district court’s decisions regarding the patents in question. This jurisdictional basis allows the Federal Circuit to apply its specific precedents regarding patent law while simultaneously adhering to the procedural rules of the regional circuit where the trial took place. In this case, the trial occurred in the Eastern District of Texas, which falls within the Fifth Circuit. Consequently, while the Federal Circuit applies federal patent law, it must look to Fifth Circuit law for certain procedural standards, such as the standard for reviewing a denial of JMOL.

## Standard of Review for JMOL Denials: Fifth Circuit Law Applied De Novo

A central procedural concept in this opinion is the standard of review applied to the district court’s denial of JMOL. The Federal Circuit clarifies that it reviews a district court’s denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit, which in this instance is the Fifth Circuit. The opinion cites *Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.* to establish that the Fifth Circuit reviews denials of JMOL *de novo*. This means the appellate court reviews the legal question anew, without deference to the district court’s conclusion.

However, the *de novo* review is constrained by the evidentiary standard of the regional circuit. The opinion explains that the Fifth Circuit will reverse a denial of JMOL “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” This is a highly deferential standard to the jury verdict. It requires the appellate court to assume the jury believed the evidence supporting the verdict and disbelieved evidence contradicting it. The Federal Circuit applies this rigorous standard to determine if the district court erred in letting the case go to the jury. In *Finesse*, the court uses this standard to evaluate whether the expert testimony provided sufficient evidence to support the infringement finding, ultimately concluding that the evidence did not meet this high bar.

## Substantial Evidence Standard for Jury Findings of Infringement

Distinct from the review of the district court’s JMOL denial, the Federal Circuit also reviews the jury’s factual findings directly. The opinion states that the court reviews a jury’s finding of infringement for “substantial evidence.” This standard is slightly different from the JMOL standard but is closely related. A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial. The opinion cites *Apple Inc. v. Wi-LAN Inc.* to define this standard.

In the context of *Finesse*, this standard governs the sufficiency of the evidence regarding the technical elements of the patents. The court examines whether the expert testimony, such as that provided by Dr. Wells, was sufficient for a reasonable jury to conclude that the accused radios met the claim limitations. The Federal Circuit emphasizes that when the party with the burden of proof rests its case on self-contradictory testimony, the evidence may be insufficient to satisfy the substantial evidence standard. By applying this standard, the court ensures that the jury’s verdict is grounded in reliable evidence rather than confusion or speculation. The reversal in *Finesse* was driven by the conclusion that no reasonable jury could have found infringement based on the testimony presented, thus failing the substantial evidence test.

## Claim Construction Review Standards: De Novo and Clear Error

Although the reversal in *Finesse* was based on the sufficiency of evidence regarding infringement rather than an error in claim construction, the opinion outlines the standard of review for claim construction to provide a complete procedural framework. The Federal Circuit reviews claim construction *de novo*, meaning it gives no deference to the district court’s interpretation of the patent claims. However, if the claim construction relies on underlying factual findings, those specific findings are reviewed for clear error.

This distinction is vital for law students reading patent opinions. Claim construction is a question of law, but it often depends on factual determinations, such as the understanding of a person of ordinary skill in the art or the meaning of technical terms. In *Finesse*, the court notes this standard in the introductory section of the Discussion. While the specific dispute in this case turned on whether the evidence supported the infringement finding under the claim construction already established, understanding this standard helps students recognize that if the case had turned on the meaning of a term like “three signals,” the appellate court would have reviewed that interpretation independently. The clear error standard for underlying facts ensures that factual determinations supporting the construction are not lightly overturned, while the *de novo* standard ensures legal interpretations remain uniform across the Federal Circuit.
