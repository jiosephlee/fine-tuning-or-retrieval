Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### How does the passage of time affect the validity of FOIA declarations under Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
In Section II, the district court initially denied summary judgment because the Hardy declaration was 'potentially stale' due to the significant passage of time. Later, the Bender declaration was accepted as an update. I'm confused about the procedural requirement here. Does the government have a continuous burden to update declarations in FOIA litigation, or is the initial declaration sufficient unless challenged? What specific triggers the need for an updated declaration when an investigation spans several years?
Answer:
**Short Answer:** The government does not have an automatic, continuous statutory burden to update declarations in FOIA litigation. However, under Exemption 7(A), the government must prove that enforcement proceedings are **"pending or reasonably anticipated"** at the time the court rules. If a significant passage of time creates doubt about whether proceedings remain active, the court may require an updated declaration to cure the "staleness" of the initial evidence.

### The Legal Standard: Pending Proceedings
As explained in **Section IV.B** of *Farahi v. FBI*, Exemption 7(A) protects records only if their disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." The D.C. Circuit emphasizes that these proceedings must be **"pending or reasonably anticipated"** at the time of the litigation.

While the government is entitled to deference regarding its law enforcement determinations (**Section III**), it bears the burden of showing the exemption applies (**Section III**). If the initial declaration is made years before the court's decision, the government must demonstrate that the proceedings have not concluded in the interim.

### The Trigger for an Updated Declaration
In *Farahi*, the trigger for the updated declaration was not a statutory requirement for continuous updates, but rather the **district court's discretion** to ensure the evidence was current.

*   **Initial Declaration:** The FBI submitted the Hardy declaration in October 2019/2020 supporting its motion for summary judgment.
*   **The "Staleness" Issue:** In November 2022, the district court denied summary judgment because the Hardy declaration was **"potentially stale" due to the significant passage of time** (**Section II**). The court needed to confirm that enforcement proceedings were *still* pending, not just that they were pending in 2019.
*   **The Update:** The FBI then submitted the Bender declaration in 2023. The appellate court clarified that this declaration was not required to establish pendency "from scratch." Instead, it served **"only to confirm that nothing had changed while its initial summary-judgment motion was pending"** (**Section IV.B**).

### Conclusion
The opinion establishes that an initial declaration is sufficient unless the passage of time undermines the factual basis for the exemption. The government's burden is to show that proceedings are currently pending. If a court finds the initial declaration too old to reliably prove current pendency, it will order an update (as the district court did in *Farahi*). The updated declaration need only confirm the status remains unchanged, building upon the foundational facts established in the initial declaration.

### Does an ongoing criminal investigation qualify as an 'enforcement proceeding' under FOIA Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
In Section IV.B, the court states that 'ongoing criminal investigations count as proceedings for these purposes,' citing CREW. I thought Exemption 7(A) required a formal proceeding like a trial, indictment, or administrative hearing. Can you clarify the doctrinal distinction between an 'investigation' and an 'enforcement proceeding' in this context? At what point does an investigation become 'reasonably anticipated' enough to trigger the exemption?
Answer:
**Yes. Under D.C. Circuit precedent, an ongoing criminal investigation qualifies as an "enforcement proceeding" for purposes of FOIA Exemption 7(A).**

You are correct that Exemption 7(A) requires the government to show that disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." However, as the court explains in *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, the term "enforcement proceedings" is not limited to formal judicial actions like trials, indictments, or administrative hearings.

### The Legal Standard: "Pending or Reasonably Anticipated"

In Section IV.B, the court clarifies the scope of "enforcement proceedings." Relying on *Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Washington v. DOJ* (*CREW*), 746 F.3d 1082 (D.C. Cir. 2014), the court states:

> "We have held that this provision requires the proceedings to be 'pending or reasonably anticipated.' ... Ongoing criminal investigations count as 'proceedings' for these purposes."

This directly addresses your concern about formal proceedings. The doctrinal distinction is not between an "investigation" and a "proceeding," but rather whether the investigation is sufficiently active to be considered "pending or reasonably anticipated."

### When Does an Investigation Become "Reasonably Anticipated"?

To trigger the exemption, the government does not need to prove that an indictment is imminent. Instead, it must demonstrate that the investigation is active enough that a future case is concrete.

The court cites *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54 (D.C. Cir. 2008), to define this threshold:

> "[S]o long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies."

Therefore, the point at which an investigation triggers the exemption is when:
1.  The investigation is **ongoing** (actively gathering evidence); and
2.  There is a **concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding** (a possible future case) that would be harmed by disclosure.

### Proving the Existence of Proceedings

Finally, regarding your question about the burden of proof: the government does not need to provide detailed evidence of the future case's timeline. As noted in Section IV.B, the government may satisfy its burden "simply by attesting to their 'existence.'"

In *Farahi*, the court accepted the FBI's declarations confirming the investigation remained ongoing as sufficient proof that a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" continued to exist, even without a specific indictment date.

**Summary:** An investigation qualifies as an enforcement proceeding under Exemption 7(A) once it is actively gathering evidence for a possible future case. A formal trial or indictment is not required.

### What is the standard for 'reasonably segregable' when exempt information is interspersed throughout a document?
Question:
In Section V, the court cites Mead Data Cent. to say that if non-exempt material is 'interspersed line-by-line,' it isn't reasonably segregable. I'm confused about how this applies to a massive file like Farahi's 10,750 pages. Does the 'information' vs. 'words' distinction mean agencies can withhold entire documents just because some words are exempt? Where is the line between 'inextricably intertwined' and 'reasonably segregable'?
Answer:
**The standard for segregability turns on whether the non-exempt portions constitute "meaningful information" independent of the exempt material, rather than simply whether individual words can be redacted.**

Based on *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, here is how the court defines the line between "inextricably intertwined" and "reasonably segregable" when exempt information is interspersed throughout a document.

### 1. The "Information vs. Words" Distinction
You asked whether agencies can withhold entire documents just because some words are exempt. The *Farahi* opinion clarifies that the focus of FOIA is on **information**, not the physical text.

In Section V, the court relies on *Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force* to explain that "the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down." Consequently, the fact that "individual words or phrases in the requested records might be disclosed without harming the FBI’s interests" is "neither here nor there." If redacting the exempt words leaves behind text that does not convey meaningful information, the agency is not required to release it.

### 2. The Standard for Interspersed Material
Regarding your question on how this applies to massive files where exempt material is mixed in: the court cites the "line-by-line" standard.

In Section V, the opinion states that if a small amount of non-exempt material is randomly "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," that material is not likely to be "reasonably segregable." The sheer volume of the file (10,750 pages) does not alter this standard. Instead, the court looks at the structure of the content. If the non-exempt portions are so fragmented that they cannot be separated without destroying the context or utility of the information, segregation is not required.

### 3. Where the Line is Drawn
The line between "inextricably intertwined" and "reasonably segregable" is drawn at the threshold of **meaningful information**.

*   **Inextricably Intertwined:** The court notes that agencies may withhold records in their entirety "when nonexempt portions 'are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.'" *Juarez v. DOJ*.
*   **Reasonably Segregable:** The agency must take "reasonable steps" to segregate, but only if doing so yields releasable information.

In *Farahi*, the district court concluded that "no meaningful information was reasonably segregable" based on the FBI's ex parte submission. The appellate court affirmed, noting that the district court found a "detailed justification" for why the protected records could not be further segregated.

### Summary
Under *Farahi*, the standard is substantive, not mechanical. An agency is not required to release non-exempt words if, when isolated from exempt material, they do not constitute meaningful information. The line is crossed when the exempt and non-exempt portions are so mixed that segregation would not result in the release of usable information.

### When is a court permitted to rely on ex parte declarations to rule on segregability in FOIA cases?
Question:
In Section V, the court affirms the district court's reliance on Bender's ex parte declaration for the segregability analysis. Farahi argued this handicapped him, but the court said the problem is 'inherent' in cases where ex parte is the only way to decide without disclosure. Under what specific circumstances is the 'ex parte' process allowed to override the plaintiff's ability to challenge the withholding? Is there a limit to how much weight a court can give to sealed submissions?
Answer:
Based on the D.C. Circuit’s opinion in *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, a court is permitted to rely on *ex parte* declarations to rule on segregability when such a process is the **only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.**

While the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) generally requires agencies to segregate and release nonexempt portions of records, the court in *Farahi* clarified the specific circumstances under which *ex parte* review overrides a plaintiff’s ability to challenge the withholding, as well as the substantive limits on the weight given to those submissions.

### When *Ex Parte* Review Is Permitted
The court affirmed the district court’s reliance on an *ex parte* declaration for the segregability analysis under two primary conditions:

1.  **Necessity to Avoid Disclosure:** The *ex parte* process is allowed when it is the only mechanism to resolve the dispute without revealing the confidential information at issue. As the court noted, citing *Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs.*, the problem of handicapping the plaintiff is “inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an *ex parte* declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.”
2.  **Procedural Forfeiture:** In *Farahi*, the court noted that the plaintiff did not oppose the district court’s acceptance of *ex parte* declarations. Consequently, the plaintiff could not challenge the reliance on them later. While not a substantive limit on the doctrine itself, the opinion suggests that a plaintiff’s failure to object to the *ex parte* procedure at the district court level may forfeit the right to challenge it on appeal.

### Limits on the Weight Given to Sealed Submissions
While the court may rely on sealed submissions without placing its analysis of them on the public record, the weight given to those submissions is not unlimited. The declaration must still meet specific evidentiary standards:

*   **Reasonable Specificity:** A court may rely on government affidavits only if they show with “reasonable specificity” why withheld documents cannot be further segregated (*Juarez v. DOJ*). In *Farahi*, the court found the FBI satisfied this burden because the *ex parte* submission provided a “detailed justification” for why protected records were not reasonably segregable.
*   **Inextricably Intertwined Standard:** The agency may withhold records in their entirety only when nonexempt portions are “inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.” If nonexempt material is merely randomly “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” it is not considered “reasonably segregable” (*Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force*). The court in *Farahi* applied this standard to reject the argument that individual words or phrases could be disclosed without harm.
*   **Deference with Scrutiny:** While the FBI’s decision is entitled to deference, particularly in national security contexts, the declarations must establish a rational nexus to law enforcement duties (*Campbell v. DOJ*). The court in *Farahi* credited the FBI’s confirmation that proceedings remained ongoing, but only because the *ex parte* declaration supplemented and updated earlier public declarations that had already established the existence of the investigation.

### Summary
In short, under *Farahi*, the *ex parte* process overrides a plaintiff’s ability to challenge segregability when public review would compromise the secrecy the exemption is designed to protect. However, the court must still ensure the agency’s *ex parte* declaration meets the standard of “reasonable specificity” and demonstrates that nonexempt information is inextricably intertwined with exempt material before withholding the entire record. The court is not required to explain its analysis of the sealed material on the public record.

### What level of deference is given to the FBI's determination that records were 'compiled for law enforcement purposes'?
Question:
In Section IV.A, the court mentions the FBI is entitled to 'a fair measure of deference' but also requires a 'rational nexus' between the investigation and a law enforcement duty. How does a court balance this deference against the requirement that the agency establish a connection to a security risk or violation of law? Is the burden of proof higher for national security cases compared to standard law enforcement records?
Answer:
**Answer**

Based on *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, the court applies a **"fair measure of deference"** to the FBI’s determination that records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes," but this deference is not absolute. The agency must still satisfy a specific evidentiary threshold to establish a connection between the investigation and its duties.

Here is how the court balances this deference against the "rational nexus" requirement, and how national security factors into the analysis:

**1. The Standard of Deference**
Under Exemption 7(A), the FBI’s decision to invoke the exemption is entitled to deference because the agency "specializes in law enforcement" (*Farahi*, Section III). Specifically regarding the threshold requirement that records be "compiled for law enforcement purposes," the court grants a **"fair measure of deference"** (*Farahi*, Section IV.A).

**2. The "Rational Nexus" Limitation**
Deference does not allow the agency to make conclusory assertions without support. To overcome the plaintiff's challenge, the FBI’s declarations must establish:
*   A rational **"nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties;"** and
*   A **"connection between an 'individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.'"** (*Farahi*, Section IV.A, quoting *Campbell* and *Pratt*).

In *Farahi*, the court found this burden met because the declarations explained the records were compiled for investigations into national security and terrorism violations, supported by the plaintiff’s own concessions regarding his ties to designated terrorist organizations (*Farahi*, Section IV.A).

**3. National Security Considerations**
While the **burden of proof** remains on the government to show an exemption applies regardless of the subject matter (*Farahi*, Section III), the **level of deference** is heightened in national security contexts. The opinion states that deference is **"particularly appropriate for records implicating national security"** and that the court has "consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated" (*Farahi*, Section III).

**Conclusion**
The court balances these interests by requiring the agency to provide specific details (via public or ex parte declarations) establishing the rational nexus, while giving significant weight to the agency’s expertise and assertions when national security or counterterrorism is involved. In *Farahi*, the combination of detailed declarations and the context of the investigation allowed the FBI to satisfy the "rational nexus" requirement despite the deferential standard.

### What constitutes 'forfeiture' of an argument on appeal in a FOIA case?
Question:
In Section IV.C, the court dismisses Farahi's objection to document categories because he 'provides no specific explanation,' citing Khine v. DHS for forfeiture. Does this mean any argument not fully briefed in the initial appellate brief is waived, or is there a specific threshold for what counts as forfeiture? How does this differ from a court simply finding an argument unpersuasive?
Answer:
**Answer**

Based on the opinion in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, forfeiture of an argument on appeal occurs when an appellant raises an objection but fails to provide a **specific explanation** supporting that objection. It is not merely a matter of brevity, but rather a failure to articulate the legal or factual basis necessary for the court to review the claim.

Here is how the court distinguishes forfeiture from an argument found unpersuasive, based on the text provided:

### 1. The Standard for Forfeiture
In Section IV.C, the court addresses Farahi’s objection to the document categories used by the FBI. While the court acknowledges the legal standard (categories must be "sufficiently distinct"), it dismisses Farahi's specific claim because he **"provides no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad."**

Citing *Khine v. DHS*, the court holds that **"Farahi accordingly forfeited his arguments on this score."** This establishes that the threshold for forfeiture is the absence of a developed argument. If an appellant asserts a category is overbroad but does not explain *how* or *why*, the court will not speculate on the deficiencies; the argument is treated as waived.

### 2. Forfeiture vs. Unpersuasive Arguments
The opinion illustrates the difference between forfeiture and an unpersuasive argument by contrasting Section IV.C with Section IV.B:

*   **Forfeiture (Section IV.C):** Farahi objected to the categories but offered no explanation. The court did not engage with the merits of whether the categories were actually overbroad; it simply declared the argument forfeited.
*   **Unpersuasive (Section IV.B):** Farahi argued that the Bender declaration was "conclusory" and that the passage of time cast doubt on the pending enforcement proceedings. Here, the court **engaged with the merits**. The court wrote, "We agree with Farahi that the Bender declaration, considered in isolation, appears surprisingly terse." However, it ultimately rejected the argument based on context and other declarations.

The distinction is procedural: if the court addresses the substance of your claim but rejects it, it was unpersuasive. If the court states you provided "no specific explanation" and moves on, it was forfeited.

### 3. Forfeiture by Inaction
The opinion also notes a related form of forfeiture in Section V regarding ex parte declarations. The court states: **"Farahi did not oppose the district court’s acceptance of ex parte declarations, so he cannot challenge the district court’s reliance on them."**

This reinforces that forfeiture is not limited to poorly briefed arguments; it also encompasses a failure to object to procedural rulings at the appropriate time.

### Summary
In *Farahi*, forfeiture is triggered when an appellant fails to bridge the gap between a general objection and a specific legal justification. To avoid forfeiture, an appellate brief must not only identify an error but provide the "specific explanation" required to allow the court to evaluate whether that error occurred.
