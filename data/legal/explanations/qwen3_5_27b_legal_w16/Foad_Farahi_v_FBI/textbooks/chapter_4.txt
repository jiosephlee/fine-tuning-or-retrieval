Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 4: # Segregability and Administrative Justifications

## The Segregability Requirement Under FOIA

Even when a federal agency identifies valid grounds for withholding records under a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) exemption, the statutory scheme does not automatically permit the agency to withhold the entire document. The FOIA imposes a distinct obligation on agencies to take “reasonable steps” to segregate and release nonexempt information. This requirement is codified in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) and reinforced by the general exemption provision in § 552(b), which mandates that any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to the requester after the deletion of exempt portions. In *Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the D.C. Circuit affirmed that this obligation remains a critical component of FOIA litigation, even in cases involving sensitive national security or law enforcement records.

In the underlying litigation, the district court found that the FBI had satisfied this burden through a supplemental ex parte submission from Joseph Bender. The court noted that the FBI provided a “detailed justification” explaining why the protected records were not reasonably segregable. On appeal, Farahi challenged this finding, arguing that the sheer volume of responsive records—some 10,750 pages and 80 CDs—implied that there must be some segregable and releasable information contained within. However, the appellate court rejected the notion that volume alone dictates segregability. The court emphasized that the statutory duty is to segregate *reasonably* segregable information, not every individual word or phrase that could theoretically be redacted. This distinction sets the stage for understanding the judicial standards applied when agencies claim that redaction is not feasible.

## The Inextricably Intertwined Standard

When an agency asserts that it cannot segregate exempt from non-exempt material, courts apply the “inextricably intertwined” standard. Under this doctrine, the government may withhold records in their entirety when nonexempt portions are so closely linked with exempt portions that they cannot be separated without compromising the exempt information. The D.C. Circuit cited *Juarez v. DOJ*, which quoted *Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force*, to establish that a court may rely on government affidavits showing with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated.

In *Farahi*, the court applied this standard to reject Farahi’s argument that the volume of records necessitated some release. Relying on *Mead*, the court explained that if a small amount of non-exempt material is randomly “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” that material is not likely to be “reasonably segregable.” The court highlighted a crucial conceptual distinction from *Mead*: the focus of the FOIA is on “information,” not simply words which the Government has written down. Consequently, while Farahi was technically correct that individual words or phrases in the requested records might be disclosed without harming the FBI’s interests, such disclosure would not constitute meaningful information release. The district court’s conclusion that no meaningful information was reasonably segregable was therefore upheld, illustrating that the inextricably intertwined standard protects the integrity of the exempt information rather than serving as a mechanical test for redaction.

## Adequacy of Agency Justifications

Beyond the specific content of the records, the adequacy of an agency’s administrative justifications plays a pivotal role in FOIA adjudications. When an agency withholds documents on a category-by-category basis, it must use categories that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each category. This standard, drawn from *CREW* and *Gallant v. NLRB*, ensures that courts can meaningfully review the agency’s decision-making process without requiring a line-by-line review of every document.

In *Farahi*, the appellant briefly objected that the government did not adequately justify how it defined the categories of documents used to explain its withholding decisions. However, the appellate court dismissed this argument on procedural grounds. The court noted that Farahi provided no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad. Citing *Khine v. DHS*, the court held that Farahi had forfeited his arguments on this score. This outcome underscores a critical procedural lesson for litigants: challenging an agency’s categorization requires specific factual or legal arguments demonstrating the overbreadth or indistinctness of the categories. Generalized objections without specific explanation will not survive appellate review, even if the agency’s burden of proof is high.

## Reliance on Ex Parte Submissions

A recurring tension in FOIA litigation involving national security or law enforcement records is the use of ex parte submissions. These sealed filings allow the government to provide details to the court that would themselves reveal the sensitive information the agency seeks to withhold. In *Farahi*, Farahi complained that the district court relied too heavily on Bender’s ex parte declaration in its segregability analysis. The appellate court addressed this concern by noting that nothing in the law required the district court to place its analysis of the ex parte declaration on the public record.

The court provided two primary reasons for upholding the district court’s reliance on the ex parte material. First, Farahi had not opposed the district court’s acceptance of ex parte declarations during the proceedings, and thus he could not challenge the reliance on them on appeal. Second, and more broadly, the court acknowledged that while ex parte declarations may handicap FOIA plaintiffs, this problem is “inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Quoting *Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs.*, the court affirmed that the necessity of protecting sensitive information sometimes requires a procedural mechanism that limits the plaintiff’s access to the government’s full reasoning. This holding reinforces the principle that in cases involving significant security concerns, the judicial process may accommodate sealed submissions to balance the public’s right to know with the government’s interest in confidentiality.
