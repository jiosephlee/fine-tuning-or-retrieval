Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 1: # Factual Background and Procedural Posture

## Farahi's Immigration Status and Terrorism Allegations

To understand the scope of the FBI’s investigation and the subsequent withholding of records, it is necessary to examine the underlying facts regarding the plaintiff, Foad Farahi. Farahi is an Iranian national who has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) initiated removal proceedings against him due to a visa overstay. Throughout these immigration proceedings, Farahi applied for various forms of relief, including asylum, withholding of removal, and protection under the Convention Against Torture.

The relevance of these immigration proceedings to the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) litigation lies in the evidence presented by the government. DHS opposed Farahi’s applications for relief by presenting documents and live testimony from an FBI agent. This testimony documented Farahi’s alleged connections to high-level terrorists. Specifically, public records from the immigration proceedings indicated allegations that Farahi was a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members, and linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists. These allegations provide the factual context for why the FBI maintained a file on Farahi and why the agency later claimed the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes related to national security and international terrorism.

## The FOIA Request and FBI Withholding

The litigation began when Farahi submitted a FOIA request seeking his FBI file. The agency responded by locating a substantial volume of potentially responsive information, totaling approximately 10,750 pages and 80 compact discs. After reviewing these materials, the FBI disclosed some records to Farahi but withheld the majority of them.

Farahi filed suit after the FBI missed the statutory deadline for responding to the request. In FOIA cases, the government bears the burden of showing that a statutory exemption applies to the withheld records. Here, the FBI asserted Exemption 7(A), which protects records compiled for law enforcement purposes if their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings. The procedural posture of the case was driven by the agency’s need to justify this withholding through sworn declarations. The FBI initially supported its position with a declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). This declaration asserted that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their release could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.

## District Court Summary Judgment Motions

FOIA cases are typically resolved through summary judgment, where the court reviews the administrative record and agency declarations to determine if an exemption applies. In October 2019, the FBI moved for summary judgment in the district court. To support this motion, the agency submitted the public Hardy declaration and moved to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte. An ex parte submission allows the government to present sensitive information to the court without disclosing it to the plaintiff, which is often necessary in cases involving national security or ongoing investigations. Farahi did not oppose this motion, and the district court granted it in October 2020.

However, the district court did not immediately rule on the merits of the exemption. In November 2022, the court addressed the summary judgment motion. While the court concluded that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes, it expressed concern regarding the timing of the FBI’s evidence. The court noted that the declarations submitted in 2019 were becoming stale given the significant passage of time. Consequently, the court ordered the FBI to update its assessment of whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated. Additionally, the court indicated it needed more information regarding segregability—the requirement that agencies release nonexempt portions of records—but could not state the reasons for this need on the public record. For these reasons, the district court denied the FBI’s initial summary judgment motion without prejudice.

## The Requirement for Updated Declarations

Following the district court’s order, the FBI submitted updated declarations and renewed its motion for summary judgment. This time, the agency provided both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who was then the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. Bender’s public declaration explained that RIDS had contacted the agent overseeing Farahi’s file, who confirmed that investigations remained ongoing. The ex parte declaration provided further details regarding the nature of the investigation and addressed the court’s concerns about segregability.

The district court granted the renewed motion, crediting the FBI’s confirmation that the relevant proceedings remained ongoing. The court also concluded, based on its review of the ex parte submission, that the FBI had adequately explained why exempt material was not reasonably segregable. This procedural history highlights a critical aspect of FOIA litigation: the government’s burden to demonstrate that an exemption applies at the time of the lawsuit, not merely when the records were originally created. The requirement for updated declarations ensures that the court’s decision rests on current facts regarding the status of enforcement proceedings. Farahi appealed the district court’s grant of summary judgment, challenging the sufficiency of the updated declarations and the segregability analysis.
