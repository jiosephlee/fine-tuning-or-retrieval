Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Heightened Deference: National Security and the Law Enforcement Purpose Prong

The Freedom of Information Act is designed to shine a light on government operations, yet its exemptions often allow agencies to keep that light off, particularly when national security is at stake. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, decided on September 30, 2025, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit reinforced just how difficult it is for individuals to pierce the veil of secrecy surrounding counterterrorism investigations. Writing for the panel, Judge Katsas affirmed the district court’s decision to uphold the FBI’s withholding of thousands of pages from Farahi’s file under Exemption 7(A). While the holding is consistent with existing precedent, the opinion offers a clear roadmap for how courts should weigh agency assertions of national security risk against the statutory mandate for transparency, particularly regarding the "law enforcement purpose" requirement.

The case matters not only for Farahi, an Iranian national involved in long-running immigration proceedings, but for the broader doctrine of FOIA exemptions in the post-9/11 era. The core tension in FOIA litigation is often between the plaintiff’s right to know and the government’s burden to justify secrecy. Here, the court navigated that tension by applying a standard of heightened deference to the FBI, specifically because the records implicated counterterrorism duties. This approach signals that when the Executive Branch invokes national security concerns, the judicial gatekeeping function will be exercised with significant restraint, provided the agency maintains a procedural paper trail that demonstrates the investigation remains active.

## The Law Enforcement Purpose Prong and Deference

To invoke Exemption 7(A), an agency must first show that records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes." While this seems like a threshold inquiry, the D.C. Circuit treated it as an area where the FBI’s judgment commands significant weight. Relying on *Campbell v. DOJ* and *Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ* (CNSS), the court reiterated that the FBI’s decision to invoke Exemption 7 is entitled to deference, which is "particularly appropriate for records implicating national security."

This deference was not blind, however. The court required a rational nexus between the investigation and the agency’s duties, as well as a connection between the individual and a potential security risk. In this instance, the court found that nexus satisfied not only through FBI declarations but through Farahi’s own litigation history. The opinion notes that Farahi had conceded in district court that the FBI investigated him for ties to foreign terrorist organizations and individuals later convicted of terrorism. Furthermore, the court looked to DHS documents from Farahi’s immigration proceedings, which cited FBI testimony regarding his connections to high-level terrorists. By cross-referencing the FOIA litigation with the parallel immigration case, the court anchored the "law enforcement purpose" prong in facts already admitted by the plaintiff. This analytical move is instructive: it suggests that a subject’s admissions in related administrative or civil proceedings can effectively bolster the government’s burden in FOIA litigation, making it harder for a plaintiff to argue that an investigation is merely pretextual.

## Pendency and the Staleness Argument

The second prong of Exemption 7(A) requires that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are "pending or reasonably anticipated." Farahi argued that the passage of time cast doubt on whether the investigation was truly ongoing, invoking the principle that vague assertions of pendency are insufficient. This is a common challenge in FOIA cases involving cold files, where plaintiffs argue that an investigation has effectively gone dormant.

The court rejected this argument by contextualizing the FBI’s updated declarations. Although the supplemental declaration from Acting Section Chief Joseph Bender was terse, the court viewed it not as a standalone proof of pendency but as an update to the more detailed initial declarations from Section Chief David Hardy. The court reasoned that the government had already established the pendency of proceedings in the public and ex parte Hardy declarations, and the Bender declaration served to confirm that nothing had changed during the litigation. This reasoning highlights a procedural advantage for agencies: once an initial showing of pendency is made with sufficient specificity, a subsequent update need not repeat that detail to survive a staleness challenge. It effectively lowers the administrative burden on the FBI to maintain exemptions over multi-year litigation periods, provided they can confirm the status quo remains unchanged.

## Segregability and the Ex Parte Dilemma

Even when records are exempt, the FOIA requires agencies to segregate and release nonexempt portions. Farahi argued that the FBI failed to justify why it withheld entire categories of documents rather than redacting sensitive lines. The court affirmed the district court’s finding that the exempt and nonexempt portions were "inextricably intertwined," a standard that allows for total withholding when the information cannot be separated without revealing the secret.

Significantly, the court upheld the district court’s reliance on ex parte declarations to make this determination. Farahi contended that the court relied too heavily on sealed submissions, but the panel noted that Farahi had not opposed the ex parte filings in the lower court. More broadly, the opinion acknowledges the inherent handicap ex parte review places on FOIA plaintiffs. Judge Katsas wrote that this problem is "inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." This validates the use of sealed materials in national security FOIA cases as a necessary evil, reinforcing the "black box" nature of these proceedings where plaintiffs must often trust the court’s review of secret evidence without seeing it themselves.

## Doctrinal Implications

The *Farahi* opinion solidifies a high bar for plaintiffs seeking access to their own files in the context of counterterrorism. By combining heightened deference on the "law enforcement purpose" prong with a flexible approach to the "pending proceedings" requirement, the D.C. Circuit has made it clear that the government’s interest in protecting ongoing investigations outweighs the general presumption of disclosure when national security is involved. The reliance on Farahi’s immigration court admissions to satisfy the nexus requirement is particularly notable, suggesting that the government can leverage a subject’s litigation strategy in one forum to defeat their transparency claims in another.

Downstream, this decision likely encourages agencies to maintain broader categories of withholding in sensitive cases, knowing that courts will accept updated, less detailed declarations as sufficient to maintain exemptions over time. For litigants, the takeaway is that challenging an FBI file on national security grounds requires more than pointing to the passage of time; it requires concrete evidence that the investigation has actually ceased, which is difficult to prove when the agency controls the relevant information. As national security investigations become increasingly complex and long-term, *Farahi* ensures that Exemption 7(A) remains a robust shield for the Executive Branch.
