Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Factual Background and Procedural Posture

## Farahi's Immigration Status and Terrorism Allegations

To understand the scope of the FBI’s investigation and the subsequent withholding of records, it is necessary to examine the underlying facts regarding the plaintiff, Foad Farahi. Farahi is an Iranian national who has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) initiated removal proceedings against him due to a visa overstay. Throughout these immigration proceedings, Farahi applied for various forms of relief, including asylum, withholding of removal, and protection under the Convention Against Torture.

The relevance of these immigration proceedings to the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) litigation lies in the evidence presented by the government. DHS opposed Farahi’s applications for relief by presenting documents and live testimony from an FBI agent. This testimony documented Farahi’s alleged connections to high-level terrorists. Specifically, public records from the immigration proceedings indicated allegations that Farahi was a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members, and linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists. These allegations provide the factual context for why the FBI maintained a file on Farahi and why the agency later claimed the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes related to national security and international terrorism.

## The FOIA Request and FBI Withholding

The litigation began when Farahi submitted a FOIA request seeking his FBI file. The agency responded by locating a substantial volume of potentially responsive information, totaling approximately 10,750 pages and 80 compact discs. After reviewing these materials, the FBI disclosed some records to Farahi but withheld the majority of them.

Farahi filed suit after the FBI missed the statutory deadline for responding to the request. In FOIA cases, the government bears the burden of showing that a statutory exemption applies to the withheld records. Here, the FBI asserted Exemption 7(A), which protects records compiled for law enforcement purposes if their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings. The procedural posture of the case was driven by the agency’s need to justify this withholding through sworn declarations. The FBI initially supported its position with a declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). This declaration asserted that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their release could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.

## District Court Summary Judgment Motions

FOIA cases are typically resolved through summary judgment, where the court reviews the administrative record and agency declarations to determine if an exemption applies. In October 2019, the FBI moved for summary judgment in the district court. To support this motion, the agency submitted the public Hardy declaration and moved to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte. An ex parte submission allows the government to present sensitive information to the court without disclosing it to the plaintiff, which is often necessary in cases involving national security or ongoing investigations. Farahi did not oppose this motion, and the district court granted it in October 2020.

However, the district court did not immediately rule on the merits of the exemption. In November 2022, the court addressed the summary judgment motion. While the court concluded that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes, it expressed concern regarding the timing of the FBI’s evidence. The court noted that the declarations submitted in 2019 were becoming stale given the significant passage of time. Consequently, the court ordered the FBI to update its assessment of whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated. Additionally, the court indicated it needed more information regarding segregability—the requirement that agencies release nonexempt portions of records—but could not state the reasons for this need on the public record. For these reasons, the district court denied the FBI’s initial summary judgment motion without prejudice.

## The Requirement for Updated Declarations

Following the district court’s order, the FBI submitted updated declarations and renewed its motion for summary judgment. This time, the agency provided both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who was then the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. Bender’s public declaration explained that RIDS had contacted the agent overseeing Farahi’s file, who confirmed that investigations remained ongoing. The ex parte declaration provided further details regarding the nature of the investigation and addressed the court’s concerns about segregability.

The district court granted the renewed motion, crediting the FBI’s confirmation that the relevant proceedings remained ongoing. The court also concluded, based on its review of the ex parte submission, that the FBI had adequately explained why exempt material was not reasonably segregable. This procedural history highlights a critical aspect of FOIA litigation: the government’s burden to demonstrate that an exemption applies at the time of the lawsuit, not merely when the records were originally created. The requirement for updated declarations ensures that the court’s decision rests on current facts regarding the status of enforcement proceedings. Farahi appealed the district court’s grant of summary judgment, challenging the sufficiency of the updated declarations and the segregability analysis.

# Legal Standards and Burden of Proof

## Overview of FOIA Disclosure Requirements

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) establishes a statutory framework that generally mandates federal agencies to release records to the public upon request. As the court explains in Section I of the opinion, this disclosure requirement is not absolute; it applies unless an enumerated statutory exemption prevents release. In *Farahi v. FBI*, the dispute centers on whether the FBI properly invoked such an exemption to withhold the appellant's file. Beyond the core mandate to release or withhold, the statute imposes a specific duty on agencies regarding the content of the records. Even when an exemption applies, agencies must take reasonable steps to segregate and release any nonexempt information that can be reasonably separated from the exempt portions. The court notes that this segregability requirement is a critical component of the FOIA framework, ensuring that exemptions do not swallow the entire record when only specific parts are protected. Understanding this baseline obligation is essential for analyzing the court's later discussion on whether the FBI adequately justified withholding the records in their entirety.

## Elements of Exemption 7(A)

The central legal provision at issue in this case is Exemption 7(A) of the FOIA. According to the court’s analysis in Sections I and IV, this exemption protects records that meet two distinct criteria. First, the records must be “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” Second, the disclosure of those records “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” The court clarifies that for the second prong to be satisfied, the enforcement proceedings must be either “pending or reasonably anticipated.” This definition encompasses ongoing criminal investigations, provided that the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future case and that the premature release of that evidence would jeopardize the case.

Furthermore, the opinion identifies specific types of interference that satisfy the statutory standard. The court recognizes that disclosure can interfere with proceedings by tipping off the targets about the scope of the investigation. This justification is described as widely accepted in Exemption 7(A) jurisprudence. In applying these elements, the court emphasizes that the exemption is not limited to formal judicial proceedings but extends to investigative phases where the government is actively gathering information. Consequently, the legal standard requires a factual showing that an investigation is active and that releasing the specific documents would harm that active process.

## Burden of Proof on the Agency

In FOIA litigation, the burden of proof rests squarely on the government to demonstrate that a statutory exemption applies. As detailed in Section III of the opinion, the agency cannot merely assert that an exemption exists; it must substantiate that claim through specific evidence. To meet this burden under Exemption 7(A), the FBI must show that the withheld records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their disclosure could interfere with pending proceedings. The court notes that agency declarations must establish a rational “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties,” as well as a connection between the individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.

The opinion highlights that the government may rely on affidavits or declarations to satisfy this burden, provided they describe the nature of the investigation with reasonable specificity. In *Farahi*, the FBI submitted declarations from agency officials attesting to the ongoing nature of the investigation and the potential harm of disclosure. The court indicates that such declarations are sufficient to carry the burden if they provide enough detail to allow the court to verify the exemption's applicability. However, the burden remains on the agency throughout the litigation; if the agency fails to provide adequate justification, the presumption of disclosure under FOIA prevails.

## Standard of Review and Deference to the FBI

The appellate court in *Farahi* reviews the district court’s grant of summary judgment de novo, meaning the appellate court examines the legal issues anew without deferring to the lower court’s legal conclusions. However, while the standard of review is de novo, the court applies a specific level of deference to the FBI’s factual determinations regarding the invocation of Exemption 7. The opinion states that because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke this exemption is entitled to deference. This deference is particularly pronounced when the records implicate national security concerns.

The court reiterates the principle of deference to the executive branch in the FOIA context, specifically in cases involving counterterrorism investigations. This deference acknowledges the agency’s expertise and the sensitive nature of the information involved. Nevertheless, this deference is not unlimited; it operates within the bounds of the statutory requirements. The agency must still meet its burden of proof, but the court gives weight to the agency’s judgment regarding the potential interference with enforcement proceedings. In *Farahi*, the court applied this deferential standard to uphold the FBI’s assertions regarding the ongoing nature of the investigation and the risks associated with disclosure, ultimately affirming the district court’s decision.

# Substantive Analysis of Exemption 7(A)

## Establishing the Law Enforcement Purpose Nexus

To invoke Exemption 7(A) of the Freedom of Information Act, the government must first satisfy a threshold requirement: the records must be "compiled for law enforcement purposes." In *Farahi*, the D.C. Circuit explained that while the FBI is inherently a law enforcement agency and its determination on this point is entitled to a fair measure of deference, the agency’s declarations must still establish a rational connection. Specifically, the court noted that the government must show a nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties, as well as a connection between the individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.

The court found that the FBI met this burden through both public and ex parte declarations. The public declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section, stated that the withheld records were compiled in furtherance of investigations into national security and international terrorism violations. This assertion was supported by broader context admitted in the litigation. During the district court proceedings, Farahi himself conceded that the FBI had investigated him for ties to organizations designated as foreign terrorist organizations and for connections to individuals detained as enemy combatants. The appellate court concluded that these declarations and concessions were sufficient to demonstrate that Farahi’s file was compiled for law enforcement purposes, satisfying the first prong of Exemption 7(A).

## Defining Pending or Reasonably Anticipated Proceedings

The second prong of Exemption 7(A) requires that the disclosure of the records "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." The court clarified that for this exemption to apply, the proceedings must be "pending or reasonably anticipated." In this context, the court recognized that ongoing criminal investigations qualify as enforcement proceedings. The government satisfies this requirement by attesting to the existence of such proceedings. The court emphasized that so long as an investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and the premature release of that evidence would jeopardize the case, Exemption 7(A) applies.

In *Farahi*, the government argued that releasing the information would interfere with the investigation by tipping off the targets about the scope of the inquiry. The court accepted this as a widely accepted justification in Exemption 7(A) cases. The Hardy declaration attested that the relevant investigation was "ongoing," and the subsequent Bender declaration confirmed that the investigation remained active as of March 2023. Because the FBI established that the investigation was continuing and that disclosure could harm it by revealing the scope of the inquiry to potential targets, the court concluded that the records were protected under the interference prong of the exemption.

## Addressing the Passage of Time Argument

A significant issue in the appeal was whether the passage of time rendered the government’s assertions regarding pending proceedings too vague or stale. Farahi argued that the government’s initial declaration was made years prior and that the supplemental declaration from Joseph Bender was too conclusory to support a finding that proceedings were still pending. He relied on precedent suggesting that a vague assertion that investigations remain ongoing may not suffice.

The court rejected this argument by examining the context of the declarations. It reasoned that the Bender declaration should not be considered in isolation. Rather, it functioned as an update to the earlier Hardy declarations, which had already established the pendency of enforcement proceedings with greater detail. The district court had previously denied summary judgment without prejudice specifically to allow the FBI to update its assessment, having already concluded the agency "seemingly" met its burden initially. Therefore, the Bender declaration served to confirm that nothing had changed since the initial filing. Furthermore, the court noted the breadth of the underlying investigation, citing public allegations in Farahi’s immigration proceedings regarding ties to Al Qaeda and convicted terrorists. This context made it plausible that a concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding continued to exist despite the passage of time.

## The Role of Ex Parte Declarations

The court’s analysis relied heavily on ex parte declarations submitted by the FBI, which provided details about the investigation that could not be disclosed on the public record. Farahi objected to the district court’s reliance on these sealed submissions, particularly regarding the segregability analysis. However, the appellate court affirmed the use of ex parte declarations, noting that Farahi had not opposed the district court’s acceptance of them during the lower court proceedings.

The court explained that ex parte declarations are sometimes necessary in FOIA cases where public disclosure of the justification would reveal the very material sought to be kept secret. In such instances, the problem of handicapping the plaintiff is inherent to the dispute. Because the government cannot explain the specific risks of disclosure without disclosing the secrets, the court may rely on ex parte submissions to determine whether an exemption applies. The district court was not required to place its analysis of the ex parte declaration on the public record. Consequently, the appellate court accepted the sealed declarations as valid evidence supporting the substantive findings that the investigation was ongoing and that the records were not reasonably segregable.

# Segregability and Administrative Justifications

## The Segregability Requirement Under FOIA

Even when a federal agency identifies valid grounds for withholding records under a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) exemption, the statutory scheme does not automatically permit the agency to withhold the entire document. The FOIA imposes a distinct obligation on agencies to take “reasonable steps” to segregate and release nonexempt information. This requirement is codified in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) and reinforced by the general exemption provision in § 552(b), which mandates that any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to the requester after the deletion of exempt portions. In *Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the D.C. Circuit affirmed that this obligation remains a critical component of FOIA litigation, even in cases involving sensitive national security or law enforcement records.

In the underlying litigation, the district court found that the FBI had satisfied this burden through a supplemental ex parte submission from Joseph Bender. The court noted that the FBI provided a “detailed justification” explaining why the protected records were not reasonably segregable. On appeal, Farahi challenged this finding, arguing that the sheer volume of responsive records—some 10,750 pages and 80 CDs—implied that there must be some segregable and releasable information contained within. However, the appellate court rejected the notion that volume alone dictates segregability. The court emphasized that the statutory duty is to segregate *reasonably* segregable information, not every individual word or phrase that could theoretically be redacted. This distinction sets the stage for understanding the judicial standards applied when agencies claim that redaction is not feasible.

## The Inextricably Intertwined Standard

When an agency asserts that it cannot segregate exempt from non-exempt material, courts apply the “inextricably intertwined” standard. Under this doctrine, the government may withhold records in their entirety when nonexempt portions are so closely linked with exempt portions that they cannot be separated without compromising the exempt information. The D.C. Circuit cited *Juarez v. DOJ*, which quoted *Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force*, to establish that a court may rely on government affidavits showing with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated.

In *Farahi*, the court applied this standard to reject Farahi’s argument that the volume of records necessitated some release. Relying on *Mead*, the court explained that if a small amount of non-exempt material is randomly “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” that material is not likely to be “reasonably segregable.” The court highlighted a crucial conceptual distinction from *Mead*: the focus of the FOIA is on “information,” not simply words which the Government has written down. Consequently, while Farahi was technically correct that individual words or phrases in the requested records might be disclosed without harming the FBI’s interests, such disclosure would not constitute meaningful information release. The district court’s conclusion that no meaningful information was reasonably segregable was therefore upheld, illustrating that the inextricably intertwined standard protects the integrity of the exempt information rather than serving as a mechanical test for redaction.

## Adequacy of Agency Justifications

Beyond the specific content of the records, the adequacy of an agency’s administrative justifications plays a pivotal role in FOIA adjudications. When an agency withholds documents on a category-by-category basis, it must use categories that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each category. This standard, drawn from *CREW* and *Gallant v. NLRB*, ensures that courts can meaningfully review the agency’s decision-making process without requiring a line-by-line review of every document.

In *Farahi*, the appellant briefly objected that the government did not adequately justify how it defined the categories of documents used to explain its withholding decisions. However, the appellate court dismissed this argument on procedural grounds. The court noted that Farahi provided no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad. Citing *Khine v. DHS*, the court held that Farahi had forfeited his arguments on this score. This outcome underscores a critical procedural lesson for litigants: challenging an agency’s categorization requires specific factual or legal arguments demonstrating the overbreadth or indistinctness of the categories. Generalized objections without specific explanation will not survive appellate review, even if the agency’s burden of proof is high.

## Reliance on Ex Parte Submissions

A recurring tension in FOIA litigation involving national security or law enforcement records is the use of ex parte submissions. These sealed filings allow the government to provide details to the court that would themselves reveal the sensitive information the agency seeks to withhold. In *Farahi*, Farahi complained that the district court relied too heavily on Bender’s ex parte declaration in its segregability analysis. The appellate court addressed this concern by noting that nothing in the law required the district court to place its analysis of the ex parte declaration on the public record.

The court provided two primary reasons for upholding the district court’s reliance on the ex parte material. First, Farahi had not opposed the district court’s acceptance of ex parte declarations during the proceedings, and thus he could not challenge the reliance on them on appeal. Second, and more broadly, the court acknowledged that while ex parte declarations may handicap FOIA plaintiffs, this problem is “inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” Quoting *Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affs.*, the court affirmed that the necessity of protecting sensitive information sometimes requires a procedural mechanism that limits the plaintiff’s access to the government’s full reasoning. This holding reinforces the principle that in cases involving significant security concerns, the judicial process may accommodate sealed submissions to balance the public’s right to know with the government’s interest in confidentiality.

# Holding, Disposition, and Significance

## Affirmance of the District Court's Judgment

The appellate court’s primary task in this case was to determine whether the district court correctly applied the law to the facts presented by the FBI and Farahi. In Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) litigation, the standard of review for a grant of summary judgment is de novo, meaning the appellate court reviews the legal conclusions without deference to the lower court’s decision-making process. However, while the review is de novo, the government retains the burden of proving that a FOIA exemption applies. In *Farahi*, the D.C. Circuit concluded that the district court correctly applied Exemption 7(A) to the withheld records. The appellate court agreed with the district court’s findings that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their release could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.

This affirmation signifies that the appellate court found no error in the district court’s reasoning regarding the core requirements of the exemption. Specifically, the court accepted the FBI’s updated declarations confirming that the investigations remained ongoing as of March 2023. The court also upheld the district court’s finding on segregability, agreeing that the FBI had provided a detailed justification for why exempt and non-exempt portions were inextricably intertwined. By affirming, the D.C. Circuit validated the lower court’s conclusion that the FBI met its burden of proof, thereby sustaining the withholding of the requested records. For a law student reading this section, it is crucial to note that affirmation does not merely mean the appellate court liked the outcome; it means the legal standard was satisfied based on the record before the court.

## Forfeiture of Procedural Arguments

In addition to the substantive issues regarding Exemption 7(A), the opinion addresses a procedural objection raised by Farahi concerning the agency’s description of the withheld documents. When an agency withholds records, it often groups them into categories to explain the withholding decisions. The law requires these categories to be sufficiently distinct so a court can determine if exemptions are properly applied. Farahi objected to the FBI’s categorization but failed to provide a specific explanation for why the categories were overbroad or improper.

The court ruled that Farahi forfeited this argument. In legal procedure, forfeiture occurs when a party fails to adequately develop an argument, thereby waiving the right to have the court address it on appeal. The opinion cites *Khine v. DHS* to support the principle that a general objection is insufficient to preserve a claim for appellate review. By failing to articulate specific defects in the FBI’s categories, Farahi left the court with no basis to evaluate the claim. This section of the opinion teaches students that raising an issue is not enough; a litigant must support their objection with specific reasoning to avoid forfeiture. The court explicitly noted that because Farahi provided no specific explanation, the argument could not be entertained, reinforcing the importance of precise legal briefing in FOIA litigation.

## Implications for Counterterrorism FOIA Requests

The *Farahi* opinion provides significant guidance on how courts should handle FOIA requests involving national security and counterterrorism investigations. The court reiterated that the FBI’s decision to invoke Exemption 7 is entitled to deference, particularly when national security concerns are implicated. Citing prior precedent such as *Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ*, the court emphasized the principle of deference to the executive branch in these contexts. This deference acknowledges the specialized nature of law enforcement agencies and the sensitivity of counterterrorism work.

Furthermore, the opinion clarifies the role of ex parte declarations in such cases. Because public disclosure of certain details could defeat the purpose of the exemption, the court permitted the FBI to submit sealed, ex parte declarations to explain the nature of the investigation and the need for withholding. The court noted that while ex parte declarations can handicap plaintiffs, they are sometimes the only way to decide a dispute without disclosing the very material the government seeks to protect. Additionally, the court addressed the passage of time, rejecting Farahi’s argument that the length of the investigation made the exemption inapplicable. The court held that as long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, Exemption 7(A) remains valid. These points collectively signal to students that in counterterrorism contexts, courts will closely scrutinize but ultimately defer to agency assertions regarding ongoing investigations and the necessity of secrecy.

## Final Disposition

The final section of the opinion formally concludes the litigation. After reviewing the district court’s judgment, the substantive arguments regarding Exemption 7(A), the procedural issue of forfeiture, and the segregability of the records, the D.C. Circuit issued its final order. The court stated, "For these reasons, we affirm the district court. So ordered." This disposition means that the judgment of the district court stands as the final ruling on the merits of this appeal.

Practically, this disposition means that Farahi’s request for the FBI file remains denied to the extent the records fall under Exemption 7(A). The FBI is not required to release the withheld portions of the 10,750 pages and 80 CDs identified in the case. For the law student, the final disposition confirms that the appellate process has reached its end regarding this specific judgment. Unless further review is sought from the Supreme Court, which is not discussed in this text, the district court’s order granting summary judgment for the FBI is the binding resolution of the dispute. The "So ordered" language is the formal judicial command that executes the court’s decision, closing the chapter on this specific litigation.
