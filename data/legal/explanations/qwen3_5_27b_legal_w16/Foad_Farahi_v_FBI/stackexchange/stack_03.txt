Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What is the standard for 'reasonably segregable' when exempt information is interspersed throughout a document?
Question:
In Section V, the court cites Mead Data Cent. to say that if non-exempt material is 'interspersed line-by-line,' it isn't reasonably segregable. I'm confused about how this applies to a massive file like Farahi's 10,750 pages. Does the 'information' vs. 'words' distinction mean agencies can withhold entire documents just because some words are exempt? Where is the line between 'inextricably intertwined' and 'reasonably segregable'?
Answer:
**The standard for segregability turns on whether the non-exempt portions constitute "meaningful information" independent of the exempt material, rather than simply whether individual words can be redacted.**

Based on *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, here is how the court defines the line between "inextricably intertwined" and "reasonably segregable" when exempt information is interspersed throughout a document.

### 1. The "Information vs. Words" Distinction
You asked whether agencies can withhold entire documents just because some words are exempt. The *Farahi* opinion clarifies that the focus of FOIA is on **information**, not the physical text.

In Section V, the court relies on *Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force* to explain that "the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down." Consequently, the fact that "individual words or phrases in the requested records might be disclosed without harming the FBI’s interests" is "neither here nor there." If redacting the exempt words leaves behind text that does not convey meaningful information, the agency is not required to release it.

### 2. The Standard for Interspersed Material
Regarding your question on how this applies to massive files where exempt material is mixed in: the court cites the "line-by-line" standard.

In Section V, the opinion states that if a small amount of non-exempt material is randomly "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," that material is not likely to be "reasonably segregable." The sheer volume of the file (10,750 pages) does not alter this standard. Instead, the court looks at the structure of the content. If the non-exempt portions are so fragmented that they cannot be separated without destroying the context or utility of the information, segregation is not required.

### 3. Where the Line is Drawn
The line between "inextricably intertwined" and "reasonably segregable" is drawn at the threshold of **meaningful information**.

*   **Inextricably Intertwined:** The court notes that agencies may withhold records in their entirety "when nonexempt portions 'are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.'" *Juarez v. DOJ*.
*   **Reasonably Segregable:** The agency must take "reasonable steps" to segregate, but only if doing so yields releasable information.

In *Farahi*, the district court concluded that "no meaningful information was reasonably segregable" based on the FBI's ex parte submission. The appellate court affirmed, noting that the district court found a "detailed justification" for why the protected records could not be further segregated.

### Summary
Under *Farahi*, the standard is substantive, not mechanical. An agency is not required to release non-exempt words if, when isolated from exempt material, they do not constitute meaningful information. The line is crossed when the exempt and non-exempt portions are so mixed that segregation would not result in the release of usable information.
