Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### Does an ongoing criminal investigation qualify as an 'enforcement proceeding' under FOIA Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
In Section IV.B, the court states that 'ongoing criminal investigations count as proceedings for these purposes,' citing CREW. I thought Exemption 7(A) required a formal proceeding like a trial, indictment, or administrative hearing. Can you clarify the doctrinal distinction between an 'investigation' and an 'enforcement proceeding' in this context? At what point does an investigation become 'reasonably anticipated' enough to trigger the exemption?
Answer:
**Yes. Under D.C. Circuit precedent, an ongoing criminal investigation qualifies as an "enforcement proceeding" for purposes of FOIA Exemption 7(A).**

You are correct that Exemption 7(A) requires the government to show that disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." However, as the court explains in *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, the term "enforcement proceedings" is not limited to formal judicial actions like trials, indictments, or administrative hearings.

### The Legal Standard: "Pending or Reasonably Anticipated"

In Section IV.B, the court clarifies the scope of "enforcement proceedings." Relying on *Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Washington v. DOJ* (*CREW*), 746 F.3d 1082 (D.C. Cir. 2014), the court states:

> "We have held that this provision requires the proceedings to be 'pending or reasonably anticipated.' ... Ongoing criminal investigations count as 'proceedings' for these purposes."

This directly addresses your concern about formal proceedings. The doctrinal distinction is not between an "investigation" and a "proceeding," but rather whether the investigation is sufficiently active to be considered "pending or reasonably anticipated."

### When Does an Investigation Become "Reasonably Anticipated"?

To trigger the exemption, the government does not need to prove that an indictment is imminent. Instead, it must demonstrate that the investigation is active enough that a future case is concrete.

The court cites *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54 (D.C. Cir. 2008), to define this threshold:

> "[S]o long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies."

Therefore, the point at which an investigation triggers the exemption is when:
1.  The investigation is **ongoing** (actively gathering evidence); and
2.  There is a **concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding** (a possible future case) that would be harmed by disclosure.

### Proving the Existence of Proceedings

Finally, regarding your question about the burden of proof: the government does not need to provide detailed evidence of the future case's timeline. As noted in Section IV.B, the government may satisfy its burden "simply by attesting to their 'existence.'"

In *Farahi*, the court accepted the FBI's declarations confirming the investigation remained ongoing as sufficient proof that a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" continued to exist, even without a specific indictment date.

**Summary:** An investigation qualifies as an enforcement proceeding under Exemption 7(A) once it is actively gathering evidence for a possible future case. A formal trial or indictment is not required.
