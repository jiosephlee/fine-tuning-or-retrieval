Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# The Staleness Problem: Updating FOIA Declarations in Long-Running Investigations

The Freedom of Information Act is designed to shine a light on government operations, but Exemption 7(A) serves as a shield for ongoing law enforcement investigations. For decades, the tension between public transparency and the need to protect sensitive investigative techniques has played out in the D.C. Circuit. A new opinion from that court, *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, issued on September 30, 2025, addresses a specific procedural friction point that often arises in these cases: what happens when a FOIA lawsuit drags on for years, and the government’s initial justification for withholding records begins to look stale? Judge Katsas’s opinion clarifies that agencies do not necessarily need to re-litigate the existence of an investigation from scratch every time a declaration ages, provided they can demonstrate that the underlying enforcement proceedings remain active.

The case involves Foad Farahi, an Iranian national who has lived in the United States since 1993 and has been the subject of long-running immigration and terrorism-related investigations. After requesting his FBI file, the agency withheld the vast majority of over 10,000 pages and 80 CDs, citing Exemption 7(A). The district court initially denied the FBI’s motion for summary judgment in 2022, not because it doubted the exemption applied, but because the agency’s declarations were from 2019. The court ordered the FBI to update its assessment to confirm whether enforcement proceedings were still pending. When the FBI submitted updated declarations in 2023, the district court granted summary judgment, and Farahi appealed, arguing that the updated declaration was too conclusory to support the withholding after such a significant lapse in time.

In affirming the district court, the D.C. Circuit focused heavily on the standard for determining whether enforcement proceedings are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The court reiterated that ongoing criminal investigations count as proceedings under Exemption 7(A), and the government generally satisfies this burden by attesting to their existence. Farahi’s core argument was that the updated declaration from Joseph Bender, which simply confirmed the investigation remained ongoing, was insufficiently specific compared to the earlier, more detailed declaration from David Hardy. However, Judge Katsas rejected the notion that the Bender declaration had to stand alone. Instead, the court viewed it as an update to the Hardy declarations, which had already established the pendency of the proceedings and the nature of the investigation. By reading the declarations in context, the court concluded the FBI had met its burden to show a concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding continued to exist.

This reasoning offers a significant analytical pivot for FOIA practitioners. In many cases, plaintiffs argue that the passage of time inherently weakens the government’s claim that an investigation is active, effectively using litigation delay as a tool to force disclosure. *Farahi* suggests that while agencies cannot rely on indefinitely stale declarations, they are not required to re-prove the entire scope of an investigation if the litigation timeline is the cause of the delay. The court emphasized that the FBI’s initial burden was met, and the subsequent declaration merely confirmed that nothing had changed. This contextual approach prevents plaintiffs from leveraging procedural delays to defeat Exemption 7(A) claims, particularly in counterterrorism cases where investigations naturally span decades.

The opinion also reinforces the high level of deference courts afford the executive branch in national security contexts. Judge Katsas noted that the D.C. Circuit has consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive when national security concerns are implicated. This deference was bolstered by the public record in Farahi’s parallel immigration proceedings, where DHS agents testified to Farahi’s connections to high-level terrorists. By integrating evidence from related proceedings, the court found the FBI’s claim of an ongoing investigation plausible without needing to disclose the sealed ex parte details. This signals to lower courts that they should look beyond the four corners of the FOIA declarations if the broader context supports the agency’s position.

Finally, the decision touches on the segregability requirement, which mandates that agencies release any non-exempt portions of a record. Farahi argued that some information must be releasable given the volume of records, but the court upheld the district court’s finding that non-exempt material was inextricably intertwined with exempt material. The court also declined to criticize the district court for relying on ex parte declarations to make this determination, noting that Farahi had not opposed the use of ex parte submissions and that such procedures are sometimes the only way to resolve the dispute without compromising the very secrets at issue. This aspect of the ruling underscores the practical reality that in high-stakes national security FOIA cases, the public record will often remain silent on the specific reasons for withholding.

*Farahi v. FBI* serves as a reminder that the “staleness” of a FOIA declaration is not a rigid clock but a contextual inquiry. For agencies, it provides a roadmap for maintaining withholdings in long-running litigation: if the initial declaration was robust, a subsequent update confirming continuity may suffice. For plaintiffs, it raises the bar for challenging the pendency of investigations in cases where the government has already laid a substantial groundwork. Ultimately, the ruling prioritizes the integrity of ongoing law enforcement operations over the theoretical possibility of releasing fragmented information that might compromise those operations.



# Behind Closed Doors: Ex Parte Submissions and the Segregability Inquiry

The Freedom of Information Act promises a presumption of openness, yet its most contentious battles often take place behind closed doors. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, decided September 30, 2025, the D.C. Circuit reaffirmed the substantial deference courts afford the executive branch when national security and ongoing law enforcement investigations collide with public transparency. While the case turns on the application of Exemption 7(A), its procedural posture highlights a persistent tension in FOIA litigation: how can a plaintiff effectively challenge an agency’s claim that information must remain secret when the very evidence needed to challenge that claim is the subject of the secrecy itself?

The core dispute in *Farahi* involves a request for the plaintiff’s own FBI file, which contains over 10,000 pages of records. The FBI withheld the vast majority of these documents, arguing that their release could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings. Under Exemption 7(A), the government must show that records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that disclosure could interfere with proceedings that are pending or reasonably anticipated. Judge Katsas, writing for the panel, affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment for the FBI. The opinion underscores that in the realm of counterterrorism, investigations can span decades without losing their status as "pending," provided the agency can demonstrate a rational nexus to ongoing law enforcement duties. Farahi’s immigration proceedings, which began in 2002 and involved allegations of ties to designated terrorist organizations, provided the backdrop for this determination.

However, the most significant doctrinal contribution of the opinion lies in its treatment of segregability and the use of ex parte declarations. FOIA requires agencies to release any reasonably segregable nonexempt portions of a record. In practice, this often requires the court to look at the redacted documents to determine if the redactions are too broad. In *Farahi*, the FBI submitted supplemental ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender to address segregability concerns raised by the district court. Farahi challenged the reliance on these sealed submissions, arguing that the court could not properly assess segregability without a public record. The D.C. Circuit rejected this, noting that Farahi had not opposed the ex parte submission in the district court and that the process is sometimes the only way to resolve the dispute without disclosing the material sought to be kept secret.

This reliance on ex parte review reinforces a procedural reality that often disadvantages FOIA plaintiffs. The court noted that while ex parte declarations handicap plaintiffs, the problem is inherent in cases where public disclosure of the agency’s reasoning would compromise the investigation. By accepting the FBI’s assertion that nonexempt material was "inextricably intertwined" with exempt portions, the court deferred to the agency’s judgment that no meaningful information could be released without harming the investigation. The opinion cites the principle that the focus of FOIA is information, not simply words, suggesting that even if individual words could be released, they might not constitute segregable information if they lack context.

The decision has downstream implications for how courts evaluate the longevity of law enforcement investigations under Exemption 7(A). Farahi argued that the passage of time should cast doubt on whether proceedings were still pending, invoking prior precedent suggesting vague assertions of ongoing investigations are insufficient. The court distinguished this case by pointing to the breadth of the underlying investigation and the specific details provided in the Hardy and Bender declarations. The court reasoned that the Bender declaration served as an update to confirm nothing had changed, rather than a standalone assertion. This suggests that for counterterrorism investigations involving significant threats, the "pending" clock may run much slower than in ordinary criminal cases, provided the agency maintains a consistent record of active oversight.

Ultimately, *Farahi* illustrates the high bar plaintiffs face when challenging FOIA withholdings in the national security context. The court’s willingness to rely on sealed declarations for segregability analysis limits the ability of litigants to scrutinize the scope of redactions. While the opinion adheres to established D.C. Circuit precedent regarding deference to the FBI, it solidifies a procedural framework where the government’s ex parte assurances carry significant weight. For future litigants, the takeaway is clear: challenging the segregability of national security records requires overcoming not just the substantive exemption, but the procedural hurdles designed to protect the secrecy of the underlying investigation.



# Heightened Deference: National Security and the Law Enforcement Purpose Prong

The Freedom of Information Act is designed to shine a light on government operations, yet its exemptions often allow agencies to keep that light off, particularly when national security is at stake. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, decided on September 30, 2025, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit reinforced just how difficult it is for individuals to pierce the veil of secrecy surrounding counterterrorism investigations. Writing for the panel, Judge Katsas affirmed the district court’s decision to uphold the FBI’s withholding of thousands of pages from Farahi’s file under Exemption 7(A). While the holding is consistent with existing precedent, the opinion offers a clear roadmap for how courts should weigh agency assertions of national security risk against the statutory mandate for transparency, particularly regarding the "law enforcement purpose" requirement.

The case matters not only for Farahi, an Iranian national involved in long-running immigration proceedings, but for the broader doctrine of FOIA exemptions in the post-9/11 era. The core tension in FOIA litigation is often between the plaintiff’s right to know and the government’s burden to justify secrecy. Here, the court navigated that tension by applying a standard of heightened deference to the FBI, specifically because the records implicated counterterrorism duties. This approach signals that when the Executive Branch invokes national security concerns, the judicial gatekeeping function will be exercised with significant restraint, provided the agency maintains a procedural paper trail that demonstrates the investigation remains active.

## The Law Enforcement Purpose Prong and Deference

To invoke Exemption 7(A), an agency must first show that records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes." While this seems like a threshold inquiry, the D.C. Circuit treated it as an area where the FBI’s judgment commands significant weight. Relying on *Campbell v. DOJ* and *Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ* (CNSS), the court reiterated that the FBI’s decision to invoke Exemption 7 is entitled to deference, which is "particularly appropriate for records implicating national security."

This deference was not blind, however. The court required a rational nexus between the investigation and the agency’s duties, as well as a connection between the individual and a potential security risk. In this instance, the court found that nexus satisfied not only through FBI declarations but through Farahi’s own litigation history. The opinion notes that Farahi had conceded in district court that the FBI investigated him for ties to foreign terrorist organizations and individuals later convicted of terrorism. Furthermore, the court looked to DHS documents from Farahi’s immigration proceedings, which cited FBI testimony regarding his connections to high-level terrorists. By cross-referencing the FOIA litigation with the parallel immigration case, the court anchored the "law enforcement purpose" prong in facts already admitted by the plaintiff. This analytical move is instructive: it suggests that a subject’s admissions in related administrative or civil proceedings can effectively bolster the government’s burden in FOIA litigation, making it harder for a plaintiff to argue that an investigation is merely pretextual.

## Pendency and the Staleness Argument

The second prong of Exemption 7(A) requires that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are "pending or reasonably anticipated." Farahi argued that the passage of time cast doubt on whether the investigation was truly ongoing, invoking the principle that vague assertions of pendency are insufficient. This is a common challenge in FOIA cases involving cold files, where plaintiffs argue that an investigation has effectively gone dormant.

The court rejected this argument by contextualizing the FBI’s updated declarations. Although the supplemental declaration from Acting Section Chief Joseph Bender was terse, the court viewed it not as a standalone proof of pendency but as an update to the more detailed initial declarations from Section Chief David Hardy. The court reasoned that the government had already established the pendency of proceedings in the public and ex parte Hardy declarations, and the Bender declaration served to confirm that nothing had changed during the litigation. This reasoning highlights a procedural advantage for agencies: once an initial showing of pendency is made with sufficient specificity, a subsequent update need not repeat that detail to survive a staleness challenge. It effectively lowers the administrative burden on the FBI to maintain exemptions over multi-year litigation periods, provided they can confirm the status quo remains unchanged.

## Segregability and the Ex Parte Dilemma

Even when records are exempt, the FOIA requires agencies to segregate and release nonexempt portions. Farahi argued that the FBI failed to justify why it withheld entire categories of documents rather than redacting sensitive lines. The court affirmed the district court’s finding that the exempt and nonexempt portions were "inextricably intertwined," a standard that allows for total withholding when the information cannot be separated without revealing the secret.

Significantly, the court upheld the district court’s reliance on ex parte declarations to make this determination. Farahi contended that the court relied too heavily on sealed submissions, but the panel noted that Farahi had not opposed the ex parte filings in the lower court. More broadly, the opinion acknowledges the inherent handicap ex parte review places on FOIA plaintiffs. Judge Katsas wrote that this problem is "inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." This validates the use of sealed materials in national security FOIA cases as a necessary evil, reinforcing the "black box" nature of these proceedings where plaintiffs must often trust the court’s review of secret evidence without seeing it themselves.

## Doctrinal Implications

The *Farahi* opinion solidifies a high bar for plaintiffs seeking access to their own files in the context of counterterrorism. By combining heightened deference on the "law enforcement purpose" prong with a flexible approach to the "pending proceedings" requirement, the D.C. Circuit has made it clear that the government’s interest in protecting ongoing investigations outweighs the general presumption of disclosure when national security is involved. The reliance on Farahi’s immigration court admissions to satisfy the nexus requirement is particularly notable, suggesting that the government can leverage a subject’s litigation strategy in one forum to defeat their transparency claims in another.

Downstream, this decision likely encourages agencies to maintain broader categories of withholding in sensitive cases, knowing that courts will accept updated, less detailed declarations as sufficient to maintain exemptions over time. For litigants, the takeaway is that challenging an FBI file on national security grounds requires more than pointing to the passage of time; it requires concrete evidence that the investigation has actually ceased, which is difficult to prove when the agency controls the relevant information. As national security investigations become increasingly complex and long-term, *Farahi* ensures that Exemption 7(A) remains a robust shield for the Executive Branch.
