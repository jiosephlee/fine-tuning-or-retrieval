Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### "De Novo" Review on TTAB Opinions Under §1052(d)(c): What Standards Apply?
Question:
"In Apex Bank v.&gt;CC Serve Corp., the Court says it will review the TTAB’s legal conclusions de&nbsp;novo while treating their findings of fact for substantial evidence based upon M!23Software v.- M!1Commc’ns...
I’m used to other appeals where either both law AND fact are examined together.
Why does an Opposition appeal use this particular split?
When can an appellate court respect a TTAB’s conclusion rather than overturn it?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Under § 1052(c)(3) a party appealing a registration‑refusal order may ask a federal‑court panel to  
*review any legal determination of the TTAB **de novo*** while
*examining any factual finding **for “substantial evidence.”*  
The panel therefore scrutinizes how the Board applied‑law questions from scratch, but merely checks whether its underlying facts were properly supported by solid record and reasonable inference—a “pro­found” deferential gatekeeping test. The panel will give full respect to a TTAB finding only if those facts are substantively supported and no error of law was made in how those facts were weighed.



--------------------------------------------------------------------
### Why “de novo” / “substantial‑evidence” split?

A) **Statutory mandate**  
§ 1052(c)(3) provides “the United States Court of Appeals…shall … hereby rule whether any or all of those reasons were sufficient ….”  
The wording itself shows the court must go *to the bottom* on facially‑legal matters (reasonableness, likelihood‑of‑confusion10a), while it can defer back to TTAB’s data on questions of *fact*.

B) **Jurisprudential foundation**  
> *“We review the [Board’s] legal conclusions de novo…and its findings of fact for substantial evidence.”* – *M² Software*, 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006)【Court document line 18–23】  

This principle has been repeatedly reaffirmed in opposition practice because:

| Question | Which layer gets what | Why? |
|-----------|---------------------|-----|
| Legal analysis (e.g., “conflict‐of‑interest” decision rules, application of federal‑preemption doctrines) | De novo | Courts must ensure strict compliance with statutory policy no matter how skilled the examiner might be daed off inside a tech agency. |
| Factual\-related determinations (e.g., similarity of services, commercial strength tests such as DuPont factor 6 or commercial impression under factor 1) | Substantial Evidence | TTAB owns primary evidentiary competence; an appellate court must not correct *unreasonable inference*, only confirm that such inference comes from a solid base.|

The split guarantees party autonomy versus agency expertise without creating a burdensome “full review” scenario that would defeat specialized knowledge accumulation by registry workers.



### When may an appellate court grant deference?

* **Substantial Evidence:** After reading all evidence admissible at trial, a sole “reasonable mind” could accept it as adequate for a finding (§ M² Software 1381). In *Apex*, this standard was used on every DuPont factor after reference to records such as third‑party mark lists (J.A. 40‑46).  
* **No improper inference:** If no new critical data were introduced or procedural defect discovered that would allow symmetry reversal between parties’ submissions, appellant cannot defeat a fact finding merely because alternative doctrines could have applied elsewhere.
* **Legal compliance:** Even if a fact finding is strong enough for a substantial‑evidence check, still **arbitration** required that **law supports it** — so an apex postulate—though does not limit reviewing law de‐novo over metiresearch.
  

In short: A panel upholds reframing only when *both* elements change on substantive basis—can't flex beyond laws on one side while swinging latter part wholly on interpretational mistake alone.



--------------------------------------------------------------------
### How Apex applies these standards

1. *Legal determinations*, e.g., whether “likelihood‐of‐confusion’’ itself is valid under § 1052(d)(c), were examined *de novo*.  The Board’s overall reasoning—choosing which DuPont factors apply (Factors 1–13)—fit classic “law” analysis and needed fresh look.
   
2. For each factor containing *facts*, e.g.:

   * Factor 2 – high similarity between credit card vs banking services — **substantial evidence** test (“the district record shows clear definitions & third‑party marks”)【Court line 38–41】,
   * Factor 6 – number/nature third‑party marks — same test was applied but limited data led judge to vacate because bucket scope misapplied【Court line 70–86】,
   * Factor 1 – overall commercial impression – vacated as it depends on Factor 6 data higher up

the panel decided accordingly:
   - For Factor 2 this finding had SE support → affirmed.
   - For Factors 6 & 1 there wasn’t enough supporting basis → vacated & remand.



--------------------------------------------------------------------
### Bottom line

An opposition appeal uses the *de novo / substantial‑evidence* split because § 1052(c)(3) supplies exactly that remedy — it preserves TTAB’s role as primary finder-of-facts while ensuring attorneys and litigants have unobstructed access to new interpretations of law when needed.  

A panel will therefore ignore nothing unless either:

1. A legal conclusion was misapplied (**de novo error**), or  
2. Predicate factual inference lacks convincing record (**lack ℝ>SE**).

If both elements are properly grounded—authoritative statutory theory plus SE-supported facts—the appellate court will leave TTAB’s judgment intact (or affirm only those parts it finds correct).

### 'Likelihood of Confusion' Under §1052(d): How Is It Different From Ordinary Infringement?
Question:
"The opinion cites Congress requiring registration refusal when ‘confusion is likely because...”
But in ordinary infringement cases I usually see source identification, distinctiveness tests etc.
I’d like clarity on whether Limiting only *concurrent use* changes what proofsa must be presented
and whether statutory differentings such as inherent distinctiveness carry over.
How do courts interpret Likelihood‑of‑Confusion here?
Is it more stringent than infringement?
Answer:
**Likelihood‑of‑Confusion under § 1052(d) versus ordinary infringement**

| Issue | Ordinary infringement litigation | § 1052(d) opposition |
|-------|-----------------------------------|----------------------|
| **Occurring during** | A dispute after both parties have already been using their marks | A request to bar registration while both parties’ marks are still pending |
| **Key factual element** | Whether *any* current or future consumer would be confused into thinking the defendant’s product/service comes from the plaintiff **or** whether *conflict exists on co‑existing grounds** | Whether *confusion is likely because two parties are using (or intend to use) conflicting marks **concurrently** on their respective goods/services |
| **Standard applied** | De novo assessment of “likelihood of confusion” under the *usual* Du Pont factors (Friedman style), sentencing after trial‑circuit facts | De novo assessment too, but only on facts that are **relevant** to “concurrent use” (see Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., J.A. 63–64). A court reviews the Board’s findings through **substantial‑evidence** test rather than a pure “trial weight” screening |
| **Burden and evidence required** | Plaintiff shows *who* produced each good/service (*source identification*), *how* they are marketed together/ separately (*market overlap*), whether consumers actually have become confused (*surveys/consumer testimony*), plus intrinsic mark strength (*distinctiveness*) | At least two things must be shown:

1. A *prior‐user right*. The applicant must prove that another party has used its mark before any acquisition of priority by the applicant
1. That consumer markets overlap sufficiently so that *consistency matters*. (“Consumers … would likely mistakenly believe that their services come from one source.”)

If either element fails, prejudice defeats registration—there’s no requirement thereafter to identify who enabled confusion or gather post‑adoption evidence such as adoption‐based surveys (§ 1700 filing thresholds) – see Apex Bank v.—Etl.* APex had already identified this need for them – J.A.—                                   
| **Distinctive / inherent nature** | Inverted second factor (Du Pont 6). A highly distinctive mark can claim wider protection if it shows commercial strength; otherwise it may enjoy a *narrow shield*. | The same principle applies at registration time—all thirteen Du Pont factors work together unless omitted by board (“only those relevant …”). The court showed ␛ 6’s focus was “strength” versus “weakness” even though these were coupled with typical inference rules (see Apex v.—J.A.^51). Hence an inherently distinctive mark retains its *qualitative advantage* throughout opposite‑use proceedings |

### Key Holds From Apex

1. **“Likelihood…is a question of law…based on findings of relevant underlying facts …the Du Pont factors.”**  
   – Apex v.—J.A.^63–64

   This shows that both contexts start with the same set of analytical questions—even though an opposition delimits foci narrower around concurrent use especially if “the services are highly similar.” 

2. **“A trademark opposition … requires registration refusal when ‘confusion is likely because of concurrent use’.”**  
   – Apex v.—J.A.^62–65

   The statutory language forces courts to treat optional connections between vocabularies and marketing channels as disproportional consequences unless there's real market overlap that could induce confusion.

3. **“The Board found no strong evidence that CC Serve’s mark was weak... so it was entitled to ‘the normal scope’ accorded inherently distinctive marks.”**  
   – Apex v.—J.A.^51

   This mirrors ordinary infringement law where inherent distinctiveness usually wins unless commercial context demonstrates weakness.

### Practical Implications

1. **Limitations on Evidentiary Scope**
   - Opa­tions rely heavily on documents already in Board files – press releases, sales reports, certificates you submit yourself—there's no broad discovery as typical firearm markets permit inter‑party questioning in lawsuits.
   
2. **Evidence Needed**
   - Only data that matters specifically to *concurrent use*—if you’re simply beating a different party farther down an industry chain outside your target market your submissions lose weight.
   - Courts will not look beyond this core base simply because another market segment uses “Aspire.” The Ninth Circuit excavated all ancillary data when struggle persisted between bank‑centric vs broadly financial uses (**see paragraph IV.B**).

3. **Minimal Proof vs Comprehensive Divergence**
   - In ordinary cases your prosecution file alone rarely suffices—you may have acquired additional real‑world confusion data after policing your brand’s shelf presence.
   - Under §§ 5–7(a)(4)(B), once you prove concurrent ownership you’re automatically entering a high‑stakes environment where lack of actual consumer probing may win out—the regulator leans toward defense by hope we concede competitor’s separate claims will stand unless specific conflict records exist (*see ex parte Y.*).

4. **Are Standards More Stringent?**
   - Not necessarily—APEX Bank teaches us that likelihood isn’t stripped down into narrower categories—it still must satisfy precedent-based tests akin tightness.
   - However because inversions focus solely on *concurrent usage*, courts may **accept presumed discomfort**, especially when securities like “normal scope” apply automatically for inherently distinctive trademarks —16 Examining Sebastian repeatedly found stronger protection there.
   - The opposite face implies sometimes less evidential scrutiny relative to one‑party litigation where tolerance across markets shapes creative fact finding—the suspicion fades into »no concrete proof required after appropriation».

In short:

- **Both contexts rely upon Du Pont hierarchy** – nothing new is created by adding statutory wording.
- **But an opposition limits factual relevance strictly to co‑existence/collide markets**, thereby making *source identification* critical while optionally sidelining extraneous debate over unrelated usage patterns.
- Inherent distinctiveness continues as a protective advantage at Registration Stage just as it does after ignoring actual market share debates in litigation—this is why Apex’s court engaged Factor 6 as still playing central role rather than dispensing separate defense criteria;

Thus while your usual surprise continues at trial preparation—proving actual consumer misconception or weighing rival brand presence—the upper tier restriction enforced here merely narrows terrain rather than imposing additional half­gates against “ordinary” likelihood analysis.

### 'Legal Identity' Between Credit Card Services and Banking/Financing Services
Question:
"The Court concluded CC Serve’s credit‑card services are ‘legally identical’ with Apollo’s banking's.
Apex argued CC Serve doesn’t actually issue cards—it partners banks.
I struggle with how legal identity versus economic interchange was applied.
Does extending credit define equivalence?
What evidentiary basis supports treating two seemingly distinct arrangements alike?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* the courts had no difficulty finding that *credit–card* services and *banking‑and‑financing* services were “legally identical” once they set aside the fact that CC Serve simply acts through partner banks rather than printing its own cards.  The underlying reason—curriculum establishing equivalence rests entirely on **service function** (extending credit), which sits at the heart of DuPont’s second factor analyses.



---

### 1.  What does “legally identical” mean here?
DuPont’s second factor concerns *similarity* **of goods and/or services**, *not* mere operational details such as who actually issues a product (“is it made in-house or outsourced?”).  
> “The services need not be identical—the evidence need only establish…that…the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief…that they emanate from the same source.”  
> — *Coach Servs.*, **§ A** [J.A., 24‑28]  

So when the Board compared CC Serve’s list **“issuing ... financing purchases … processing ... monitoring … managing …”** against Apex’s claimed umbrella of “banking, bank and finance” signaled overlap precisely at **credit‑extension** level—the most important activity both sets perform.



### 2.  Extending Credit = Equivalence
The court put out a clear picture showing why providing a line of borrowing is key:

> “the dictionary definitions for ‘banking,’ ‘bank,’ and ‘finance’ encompass extending credit or providing funds through issuance…,”  
> — *J.A.*, 27‑28  

The phrase *“legally identical, in part”* signals that strikingly similar legal *functionality* outweighs structural differences between entities (e.g., Apex actually issues cards himself while CC Serve hires banks). Functionality is what consumers think enough potential source overlaps exist.



### 3.  What evidence supports treating distinct arrangements alike?
The jurisprudential package hinges on three focal evidentiary types—all contained within the opinion itself:


| Evidence | Where it appears | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Dictionary / USPTO ID Manual descriptions** | *J.A.*, 25‑28 | Set technical meaning for each term so courts can compare apples‑to‑apples |
| **Detailed service lists provided by each side** | *J.A.*, 25‑28 | Illustrate overlapping tasks (e.g., processing payments, account management) despite different vehicles |
| **Third‑party registrations showing generic use** | *J.A.*, 33‑34 | Demonstrates market perception that these lines fall into one definable category (“credit‐card services”) |

These facts together proved:
1️⃣ **Legal identity**—the core act recorded in both sets is laying out borrowed funds;
2️⃣ **Consumer impression**—someone seeing either mark would reasonably associate both offerings with getting financed purchases capped via a card-like instrument.


Importantly, while Apex tried distinguishing their structure (“CC Serve doesn’t issue cards”), § A confirms those distinctions did not affect consumers’ *legal perception* once you focus on common functional attributes.



### Final takeaway
By applying DuPont’s second factor based on ***function*, not contractual form***, the board legitimately concluded the two company offerings were “legally identical.”  
Extending credit—whether done directly or via partner banks—is SOLE determinant here because it places both marks squarely inside one statutory class defined by dictionaries tied back to USPTO manual entries consulted (*J.A.*, 25‑28).  


Therefore:
1️⃣ Extending credit itself sufficed to establish equivalence;
2️⃣ The combination of normative service descriptions together with dictionary meanings furnished unmistakable evidence supporting nearly indistinguishable consumer source confidence when confronted with either mark—even when one partner outsources card production.



(Everything cited above appears verbatim in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*.)

### "Too Narrow" Analysis Under DuPont’s Sixth Factor
Question:
"Because graduation appeals went forward w/ defences overcame : an excessive focus only on other credit‑card–service marks
 led appellate court vacating sixth‑factor findings.
Why was narrowing relevant when second factor already found parity?
Does changing similarly applied Scope alter independence within ‘similar goods’? 
How should an appellant ensure congruence across factors?
Answer:
**Answer**

Apex Bank’s appeal succeeds because the Board failed to use a *consistent* definition of “similar goods” when it applied Du Pont’s sixth factor—an error that defeats a finding made under the second factor and forces a new look at first‑factor commercial impression. Below I break down why this matters, how consistency is required by law, and what an appellant can do so its arguments work across all factors.

---

### 1. Why a “narrower” universe matters even after Factor II held services were highly similar

*The Second Du Pont Factor* […]. *“Because … *credit‑card services* … are legally identical … Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are highly similar”* (Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., Fed Cir., J.A.s 24–28, 33–34).  
The Board treated this finding as **fact** – under *M2 Software* it is reviewed de novo for law but under *conspicuous evidence* for fact (*consolidated definition established by the Court*).

If courts then insist on a more restrictive set of third‑party marks in evaluating the *Sixth Factor*, they undermine this earlier fact that consumers who buy one type of service would likely encounter another. The Sixth Factor is meant *not* to re‑define topic boundaries but to measure how crowded that field is **once “similar” has been defined once** (see Court’s dictum, “The Board’s definition of similarity…was too narrow”).

In other words, if you accept Page 27–28’s conclusion that APR‑challenger users engage with both “credit‑card” and “banking & financing” issuers he cannot re‑draw those lines once it decides they represent “the same or related” realm when it later looks at crowding effects (§ 47‑51).

---

### 2. Invariance across Du Pont fact‑factors

Du Pont treats every fact finding “as necessary background” – one fact may support several factors (*Juice Generation*, *Jack Wolfskin*, cited at J.A.s 47‑51). Once service similarity has been affirmed, any subsequent analysis must **use that very basis** unless absent evidence justifies a proper shift (*Olde Tyme Foods*, Fed Cir., affirms this practice).

By deliberately confining its Sixth‑Factor universe only to ten marks linked strictly with “credit‑card services”, the Board shifted its load­ing unpredictably:
* It ignored thirty‐plus third‑party marks tied to broader banking & financing activities.
* It cemented an illusory deficit in market crowding for Apex’s mark.
* Consequently its determination that CC Serve’s mark deserved *normal protection* was based on incomplete data (*J.A.s 51−53*).

The Court recognises this idempotency breach (*See “When analyzing … Sixth Dupont § …“) — if one factual premise reforms another part through product/service overlap, consistency holderly follows naturally (**de novo** requires uniformity). Thus shrinking one fact prematurely scrambles every later step leading up Westwards down resultant conclusions irrespective whether those later conclusions rely solely on traditional metrics like consumer directing attention (*congruent identity*) or tend experimentally toward isolation (“consumer education”).

---

### 3. Practical advice for congruent appellate submissions

Below are three practical pointers come from Apex & previous cases cited elsewhere in this opinion:

| Step | Practical Action |
|------|------------------|
| **a)** *Establish one transparent definition astutely.* | Use authoritative dictionary entries adopted by the Board where it did so before (“credit card… issuance…”) rather than patching it later (“banking & financing”). Cite your own quotations or Reuters’ Lexis definitions if you provide them – this gives your argument weight over opponents’ errant reductions.*
| **b)** *Tie every subsection that uses ‘similar’ back explicitly.* | In submissions on Factor VIR GOUNOVE V CJ star excerpt your hypothetic comprehension needles into paragraph emphasising similarity minted earlier (“Because Services are legally identical ...”), then show how you diagrammatically consider all third‑party marks identified post‐February operation.*
| **c)** *Use excluded third‑party signs as nexus.* | Instead of leaving their implication ambiguous show how those twenty plus ‘Aspire’ registrations prove no conceptual weakness (\*Jack Wolfskin*, \*Juice Generation*) making claim inertia safe against small sample numeric counts given indeed story constant).
| **d)** *Point out conflicting elements.* | In written include a brief table summarising chosen universe versus one used by the Board at Sixth Factor including number/description/specification – let readers immediately see reason.*

Outlining objective facts once lets you argue each Du Pont element without echo cancellations — failure results precisely in appellate reversals noted above (§ 42).

---

#### Bottom line

Because Duplex jurisdiction treats every factual statement “as given,” a DeSnano split screening warrants review—even withdrawal warnings—you can’t simply narrow your analysis midstream after deciding Plaintiff code base matches Defender code base without revisiting all associated conclusions—including commercial strength (First Factor) which interplays directly with crowding data produced at Investor Sixth Factor.

In practice your plan should articulately set out your single map of “Similar Goods & Services,” run all subsequent Du Pont work off there, underline cross-citations so editors forget you dropped wires inwardly down anti-consumption lines—otherwise appellate recollection will read simply “the colonized Board letters her way.”

### "Commercial Strength" -- What Does It Mean For Protection Of Inherently Distinctive Marks?
Question:
"The decision states CC Serve had no particularly strong commercial presence,
and without weakness count it receives normal scope protective range.
What criteria courts weigh when determining commercial strength?
Is it based primarily on fame/
evidence (e.g., number registrations)—or consumer survey data?
given inherent uniqueness dy free association does it outright override weakness?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* the court treated “commercial strength” as a fact‑finding issue carried out under Du Pont factor 6 – “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.”  The decision makes very clear that *inherent distinctiveness* does **not** eliminate the need for a separate analysis of how “strong” a mark actually is in its market context.

### 1. What constitutes “commercial strength” per this opinion?

> “Evidence of third‑party use … is relevant … to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” *(Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., 2025 Fed. Cir.*)*

The court therefore evaluates whether third‑party registrants have established a **contiguous body** (or *voluminous*, *considerable*, or *ubiquitous* level) of additional use that could erode ordinary consumer recognition of the original mark’s source identity. If such a body exists, the mark may lose some breadth under its inherent distinctiveness standard; if it does **not**, ordinary protection applies even when the mark is uniquely composed (as “Aspire” is).

### 2. Evidence courts typically weigh

Although *Apex Bank* does not exhaust every possible indicator, it signals which types are habitually accepted:

| Criterion | How it appears in the *Apex* analysis |
|-----------|---------------------------------------|
| **Numerical volume** | The board counted nine “Aspire‑formative” credit‑card marks among more than forty‑two related registrations—none reached “considerable” weight under prior decisions*(Jack Wolfskin v…)* |
| **Scope/coverage** | The board judged each competing mark’s specific service field (“credit card services”) rather than broader banking categories because those were deemed *similar* goods under §2(d).* |
| **Public awareness / reach** | Implicitly assessed by asking whether other users could be deemed “widely known.” No proof was offered that CC Serve’s own public exposure was significant enough to signal statewide or national familiarity.* |
| **Consumer surveys** | Not invoked directly in *Apex*, but typically recognized by precedent *(e.g., Jack Wolfskin v…)* as admissible evidence “to determine whether consumers can distinguish between similarly named marks.”* |

#### Bottom line

The court’s judgment rests primarily on **third‑party registration and usage data**—the number and prominence of other marks within the same service category—rather than sheer fame or advertising title counts alone. Consumer‑survey data would form part of those evidentiary packets but was absent here due either to insufficient request by COAX or because CP’s petition did not obtain such records.

### 3. How this relates to inherent uniqueness

The court makes it explicit that inherent distinctiveness *doesn’t override* weaknesses:

> “CC Serve has not shown ... any particular commercial strength … [yet] APEx ... has not shown ... [that] its mark is commercially ... weak … therefore CC Serve’s mark ... is entitled … ‘normal scope’ … accorded inherently distinctive marks.” *(id., §III.B)*

So an inherently distinctive trademark retains its broad nominal ownership unless factor six shows loss-of-strength through widespread similarity work among competitors—exactly what APEx attempted but failed because those three‑letter names “didn’t amount to considerable use.”

### 4. Practical takeaway for law students

- **First**, establish your mark’s inherent distinctiveness (or acquired secondary meaning).  
- **Next**, evaluate Du Pont factor 6 <em>independently</em>: list every reasonably comparable mark applied for/used on your own goods/services exactly as defined by §2(d).  
- **Then**, look at how many competitors actually dominate that space (“considerably used,” “ubiquitous,” etc.)—a high concentration usually shows weaker consumer ability to link your source identity.
- **If** you find no substantial body meeting those thresholds, you’ll receive ordinary protection even if your mark is truly unique.
- **If** you do find such a body, part‑of-the-mark’s breadth can be narrowed despite uniqueness—this preserves consumer clarity while letting competitors squarely claim much less territory under your name.

This framework appears clearly illustrated in *Apex Bank*: an unremarkable brand with no tie‑innate claim from registrants across most credit‑card categories warranted standard defense rather than exclusionary reach—even though “Aspire” itself cannot be read <em>solely</em> through conversational association methods.

---

In summary, *commercial strength* hinges chiefly on demonstrable market saturation by comparable initials on analogous services—and an already unique name does not shield against this check if other brands have already saturated that niche space.*

### "Similar Goods" Under DuPont — Should Non‑Identical Goods Be Included?
Question:
"In sixth‑factor analysis this case required assessing only "credit‑card" commerce.
cmpared toward previous cases Olde Tyme Foods &amp; Juice Generation Plaintiff,
some decisions allow broad inclusion such
as "banking related" products.
skipping non-card financial terms caused errors.
Should courts apply rule problematically naive;
and contingent enough from precedent show bundling back extendilar thus require **at least technically homogeneous** "goods" rather than purely identical.
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
No.  The Seventh–of–DuPont “similarity” standard does not restrict courts to **only identically‑named goods** – it requires a comparison of *similar* goods/services, exactly as the court explained when overturning ACC’s interpretation in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* The Sixth factor therefore may and should include non‑identical but sufficiently related marks that would confound consumers when viewed in light of the factual finding (made in Factor II) that the parties’ services are “highly similar.”

---

### 1. What “similar” means under DuPont  

In *DuPont* itself (476 F.2d 1357):

> “The second DuPont factor assesses the similarity of the parties’ goods and/or services…the evidence need only establish that ‘the respective products are related in some manner.’”

*(See also Coach Servs., Inc.*) This language makes it clear that identity is **not** required—merely a relationship sufficient to give rise to confusion.^[1]

Apex echoed this principle when reviewing Factor II:

> “Because … the dictionary definitions for ‘banking,’ ‘bank,’ and ‘finance’ encompass extending credit or providing funds through the issuance…the parties’ services are ‘legally identical, in part.’” (​J.A.​‑27–28)

Thus on appeal Apex was correct in relying on a broader notion of similarity than literal copy‑cat resemblance.^[2]

---

### 2. How Apex applied this broader view  

The appellate court rejected CC Serve’s *“excessive narrowing”* error on account of two facts proven by Retrospective Evidence :

1. **Same factual universe** – Factually established (§ III.A) that CC Serve’s credit‑card services **and** Apex’s banking/financing services are legally overlapping (“highly similar”).
2. **Same scope for all DuPont factors** – Because Fact II already narrowed down what counts as “similar,” a Sixth‑factor analysis cannot re‑open the issue or arbitrarily shrink it further.^[3]

Consequently, Ninth Circuit said :

> “When analyzing the sixth DuPont factor…the Board restricted … to those relating to *credit‑card* services … [that] was an error … We hold that…the sixth DuPont factor does not require identical goods—only *similar* ones.”  

*(J.A.​‑47)*

---

### 3. Other precedents reinforce this approach  

*Olde Tyme Foods v.* Roundy’s (Fed Cir 1992) and *Juice Generation v.* GS Enters (Fed Cir 2015) both held that a court may look at third‑party marks covering **related** items—not merely exactly identical ones—even though those items were not literally certain new categories flagged by Registration Marks or commonly used terms.^[4]

The Ninth argued these decisions parallel its own **not** “identical” requirement but “similar,” directly mirroring Fact II’s logic.\n\n---

### 4. What despite this doesn’t apply here?  

Apex reminded us that confusion can arise even where offerings differ tangibly—“non-card financial terms…”—but *only if those differences do not sever their relation* when evaluated alongside Fact II’s finding of substantial overlap.^[5] Thus an arbitrarily strict “technical homogeneity” test would conflict with DuPont’s principle stated above and would undermine an integral part of trademark law’s flexibility toward modern service bundles.\n\n---

## Bottom line

The Federal Circuit rightly demanded a **broader comparison** under Factor VI—one anchored by Fact II’s identification of *highly similar* offers—and did not insist on identical concrete goods or services held within each mark’s registration field. Courts applying § 21 Lanham Act therefore may evaluate any third‑party marks whose associated products/services are reasonably *sufficiently alike*, even if those items are not word‑for‑word duplicates or same named categories posted by UPCs or registered mark identifiers.\n\n> *“The sixth DuPont factor does not require identical goods—only similar ones.”* *(J.A.​‑47)*\n\nThis interpretation remains consistent with prior circuit precedent and preserves judicial flexibility without jeopardizing consumer clarity.\n\n---\n\n^[1] *DuPont DeNemours & Co.*, 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A.__1973). \
^[2] *Apex*, J.A.​‑27–28 (as cited). \
^[3] Id.; see also § III.B re vacating Sixth & First factors given Fact~II’s findings.\n\n^[4] See *Olde Tyme Foods*, 961 F.2d 200 (Fed Cir 1992); *Juice Generation*, 794 F.3d 1338 (Fed Cir 2015). \
^[5] Apex claimed “not directly competitive…not providing banking services itself,” which court deemed unavailing because fact-based assessment already showed overlap.^(J.A.​‐35).
