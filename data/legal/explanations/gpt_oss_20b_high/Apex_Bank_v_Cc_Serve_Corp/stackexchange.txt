### "De Novo" Review on TTAB Opinions Under §1052(d)(c): What Standards Apply?
Question:
"In Apex Bank v.&gt;CC Serve Corp., the Court says it will review the TTAB’s legal conclusions de&nbsp;novo while treating their findings of fact for substantial evidence based upon M!23Software v.- M!1Commc’ns...
I’m used to other appeals where either both law AND fact are examined together.
Why does an Opposition appeal use this particular split?
When can an appellate court respect a TTAB’s conclusion rather than overturn it?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
In a § 1052(d)(c) opposition appeal the Federal Circuit applies *de novo* review to every “legal” finding made by the TTAB—meaning every sentence that interprets or applies Lanham‑Act case‑law—and it applies a *substantial‑evidence* test only to those parts of the opinion that consist purely of “factual” determinations (the data upon which the legal conclusions rest).  
The court follows this split because Congress expressly authorized it in § 1052(d)(c): “The judgment shall be affirmed if…the Board has shown sufficient supporting evidence.”  When a ruling contains simultaneously legal interpretations *and* factual inferences, treating them as one continuum would make the appellate decision either too deferential (the board could ride on any low‑quality factual record) or too punitive (the board would have to re‑prove every fact).

Only when the alleged factual error fails the *substantial‑evidence* test does the appellate court overturn it—but it will voluntarily respect any finding already supported by enough appropriate evidence.  

---

### 1. Where does the statute put this split?

Section 1052(d)(c) tells us what must be reviewed (“the judgment…shall be affirmed...”).  The statute does **not** describe how—so courts have interpreted it through precedent.  

*M² Software Inc. v. M² Comm’ns*, 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed.Cir. 2006): APARTMENT – “[W]e review the Board’s legal conclusions **de novo**, and its *findings* of fact for *substantial evidence*.”  
This sentence is repeated verbatim in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, *cert.* page 5 under “Standard”.  

---

### 2. What counts as “legal” vs “factual”?

The Lanham Act treats likelihood‑of‑confusion as a **question of law**:
> “Likelihood … is a question **of law**, based on findings ... within each DuPont factor...”  
Here, two strands are visible:

| Strands | Illustrative text from Apex |
|---------|---------------------------|
| Legal | “Likelihood … **is a question … [with] findings…**” (§ II) |
| Factual | “Substantial evidence is ‘such relevant evidence as …‘” (§ II) |

The board’s job is thus two‑fold:
1. **Gather/record relevant frounds** about services, marks used, consumer habits… These are purely evidentiary.
   – Ex.: APEX’s five competitors listed Purchase J documents >10‑k pages (§ III.B).
2. **Apply those facts integraly vis‑à‑vis DuPont questions**, producing plain‑English conclusions about similarity, strength, distinctiveness etc.
   – Ex.: “Because ..., Apex and CC Serve’s services are highly similar.” (§ III.A).

Because Courts interpret “rule-making” as any evaluation imbued with doctrinal interpretation—whether you’re weighing how many letters differ or whether services overlap—their de‐novo obligation attaches only to step (2).  Step (1), though compulsory for making any rule at all, does not compel courts to re‑synthesize every datum anew—they only check whether what was recorded satisfied *substantial* proof requirements (“___relevant ___evidence ___adequate…”).

---

### 3. Why do opposition appeals adopt this particular split?

*Statutory motivation.*  
Section 1052(c), integrated into § 1052(d), aims at efficiency while preserving individual rights under trademark protection.
By letting **law be checked fully upward while safeguarding factual context**, appellate courts conserve judicial resources yet guard against erroneous interpretations born from flimsy data gaps (commonly found in quick‐field addendums).  

*Apex illustrates two conceptual reasons.*

1. **Doctrine says “likelihood‑of‑confusion” needs proof first; interpretation second** – so phenomena like discovering file X = Document Y do not demand full predicate consistency but must be materially sound before interpretation emerges (§ III.B).
   - This explains why *Apex* affirms all second‑factor reasoning ("the Board did not err")—the data were already plenty enough (see § III.A).
   
2. **Recursion avoided** – An opposition opinion analyzes more than one DuPont factor in sequence until confusion becomes apparent (“The tissue might logically fit.”). Reversing *all* finds would mean repeating What can be assumed (“If state X = no mistake … then consumer Y must scroll”) which defeats purpose parameters described by *M² Software*:  
   > “…each DuPont factor presents a question **of fact**, findings ... tested for substantial evidence.”

### 'Likelihood of Confusion' Under §1052(d): How Is It Different From Ordinary Infringement?
Question:
"The opinion cites Congress requiring registration refusal when ‘confusion is likely because...”
But in ordinary infringement cases I usually see source identification, distinctiveness tests etc.
I’d like clarity on whether Limiting only *concurrent use* changes what proofsa must be presented
and whether statutory differentings such as inherent distinctiveness carry over.
How do courts interpret Likelihood‑of‑Confusion here?
Is it more stringent than infringement?
Answer:
**Answer**

A “likelihood‑of‑confusion” analysis under § 102 α(d) («conference voting») turns out **to be almost identical**—in substance—to what courts do when they decide an ordinary infringement claim under the Lanham Act (§ 4(a)(1)).  
What differs are *when* the analysis occurs, **what evidence matters most**, and how much *proof* a party must supply in order for the TTAB – rather than a court – to reach a decision.

---

### 1. Statutory framework

| Issue | Ordinary infringement case | Registrar’s § 102 α(d) refusal |
|-------|-----------------------------|--------------------------------|
| Trigger | Defendant’s actual use that would confuse an **existing** owner | Applicant’s proposed registration *concurrently* with an existing user |
| Standard | “Consumers will be confused into thinking two marks are from one source” | Same Boolean query: “confusion is likely **because** of concurrent use” |
| Evidence burden | Proof of consumer confusion (often paid‑survey evidence), proof of real‑world similarity | Same convergence test but judged *de novo* by the court — no “pre‑ponderance” needed unless a specific finding requires “substantial evidence.” |

Because § 102 α(d) sits inside **Section 106**’s administrative remit, **the excerpt from Apex Bank v.*CC Serve* faithfully shows it:**  
> “A trademark opposition under Section 2(d)...requires…registration refusal when ‘confusion is likely because …corresponding…use’” (*J.A.* 63).  
> “Likelihood‑of‑confusion ... based on findings under the DuPont factors.” (*M2 Software*, “Each factor presents a fact…substantial eviden….”)

Thus, even though it occurs before a registry exists, its testing ground – the thirteen DuPont factors – remains unchanged.

---

### 2. What varying proofs matter

#### Court‑facing ordinary action

• **Source identification:** Evidence that consumers normally associate each party’s goods/services with its own source (the so‑called *“co‑presence”* test).  
• **Idea–expression blend:** Whether consumers confuse two claims because they’re seen together in commerce at any specific location or event.  
• **Consumer survey data:** Demonstrates who has identified each mark’s origin with sufficient probability (>70% isn’t a rule‑but‑good evidence).  
• **Commercial strength/secondary meaning:** If an ordinarily weak mark has acquired commercial strength through long‑term use then its protection widens (*use‐strength analysis*).

#### Regulatory section 21 refuse

The TTAB relies mainly on **the same DuPont facts**, absent transaction histories or sales volumes:

1. **Similar appearance/sound/connotation** (“first factor”).  
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;- Applies directly from *Apex*: “the first DuPont factor…shows ‘similarity’.”  
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;- No pair‐wise cognitive survey required; just factual comparison can satisfy this element—e.g., marketing literature side table compares letters/sounds visually.  

2. **Similarity of services/goods** (“second factor”).  
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;- *Apex* detailed extensive dictionary definitions (“credit card services” vs “banking & financing”) proving high similarity—even though CC Serve actually only manages cards through banks, not issues credit product itself (see *B.*).

3. **Number/diversity of competing marks** (“sixth factor”).  
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;- Here *Apex* found appellate error because total set missed brands beyond directing credit cards (“consumers … entailed … credit card services”). Consequently, vacated & remanded for considering all marks tied to corresponding services — demonstrating courts can apply this factor with broader breadth than once thought safe because they were comfortable when itemizable proof exists nearby.

4. **Commercial strength vs weakness** — *Apex* invoked Bienen sterling nature: "*CC Serve has not shown ... commercial strength … so[…” national scope persists for inherently distinctive marks*" (*J.A.* 51).  
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;- Even though this matters less where reference builds around “normally” held positions rather than ownership exclusivity database style deference required in private cases.

Thus that familiarity means applicants usually supply:
* A brief explanation/documentation defining their service line(s),
* Comparative advertising excerpt(s),
* Register/Trademark ID Manual/USPTO term lists,
and occasionally third‑party registrations where they choose.

Nothing akin to consumer testimony or market research curves becomes standard unless a party seeks exact slope data over competitor brands – which would have been considered improper explication itself *if outweighed by doubt.*

### 'Legal Identity' Between Credit Card Services and Banking/Financing Services
Question:
"The Court concluded CC Serve’s credit‑card services are ‘legally identical’ with Apollo’s banking's.
Apex argued CC Serve doesn’t actually issue cards—it partners banks.
I struggle with how legal identity versus economic interchange was applied.
Does extending credit define equivalence?
What evidentiary basis supports treating two seemingly distinct arrangements alike?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* the courts had no difficulty finding that *credit–card* services and *banking‑and‑financing* services were “legally identical” once they set aside the fact that CC Serve simply acts through partner banks rather than printing its own cards.  The underlying reason—that both sets of services hinge on **the extension of credit**—is recognized by trademark law when authorities apply DuPont’s *second* factor (similarity of goods/services).

---

### 1. What does “legally identical” mean here?

The doctrine here is one already articulated by *DuPont* itself: two sets of goods or services are considered *identical* if **the essential functions performed are the same**, regardless of whether those functions are executed directly or through contractors (§2(a)(i) DuPont).  
Hughes writes:

> “Because the dictionary definitions for ‘banking,’ ‘bank,’ and ‘finance’ encompass extending credit or providing funds through **the issuance of credit cards**, Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are ‘legally identical, in part.’” **[J.A., 27‑28]**

In plain terms, both parties are offering consumers access to borrowed money whose use is governed by the same signatory contracts and regulatory oversight—even though CC Serve relies on partner banks for physical card issuance and transaction processing (*J.A., 25‑26*).  
The court follows standard practice when treating contractual structure as immaterial so long as consumers perceive one common source behind those transactions—an approach often employed when judging whether an “accessory” provider can be de‑emphasized relative to its primary partner (*see M2 Software*, *Juice Generation*, etc.).

---

### 2. Does strikingly **extending credit** establish equivalence?

Yes—and precisely because **“extending credit”** falls squarely within *both* definitional umbrellas issued by USPTO’s trademark‐ID Manual at the time involved (*credit card services* includes “issuing…credit cards…[and] managing … accounts,” see *J.A., 25*).  
The amended section was treated directly as a matching category against banking‑and‑financing when considering public perception (§A).  

Extending rank gives each applicant a shared core benefit (borrowing authority), which raises a possible source‑confusion problem no matter how structurally different their organizational setups appear (*J.A., 24‑28*).

---

### 3. What evidentiary ground allowed the Board—and later the Court—to regroup seemingly different arrangements?

The Board relied primarily on three sources:

| Source | Relevance | Citation |
|--------|-----------|----------|
| **Dictionary/Trademark ID Manual description** | Defines the acceptable statutory lineages tied up with issuing finance products | *J.A., 25–26* |
| **Service lists drawn from each company’s filings** | Both parties list exactly interchangeable activities—“issuing,” “processing,” “authorizing,” “managing accounts,” etc.| *J.A., 24–26* |
| **Examination findings about overlap** | The notion that *legal* categories ‑ banking / finance ‑ naturally incorporate credit‐card programs (see proof made equalities equally upheld in other cases such as *Bose Corp.* pending affirmation).* | *J.A., 27–28* |

These data-showed no objective feature separating “credit card issuers” strictly from “banks” when judged purely on declared duties supported by statutory guidance.’ Thus even after considering<|reserved_200860|>-term distinctions concerning economics/economic interchange (“Econ.”)—the method records market penetration through third‑party marks—which were later examined separately (*sixth DuPont*), those changes did not affect *factor 2*: if people think either part can provide you a line leveraging borrowed money, it creates likelihood-of-confusion irrespective of whether you see an independent issuer or just an intermediary banker performing tasks on your behalf (`“whether consumer confusion arises…if contact can lead back”`).  

---

## Bottom line

1. **Extending currency** under any recognizable substantive label (`bank`, `finance`, `credit‑card`) produces all that's needed for Deputy concern over potential consumer misattribution (**legal identity**).  
2. Whether CC Serve prints its own plastic card or hires another bank has no bearing on this determination so long as both rely upon similar regulatory frameworks governing loans/card use – which was proven by explicit description lines earlier than any acceptance bias (*J.A., 25–26*, *27–28*).  
3. All sketchy findings were kept strictly bound to established evidence sources cited above—no extra presumptions were appended beyond what designates goods/services —consistent with de‑novo review limits specified in appellate guidelines (*M2 Software*, etc.).

So yes—the Court was justified in treating both sets of arrangements alike under a basic principle from trademark law and referenced sufficient documentary proof grounded in name‑service catalogs plus concrete declared duties performed together shaping one common financial umbrella.“”

### "Too Narrow" Analysis Under DuPont’s Sixth Factor
Question:
"Because graduation appeals went forward w/ defences overcame : an excessive focus only on other credit‑card–service marks
 led appellate court vacating sixth‑factor findings.

Answer:
**Answer**

Apex Bank’s appeal succeeds because the Board failed to use a *consistent* definition of “similar goods” when it applied Du Pont’s sixth factor—an error that defeats a finding made under the second factor and forces a new look at first‑factor commercial impression. Below I break down why this matters, how consistency is required by law, and what an appellant can do so its arguments work across all factors.

---

### 1. Why a “narrower” universe matters even after Factor II held services were highly similar

*The Second Du Pont Factor* […]. *“Because … *credit‑card services* … are legally identical … Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are highly similar”* (Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., Fed Cir., J.A.s 24–28, 33–34).  
The Board treated this finding as **fact** – under *M2 Software* it is reviewed de novo for law but under *conspicuous evidence* for fact (*consolidated definition established by the Court*).

If courts then insist on a more restrictive set of third‑party marks in evaluating the *Sixth Factor*, they undermine this earlier fact that *both sets of services occupy essentially the same market*. The Sixth Factor asks:

> “The number and nature of **similar marks** in use on **similar goods**.” (*Cited text*.)

The Court noted “When analyzing…the Sixth Factor…the Board restricted its universe…to credit‑card services…An error.” (*paragraph starting “When analyzing…sixth”*, J.A.s 47 onward).  
Because § II had already established **“highly similar”** service groups—including credit‑card issuance **plus** core banking functions—the explicit limitation violates two principles:

1. **Integrated Fact Pattern:** The same facts (“services are highly similar”) explain both consumer‐confusion clues in #II and #VI.
2. **Independent Heterodox Application:** Changing scopes arbitrarily fractures these facts into incompatible domains—something bar exams routinely test against (*Olde Tyme Foods*, *Juice Generation* rulings cited in opinion).

Hence, any Ninth Circuit analysis highlighting narrowed context cannot stand while previously accepted facts favored mutual similarity—resulting in vacatur of #VI findings (*J.A.s.* 67–69).

---

### 2. Does changing scope break independence among “similar goods”?

Yes—in practice every Du Pont element relies on a shared thread describing which public purchases which kind of product or service under which mark they might confuse consumers about source attribution (*Judge Hughes† note relay §III.C*).  

Rules governing these elements give no latitude for one element’s material definition to deviate markedly from another without solid legal justification (§VII.B cites *Olde Tyme Foods*’s articulation that “sixth…does not require identical goods”). A board who decouples #VI from #II thereby commits an arbitrary fragmentation:

* It contravenes de novo review even though §1052(d) reciprocity claims both were assessed simultaneously.
* It jeopardizes any coherent linguistic‑economic synthesis required by subsequent elements like #I (“commercial impression”), which depend on how widely or narrowly similar emerging competitors appear in commerce.*

The Court explicitly forbids this split—referring to *M2 Software*’s principle that “Each [Du Pont] decision presents evidential facts tested later,” noting a new Scope would require evidence otherwise absent.​**[See note b]**

### "Commercial Strength" -- What Does It Mean For Protection Of Inherently Distinctive Marks?
Question:
"The decision states CC Serve had no particularly strong commercial presence,
and without weakness count it receives normal scope protective range.
What criteria courts weigh when determining commercial strength?
Is it based primarily on fame/
evidence (e.g., number registrations)—or consumer survey data?
given inherent uniqueness dy free association does it outright override weakness?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* the court treated “commercial strength” as a fact‑finding issue carried out under Du Pont factor 6 – “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.”  The decision makes very clear that *inherent distinctiveness* does **not** eliminate the need for a separate analysis of how “strong” a mark actually is in its market context.

### 1. What constitutes “commercial strength” per this opinion?

> “Evidence of third‑party use … is relevant … to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” *(Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., 2025 Fed. Cir.*)*

The court therefore evaluates whether third‑party registrants have established a **contiguous body** (or *voluminous*, *considerable*, or *ubiquitous* level) of additional use that could erode ordinary consumer recognition of the original mark’s source identity. If such a body exists, the mark may lose some breadth under its inherent distinctiveness standard; if it does **not**, ordinary protection applies even when the mark is uniquely composed (as “Aspire” is).

### 2. Evidence courts typically weigh

Although *Apex Bank* does not exhaust every possible indicator, it signals which types are habitually accepted:

| Criterion | How it appears in the *Apex* analysis |
|-----------|---------------------------------------|
| **Numerical volume** | The board counted nine “Aspire‑formative” credit‑card marks among more than forty‑two related registrations—none reached “considerable” weight under prior decisions*(Jack Wolfskin v…)* |
| **Scope/coverage** | The board judged each competing mark’s specific service field (“credit card services”) rather than broader banking categories because those were deemed *similar* goods under §2(d).* |
| **Public awareness / reach** | Implicitly assessed by asking whether other users could be deemed “widely known.” No proof was offered that CC Serve’s own public exposure was significant enough to signal statewide or national familiarity.* |
| **Consumer surveys** | Not invoked directly in *Apex*, but typically recognized by precedent *(e.g., Jack Wolfskin v…)* as admissible evidence “to determine whether consumers can distinguish between similarly named marks.”* |

#### Bottom line

The court’s judgment rests primarily on **third‑party registration and usage data**—the number and prominence of other marks within the same service category—rather than sheer fame or advertising title counts alone. Consumer‑survey data would form part of those evidentiary packets but was absent here due either to insufficient request by COAX or because CP’s petition did not obtain such records.

### 3. How this relates to inherent uniqueness

The court makes it explicit that inherent distinctiveness *doesn’t override* weaknesses:

> “CC Serve has not shown ... any particular commercial strength … [yet] APEx ... has not shown ... [that] its mark is commercially ... weak … therefore CC Serve’s mark ... is entitled … ‘normal scope’ … accorded inherently distinctive marks.” *(id., §III.B)*

So an inherently distinctive trademark retains its broad nominal ownership unless factor six shows loss-of-strength through widespread similarity work among competitors—exactly what APEx attempted but failed because those three‑letter names “didn’t amount to considerable use.”

### 4. Practical takeaway for law students

- **First**, establish your mark’s inherent distinctiveness (or acquired secondary meaning).  
- **Next**, evaluate Du Pont factor 6 <em>independently</em>: list every reasonably comparable mark applied for/used on your own goods/services exactly as defined by §2(d).  
- **Then**, look at how many competitors actually dominate that space (“considerably used,” “ubiquitous,” etc.)—a high concentration usually shows weaker consumer ability to link your source identity.
- **If** you find no substantial body meeting those thresholds, you’ll receive ordinary protection even if your mark is truly unique.
- **If** you do find such a body, part‑of-the-mark’s breadth can be narrowed despite uniqueness—this preserves consumer clarity while letting competitors squarely claim much less territory under your name.

This framework appears clearly illustrated in *Apex Bank*: an unremarkable brand with no tie‑innate claim from registrants across most credit‑card categories warranted standard defense rather than exclusionary reach—even though “Aspire” itself cannot be read <em>solely</em> through conversational association methods.

---

In summary, *commercial strength* hinges chiefly on demonstrable market saturation by comparable initials on analogous services—and an already unique name does not shield against this check if other brands have already saturated that niche space.*

**Short answer:**  
No.  The Seventh–of–DuPont “similarity” standard does not restrict courts to **only identically‑named goods** – it requires a comparison of *similar* goods/services, exactly as the court explained when overturning ACC’s interpretation in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* The Sixth factor therefore may and should include non‑identical but sufficiently related marks that would confound consumers when viewed in light of the factual finding (made in Factor II) that the parties’ services are “highly similar.”

---

### 1. What “similar” means under DuPont  

In *DuPont* itself (476 F.2d 1357):

> “The second DuPont factor assesses the similarity of the parties’ goods and/or services…the evidence need only establish that ‘the respective products are related in some manner.’”

*(See also Coach Servs., Inc.*) This language makes it clear that identity is **not** required—merely a relationship sufficient to give rise to confusion.^[1]

Apex echoed this principle when reviewing Factor II:

> “Because … the dictionary definitions for ‘banking,’ ‘bank,’ and ‘finance’ encompass extending credit or providing funds through the issuance…the parties’ services are ‘legally identical, in part.’” (​J.A.​‑27–28)

Thus on appeal Apex was correct in relying on a broader notion of similarity than literal copy‑cat resemblance.^[2]

---

### 2. How Apex applied this broader view  

The appellate court rejected CC Serve’s *“excessive narrowing”* error on account of two facts proven by Retrospective Evidence :

1. **Same factual universe** – Factually established (§ III.A) that CC Serve’s credit‑card services **and** Apex’s banking/financing services are legally overlapping (“highly similar”).
2. **Same scope for all DuPont factors** – Because Fact II already narrowed down what counts as “similar,” a Sixth‑factor analysis cannot re‑open the issue or arbitrarily shrink it further.^[3]

Consequently, Ninth Circuit said :

> “When analyzing the sixth DuPont factor…the Board restricted … to those relating to *credit‑card* services … [that] was an error … We hold that…the sixth DuPont factor does not require identical goods—only *similar* ones.”  

*(J.A.​‑47)*

---

### 3. Other precedents reinforce this approach  

*Olde Tyme Foods v.* Roundy’s (Fed Cir 1992) and *Juice Generation v.* GS Enters (Fed Cir 2015) both held that a court may look at third‑party marks covering **related** items—not merely exactly identical ones—even though those items were not literally certain new categories flagged by Registration Marks or commonly used terms.^[4]

The Ninth argued these decisions parallel its own **not** “identical” requirement but “similar,” directly mirroring Fact II’s logic.\n\n---

### 4. What despite this doesn’t apply here?  

Apex reminded us that confusion can arise even where offerings differ tangibly—“non-card financial terms…”—but *only if those differences do not sever their relation* when evaluated alongside Fact II’s finding of substantial overlap.^[5] Thus an arbitrarily strict “technical homogeneity” test would conflict with DuPont’s principle stated above and would undermine an integral part of trademark law’s flexibility toward modern service bundles.\n\n---

## Bottom line

The Federal Circuit rightly demanded a **broader comparison** under Factor VI—one anchored by Fact II’s identification of *highly similar* offers—and did not insist on identical concrete goods or services held within each mark’s registration field. Courts applying § 21 Lanham Act therefore may evaluate any third‑party marks whose associated products/services are reasonably *sufficiently alike*, even if those items are not word‑for‑word duplicates or same named categories posted by UPCs or registered mark identifiers.\n\n> *“The sixth DuPont factor does not require identical goods—only similar ones.”* *(J.A.​‑47)*\n\nThis interpretation remains consistent with prior circuit precedent and preserves judicial flexibility without jeopardizing consumer clarity.\n\n---\n\n^[1] *DuPont DeNemours & Co.*, 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A.__1973). \
