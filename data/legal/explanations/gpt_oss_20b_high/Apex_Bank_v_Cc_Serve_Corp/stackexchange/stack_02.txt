### 'Likelihood of Confusion' Under §1052(d): How Is It Different From Ordinary Infringement?
Question:
"The opinion cites Congress requiring registration refusal when ‘confusion is likely because...”
But in ordinary infringement cases I usually see source identification, distinctiveness tests etc.
I’d like clarity on whether Limiting only *concurrent use* changes what proofsa must be presented
and whether statutory differentings such as inherent distinctiveness carry over.
How do courts interpret Likelihood‑of‑Confusion here?
Is it more stringent than infringement?
Answer:
**Likelihood‑of‑Confusion under § 1052(d) versus ordinary infringement**

| Issue | Ordinary infringement litigation | § 1052(d) opposition |
|-------|-----------------------------------|----------------------|
| **Occurring during** | A dispute after both parties have already been using their marks | A request to bar registration while both parties’ marks are still pending |
| **Key factual element** | Whether *any* current or future consumer would be confused into thinking the defendant’s product/service comes from the plaintiff **or** whether *conflict exists on co‑existing grounds** | Whether *confusion is likely because two parties are using (or intend to use) conflicting marks **concurrently** on their respective goods/services |
| **Standard applied** | De novo assessment of “likelihood of confusion” under the *usual* Du Pont factors (Friedman style), sentencing after trial‑circuit facts | De novo assessment too, but only on facts that are **relevant** to “concurrent use” (see Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., J.A. 63–64). A court reviews the Board’s findings through **substantial‑evidence** test rather than a pure “trial weight” screening |
| **Burden and evidence required** | Plaintiff shows *who* produced each good/service (*source identification*), *how* they are marketed together/ separately (*market overlap*), whether consumers actually have become confused (*surveys/consumer testimony*), plus intrinsic mark strength (*distinctiveness*) | At least two things must be shown:

1. A *prior‐user right*. The applicant must prove that another party has used its mark before any acquisition of priority by the applicant
1. That consumer markets overlap sufficiently so that *consistency matters*. (“Consumers … would likely mistakenly believe that their services come from one source.”)

If either element fails, prejudice defeats registration—there’s no requirement thereafter to identify who enabled confusion or gather post‑adoption evidence such as adoption‐based surveys (§ 1700 filing thresholds) – see Apex Bank v.—Etl.* APex had already identified this need for them – J.A.—                                   
| **Distinctive / inherent nature** | Inverted second factor (Du Pont 6). A highly distinctive mark can claim wider protection if it shows commercial strength; otherwise it may enjoy a *narrow shield*. | The same principle applies at registration time—all thirteen Du Pont factors work together unless omitted by board (“only those relevant …”). The court showed ␛ 6’s focus was “strength” versus “weakness” even though these were coupled with typical inference rules (see Apex v.—J.A.^51). Hence an inherently distinctive mark retains its *qualitative advantage* throughout opposite‑use proceedings |

### Key Holds From Apex

1. **“Likelihood…is a question of law…based on findings of relevant underlying facts …the Du Pont factors.”**  
   – Apex v.—J.A.^63–64

   This shows that both contexts start with the same set of analytical questions—even though an opposition delimits foci narrower around concurrent use especially if “the services are highly similar.” 

2. **“A trademark opposition … requires registration refusal when ‘confusion is likely because of concurrent use’.”**  
   – Apex v.—J.A.^62–65

   The statutory language forces courts to treat optional connections between vocabularies and marketing channels as disproportional consequences unless there's real market overlap that could induce confusion.

3. **“The Board found no strong evidence that CC Serve’s mark was weak... so it was entitled to ‘the normal scope’ accorded inherently distinctive marks.”**  
   – Apex v.—J.A.^51

   This mirrors ordinary infringement law where inherent distinctiveness usually wins unless commercial context demonstrates weakness.

### Practical Implications

1. **Limitations on Evidentiary Scope**
   - Opa­tions rely heavily on documents already in Board files – press releases, sales reports, certificates you submit yourself—there's no broad discovery as typical firearm markets permit inter‑party questioning in lawsuits.
   
2. **Evidence Needed**
   - Only data that matters specifically to *concurrent use*—if you’re simply beating a different party farther down an industry chain outside your target market your submissions lose weight.
   - Courts will not look beyond this core base simply because another market segment uses “Aspire.” The Ninth Circuit excavated all ancillary data when struggle persisted between bank‑centric vs broadly financial uses (**see paragraph IV.B**).

3. **Minimal Proof vs Comprehensive Divergence**
   - In ordinary cases your prosecution file alone rarely suffices—you may have acquired additional real‑world confusion data after policing your brand’s shelf presence.
   - Under §§ 5–7(a)(4)(B), once you prove concurrent ownership you’re automatically entering a high‑stakes environment where lack of actual consumer probing may win out—the regulator leans toward defense by hope we concede competitor’s separate claims will stand unless specific conflict records exist (*see ex parte Y.*).

4. **Are Standards More Stringent?**
   - Not necessarily—APEX Bank teaches us that likelihood isn’t stripped down into narrower categories—it still must satisfy precedent-based tests akin tightness.
   - However because inversions focus solely on *concurrent usage*, courts may **accept presumed discomfort**, especially when securities like “normal scope” apply automatically for inherently distinctive trademarks —16 Examining Sebastian repeatedly found stronger protection there.
   - The opposite face implies sometimes less evidential scrutiny relative to one‑party litigation where tolerance across markets shapes creative fact finding—the suspicion fades into »no concrete proof required after appropriation».

In short:

- **Both contexts rely upon Du Pont hierarchy** – nothing new is created by adding statutory wording.
- **But an opposition limits factual relevance strictly to co‑existence/collide markets**, thereby making *source identification* critical while optionally sidelining extraneous debate over unrelated usage patterns.
- Inherent distinctiveness continues as a protective advantage at Registration Stage just as it does after ignoring actual market share debates in litigation—this is why Apex’s court engaged Factor 6 as still playing central role rather than dispensing separate defense criteria;

Thus while your usual surprise continues at trial preparation—proving actual consumer misconception or weighing rival brand presence—the upper tier restriction enforced here merely narrows terrain rather than imposing additional half­gates against “ordinary” likelihood analysis.
