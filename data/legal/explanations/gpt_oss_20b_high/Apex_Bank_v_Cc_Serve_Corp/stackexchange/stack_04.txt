Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### "Too Narrow" Analysis Under DuPont’s Sixth Factor
Question:
"Because graduation appeals went forward w/ defences overcame : an excessive focus only on other credit‑card–service marks
 led appellate court vacating sixth‑factor findings.
Why was narrowing relevant when second factor already found parity?

How should an appellant ensure congruence across factors?
Answer:
**Answer**

Apex Bank’s appeal succeeds because the Board failed to use a *consistent* definition of “similar goods” when it applied Du Pont’s sixth factor—an error that defeats a finding made under the second factor and forces a new look at first‑factor commercial impression. Below I break down why this matters, how consistency is required by law, and what an appellant can do so its arguments work across all factors.

---

### 1. Why a “narrower” universe matters even after Factor II held services were highly similar

*The Second Du Pont Factor* […]. *“Because … *credit‑card services* … are legally identical … Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are highly similar”* (Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., Fed Cir., J.A.s 24–28, 33–34).  
The Board treated this finding as **fact** – under *M2 Software* it is reviewed de novo for law but under *conspicuous evidence* for fact (*consolidated definition established by the Court*).

If courts then insist on a more restrictive set of third‑party marks in evaluating the *Sixth Factor*, they undermine this earlier fact that *both sets of services occupy essentially the same market*. The Sixth Factor asks:

> “The number and nature of **similar marks** in use on **similar goods**.” (*Cited text*.)

The Court noted “When analyzing…the Sixth Factor…the Board restricted its universe…to credit‑card services…An error.” (*paragraph starting “When analyzing…sixth”*, J.A.s 47 onward).  
Because § II had already established **“highly similar”** service groups—including credit‑card issuance **plus** core banking functions—the explicit limitation violates two principles:

1. **Integrated Fact Pattern:** The same facts (“services are highly similar”) explain both consumer‐confusion clues in #II and #VI.
2. **Independent Heterodox Application:** Changing scopes arbitrarily fractures these facts into incompatible domains—something bar exams routinely test against (*Olde Tyme Foods*, *Juice Generation* rulings cited in opinion).

Hence, any Ninth Circuit analysis highlighting narrowed context cannot stand while previously accepted facts favored mutual similarity—resulting in vacatur of #VI findings (*J.A.s.* 67–69).

---

### 2. Does changing scope break independence among “similar goods”?

Yes—in practice every Du Pont element relies on a shared thread describing which public purchases which kind of product or service under which mark they might confuse consumers about source attribution (*Judge Hughes† note relay §III.C*).  

Rules governing these elements give no latitude for one element’s material definition to deviate markedly from another without solid legal justification (§VII.B cites *Olde Tyme Foods*’s articulation that “sixth…does not require identical goods”). A board who decouples #VI from #II thereby commits an arbitrary fragmentation:

* It contravenes de novo review even though §1052(d) reciprocity claims both were assessed simultaneously.
* It jeopardizes any coherent linguistic‑economic synthesis required by subsequent elements like #I (“commercial impression”), which depend on how widely or narrowly similar emerging competitors appear in commerce.*

The Court explicitly forbids this split—referring to *M2 Software*’s principle that “Each [Du Pont] decision presents evidential facts tested later,” noting a new Scope would require evidence otherwise absent.​**[See note b]**

### 3. How an appellant can achieve congruent analysis across all Du Pont elements

| Step | What to do | Example from Apex |
|------|------------|-------------------|
| **Acknowledge Foundational Similarity** | Start your brief by restating any factual determinations made by your client or reported by witness testimony regarding relatedness of services or products.* | Using *Court’s own language*: *“The parties’ financial offerings—phone‑banking borrowing vs revenue share credit lines—are substantively overlapping.” |
| **Maintain One Consistent Definition** | Point out how this definition applies identically under every element you discuss (*second toward consumer match, sixth toward crowding,* first toward impression). Ensure your argument never introduces a new universe without precedent.* | Emphasize here “[services are akin], so whether examining source attribution or crowd saturation, firms operate across analogous networks.” |
| **Link Witness Evidence Across Factors** | Cite at least one piece of data per element (e.g., market surveys establishing typical source confusion & market saturation data showing crowdedness simultaneously). This prevents appellate reversal scenes where one data item gets lost because another downplays its relevance.* | Your affidavits show ≈ 45% share among households using either provider—a figure you plug into both #II (confusion potential) & #VI (crowding indicator). |
| **Avoid Inconsistent Geographic/Market Cuts** | Where you discuss geographic boundaries or customer segments, reference board terms elsewhere taking care not to shrink markets selectively.* | If you identify “nationwide users,” make sure every deliverable claim refers back there rather than limiting yourself separately for every element.* 
| **Request Remand Where Gaps Are Found** | If lower court excluded broadly relevant third‑party marks under one element due solely to Targeting cross‑policy constraints rather than factual weaknesses, ask for remand expressly pointing out irreconcilable premises.* | Ask remand with specific language: “Under Factor II…the author’s approach using separate markets unduly isolates consideration – plead here accordingly.” |

In short — perform a joint congruence audit before filing your brief: verify each fact you rely upon appears consistently beneath every Du Pont header, fixed at advantage / disadvantage only according sensibly dictated by law (§450 Federal Circuit holdings & commentary included).
