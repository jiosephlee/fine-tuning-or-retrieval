# Credit Cards Are Banks? Reexamining Service Definitions After *Apex* v. *CC Serve*

## Why This Decision Matters

A simple question—whether “credit‑card services” are truly a subset of “banking & financing”—may sound like nomenclature, yet it carries profound strategic weight in the trademark arena. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit ruled that these two labels are not simply linguistic niceties but are legally entwined so tightly that they invite “highly similar” assessments under the second DuPont factor governing likelihood of confusion. Consequently, an applicant who tries to trim their description narrowly—e.g., by filing a mark only for “internet banking” while ignoring co‑existent credit‑card offerings—does not escape scrutiny if part of its business mirrors a prior mark’s goods or services directly enough for consumers to assume common source.

The case underscores that when federal trademark law asks whether two marks are confusingly similar, it looks beyond statutory glossaries or intuitive taxonomies toward what ends up being offered in market reality. It reminds registrants that any gear room where consumers encounter both cash management steps—and effortlessly hop between them—constitutes “the same field.” For Office examiners, it serves as an explicit warning against over‑reliance on category names alone when evaluating a mark’s reach.

Below we unpack how *Apex* resolved each DuPont factor and lay out what this means moving forward for both maverick innovators seeking new brands and seasoned attorneys expected to defend them at appeal.

Drawing on language from *Coach Servs.*, *DuPont*, and earlier Office jurisprudence, the Board held that “credit‑card services” includes issuing cards as well as overseeing account activities—a bundle essentially identical to tasks ordinarily performed by banks (“extending credit or providing funds through issuance of credit cards”). Because both share these core activities, Apex could plausibly claim source overlap even if one category is named less explicitly than another.

The Court affirmed this interpretation of fact‐finding precision while stressing de novo review over legal conclusions—an echo all parties anticipate when dealing with intellectually begging statutes like §1052(d).
