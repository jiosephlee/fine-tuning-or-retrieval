# Lost Hashtags Study – Understanding Commercial Strength Through Third‑Party Use

## Why This Case Matters

In *Apex Bank v.\ CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit reminds us that a mark’s commercial strength is not simply “the lack of activity” when viewed through a cold–case lens. Instead, it demands careful attention to whether third‑party use truly competes on a *similar* field of goods or services. For companies building brands in crowded sectors—think fintech startups eyeing crumbling “aspire” names—this ruling warns against hastily treating an unfamiliar or underused sign as weak when established users show resilience against dilution attempts. As brands continue to launch online calendars of hashtag dreams—those “lost hashtags”—urging their audiences into deeper engagement, commercial impressiveness can be defined by *how* others actually use similar language within comparable markets.

## Factual Overview

CC Serve owned an inherently distinctive word mark “ASPIRE” for credit‑card services since 1996 and successfully registered it in 1998. Apex Bank announced a new internet‑banking venture under the word “ASPIRE BANK”, filing intent‑to‑use applications for both word and design marks in August 2019 aimed at banking and financing services—sectorally overlap points with CC Serve’s credit‑card business were stark enough that CC Serve opposed with a Section 2(d) action. The Trademark Trial & Appeal Board (TTAB) refused registration citing likelihood of confusion across several DuPont factors: especially similarity of goods/services (factor 2), appearance/connotation (factor 1), and third‑party marks’ market saturation (factor 6).

Apex appealed, arguing several TTAB findings were unlawful or insufficiently supported by evidence. Importantly:

| Factor | TTAB Finding | Appeal Argument |
|--------|--------------|----------------|
| Factor 2 | Services highly similar | Apex said they’re distinct |
| Factor 6 | Only nine “aspire” marks used in credit‑card space is not “considerable” | Apex noted many other “aspire” uses across finance |
| Factor 1 | Marks look similar given factor 6’s conclusion | If factor 6 changes, factor 1 may shift |

The Court affirmed factor 2 but vacated factors 6 and 1 after remand tasks centered on including *all* comparable marks use-case fields recognized by T & I board substantive reasonings covered under DuPont guidance *beyond* credit cards alone.

## Likelihood‑of‑Confusion Analysis Unpacked

### Bottom Line on Factor 2

The Board already applied DuPont’s criteria—that similarity may arise from related goods + marketing context—and found Cic∞ thus bordering with prior knowledge that credit cards *are* financing instruments facilitated by banks sometimes pushing bank agents plus payment-processing ecosystems. Congress intended this fixpoint: refusal was inevitable once two users engaged in **essentially identical** service sets—even if neither outright sold credit cards as primary product—but served them through partnerships viscous enough that end users could confound sources (`adding debt managers` pairings). Setting aside political debate over purely theoretical separability between “banking” vs “credit‐card issuance”, the Court disciplined Jacobs’ objection saying enough evidence existed for high similarity after examining mutual service enumerations via updated dictionary definitions plus cross‑posting examination hallmarks (e.g., word “credit”).

### The Real Gamble Lies With Factor 6

Factor 6’s challenge arose because CC Serve counted only nine concurrent “aspire” registrations tied specifically to **credit‑card** goods/services while ignoring dozens more within broader banking/financing categories—e.g., “Aspire Loans”, “Aspire Asset Management”, or `Aspire Payment Platforms`. Under well‑established precedent (*Juice Generation*, *Jack Wolfskin*), such breadth can strengthen an argument that an inherently distinctive mark remains robust even where multiple competitors exist: consumers can still parse out which company menus something like `Aspire` refers to if they see separate contexts framed distinctly by consumer group exposure patterns (`online retail x bank` vs `crowdfunding x personal loan`). Yet the TTAB restricted its field problematically while maintaining its sales similarities flourish (a form of double counting where effectiveness falls apart once evidential infiltration uncovered cross‑service synergy neurological patterns emerging).

The Court correctly rebuked that restriction under §1052(d) standards and advisor‐for‐analysis guidelines:

1. **Similarity** under DuPont *doesn't require identical goods*—the Court emphasised gauge entire service families.
2. Because Appellants had already found services highly to be equal **in their own analysis** (fact), any limitation imposed subsequently de facto untrustworthy.
3.The legal standard thereby required systematic rebalancing of *all* relevant marks showcasing entrepreneurship across overlapping sectors—not exclusively credit card governed classifies.
