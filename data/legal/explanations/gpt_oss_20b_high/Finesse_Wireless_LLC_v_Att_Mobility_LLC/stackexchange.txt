Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### "Why does the Fifth Circuit review denial of Judgment-as-a-Matter-of-Law de novo?
Question:
"I’m puzzled why appellate courts look at infringing judgments de novo instead giving respect—the jurisprudence cited Wi‑LAN confirms apps‑style lack persists.

Which exact bar holds judges?

Whether strong inference toward one-party judgment alone makes reasonable jurors incapable any contrary conclusion?

And how this fits civil trial standards?

Could someone clarify what exact threshold judges apply here?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The Fifth Circuit reviews a district court’s denial of judgment‑as‑a‑matter‑of‑law **de novo** because its own precedent—*Wi‑LAN Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed Cir. 2016)—teaches that an appellate court is required to make its own assessment of whether the record evidences one side or another before deciding whether the lower court erred by refusing an *motion for judgment as to noninfringement* (“JMOL”).  
The court *will reverse* only when it views **all admissible evidence in light most favorable to each side** and finds that

> “the evidentiary record points **so strongly and overwhelmingly** in favor of **one party that **reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.”*  
> —*Wi‑LAN*, 811 F.3d at 461

(See also *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, 2025 U.S. Appx., № 2024‑1039.)

That declaration sets the precise threshold—*universal swiftness* is never enough unless **no reasonable jury could disagree**—and frames how Fifth‑Circuit appellants must approach any violation or nonviolation when litigants ask for post‑trial remedy or a new trial on damages.



---

### Why *de novo* rather than “postponed” or “respectful” review?

1. **Jurisdictional mandate:** Under § 1295(a)(1) a federal appellate court reviews measures related to a *final judgment* *de novo*, except where specific rules restrict factual findings or jury verdicts (*reversible error*).  

2. **Nature of an order for JMOL:** An order denying an *motion for judgment as to noninfringement* balances *law and fact*. The petitioning party claims who infringed what proof suffices for enforcement (§ 101–104). Because that mix–mix involves *interpretation* (*claim construction*) *and potential inference from expert testimony*, the appellate panel treats it as a fresh evidentiary inquiry—hence “de novo.”  

3. **Policy prediction:** Some circuits attach “respective weight” or “triple consideration” to JMOL referrals when it meets certain strict criteria (e.g., Elec.*<|reserved_200466|>・etc). But every circuit follows Fed Cir.’s stipulation that rules will be invalidated only on an *overwhelming dictates*. That phrasing tells us why our Fourth/Third/Second Circuits treat certain statements about “strength” differently—they are dictated by their legislative histories rather than definitionally separate logos.



### Exact threshold set by Fifth Circular law

> “‘[We] review [denials] **only if**…the evidentiary record…points so strongly and overwhelmingly …reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.' – *Wi‑LAN*, 811 F.3d 461.”  

When applying this standard,

1. The appellate parchment looks at every admissible item—expert reports, witness testimony, demonstrative exhibits—as though freshly presented (**light most favorable**).

2. It compares them against what would be required for *substantial evidence*.  
   In *Finesse*, after evaluating Dr Wells’ testimony about sampling both “signals of interest” and “interference‐generating signals,” the Court concluded **no substantial support** was present because Dr Wells failed to map those two signals specifically onto their claim elements (**Id s.v., substantiate by referencing J.As.~14918–15019**).

3. If even after light‐most-favorable scrutiny none abound—if no balanced chain meets even minimal corroboration—the Court will overturn the district court’s denial on rights grounds.



### Clarifying ceasefire between “strong inference” and “reasonable juror”

Your second query concerns whether simply having some experiment leaning toward one set of facts suffices:

> **No** – mere preference matters as an inferential weight; it does not preclude alternative conclusions unless every admissible route burdens ignoring it reaches failure (**see *Johns Hopkins Univ.* v.~Datascope Corp.*, 543 F.Repr 1342–49; *Finesse* §I.B case narrative**).

A party’s appeal must show:

|Outcome|Evidence Availability|Judicial outcome|
|------|--------------------|----------------|
|Evidence balances starkly toward A | District likely found insufficient | Denial stands |
|Evidence weights sharply toward A yet still offers viable rebuttal | District can still support **juror inference** | Appeal can snap block |

So while it uses “strong” wording (“so strong and overwhelming”), you can't excused an unmet evidentiary argument vector just because another side has some plausible tie cracks.




### Relation to civil trial standard

The two benchmarks are interconnected:

|Standard|Purpose|
|-------|-------|
|Substantial Evidence (post‐trial rulings) | To satisfy defendant’s burden over extravagant attorneys claims – meaning facts presented could realistically lead a juror to vote that way (see *Apple v.~Wi‐LAN*, 25 F4th 960–969).|
|“So strongly points …reasonable juror cannot disagree” | Applicability to consent or refusal-defect rules which combine statutory clear phrasing with opinions – effectively says facts must be unambiguous before we grant breadth defences.|

In civil cases where pleadings settle disputes over rights — e.g., noninfringement or damages — appeals move under these two lenses simultaneously;
you are given shift `light most favorable`, then dig into whether any factual seam exists that contends with jury verdict—the analysis basically checks whether there exists another plausible factual path accepted by at least half **cautious experts/jurors** distinct from what you argued.


---

### Bottom line

1. The Fifth Circuit must independently reason through all possible facts when reviewing denied JMOL—a practice seeded by its own rule clause weighing evidentiary weight against possibility.
2. The operative threshold is more rigorous than merely “plausible inference.” It requires showing such strength that even after maximizing ambiguity any reasonable jury cannot find anything else.
3.The outcome therefore hinges on records being so complete—and sufficiently contradictory—that alone overtakes alternative narratives based on expert testimony or procedural resolution.


Feel free to ask more about how those distinctions shift your brief argument construction—especially if you’re contemplating motions based on bizarre mapping attempts like those Rosedown sets X₁/X₂ had with his construction proof!

### "What suffices substantive proof when an expert repeatedly contradicts himself?
Question:
"In this opinion Dr Wheelly gave conflicting opinions regarding interdigital maps.

District offered his statements adequate yet Johns Hopkins Univ.v.Datascope Corp shows courts refuse self–contradictory witnesses unless additional support exists
(
Johns Hopkins Univ.*v.*Datascope Corp.*,543 F​.3d134)

When an expert repeatedly contradicts himself—is contradiction alone enough sufficient compelling reasoning 
or does factual plasma required forever?
Answer:
**Short answer**

A self‑contradictory testimony is *not* sufficient on its own for a jury verdict.  
Under § 28 § 31 (and Five‑Circuit precedent), a party may rely on an expert’s
opinion only as long as *the overall record* provides “substantial evidence”
supporting that opinion—namely, consistent data, corroborating documentation,
or additional expert analysis that resolves any apparent contradictions.
When those extra pieces are missing, as in *Finesse Wireless v. AT&T* (2025),
the appellate court reinstated JMOL and vacated damages because the jury’s
inference rested on “confusing” and “non‑conclusive” remarks from Dr. Wells
without any supporting documentary evidence that clarified his mapping of
the signals.

---

## What the case says

In *Finesse*, the team relied almost exclusively on Dr. Wells’ testimony
that two signals—DL(TX) reference and “modeled PIM path”—were
respectively the “signals of interest” and the “interference‑generating
signals.”  J.A.s 14914–21 were `confusing` when he pivoted after cross‑examination
to say that those two signals were actually x1 and x2—inputs generated downstream
of the ADC.  The Court called this “a lot more confusing than any single
swarm of statements,” citing pages 15010–19.  Importantly:

> *“There is nothing clear about Dr. Wells’ testimony… he never testified
> x1 and x2 correspond to the ‘signals of interest’ and ‘interference‑generating
> signals.’”*  J.A. 15010–19
> 
> 
> Because no corroborating documentary evidence was offered – e.g., a schematic,
> lab data, or a separate expert who confirmed or corrected his mapping –  
> **the record contained no substantial evidence** to support that inference of infringement.*

The Court explicitly linked this conclusion to *Johns Hopkins Univ. v.
Datascope Corp.*, 543 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2008), where self‑contradictory
expert statements (“one device contacts vessel in 2 places … contrary to an earlier claim
of three”) were deemed insufficient for a reasonable jury verdict when no other
material clarified how many contacts actually occurred.

---

## Why mere contradiction isn’t enough

1. **Standard for infringement verdicts**  
   *25 F.~4th 960* sets forth “a factual finding is supported by substantial
   evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of its proponent in light
   of all material before it.”  Competent courts review such findings at trial;
   appellate courts consider whether *viewing the entire record most favorably toward*
   one side would still produce that conclusion (*Wi‑LAN*, 811 F.~3d 455).

2. **Expert reliability responsibility**  
   Under *Daubert* frameworks applied through Ninth‑Circuit rulings,
   an informant’s reliability hinges on **(i)** methodology, **(ii)** consistency,
   **(iii)** peer review, and **(iv)** verifiable data**.*  If an expert’s own opinions
   conflict with themselves or with verifiable facts, courts treat those statements as unreliable,
   but they will still allow them if other sources provide clarity (*Hong Kwan Yeung/Berkshire*
   contexts).  In *Finesse*, none such supporting sources were materialized.
   
3. **What appellate courts look for**  
   An appellate decision will typically:
   
   - determine whether alternative explanations exist in the record (*e.g.*, misprinted schematics);
   - evaluate whether opposing counsel proposed extrinsic verification (*e.g.*, secondary tests);
   - assess whether adjudication parties could reconcile contradictions with public science or industry standards.
   
   Lacking those ingredients—as was plainly noted by Chief Judge Moore—the court held there was no substantial basis for finding infringement (*see paragraph preceding §II*).

---

## Bottom line for your case

If your client faces similar objections:
   
| Element                               | What you must prove                     | How you can show it |
|---------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|---------------------|
| **Self‑contradictory claims**         | Contradiction alone ≠ sufficiency      | Provide corroboration—another qualified witness / lab results |
| **Mapping technical details**         | Clear correspondence between symbols<sup>†</sup> & theory | Explicit diagrams + calculation steps |
| **Undisputed limitation**             | Override denied by existing data & law | Exhibit peer‐reviewed publications or industry specs |

† *See *Finesse*, J.A.s 15010–19 (the Court noted Dr., W.) struggled even with basic labeling.*

Remember that substantiational proof means more than hearsay opinions—there must be material which showcases how your testable hypothesis actually works in practice and eliminates doubt raised by contradictory testimony.

---

### Take‑away

A conflicting witness damages credibility—but credibility loss only matters if it removes all other evidence capable of supporting a verdict.

The Federal Circuit made it unmistakably clear in *Finesse Wireless*:
 “If an expert’s own statements keep changing or remain ambiguous,
 and there’s no other admissible material clarifying them,
 then you cannot rely on those statements alone—
 the record contains insufficient substantial evidence.”

So equip your case with verifiable data—or risk JMOL reversed at appellate level just as it was here.*
