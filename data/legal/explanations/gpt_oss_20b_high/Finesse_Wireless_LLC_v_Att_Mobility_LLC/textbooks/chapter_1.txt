Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 1: # Case Background & Procedural History

The dispute between Finesse Wireless LLC (“Finesse”), AT & T Mobility LLC (“AT&T”) and Nokia of America Corporation (“Nokia”) centers around two U.S. patents owned by Finesse that cover methods for reducing intermodulation‑product (IMP) interference in radio receivers. Patent 7‑346‑134 (‘34) teaches a multi‑step procedure for isolating “signals‑of‑interest” from interference‑generating signals within a sampled passband and canceling IMPs using computed estimates derived from those sources through phase–amplitude adjustment loops. Patent 9‑548‑775 (‘77) further teaches an implementation strategy that digitally multiplies three signals—S₁, S₂ and S₃—in seven distinct third‑order combinations to generate IMP cancellation waves within a receiver whose transmitter‐components may already generate passive intermodulations (PIMs).  

In its complaint filed in the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas, Finesse asserted infringement against each specified claim subject‐matter carried out within mobile radios sold under AT&T’s commercial network contracts through Nokia’s chipset technology. As part of its litigation strategy, Finesse’s counsel identified components embedded in certain Nokia units sold exclusively to AT&T customers—the so called “PIM–cancellation” feature—which exact mechanistic steps align directly with claim limitations enumerated above (§1(a)–(f)) and (§1(d)).  

### Discovery Findings Supporting Infringement Allegations

During discovery Finesse obtained multiple internal technical documents issued by Nokia—including design schematics, implementation notes, and operational wiring diagrams—that described precisely how the chipset sampled received passbands using an RF analog‑to‑digital converter (RF ADC) while feeding both downlink transmission reference signals (“DL(TX)” prototype signals) and an autogenerated “model PIM” path into a closed‐loop estimator engine housed in the GROOT field‑programmable gate array (FPGA). These documents recurred several times throughout proceedings illustrating analog sampling paths matching limitation [a]–[e]('34)’s description as well as numerically grounded multiplications equivalently described in '77’ (§1(d)).  

An expert witness retained by Finesse obliquely accessed this documentation during pre-trial depositions and subsequently testified that these scheme elements fulfilled each sequential requirement embodied within both patents’ operative claim restrictions when translated into generic signal nomenclature used at trial ("signal(s) of interest"; “interference generating signal(s)"). The breadth of disclosed device architecture – particularly references indicating coexistence between transmitted carrier estimation circuitry ("cross Talk") alongside active PIM formation logic – provided conclusive proof demonstrative enough that cable developers at “Nokia” explicitly employed procedures consistent with those claimed methods when engineering radios configured exclusively for AT&T platforms.
