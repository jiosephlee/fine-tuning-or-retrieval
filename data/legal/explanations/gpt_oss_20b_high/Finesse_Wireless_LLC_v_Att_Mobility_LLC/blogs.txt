# The Federal‑Circuit Reaffirms De Novo Review When Denying Judgment as a Matter of Law  

## Why This Decision Matters  

In prevailing telecom patent wars a lost judgment‑as‑a‑matter‑of‑law (JMOL) against infringement provides an almost “clean break” for defendants—their trial judge effectively declares they win one day’s work without litigation costs pending further appeal. Conversely, plaintiffs who rely heavily on expert testimony find a JMS denial disastrous because once the court dismisses liability they are barred from appealing except by unusual correctives such as Remand or Post‑Judgment Discovery Relief, which rarely succeed when patents hinge upon specialized technical arguments.[^1]  

The Federal‐Circuit’s November ruling in **Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC** overturns both denied JMOLs and the related lump‐sum damages award worth $166 million issued by a Texas jury under *Wi‑LAN*’s de novo standard. Far beyond its own facts, the opinion clarifies how district courts must apply Dobbs’ doctrine in infringement cases involving opaque technology—a clarification that will reverberate throughout third‐party vendor disputes over next generation radio hardware.[^2]  

The decision reminds litigants that an appellate court will scrutinize both claim construction conclusions presented at trial **and** whether those conclusions rested on irreconcilably weak or contradictory expert testimony before asking whether any jury could reach an opposing conclusion.[^3]   

That level of scrutiny could affect future lawsuits in several ways:

| Aspect | Potential Impact |
|--------|-------------------|
| **Expert Preparation** | A single contradictory statement can threaten an entire infringement verdict—experts now face sharper expectations for technique consistency and documentary support[^4]. |
| **Claim Construction** | Judges must comply with their own circuits’ “de n/w\" regimes—particularly when districts defer to extrinsic evidence contrary to intrinsic indications.[^5] |
| **Damages Considerations** | Vacating damages only once infringement findings are settled eliminates “back‑loading” defendants who prefer high awards over uncertain appeals.[^6] |

In short, *Finesse* refocuses the contingency model around substantive factual determinacy rather than pleading bias—a shift aligning closely with contemporary policy debates over “patent trolls” who file litigative burdens purely using weak evidence.`

## De Novo Review Under BSD Circuit Canon 

The crux lies in how appellate courts treat denyances of JMOLs—all past opinions depended largely upon whether “clear error” prevailed or whether districts exercised discretion properly—but regional precedent increasingly imposes stricter review standards devoid of regulatory discretion.[^7]

### Wi‑LAN Inc. v Apple Inc.: A Turning Point  

The landmark Texas Decision established that any district judge who denies a defendant’s request for JMOL “must meet its burden de novo.” In practice it means a naive “distributor found no contingency” cannot stand except when every possible reasonable interpretation yields the same conclusion—to wit “the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly” one side.^[8]  

Subsequent Fifth–Circuit rulings adopted similar tests—”no reasonable jurors could doubt it.”[^9] In those courts judges do not sit back and weigh panels’ presumption muscle banditry versus defendants abuse‐of‐discretion writs except consult public record outcomes against venue expectations.^[10]

# Marriage Between Claims and Amendments: What “Three Signals” Add Up To

## Why This Case Matters

Patent owners who sharpen their claims by inserting subtle numeric limits—such as specifying “three signals” instead of “two or three”—must still prove that those limits truly cut back the claim scope during prosecution. The United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit’s decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* is a touchstone reminder that an amendment’s surface wording does not automatically translate into a narrower protection if the language has not been clarified as a clear abandonment of previously asserted embodiments. For holders of interference‑mitigation patents—or any technology hinging on fine‑grained numerical parameters—this ruling poses both a cautionary note about protracted litigation risk and a window into how the Court applies doctrine around prosecution histories.

## The Anatomy of Claim Amendments

At issue here was that Finesse amended its ’775 patent to recite a limit requiring “digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven third‑order multiplications in order to cancel passive intermodulation products. The original specification had allowed “two or three signals,” which could encompass the two‑signal configuration employed in Nokia radios accused of infringing the patentee’s claim set.

The central question was whether Finesse reduced its claim scope by tightening that limitation enough to disavow infringement based on the accused plan’s two‑signal implementation. Under § 112(c), an amendment must **be** a clear and unambiguous disavowal to constitute a legitimate prosecution‑history adjustment—that is, amending a claim clause must be followed by explicit prose where the patentee openly states that those embodiments are no longer sought after by the invention (see *Crown Castle* [explanatory pre‑amble].) The rule accounts for defensive drafting necessities while preventing parties from later skirting around claims they previously claimed to have abandoned.

The court noted that while Finesse added language demanding three separate signals be multiplied in seven distinct ways, it failed to rework inherent disclosure into an affirmative statement of exclusion of two‑signal practices. There was no separate paragraph (e.g., “The invention does **not** include embodiments where fewer than three signals are multiplied...”) or transcript evidence indicating that Finesse intentionally surrendered coverage over two signals during prosecution under claim amendment proceedings such as Office Action rejections or inter‑office communications.

Because steps toward Scope Reduction under § 112(c) hinge upon **direct** evidence that an inventor purposely withdrew earlier broad coverage—through clear exculpatory words or change‐in‑position—it remains insufficient when an amendment merely narrows without an accompanying explicit disclaimer. This outcome echoes *Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, where the court reversed an infringement award because an amendment failed to statefully disavow a claimed embodiment even though it seemed technically intended to do so.

## Filipovic vs Cont’l Circuits

An instructive comparison exists with *Filipovic*, which addressed patent claims limiting operation “over receivers employing passive impedances.” In that case, applicants also contemplated narrowing claims when confronted by refusals based on prior art teaching only active mitigation techniques—not passive ones as set forth in their own specification. *Filipovic* ultimately focused heavily on demonstrable withdrawal from all passive embodiments; it required purchasers’ demonstration that applicants had given up all protective routes over passive components through explicit language or cessation of support during pertinant parts of prosecution (c.f., *Filipovic*, 1981).

In contrast, *Cont’l Circuits*—the precedent invoked here—underlined that narrowing claims simply by altering numeric thresholds without backing them up through “clear and unambiguous” textual expression falls short of permissible reduction in coverage per § 112(c). This is precisely what happened at *Finesse*: while the amendment practiced narrowing toward strictly “three signals,” it did not promise nor articulate crushing doubt about acceptance of any prior two‑signal embodiments; thus courts could view it as merely tightening wording without closing windows previously open during filing.

The difference matters because defendants often argue their product uses fewer inputs than those first captured by amended claims (apparent publisher‐model mismatch). By recalling how *Filipovic* emphasized substantive disclosure shift versus mere linguistic trimming—and contrasting it against *Cont’l Circuits*, which underlined textual certainty—we see why narrow numeric limits evaporate unless they’re coupled with additional disavowals or textual clarifications known throughout prosecutorial course.

## Implications for Interference-Mitigation Patents

Intermodulation cancellation patents frequently revolve around circuit architectures where multiplicity—number of signal paths or multiplications—influences complexity and performance attributes critical to industry standards (3GPP LTE/5G NR). In these cases, modifying clauses like “three signals” may appear innocuous yet carry commercial weight; insurers may mistake omitted plural forms as latent broadness coverable by later designs employing only two inputs exchanged throughout signal pathways.

This case informs drafters on several fronts:

1) **Pedantic clarity over phenotypic nuance:** Numeric changes embedded alone do not guarantee truncated coverage; they must be supported by unequivocal words clarifying exclusions.
2) **Drafting style and prolations:** When one wants defenders covered by hi‑performance multi‑input architectures versus lower‑order baseline technologies, practice should incorporate extra disclosure – such as enumeration diagrams explicitly showing all absent variants – feeding back into amendment instructions that practitioners interpret as elimination.
3) **Litigation predictability:** Plaintiffs who want narrow protection will likely provide defense counsel with explicit illustrative repositories showcasing negative embodiments, since this confirms directors' resolve amid hindsight that they deliberately excluded specific embodiments e.g., two‑signal preamplifiers.
4) **Standard-Set interplay:** Standard Entity attorneys consider whether patented duplication diminishes standard specifications regarding enhanced IM mitigation pathways; neutralizing ambiguity ensures field‑of​-desisked innovation persists with device‐manufacturers circumventing multi-index configurations mandated under ITU band splitting guidelines before FCC callouts triggered potential clarities regarding device approval licensing decisions akin to FCC Ruling R802 under exemption Section _______ schedule _E_ etc.

## Why this case matters

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit wrestled with a deceptively simple question: Did a smartphone radio sample an input “signal” that didn’t exist yet? That bits‑and‑pieces outcome carried far‑reaching implications for anyone who ever hires an engineer to serve as a patent expert in a high‑stakes litigation involving complex semiconductor circuitry. The panel’s reversal on both patents hinged entirely on whether an alleged “interference‑generating signal” was physically present before it was *measured*. This decision reminds litigators that a circuit diagram is only as useful as the clear, consistent terminology used by their witness—and that any veil over how terms map onto real hardware can cost millions in damages—and that there is no excuse when an expert’s testimony drifts drama count upon itself.



## A quick look at the patents

Both Finesse’s construction claims involve mitigating **passive intermodulation products** (PIM), unwanted mixed‑frequency artifacts generated when high‑power transmit antennas encounter passive elements in an environment such as metal fences or unsealed connectors. For the ’134 patent, Claim 1 sets up a five‑step method: 
1) sample a receive band containing *signals of interest* **and** *interference‑generating* signals;
2) separate these two streams using filter banks;
3) form an estimate of each PIM from the interference stream;
4) cancel those estimates from the received data;
5) fine‑tune phase adjustments in a closed‑loop loopback via sub‑sample shifts (the entry cooldows are realized in FPGA logic).

The ’775 patent expands on this idea by insisting on **seven** specific third‑order multiplications among three distinct transmitter replica copies created inside the receiver’s baseband pipeline—an elaborate digital analogue that yields valuable sideband subtraction.
