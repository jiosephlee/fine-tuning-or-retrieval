# “The ‘Three Signal’ Puzzle”

## Why It Matters

When a wireless chipset receives an uplink packet and emits a downlink burst simultaneously while internally generating intermodulation products (IMPs), designers have long fought over how best to cancel those unwanted frequencies so users can enjoy clean reception without additional power amplification costs. Patents covering methods such as those found in Finesse Wireless’s ’134 and ’775 inventions sit at the center of many recent litigations involving major carriers and handset makers. 

What is especially noteworthy about *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* is how its Federal Circuit rendered plain what has confounded both practitioners and courts alike: the meaning of “three separately identifiable signals” in a patent claim covering digital multiplications intended to suppress passive IMPs—and whether a circuit driven by only two physical inputs could satisfy an abstract requirement demanding three procured inducers multiplied in seven different ways. For industry participants governing licensing deals or drafting new claims strategically designed around analog architectures, striking any misstep could mean millions paid instantly—this case offers clear guidance on avoiding such pitfalls going forward.
