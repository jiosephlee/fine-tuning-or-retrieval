### 1. Where does statutory authority begin?

* **§ 5 U.S.C. § 1214** – “Corrective action” for **prohibited personnel practices**  
* **§ 5 U.S.C. § 1215** – “Disciplinary action” for officials who violate civil‑service rules

Section 1216(a)(3) expressly allows OSC *to* investigate alleged arbitrary or capricious withholding of information (`...“shall … conduct an investigation…` – *Id.* § 1216(a)(3)). Part c adds one further statutorily‑driven *option*:  

> *“If satisfied… ‘OSC may investigate and seek corrective action under § 1214 and disciplinary action under § 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.’* — *Id.* § 1216(c)

Answer:
**Concrete informational injuries will yield Article III standing only when they reflect an actual deprivation of a *legally protected* right that causes real damage and can be remedied by the court’s judgment.**  
The *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* opinion spells out exactly how courts evaluate such claims and why AFL was denied standing.

---

### 1. Standing fundamentals

Article III requires proof of **injury‑in‑fact** that is
1. **concrete and particularized**, and  
2. **actual or imminent**, not merely conjectural (Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife).

The district court therefore evaluates whether AFL actually suffered some tangible loss because OSC refused to investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA withholdings.

---

### 2. Procedural vs substantive informational injuries

AFL tried to argue that OSC’s refusal constituted an **injury‑in‑fact** under an “informational” claim—essentially a procedural violation under § 1216(a)(3).  
Courts routinely relax only two elements—imminence and redressability—when a plaintiff alleges infringement of a **procedural right** secured by statute (Ctr.\ for Law & Educ.\ v.\ Dep’t of Educ.).  

However, **summons** of each remaining element remains strict:

| Element | What AFC had NOT proven |
|---------|------------------------|
| **Concrete injury** | No cable‑level deprivation was shown; lack of any *statutory entitlement* × lack of withholding by OSC itself |
| **Causation** | The alleged chain “OSC → MSPB → DOJ” was speculative and involved actors outside the proceeding |
| **Redressability** | A favorable ruling would not directly compel either DOJ’s disclosure or MSC’s action |

These failures are explicitly noted in *America First*:

> “To overcome…infringement…plaintiff must show…‘the violation … resulted in injury’…AFL satisfies neither those requirements nor even a relaxed standard of redressability.”  
> ―*America First*, § II

---

### 3. When an informational claim meets the test

Courts rely on prior jurisprudence—most notably **Summers v.\ Earth Island Inst.^(sum)**—to say that merely claiming an “informational” loss is insufficient unless those facts directly connect to *statutorily protected interests*.  Two key conditions arise:

1. **Statutory Entitlement:** The claimant must have an explicit legal right *to receive* the information sought (e.g., FOIA entitlement).  
   § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) authorizes OSC investigations *only* when it is invoked via FCC.S earlier procedures—this did not create a claim for recipients here.
2. **Loss from Denial:** There must be some real harm beyond “frustration”—typically evidenced by evidence required for litigation, testimony preparation, regulatory compliance, or other substantive goals ([FMCH](https://lawcat.com/Article/548729)).

If both hold—say, an agency refuses workbook data confirmed by FOIA letter denial, depriving investigators of crucial evidence—then the deprivation often qualifies as “concrete” under Lujan 504 U.S.”\& the defendant cannot proceed beyond sufficiency analysis (§ Larry).

In *America First*, AFL attempted precisely such reasoning ("preventing AFL from receiving records it requested under FOIA") but could not tie DOJ’s outright refusal — which was independent of OSC — back into its own typical chain (§ II°).

---

### 4. Practical take‑away

For aspiring litigants, ensure your informational claim satisfies:

1. **Statutory right:** The law expressly grants you access (FOIA amendment notice? ACLU‑type statute?).
2. **Concrete deprivation:** The agency has actually withheld or refused those records.
3. **Causal link & redressability:** It is shown that relief (a court order compelling disclosure) directly remedies your loss without relying on speculative actions by superior agencies or discretionary decisions beyond judicial control (see FDA v.\ All.§ Other).

*America First Legal Foundation v.\ Jamieson Greer* shows what happens when any one of these links fails—the case dies on lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction because no Article III standing exists at all.



---

#### Summary

A concrete informational injury satisfies Article III standing only when:
- you possess a *statute‑enforced right* to obtain the documents,
- you can prove *tangible harm* caused by the agency’s withholding,
- you demonstrate that relief will *actually correct* that harm—and that connection isn’t overly speculative or subject to independent discretionary choices beyond your control.

In *America First*, all three were missing for AFL – no statutory entitlement arose from § 1216(a)(3), its alleged harm was traced exclusively back to DOJ rather than OSC, and speculation about downstream agency actions weakened any argument for redressability.*

* There is no statutory commitment that gives the plaintiff any actual claim against OSC;

---

### When—if ever—relief might exist

The only way a defendant could face enforce­able sanctions over non‑posting is if Congress had expressly provided either

1. an entitle­ment in statute “for recipients *to receive* specific information even absent an actual unreasonable withholding,” or  
2. made notice mandatory for all investigations so its omission constitutes plain contempt / misconduct subject magistrate review (“procedural right” possessing *enjoinment* sanction).

Neither circumstance appears here—Congress has left ▸§​1216 investigations largely discretionary; §​1216 does **not** confer special entitlement nor require adherence to ▸§​1214(b)(c).[1] Accordingly, what remains are administrative complaints filed directly with agencies or alternatives adopted by agencies themselves—not judicially ordered remedies triggered solely by refusal to post.

---

**Conclusion:** When an agency declines while exercising discretion under §​5 U.S.C.’s §§​1216/1223, plaintiffs lack Article III standing and thus have no path—from courts—to compel posting under §​1214(b)(c)’s notification scheme—or indeed any other inked remedy arising purely from non‑posting.

Katsas cited the Court’s “relaxed” test for procedural‑rights claims (Ctr. for Law & Educ.; Fla. Audubon Soc.) but clarified insurers still must satisfy:
* A real concern for loss derives **immediately** from violating a statutory procedure,*  
* And loss must hinge necessarily on those very procedures.*
