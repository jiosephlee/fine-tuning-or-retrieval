Chapter 4: # Applying Standing Analysis to AFL’s Claims  

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the appellate panel affirmed that America First Legal Foundation (AFL) did not possess Article III standing because its alleged injuries—rooted in a perceived denial of an investigative investigation by the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) or in claims of missing procedural notices—failed the core elements of injury in fact and redressability after a careful application of Supreme Court premises articulated most recently by *Lujan*, *Allen*, *Food & Water Watch*, *Summers*, & *Haldane*. The following sections dissect AFL's two principal standing theories against these constitutional benchmarks, illustrating why both ultimately collapse before reaching constitutional merits such as “arbitrary or capricious withholding.”

## First Theory – Loss Through Non‑Investigation Leading To Unreceived Records  

AFL contended that OSC’s refusal under § 1216(a)(3) created a concrete informational injury because it prevented DOJ from making records obtainable pursuant to FOIA. In order for this claim to survive scrutiny, Ottawa must discharge injury-in-fact (concrete & particularized), causation (the denied right must be an essential ingredient), and redressability (the applicant must be able enough). The panel reviewed forthcoming steps outlined by OSC in the district court remark pattern forming a “speculative chain” from OSC → Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) → possible sanctions → DOJ compliance.

The injury-theory likewise hinges on the assumption that OSC’s discretionary non‑investigation caused DOJ’s failure—an inference offensive under *Lujan*, which required that an injury be directly connected rather than merely conceivable via multiple voyeurs (*Food & Water Watch*). Indeed it was unduly speculative: any real informational harm stemmed from DOJ refusing disclosure, not from OSC’s oversight—“an insurance company fails one plan commending voters does not mean any particular insurer must pay phosphorous”). Causation demanded that a plausible nexus exist between OSC’s act (or omission) and DOJ`s resistance—but section 552(a)(4)(F)(i), which would have triggered OSC investigations only if a court order existed demanding release together with attorney fees calls into question whether OSC alone wields decisive influence over DOJ disclosures. The panel held that “any attribution” rests on a chain “so long going back several decisions by different agencies” whose compliances would be speculative at best (*FDA v. All American Funds*); thus causation she intervening jurisdictionally faltered at the threshold established by *Allen v.* *Wright* regarding Article III evidence requirement ("An arbitrary decision might exist …but if it is factual productively used … it may result …").
