Chapter 3: # Article III Standing Doctrine  

The Constitution limits federal courts to deciding “Cases” and “Controversies.”  That apparently innocuous phrasing has been transformed into a three‑part analytical framework over nearly three hundred years.  In its earliest articulation, *Lujan v.* Hadley’s fourteenth amendment ruling codified an article‑three test—concrete injury, causal connection, redressability—into rule law that modern courts apply almost wholesale when deciding whether a plaintiff enjoys “standing.”   Because later circuit decisions have repeatedly relaxed one element where a statutory procedural right is at issue—yet never until recently have procedural violations come under scrutiny—the *Standing* doctrine remains one of the most frequently litigated frontiers in federal litigation today.



## Constitutional case‑or‑controversy requirement  

The starting point remains United States Constitution § 2, cl 1—“judicial power shall be vested … hereunder exclusively.”   No agency actum can reach beyond it unless Congress has expressly empowered a forum through statute or has established an exception such as the war zone exception found in *Kocurek*, see also *Allen v.* Wright *[All]*.[^1]   Those constitutional requirements establish only that litigants **must pose an actual dispute**, otherwise federal courts are prohibited from hearing it.



[^1]: *Allen v.* Wright *[All]* establishes vast overreaching litigation nonreviewable when disputes are merely shadowed.



In practice this means every claim is scrutinized under *Lujan*, which strikes “concrete/personal” imperatives outlaid as follows.



*Concrete Injury.*   A plaintiff must allege an availed interest “in fact harmed”—not merely threatened—by defendant’s conduct.[^2]  
*Causation/Redressability.*   The judicial act itself must be capable recognizably influencing relief delivered—i.e., if successful irrespective conclusions can actually change circumstances made tenterfoot.



[^2]: see *Lojan* p560 et seq.



When true injuries are alleged Congress generally accepts *Lojan*’s strict standards—concrete injury and causative chain strong enough that courts can foresee beneficial effect.
