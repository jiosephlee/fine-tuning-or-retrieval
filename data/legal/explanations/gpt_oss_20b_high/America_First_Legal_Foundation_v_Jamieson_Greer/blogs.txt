## A Quick Primer on Standing  

Article III limits federal courts to cases involving **injury‑in‑fact**, **causation**, and **redressability** (*Lujan* v Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555). Standing isn’t a questioning power—it requires parties to demonstrate that a specific interest has been harmed by an alleged act of the appellee and that a favorable judgment would actually resolve that harm. In practice courts scrape every complaint looking first whether it contains a concrete harm touching one’s lawful interests before they even examine lower‑court findings or merits issues.



It’s tempting, especially in procedural disputes rooted in administrative sovereignty, to assume that any breach of statutory process automatically creates damage sufficient for court review: “You’re denied your legal right to see records”—that seems definite harm already lost.



But courts rarely give such blanket relief because liability without solidity tends toward judicial overreach into decision‑making functions reserved for agencies wielding discretionary powers.



The modern equilibrium is articulated most clearly in two District‑Court landmarks cooling off those implicit promises.



**Summers** v Earth Island Institutionals (\*555 U.S.* 488) held that a mere “procedural right” never suffices unless it attaches specifically to a particular interest—the classic example being whether more *information* could change a personal decision (`procedural right` vs `substantive right`). Because the plaintiff’s suffering derives only from anticipation or **hope**—not an impediment placed upon actually attainable resources—it does not meet either element of **injury‑in‑fact**.



Later courts refined this stance without abandoning its core principle: **citizens can plead whose interests are protected** and what **facts support those claims**.^[See *Citizens’ Scholarship Association*, 396 F.3d 1152] When plaintiffs’ suit seeks relief based on having been denied information—even if such information was provided later—that freedom itself does not count as an abstract entitlement created merely by statutory definition of an informational norm.



If you lack concrete evidence that your exact legal bargaining power was removed—or at least expectancy thereof—the doctrine presumes unrebutted insufficiency.




Hence Congress sometimes attempts stronger sanctions embedded into statutes requiring mandatory proceedings under distinctive conditions (*Office Holdings* furnishes ). Ultimately those enforceable directives remain subject even then neither automatically furnishing plaintiff eligibility nor guaranteeing redress through closed circuit distances.



In short—Article III’s battery check will typically delete petitions wherein plaintiffs claim ***“my informational rights were breached.”***
