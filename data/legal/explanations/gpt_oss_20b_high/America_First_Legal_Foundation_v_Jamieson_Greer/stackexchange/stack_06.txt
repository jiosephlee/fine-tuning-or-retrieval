Answer:
**Answer**

The order that causes a judge to focus exclusively on how a *non‑party* will behave is governed by **Article III standing**—in particular Lujan v.* Defenders (>1992) together with subsequent cases that relax—but do **not** eliminate—stand‑up standards when plaintiffs allege a procedural right was violated (e.g., Ctr. for Law & Educ.* D.C. Cir.*).  
The circuit court made clear throughout its discussion whether or not America First Legal Foundation could assert such relief:

---

### 1.  Standing demands a concrete injury linked directly to the defendant

> “…to have Article III standing…the plaintiff must have suffered an **injury caused by** the defendant…and likely **to be redressed** by a favorable decision.”  
> ― *Lujan* (see §A even though AFL claims only an alleged lack of investigation).  

In our case AFL’s “infections” were:
1. DOJ refusing records under FOIA, and  
2. OSC withholding notice required under §1214(b).

Both injuries trace back only **speculatively** through two governmental entities other than any defendant before the Court—the MSPB and DOJ themselves—and are therefore attenuated too far‑removed for Article III relief—exactly what Katsas wrote when he noted how “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about … autonomous choices made by independent actors not before us.”

### 2.  Procedural‑right claims get a relaxed bar

Katsas cited the Court’s “relaxed” test for procedural‑rights claims (Ctr. for Law & Educ.; Fla. Audubon Soc.) but clarified insurers still must satisfy:
* A real concern for loss derives **immediately** from violating a statutory procedure,*  
* And loss must hinge necessarily on those very procedures.*

AFL attempted this argument twice【sect II points VII‑IX】but ultimately failed—for both reasons stated above handled sluggishly—because AFL could not plead either attributability or redressability vis‑à‑vis purely speculative effects residing in Judith's impossible maze of administrative decisionmaking.

### 3.  A statutory duty does not create enforceable entitlement

Even if §1216(a)(3)’s language obliges OSC “to investigate” arbitrary withholding (*see §B*), it does **not** create an entitlement for AFL to obtain information—or even notice—unless;
*OSC chooses* base protocols similar to §1214(b). Katsas noted this discretionary aspect (“OSC does not have to expository these notice rules”), so no inflexible statutory obligation you could compel upon DOJ or OSC exists either (though FTC may get sued when ACA fails).

---

### Bottom line

> **Rule:** Under Article III standing—including its more forgiving form against alleged procedure violations—a plaintiff may succeed only if an injury can be tied directly to acts—or omitted acts—of defendants before us *and* redressed decisively by our judgment, *but it may never hinge upon future behavior expected from unrelated third parties.*  

So in *America First \*\*\* v.* Jamieson Greer*, A.F.L.’s attempt at making DOJ pay contestants produces no valid judicial leverage because every step between their claimed loss and any favorable ruling would require specific, enforceable moves by several government bodies outside their litigation—all too speculative per Katsas’ pronouncement.<|fim_end|>#
