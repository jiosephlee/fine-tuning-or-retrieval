Answer:
**Short answer**

A plaintiff can only get a court to order an agency action when he can point at *one specific*, *concrete* injury that a favorable ruling would *directly* undo — and he can predict that a favorable ruling will actually bring about that change.  
In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* the Court found that

1.  The § 1216 “right” is *procedural* and exists only if its exercise protects a constitutionally‑protected interest;  
2.  Even if it did exist, AFL’s claims of an informational loss could not show a concrete, particularized injury traced back from OSC’s refusal through two independent agencies (MSPB and DOJ) onto the FOIA decision it challenged;  
3.  Because those injuries either do not exist or are too speculative, AFL lacked Article III standing, so the District Court was powerless and could be dismissed for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction.

**Longer explanation**

| Issue | What the Court Said | Why it matters for an appeal |
|-------|--------------------|------------------------------|
| **Does a missing OSC investigation count as an actual injury?** | Under § 1216(a)(3), OSC may investigate “arbitrary or capricious withholding … prohibited under section 552.”  But a *“procedural right”* exists only where it protects an individual’s protected interest (Supreme Court *Lujan*, *Summers*).  If the right is “in vacuo,” there is no injury because nothing was lost or harmed. | In such cases courts apply the *relaxed* standard from cases like *Citizens for Law & Educ.* v.… Only if the defendant’s breach threatens *that* protected interest can judicial review proceed.  Here AFL had no protected statutory interest—it only demanded an optional agency inquiry—so there was no real wrong–for‑this‑reason _$jury_ could order anything. |
| **Can AFL claim a concrete informational injury?** | AFL argued two harms: DOJ did not provide the records, and OSC never gave the notice required by § 1214(a)(1)(B)–(D).  The Court held both were speculative at best:<br>• Forum‑wide – DOJ’s refusal comes from its own FOIA rule‑making (unconnected to OSC).<br>• “Causal chain too attenuated.” Because any corrective action would have required decisions by MSPB and DOJ – actors outside the case – created a speculative link exceeding judicial purview (*Heckler*, *FDA v.*  All‑for‑Hippocratic Med.*). | An appellate court can’t salute relief based on an undefined damage trail between its order and the ultimate fact it seeks changed…The dissonance between “a jury rule in my favor” and “DOJ will produce documents” cannot be assumed to be reliable enough for jurisdiction |
| **Is there an entitlement to receive information from § 1216 investigations?** | No — section 1216 merely obliges OSC “[s]hall … conduct an investigation”; it does not lay out notice or disclosure rules (those belong to § 1214).  And § 1214 notice requirements are discretionary even in section 1216 sifts (*Carson v.* U.S. Offic.). AFL asserted news & disclosures anyway but found no statutory basis → incomplete |- No court remedy when there is none by law → Article III confinement |

**Bottom line**

Because article‑III pleading standards hinge on a *concrete* harm caused by an unlawful act or failure, merely denying access (or denying the agency certain prerogatives) does **not** satisfy those constraints when:

* The plaintiff’s pain is abstract speculation about another agency’s future actions;
* There is no statutory commitment that gives the plaintiff any actual claim against OSC;
* And because any remedy would require decisions by parties outside the lawsuit itself.

Absent such proof, appellate courts are barred by Article III from reviewing what they see as non‑justiciable political disagreements or policy choices, which explains why *Am First Legal Foundation* was dismissed for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction—even though its founders had “tangible desire” for records, they lacked the concrete damage that justifies federal review.*
