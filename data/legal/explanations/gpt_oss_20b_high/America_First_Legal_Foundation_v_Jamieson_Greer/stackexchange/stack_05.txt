Answer:
**Short answer:**  
No—Apl­i­cant *cannot* obtain enforce­able relief simply because an agency refuses or fails to post notification pursuant to § 5 U.S.C. § 1214(b)(c).

---

### Why “no” follows from the court’s analysis

| Issue | What the court said | Why it matters |
|-------|--------------------|----------------|
| **What § 5 U.S.C. § 1214(b)(c) requires** | “…provisions for notifying … about progress” (see discussion early in *Opinion* paragraph 1‑2). | The statute mandates notification **in a corrective‑action proceeding** *if* one takes place, but does **not** create an independent right for a complainant to demand notice in every investigation. |
| **OSC’s discretion** | “OSC’s decision whether … follow #§ 1214’s procedural requirements … is discretionary” (*Opinion* paragraph II‑11‑1). | Even when OSC opts for a #§ 1216 investigation (which is itself discretionary under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)), it is free not to adopt §§ 1214(b)(c)’s notice scheme unless Congress specifically mandates it—and Congress did not. |
| **Standing** | The court finds AFL cannot show actual injury from mere omission of statutory notice (*Opinion* paragraph II‑6 onward). Specifically: <br>• “Any informational injury … is traceable **to DOJ** not OSC.” <br>• “Causal chain … too speculative” (*Opinion* ¶ II‑9‑10). | Without proof that a lack of notice caused a concrete injury *and* that such injury could be remedied by court action on OTC’s part (*Lujan*, *Kelo* standards cited by judge), OA cannot meet Article III standing – and therefore can’t sue *anything*. |
| **Redressability** | “Any judgment would bind parties … not actors who are **not before the court**” (*Opinion* ¶ II‑8‑12). | Even if notified would be’ve otherwise held costly good standard—wouldn't automatically persuade DOJ or MSPB or Ω alter its conduct without their decision-making participation fraught speculation term => redressability fails. |

Because standing failed, **no jurisdiction** exists for a *judicial order compelling notification.* A \($(equivalent)\) order ordering ODM or any agency “to post” its notifications would violate Article III since plaintiffs have neither interest nor certificate jurisdiction over them.*  

---

### When—if ever—relief might exist

The only way a defendant could face enforce­able sanctions over non‑posting is if Congress had expressly provided either

1. an entitle­ment in statute “for recipients *to receive* specific information even absent an actual unreasonable withholding,” or  
2. made notice mandatory for all investigations so its omission constitutes plain contempt / misconduct subject magistrate review (“procedural right” possessing *enjoinment* sanction).

Neither circumstance appears here—Congress has left ▸§​1216 investigations largely discretionary; §​1216 does **not** confer special entitlement nor require adherence to ▸§​1214(b)(c).[1] Accordingly, what remains are administrative complaints filed directly with agencies or alternatives adopted by agencies themselves—not judicially ordered remedies triggered solely by refusal to post.

---

**Conclusion:** When an agency declines while exercising discretion under §​5 U.S.C.’s §§​1216/1223, plaintiffs lack Article III standing and thus have no path—from courts—to compel posting under §​1214(b)(c)’s notification scheme—or indeed any other inked remedy arising purely from non‑posting.

---

[1] This holds except where statute explicitly provides substantive rights that match those merely procedural—the distinction clarified throughout *America First Legal Foundation v.* Chevyell Greer ([Opinion](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/caedc/24-5168/24-5168.html)).
