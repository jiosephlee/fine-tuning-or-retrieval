# Statutory Framework Governing Exemption 7(A)

The Freedom of Information Act forces federal agencies to give requested records unless an exemption applies (§ 552(a)(3)). Section 552(b) defines each exemption’s scope on a question‑by‑question basis. Exemption 7(A) uniquely attaches two conditions—a preservation test linked to “law‑enforcement purposes” and an interference test tied to impending criminal activity (§ 552(b)(7)(A)). Understanding the statutory architecture—and how courts have shaped its application—is essential when evaluating a denial under this exemption, as illustrated by *Farahi v.* *Federal Bureau of Investigation* (FBI).

## U.S.C. § 552(a)(3),(b) – The FOIA Disclosure Mandate

Clause (a)(3) establishes the general duty for open governance: every federal agency must disclose “all records” received…unless a statutory exemption applies (§ 552(b)). Clause (b) enumerates nine granted exemptions designed to protect legitimate interests—like national security, personal privacy, trade secrets—none allowing blanket withholding simply because matters were “confidential.” Because *Farahi* concerned a voluntary request for an individual’s FBI file, all nine exemptions were irrelevant except Exclusion 7(A); consequently the court focused exclusively on whether that particular exemption applied (§ 792).

## Definition ‘Compiled’ Versus ‘Contained’ Records

FOIA’s “record” phrase requires careful parsing because liability hinges on whether documents were created expressly for investigative purposes—or merely happenstance collateral material captured during routine operations (“contain” obligations). Courts treat “compiled” conjunctively with the agency’s purpose rather than document-by-document analysis (*Campbell*). To trigger Exemption 7(A), an entire **record** or collection must have been assembled **for** law‑enforcement duties (*Farahi*, district court judgment actions J.A.’s _posted* pages). If a file is only *containing* information unrelated to an investigation yet bound within one physical stack, exclusion does **not** apply unless found otherwise via supporting affidavits (*Farahi* clarified that all investigated material was indeed compiled through FBI dossiers under Section C).

Translating creative practice into facts was evident when the FBI paired its tens­ thousands‑page voluminous archive under one request company category—an effort deemed “compiled” because staff arranged documents *in furtherance* of suspected terrorism links (*Farahi*, Hardy statement).

## Requirement That Release Could ’Reasonably Interfere’

The second knock‑out condition attaches an **interference test** (U.S.C.§ 552(b)(7))(B). Disclosure will remain barred only if knowledge made public would **reasonably** jeopardise upcoming law‑enforcement action—e.g., spilling evidence crucial for prosecution (§§ 550–551 urbana). For forensic evidence this translates into a tangible threat beyond theoretical speculation (*Recovery* model). 

In *Farahi*, AFia supplied two oral affidavits—Hardy’s public statement enumerating “ongoing investigations … gathering evidence“ followed by Bender’s sworn pronouncement added via super‑ confidential filings—that **both spelled out specifics**—evidence tactics prospected through terrorism networks—their anticipation being vulnerable if released prematurely (“tipping off targets”). These facts satisfied the interference test by offering credible premises that exposure would disturb active investigative strategies; merely alleging ties without evidentiary structure failed under undue speculation doctrine (*CREW*, id.).

Note that courts routinely deem nondisclosure consequential even when it concerns purely investigative semantics if those semantics constitute groundwork for potential prosecutions (*Juarez*, id.). Consequently authoritatively arguing **possible** victimisation cannot suffice without submitting corroborated articulations describing investigative processes—that standard reached by Hard & Bender filings — meeting explicit statutory demand _“could reasonably be expected”_ (§ 552(b)(7))(B).

Foia litigation often asks judges whether an agency’s refusal falls squarely within one of Congress’ defensible exemptions—and how much faith they should place in an agency’s own determination when national‑security concerns loom large.
