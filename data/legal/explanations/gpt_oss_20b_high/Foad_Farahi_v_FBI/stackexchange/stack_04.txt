### "Category” Identification Under § 552(b)– Why Must Agencies Use Specific Distinct Categories?
Question:
*Farahi* raised that excluded categories appeared overbroad because he never saw particular definitions.

I noted *CREW*, *Gallant*, and *Khine v. DHS*’s utility lists stating categories must enable courts identify applicable exemptions.

My confusions:

* What minimum level of detail distinguishes one category from another?

* If every page has both exempt information (evidence) and potential public interest facts, can any single category suffice? Or must agencies split these individually before affording partial release?

* How does failure to meet categorize expectations affect withdrawal credibility?
Answer:
**Short Answer**

The “category” test arises under § 552(b)(7)(A)’s requirement that any exempt portion “must be *reasonably segregable*” from non‑exempt material (§ 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)).  
To satisfy that standard an agency *must* divide a requested file into logically distinct groups whose boundaries let a court decide whether Exemption 7(A) applies separately to each group (*CREW*, 746 F.​3d 1088).

If every page contains both exempt evidence and potentially public‑interest data then a single broad category cannot satisfy this test unless the agency shows—by definitive line‑by‑line markup—that non‑exempt portions are truly intermixed and cannot be pulled out without loss of meaning (*Meads*, 566 F.​2d 262).  
In *Farahi*, Washington’s RIDS concluded that no “meaningful information was reasonably segregable” because “non‑exempt material was randomly dispersed” throughout each CD (*Farahi* Kt͟athas J., ¶ 139). Thus the FBI was required—and ultimately did—withhold whole CDs rather than offer a pseudo‑release built around an ambiguous aggregate grouping.

When an agency’s categorization does **not** meet this level of distinction, its denial becomes unreliable under § 552(a)(8). A court consequently treats any asserted segregation document or declaration skeptically (*Khine v DHS*, 943 F.​3d 959), potentially requiring further review or even reversal of an initial deny order if the missing detail shows non‑exempt portions could in fact have been released.

### What constitutes “sufficiently distinct” categorizations?

> “When an agency withholds documents **on a category‑by‑category basis**, it must use **categories…that are ‘sufficiently distinct’** …to allow a court …to determine whether …the specific claimed exemptions …are properly applied” (*CREW*, 746 F.​3d 1088).

For Exemption 7(A) this means producing groupings in which each bundle contains only material gathered **for** enforcement purposes and does **not** mix unrelated administrative or private sections.  
The “minimum level of detail” is thus enough resolution so a reviewer could isolate any potentially excluded fragment without needing additional internal processing—a standard implied wherever the court mentions “reasonably segregable portion.”  

In *Farahi* the FBI’s Bender declaration clarified two initiative layers:

| Category | Description   |
|----------|---------------|
| **Investigative Records** | Evidence directly supporting active investigations into terrorism ties |
| **Administrative/Case Documentation** | Forms and correspondences unrelated to investigative activity |

By parsing records into those two buckets—and then confirming which subset contained Post‑interrogation notes or fingerprint logs—the FBI demonstrated compliance with § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii).

### Can one broad “all‑pages” category suffice when every page has both types of data?

No—unless an exhaustive technologic audit proves every seemingly mixed fragment ends up embedded irrevocably so exposure would violate another statutory provision (*Meads*).  
When interception occurs line‐by‐line throughout a document—as was found when multiple CDs carried short excerpts logged by requesters—resolving those fragments would destroy context or double exposure probability (*Bender* supplemental declaration).  

The policy is starkly illustrated elsewhere in *Farahi*:

> “If *a small amount* of non‑exempt material is randomly ‘interspersed line‑by‑line’ throughout a document ‘that material is **not likely** to be **reasonably segregable**’’ (**Meads**, 566 F.​2d 261).

Consequently agencies **cannot** rely on transmitting just any one aggregate layer; they must either fully release each internally separable chunk or submit disclosure demands covering every swell dump together while arguing enforceability ambiguity (.i.e Parliamentally convening Cong.)—

> “…the FBI had provided a ‘detailed justification’ …that protective materials were **not reasonably segregable**” (*Farahi*, Kt͟athas J., ¶ 139).
