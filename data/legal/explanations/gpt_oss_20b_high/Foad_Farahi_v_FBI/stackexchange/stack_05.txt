Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### "Ex Parte" Declarations—When Are They Acceptable?
Question:
*Farahi* protested reliance upon sealed Hardy/Bender affidavits.

Is it legitimate for plaintiffs to challenge Secretary‐issued sealed submissions once a motion was approved without prejudice?

In particular:

* Under what circumstances does a summarizable request mandate presentation only in secret because disclosure jeopardizes interests listed in Section12(c),(k)?

* Does denying appellants any chance substitutes escalatory demands such as judicial notice limits—per *Perioperative Services*, "the problem is inherent" comment—or is there alternative means for verifying facts before adjudication?
Answer:
### Quick Takeaway  
In **Foad Farahi v. FBI**, the D.C.~Circuit held that an agency may submit **sealed executive‑summaries** – i.e., “ex‑parte” affidavits – when a statutory exemption hinges on sensitive information whose release would probably **interfere with existing or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings** (*See §​7(A)*).  
Plaintiffs may contest those sealed submissions **only insofar as they show failure on three fronts**:

| Point | What must be proved | Citation |
|-------|--------------------|----------|
| **(1)** That a product was actually **compiled for law‑enforcement purposes** | The agency bears *burden* (“U.S.C.§ 552(a)(4)(B)”); partial support normally affords deference | *F»Farahi*, J.A.»4 |
| **(2)** That release would **reasonably be expected** to **interfere** with proceedings currently **pending or reasonably anticipated** | Pendency requires an “existing investigation” plus a plausible threat of harm | *COPE*’s ••“[We] have held….” &#x2026; cf.*CREW*, 746 F.R.L.^1 |
| **(3)** That non‑exempt material **cannot be meaningfully segregated** | If exempt portions mix so tightly with non‑exempt ones that removal would either leave them incomplete or impossible, total withdrawal is warranted | *	F»Farahi*, §III ⁹‑10 |

If any one element fails, courts will either demand more detail, order segregation/partial release (U.S.C.§ 552(a)(8)(A)), or deny blanket exemption protection altogether ***without prejudice***.



-----------------------------------------------------------------------

## 1 Can plaintiffs litigate against “sealed summaries”?

Yes—and only if those summaries are later shown  
to fall outside one—or all—of the three enunciated elements above.
In Farahi’s case,

* The first motion relied on Hardy’s unsealed declaration,
which already satisfied elements (1)-(2); this was followed by an
ex‑parte supplemental affidavit from Bender describing ongoing investigations.
The district court therefore granted summary judgment descrip­ting,
“Bender’s submission showed nothing had changed …” (*see J.A.•138–39*).

Once denied “with prejudice,” a plaintiff could still pursue another writ
if he presents fresh evidence refuting any element:
e.g., proof that no matter‑what shooting occurred,
the alleged proceeding has long been closed so there is no interference risk.
That’s exactly why FCC allows appeals “post‑judgment” when new facts emerge;
but whatever fresh claim requires satisfying ***the same burden*** outlined above[^2].

## 2 When does an allegedly summari­zable request compel secrecy?

Summaries alone do **not** justify withholding material unless those
summaries materially bribe an exemption claim kept secret.
Sought obvi­ous keep secrets arise when either

* **Exemption apply (§§7(A), 3A–E)** plus national‑security relevance;
or
* Some other statutory prohibition bars public release,
e.g., required classification blanketing coverage (§§9▰ throughout).

The judge recalls readily:
> “The government must take *reasonable steps*…to segregate …non‑exempt portion.” (*See §V*)

Thus a request marked “summariz­able” engages two thresholds:
(i) proves there truly exist portions worthy of exposure;
(ii) demonstrates releasing them would seriously endanger litigation/materialized stabilization standpoint arising out
of national‑security contexts – echoing Tenure calligns captured at ‘Section12(c),(k)’\[*reimagined](#ftn0)* where remaining seizure concerns were explicitly identified among national‐security privileged spans (§11).  

In practice this means:\[
\begin{array}{lcl}
\text{If a single page contains both non‑exempt personal data} &\rightarrow& \text{It must lie wholly within an exempt purview}\;\;\text{unless clearly separable}.\\[0pt]
\text{If every non‑exempt phrase intermixes linearly} && \text{Secrecy prevails}.\\[0pt]
\end{array}
\]

This formula aligns perfectly with how Farahi’s district court treated the dozens‐of‐GB expanse wrapped around investigational idiosyncrasies.
There was no meaningful excise point=nonseparability \[*cf.* Mead Data Cent.^3*\].

## 3 What push backs replace hostile competing demands?

Plaintiffs lacking opportunity for traditional discovery aren’t left stranded—
the statute itself offers two snakes crawls past opaque enclosures:

1. **Limited access through *judicial notice***  
   When mere facts ×ordinary facts could place conflict implicit evidence back into review,
   courts limit exposure up strictly where **proof certainty exists**, mirroring Frequent use scheme described l&d

   _In Perioperative Servs.&Logistics_,  
   "**the problem is inherent." —signaling courts’ acceptance of involuntary prohibition when facts themselves resurface._^[4]

2. **Mandatory segregation/partial distribution**  
   As noted, governments must try segmentation unless impossibly intertwined—
   this route legitimates giving alle!ants a view via cleanly removed slices even if sovereign groups veto probable mash-ups.^[^5]

---

#### Bottom line

Farahi illustrated how courts defer heavily toward lawful agencies yet enforce ordinary reformation tools:
an agency may submit terse sealed affidavits (*Hardy/Bender*) so long they cover all three statutory caveats;
otherwise any portion deemed non‑enriched enough must surface outright,
and plaintiffs always retain recourse through segregation rules or judicious notice—all actionable within standard appeals practice without penalizing initial unresolved claims[^6].
