Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### : Deference applied where national security overlaps “law enforcement” activities
Question:
"The opinion emphasizes strong deference —a fair measure especially when national‑security concerns prevail.

In practice:
❓ Is this deference absolute, or can courts override it if they find lack of factual support?
❔ Does Congress grant implicit waiver through dual criteria—‘law‑enforcement purpose’ AND ‘interfere” budget’—when interpreting counterterrorism intelligence files?
👀 Could this stance effectively override First Amendment concerns about revealing investigative tactics?

Is there precedent where heightened scrutiny was applied despite geographic arguments given graver security threats?
Answer:
**Short answer**

| Question | Answer |
|---------|--------|
|1 Why isn’t the deference to an agency’s invocation of Exemption 7(A) “absolute”? | The D.C.—Circuit says the agency’s claim is entitled to *fair* (or “deferential”) treatment **but** it must still satisfy the statutory thresholds—record compiled “for law‑enforcement purposes” **and** that its release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings.” If those facts are lacking, the court may deny the claim despite the agency’s assertion (cf. *Foad Farahi*, J.A. 121–22; *Reps., Comm., for Freedom of the Press v./FBI*, 3 F.*4th 350). |
|2 Is there an implicit congressional waiver when both “law‑enforcement purpose” and “interfere” requirements are triggered? | No—Congress did **not** waive either element of Exemption 7(A); it set an *AND* test in §552(b)(7)(A). A claim passes only when **both** statutory conditions hold (see §552(b)(7)(A)(B)); thus dual‐criteria do not create an implied waiver but simply reinforce the threshold sample originally set by Congress (“compilation for law‑enforcement…and interference”). |
|3 Does this stance defeat First‑Amendment claims over investigative tactics? | FOIA does not normally weigh free‑speech interests against confidentiality claims unless a statute expressly does so (e.g., §552(b)(6)). Here the court applies §552(b)(7)(A)—a *statutory* exception—so any First‑Amendment argument is secondary (the exclusion survives even if exposing investigative methods would be controversial). That said, there is no separate balancing step in this case (§552(a)(8)(A)(ii) merely requires segregation). |
|4 Are there precedents with heightened scrutiny despite geographic arguments/serious threats? | Yes—the opinion cites three cases that all required detailed factual support while granting significant deference because national security lapped with law‑enforcement duties:<br>- *Campbell v./DOJ*, 164 F.*3d 20 (1998);<br>- *Citizens’ Rep’t & Ethics in Wash.* v./DOJ*, 746 F.*3d 1082 (2014);<br>- *Center for Nat’l Sec.* Stud.*, v./DOJ*, 331 F.*3d 918 (2003). Each case held that even when a threat appears remote geographically, courts must first confirm both statutory predicates and then decide whether secrets can be segregated—illustrating precisely how heightened scrutiny operates amid overlapping national‑security concerns.<br>Thus Farahi continues that chain of decisions by upholding agency authority while still requiring clear evidence.*

### Detailed Discussion

#### 1 Deference is “fair,” not `absolute`

The Court explicitly states:

> “Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement… its decision…is entitled to *deference*.”  
> “[t]hat’s fair measure” – see ¶24–25

but immediately follows it with standard procedural bars:

> • In FOIA cases…we review a grant of summary judgment **de novo**.<br>
> • The government bears the burden of showing an exemption applies (U.S.C.§552(a)(4)(B))…

and later:

> “…the government must show that …records were compiled **for law‑enforcement purposes** and …that disclosure could reasonably be expected …to interfere ….” *(§V)*

If either predicate fails due to lack of factual support—the Agency’s affidavits are deemed insufficient—the court rejects the claim even though doctrines like *Exigent Circumstances* may encourage deferential interpretations when policy matters are at stake.

#### 2 Dual conditions do not equal an implicit legislative waiver

The Court treats each requirement separately:

*“Exemption 7(A) applies only to records ‘compiled for law‑enforcement purposes.’ … [and] … release could reasonably interfere.”*  
…the sentence continues ["`requiring...ex?.`"] §V

Thus Congress established a two‑part test rather than creating an implicit allowance that any detail regarding counterterrorism automatically falls under disclaimer when either condition exists alone.

#### 3 First Amendment protestability

The Court does not conduct a viewpoint analysis because FOIA’s textual framework reserves exclusions strictly under §552(b). While revealing investigative methods could raise First Am’n concerns conceptually—particularly on whistleblower/spoiler grounds—those issues fall outside FOIA’s statutory scheme here; they would need independent constitutional confrontation outside FOIA's entirety *(see Cornell anx.*).

Hence advocacy over potential disclosure does **not** defeat exemption once both statutory tests are satisfied.

#### 4 Precedent demonstrating heightened review in similar context  

The notice lists decisions consistent with Farahi where geography/scope debates were considered yet statutes compelled agencies higher levels of privilege…

- ***Campbell*** left behind substantial room for judicial inspection while refusing overt challenge over privacy claims (*sec.*).
- ***CREW*** recognized ambiguity (“`vague nature’`) as potentially undermining Exemption® yet still largely preserved it due largely to persistence (“`the investigation remains ongoing`”) after further fact‐gathering (*paragraph S*).
- ***CNSS*** repeatedly highlighted situations where sovereign interest ≠ purely domestic threat and arguably entrenched ordinary procedural checks (*paragraph P*).

These precedents illustrate how courts apply heightened standards whenever national security narratives flag against conventional Foia presumptions and require explicit pleading by agencies beyond their typical discretionist pronouncement.

### Bottom line

*Farahi* confirms a balanced doctrine:

1. Agency takes a deferral posture only if both statutory predicates stand up upon objective inquiry.
2. Deferential treatment ceases when facts fail—obligatory sovereign accountability remains.
3. Neither annotation provides an implicit congressional waiver nor long–wavelength aborting of First Amendment rights under FOIA.
