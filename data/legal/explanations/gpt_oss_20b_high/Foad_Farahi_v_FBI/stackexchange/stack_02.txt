Answer:
**Answer**

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Foad Farahi v. FBI* confirms that an agency’s justification under FOIA § 552(b)(7)(A) receives **higher deference** than ordinary statutory claims because only the agency possessing the requisite expertise and institutional knowledge can properly evaluate whether “records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” will “reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.”  

Below I address each procedural issue raised by your question, drawing directly from the court’s reasoning and its controlling precedents.



--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 1. Why § 552(b)(7)(A) demands greater deference  

- **Statutory language**  
 § 552(b)(7)(A) makes an explicit exception—the “law‑enforcement purpose” requirement—mere factual inquiry; however, it also imposes responsibility on “any federal agency” to apply it correctly once it has identified records that were prepared *for* such investigations (“compiled for law‑enforcement purposes”). Who can most accurately determine what qualifies is itself part of what grants this clause its special weight.

- **Fedcircuit precedent**  
 The D.C. Circuit has consistently held that where an agency’s core function involves *law‑enforcement*, its own‐judgment deservingde facto appropriate review even when other courts could simply apply Code analysis. For example:
  
 *Campbell v.* DOJ, 164 F.R.D.O.J.^1 – “Because the FBI specializes … its determination ... is entitled ]to a fair measure of deference.”  
 *(See also Clemente*, 867 F.R.D.O.J.^2). These statements frame § 552(b)(7)(A)’s deference at roughly comparable level to *Chevron*: an “agency’s reasonable interpretation” gets primary evaluation unless clearly erroneous or arbitrary—that was precisely what *Farahi* reiterated in paragraph **A** (the court concluded FBI met its burden).

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 2. Governing review standard  

#### •​ Rational‑basis / deferential review  
The appropriate procedural lens here is **rational‑basis and/or a federal‑agency respect version of Chevron**, not constitutional strict scrutiny:
  
• In *Farahi*, paragraph **B** confirms the government must show two facts ‑ records are compiled *for* law‑enforcement purposes **and** release would reasonably disrupt pending or “reasonably anticipated” proceedings (*crewing*).  
• The government did so via affidavits attesting both facts (Hardy Bender declarations).  

> The reviewing court found no reasonably preferrable standard exists beyond checking *authority + rational nexus*. The emphasis was on confirming a legitimate ”law‑enforcement duty.” Hence strict scrutiny – which requires governmental action necessary , narrowly tailored & least restrictive—doesn’t apply here because § 552(b)(7)(A)’s purpose isn’t shielding citizens’ constitutional rights from prosecution ; it merely protects operational integrity against premature disclosure.*  

(keeping premise consistent with emphasis placed upon actual operational effect rather than rights infringement.)

### 3. National‑security context & First Amendment sideline
