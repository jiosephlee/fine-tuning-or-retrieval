### "Compiled for law‑enforcement purposes" under FOIA §552(b)(7)(A)
Question:

Answer:
**Short answer**

*Yes.* Records that are created *solely* for intelligence‐gathering can be covered by FOIA §552(b)(7)(A)—provided they were produced **as part of an investigative activity that could give rise to a federal prosecution or other enforcement action** (e.g., removal or denial of asylum).  
The court repeatedly held that “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” requires only a **reasonable nexus** between what was investigated and one of the agency’s statutory duties—no active indictment or trial has to exist at the time the request is made.  

The connection does not have to point directly at a current criminal prosecution; counter‑terrorism surveillance alone suffices if it was undertaken with an eye toward future enforcement—or even domestic security enforcement such as removal proceedings—so long as disclosure would likely **interfere** with those pending or anticipated procedures ("tipping‑off," revealing investigative techniques, etc.).

---

## 1️⃣ What counts as “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes”?

FOIA §552(b)(7)(A) protects *records* “compiled **for** law enforcement purposes.”  
That phrase means more than merely being produced by a police officer—it requires *intent* and *utility* for an impending federal investigative or prosecutorial activity.

In **Farahi** the district court said:

> “…the withheld records were compiled **‘in furtherance’ of investigations of violations of national security … including pending law‑enforcement proceedings…**”  
> ― Foad Farahi v. FBI, D.C. Cir., 2025, ¶ 51–52

and emphasized deference:

> Because the FBI specializes in law‑enforcement, its determination on this point is entitled to deference … The government must still show *a rational nexus*…and *a connection*…between an individual/incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.*  
> ― §A½

*Campbell* set out this classic two‑prong burden (*Nexus & Connection*) and anchored it in **Pratt vs Webster**, where field investigations targeting high‑risk individuals were treated alike—even though no immediate charges existed at that time :

> “…the government’s declaration must establish…a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one...of its law‑enforcement duties.’”  
> ― Pratt v Webster, ¶ 6

Thus deference is paid to agencies that certify their intent—but they **must prove** that their work actually falls within one of their statutory duties toward enforcing federal laws (criminal statutes, immigration statutes, national–security statutes).

---

## 2️⃣ Can records made purely for intelligence qualify?

Yes—provided those records arise from an investigative effort whose purpose is *future* enforcement or national‑security action.
The courts have repeatedly treated counter‑terrorism surveillance — even without any pending indictment — as falling under §552(b)(7)(A). See:

| Authority | Why it applies |
|---|---|
| **Farahi** | The agency claimed the files were compiled “in furtherance…of investigations…national security.” The court accepted this assertion because it met both prongs (“nexus” & “connection”). |
| **CREW** | Declared that investigators’ ongoing activities—the mere existence—are sufficient when dismissals show such activities continue toward eventual prosecution/administrative injury. |
| **Juarez** | Held agencies may withhold entire files when exempt portions are “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material because segregation would destroy intent/effectiveness—exactly what often happens when raw intel feeds later prosecutions.|

To put it simply: if your information was collected **to help build a case later**, it counts even if no case currently exists on record books.

---

## 3️⃣ How close must the link between priority action & agent duty be?

The standard is *reasonable specificity*, not concrete details about an imminent arrests schedule.
- A broad statement such
that “investigations remain ongoing” suffices when supported by additional detail ("federal threats," "international terrorism allegations," etc.).  
- The court reiterated in *Farahi* that statements like Hardy’s hard data on continuing targets were adequate evidence:
  
> “Hardy declaration explained…that disclosure could reasonably interfere by tipping off targets about scope.” ― §B½  

- Courts prefer short factual summaries followed by filing affidavits explaining what exactly was being gathered (“information on links”

So you need more than an empty promise—it has to link specific individuals/items flagging them against known terrorist organizations—or very similar data—to state‑wide legal goals like preventing terrorism or enforcing immigration laws.

## 4️⃣ Immigration proceedings count?

Removal/asylum hearings fall under **“enforcement proceedings”** for FOIA since removal removes someone from U.S., violating federal immigration law.
In *Farahi*, DHS gave witness testimony about losses linked farah Asian individuals had ties abroad and registered them as apparently posing threats—

> “…DHS presented documents… alleging Farahi’s connections”… —this indicated federal adversarial activity far ahead fiduciary plaintiffs’ insights…

Because those hearings require producing documents describing potential unlawful behavior—and because they emanate from executives demanding *law-enforcing authority*—the files gathered by the FBI during those same systems qualified under §552(b)(7)(A).

So yes – inspecting those files alone can trigger exemption after rejection because they constitute data prepared within an official procurement intended toward legal proceeding whether civil or criminal enemy actions are linked together via investigational ties among authorities involved​ (IMO).
