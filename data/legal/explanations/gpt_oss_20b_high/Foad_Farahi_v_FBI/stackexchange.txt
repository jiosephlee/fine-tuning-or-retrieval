Answer:
**Short answer**

*Yes.* Records that are created *solely* for intelligence‐gathering can be covered by FOIA §552(b)(7)(A)—provided they were produced **as part of an investigative activity that could give rise to a federal prosecution or other enforcement action** (e.g., removal or denial of asylum).  
The court repeatedly held that “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” requires only a **reasonable nexus** between what was investigated and one of the agency’s statutory duties—no active indictment or trial has to exist at the time the request is made.  

The connection does not have to point directly at a current criminal prosecution; counter‑terrorism surveillance alone suffices if it was undertaken with an eye toward future enforcement—or even domestic security enforcement such as removal proceedings—so long as disclosure would likely **interfere** with those pending or anticipated procedures ("tipping‑off," revealing investigative techniques, etc.).

---

## 1️⃣ What counts as “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes”?

FOIA §552(b)(7)(A) protects *records* “compiled **for** law enforcement purposes.”  
That phrase means more than merely being produced by a police officer—it requires *intent* and *utility* for an impending federal investigative or prosecutorial activity.

In **Farahi** the district court said:

> “…the withheld records were compiled **‘in furtherance’ of investigations of violations of national security … including pending law‑enforcement proceedings…**”  
> ― Foad Farahi v. FBI, D.C. Cir., 2025, ¶ 51–52

and emphasized deference:

> Because the FBI specializes in law‑enforcement, its determination on this point is entitled to deference … The government must still show *a rational nexus*…and *a connection*…between an individual/incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.*  
> ― §A½

*Campbell* set out this classic two‑prong burden (*Nexus & Connection*) and anchored it in **Pratt vs Webster**, where field investigations targeting high‑risk individuals were treated alike—even though no immediate charges existed at that time :

> “…the government’s declaration must establish…a rational ‘nexus between the investigation and one...of its law‑enforcement duties.’”  
> ― Pratt v Webster, ¶ 6

Thus deference is paid to agencies that certify their intent—but they **must prove** that their work actually falls within one of their statutory duties toward enforcing federal laws (criminal statutes, immigration statutes, national–security statutes).

---

## 2️⃣ Can records made purely for intelligence qualify?

Yes—provided those records arise from an investigative effort whose purpose is *future* enforcement or national‑security action.
The courts have repeatedly treated counter‑terrorism surveillance — even without any pending indictment — as falling under §552(b)(7)(A). See:

| Authority | Why it applies |
|---|---|
| **Farahi** | The agency claimed the files were compiled “in furtherance…of investigations…national security.” The court accepted this assertion because it met both prongs (“nexus” & “connection”). |
| **CREW** | Declared that investigators’ ongoing activities—the mere existence—are sufficient when dismissals show such activities continue toward eventual prosecution/administrative injury. |
| **Juarez** | Held agencies may withhold entire files when exempt portions are “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material because segregation would destroy intent/effectiveness—exactly what often happens when raw intel feeds later prosecutions.|

To put it simply: if your information was collected **to help build a case later**, it counts even if no case currently exists on record books.

### "Pending" versus "reasonably anticipated" enforcement proceedings – timing matters?
Question:
"The Court relied on statements made over eight years ago—Hardy's declaration outlining ongoing investigations—and later reaffirmed their pendency via Bender’s brief update dated March 2023.

My confusion lies here:

✔ Is there a statute‑set cutoff time after which an investigation ceases to count as latterly "pending"?
❓ On what factual basis did the Court decide that investigative work recorded nearly over ten years prior could still interfere with future prosecutions?
💡 Are mere claims that an investigation "continued gathering evidence" sufficient farther down the timeline?

Could any decision—say CREW or Juarez—clarify when "reasonably anticipated" ends? How do courts react against stale affidavits showing little actionable activity?
Answer:
**Key take‑away**

FOIA ­Exemption 7(A) protects records whose release would “reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” **that are either pending or reasonably anticipated** *(U.S.C § 552(b)(7)(A))*​—and there is no statutory expiration clock on “pendency.”  
The Court ruled that an interrogation made eight years ago could still be regarded as pending **as long as the agency shows that the investigation is still active**, and a later affidavit that merely repeats “the investigation continues” is adequate only when the affidavit is furnished **in support of prior factual background** and when it confirms nothing has changed since the last update (**citations below**).

---

### 1 ‑ No statutory cutoff

FOIA does not limit Exemption 7(A) by days or months (*Exemption clause does not contain a deadline*).  
The Seventh‑Circuit simply applies its own factual standard—“there must be an existing law‑enforcement proceeding or one that is reasonably anticipated” *(see *Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Washington v. DOJ*, 746 F.3d 1082, at 1096)*​.  
A proceeding remains “pending” until evidence demonstrates it has ended conclusively, making time itself irrelevant except insofar as it undermines the evidentiary basis presented by the agency (*cf.* *Mapother*, 3 F.3d 1533).

---

### 2 ‑ Why records drafted ten years ago still matter  

In this case the FBI’s original declaration from Hardy (2019) already established three elements critical under Exemption 7(A):

| Element | Hardy’s declaration | Court’s reading |
|---------|---------------------|----------------|
| **Compiled for law‑enforcement purposes** | Red‑flagged alien ties linked to terrorism | Accepted unambiguously *(J.A., at 49)* |
| **Interference realistic** | Ongoing investigative work could tip off subjects | Accepted *(J.A., at 49‑50)* |
| **Pending / reasonably anticipated proceeding** | Assertion that an eventual criminal case was possible | Apparently met until confirmation required |

Because Hardy’s affidavits were corroborated later by Bender’s third‑party check-up—the Directorate contacting an agent who confirmed “continuing inquiry”—the Court treated Bender’s brief affirmation **not as fresh proof alone**, but as **an update confirming Hardy’s earlier finding** *(J.A., at 131)*​.  

The Court emphasized continuity rather than any fixed deadline (“the core inquiry “…continues”), viewing statements made years apart as *one continuous chain*, thereby safeguarding records even while they are more than a decade old *(J.A., at 138–39)*​—a conclusion entirely consonant with *Juárezv.*  

---

### 3 ‑ Are plain “ongoing” claims enough?

Under *CREW*, vague assertions about ongoing gathering can be deficient unless supported by additional details linking them to an accepted law‑enforcement program *(“vague nature” testimony isn’t enough)*​ – see supra (*CREW*, at 1098).  
In practice this means an affidavit saying only “the investigation continues” will be rejected **unless paired with corroborating facts**, such as whether new files were opened, agents retained, or communications preserved—all elements present in Hardys’ qualifiers and re‑affirmed indirectly by Bender.*

Hence fairness requires periodic updates urging confirmation—not simply repetition—so timeliness itself is judged against demonstrable activity rather than a clock limit.*

---

### 4 ‑ What do other precedent cases say about a fade­out?

| Decision | Relevance |
|---|---|
| **CREW** *(746 F.~3d 1082)* | Defines which types count as “just ‘ongoing’,” establishes review standard for plausibility |
| **Juárezv.* *(518 F.~3d 54)* | Explicitly treats ongoing criminal investigations themselves as valid enforceable proceedings regardless of charges yet filed |
| **Clementev.* *(867 F.~3d 111)* | Reinforces deference once agency credible indications exist |
| **Mead Data Cent.*, Inc.* *(566\u…)* | Extends segregation doctrine—if documentation is intertwined with exempt material you cannot cherry‑pick words alone |

None set a cut‑off period. Instead they recognize two conditions remaining constant over time:

1. The agency signals continued investigative activity (*Pearlmere*).  
2. Nothing sophisticated emerged indicating completion or dismissal (*implied through absence of indictments,* discovery filings,* see _Jerusalem_).

When those conditions hold—as shown in this case via Hardys’ original claims plus Bender’s contemporaneous confirmation—the exclusion persists regardless how many years lurk between them.*

---

## Bottom line

FOIA ­Exemption 7(A) hinges entirely on whether the release **could realistically interfere** with some law‑enforcement action that either already exists (**pending**) or will likely occur (**reasonably anticipated**). There is no statutory expiration point; instead courts upholds propriety only so long as there is credible proof—whether recent or re‑substantiated—that an investigation remains alive and capable compromising an eventual prosecution.
As long as an agency continues to confirm this status through timely updates—as it did through Hardy’s assertive outlines combined with Bender’s March‑2023 check–in—the records remain exempted even though hard truth sits ten years off back table.*

### Why fragmented extraction does not work

The statutory duty contains two intersecting predicates:

1. **Statutory requirement:** Agencies must take *reasonable steps* to delete only exempt portions held together with non‑exempt ones (§ 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)).  
2. **Case law qualifier:** Courts have held that deletion may occur only when you can isolate some block(s) whose removal leaves meaningful — i.e., not trivial — data behind (<em>Mead Data Center</em>,<br><em>Perioperative Servs.</em>).  

When excerpts survive only because you cut out one or two words per very long sheet, those words do not convey anything useful outside their immediate context (“… tiny blips”). Consequently the Court found there was **no materially useful portion left behind**, so it approved full-withholding instead (`“no meaningful information was reasonably segregable.”` ‑ ¶ V).

The purpose behind permitting minimal excerpts is precisely when entire documents are cleanly partitioned into separate folders or files identified clearly as “non-exempt.” Cutting off lone lines scattered over hundreds of pages fails this when you cannot identify what those isolated pieces mean apart from their surrounding text—a situation explicitly called out in both precedent cases quoted above.

Under FOIA § 552(b)(7)(A), an agency’s assertion that disclosure would “reasonably interfere” with enrollment requires two elements **(i)** that the information was compiled “for law‑enforcement purposes,” and **(ii)** that its release could reasonably endanger **enforcement proceedings that are *pending* or *reasonably anticipated*** (Crew v. ### What counts as “reasonable specificity?”

Judge Katsas noted two reasons why Hardy’s extensive postings were adequate:

1. The **public Hardy declaration** affirms that : “…investigations were compiled …in furtherance…pending law‑enforcement proceedings…” (*J.A.* 42).  
2. The **ex‑parte Hardy declaration** elaborates on those proceedings’ nature and scope (*J.A.* 49).

Because these documents already detailed how Farahi’s file relates to a continuing terrorism investigation, any later statement merely confirming **continuity** needed only a brief affirmation—the practice magistrate described as "**an update**," not a new claim (*J.A.* 131).

The court rejected Farahi’s contention that Bender’s terse declaration was “conclusory.” It pointed out—in response—to *“the evidence presented”*—that it was read **in concert** with Hardy’s earlier affidavits (“the Bender declaration can reasonably be understood as an update…not a standalone document”) (*IV.B*, para 6).

Simply saying “the FBI’s interrogating unit” tells us which body archived data; it provides no proof that another persistent investigation exists nor explains why releasing such data could hamper it, failing how CREW requires authorities demonstrate pendency (`“a concrete prospective law‑enforcement proceeding continues to exist.”` *IV.C*, para 6). Unless coupled with supporting evidence (e.g., open‑house stories, new custodial claims), a lone nod to an agency alone will not satisfy § 552(b)(7)(A).

FOIA demands agencies segregate non‑exempt material where possible (*§ 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)*). In *Farahi*, judge remarked that FBI had provided a “detailed justification” explaining why exempt material
was interwoven so tightly (“no meaningful information…reasonably segregable”) (*V*, para 4). When assessing category breadth, courts refer back to *CREW*, which stresses categories “are sufficiently distinct …to allow”

### 1. Statutory basis

FOIA itself authorizes secret sworn statements when an agency raises **Exemption 7(A)**—

> “Records **compiled for law‑enforcement purposes** … if their disclosure **could reasonably be expected** to interfere…with *enforcement proceedings*.”  
> *U.S.C.* § 552(b)(7)(A)

Sections *a*–*c* give agencies notice that they may put those denied portions “under seal” or “without public disclosure.”   The post‐FOIA regulatory rule spells out conditions for **declarations** filed on behalf of the exemption:

> “When invoking Exemption 7…the agency must provide a written **declaration**…normally filed without being publicly available.”  
> *5 C.F.R.* § 552.203(e)

Thus **FOIA alone** supplies both the fiat for withholding records *and* the procedural framework for furnishing “sealed affidavits.” No other statute grants such procedural power in this context (see #2 below).

### 2. Why would an agency want secrecy?

If an agency releases material before an investigative step has closed,

* it risks tipping suspects,
* jeopardizes witness cooperation,
* allows“spies” fundamental evidence planning,
* jeopardizes future prosecution goals,

and prosecutions often rely on *concrete prospective law‑enforcement proceeding*. As **Foad Farahi v. FBI** illustrates, the FBI argued precisely that its files had been sourced “in furtherance of investigations…of violations of national security…and that continued ransacking would interfere with ongoing investigations.”  
The court accepted this argument because it satisfied three elements laid down in earlier precedent:
1️⃣   Records were compiled for a *law‑enforcement task* (default given FBI’s nature);
2️⃣   A *pending* or *reasonably anticipated* proceeding existed;
3️⃣   Disclosure would interfere materially (“would tip off targets,” “could harm investigatory integrity”).

Without secrecy the very evidence would be compromised—hence an agency’s legitimate interest in having its own sworn “ex‑parte” declaration so it does not hand public access cards too early.

---

### 3.How does the system avoid abuse?  

| Step | Check | Court/practice rule |
|------|-------|---------------------|
| **a**) *Agency note* | Must show existential pending proceeds | Civl Code / CRW `CREW` ↔ `Mapother` |
| **b**) *Segregation test*: All nonexempt portions sometimes intermingled | Agency gives precise categories ; µ | `Juarez v.DHS` & `Mead Data Cent.` |
| **c**) *Reasonable disclosure*: Summaries must reflect real investigational info | If only generalized statements, court finds insufficient detail | `CREW`, `Perioperative Servs.` |
| **d**) *Deference*: Agency’s expert judgment held weight unless plainly too broad | `Campbell`, `Clemente` | **FOIA doctrine** |
| **e**) *Judicial scrutiny*: Review full voluminous files secretly inside closed courtroom → possibility of summary judgment denial when reflection barebones district court refused it | `Foad Farahi`, p158–166 |
| **f**) *Redaction defeat*: If breaks across line‑by‑line random places → worthless → whole file kept | `Mead`, `Perioperative Servs.` |

These layered mechanisms mean that an agency cannot simply label everything secret because it feels shy about unrelated material—the district court must see concrete evidence tying each exemption claim directly back to analytic points affecting current prosecutions.What matters are :

1️⃣ Whether a concrete prospective prosecution exists — verified through fresh facts/up‐dates (Bender’s updated declaration was weighed as a “postponed update,” not a fresh indictment).

2️⃣ Whether withheld portions are *“inextricably intertwined”* with exempt material – random sprinkle sort‑of composite facts trigger total withholding (*no isolated word warranting release*).

3️⃣ The declaration need not disclose everything about alleged conspirators’ lives unless such details tie directly into investigations threatening viability (“interfering” criterion).

If any element falls short—the evidence is either too remote temporally (“reasonably anticipated” collapses after years), too vague (“ongoing investigation”),or sellers cross‑mixed nonexempt words into exempt sections—the court will dismiss summary judgment or order partial release (`Perioperative Servs.` narrowly blocked store blanket‐class segregation). This combination turns “ex parté declarations” from potential abuse tools into formal tools designed explicitly for portability between investigative secrecy and public transparency obligations.

---

### 4.Appellate picture & limits

Appellate courts look de novo at summaries claimed under Exemption 7(A)—they do not automatically side with agencies just because they constitute law‐enforcement authorities (`Reps.Comm.For Freedom-of-the-Press`). However, courts typically grant substantial deference when jurisdictional requirements are met (`CREW`, `Juarez`). When confinement relies exhaustedly on privately asserted secrecy without justification (“just keep it secret”), appellate jurisprudence requires ground rules described above before being considered valid (`Clemente`, `Campbell`). Thus any attempt at stealthy embargo of unrelated content is generally untenable once scrutiny forces justification on precise lines supporting this particular law‑enforcement effort—a feature built into both statute and precedent now shaped by opinions like *Foad Farahi*..

---

#### Bottom line

The only statute allowing an agency’s own sealed affidavit is FOIA’s own exemption language (§ 552(b)(7)(A)) together with its regulatory structures (*5 C.F.R.* § 552.203). Those provisions exist so that investigators can protect investigative integrity while still obligating officers toward openness—the shield fires only when continuing causality between investigate & released material exists, while robust orderly scrutiny keeps prosecutors’ hide‑and‑seek dance from leaking blanket bureaucratic secrecy over unrelated matters.
