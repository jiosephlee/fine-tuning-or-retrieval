### What constitutes ‘pending’ or ‘reasonably anticipated’ enforcement proceedings under § 552(b)(7)(A)?
Question:
In *Foad Farahi v. FBI*, the court found FBI’s investigation was still ’ongoing’ more than three years after the initial request—even though there was no formal indictment against Mr. Farahi yet.

I’m unclear how courts determine whether an investigation satisfies ‘pending’ or ‘reasonably anticipated’. Do you need an indictment, trial docket, or conviction?

Answer:
**Short answer**

For Exemption 7(A), an investigation counts as a *pending* or *reasonably anticipated* enforcement proceeding whenever it is an active law‑enforcement effort whose eventual outcome could suffer harm if its underlying evidence were disclosed—regardless of whether an indictment, trial docket, or conviction currently exists.   
The court looks at sworn agency testimony (affidavits/declarations), whatever brief update indicates that the same investigative activity continues without interruption, and whether a related civil action such as an immigration‑removal proceeding is still open.  Either type alone suffices; no formal criminal charge is required unless the agency specifically makes that connection.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1. What “pending” and “reasonably anticipated” mean

* **Statutory text** – § 552(b)(7)(A) requires that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with **enforcement proceedings** that are **pending or reasonably anticipated**.”  
* **CREW precedent** – *Citizens for Resp.& Ethics v DOJ*, 746 F.3d 1082 (D.C.~Cir.~2014), held that “ongoing criminal investigations count as ’proceedings’,” and a proceeding need only exist *enough* that it could be completed later (“...the investigation continues to gather evidence...and that case would be jeopardized …”)【999】.  
* **Application in *Farahi*** – the district court adopted exactly this language:[R]=“Ongoing criminal investigations…count … [as]’proceedings.’”  
   The court therefore treated the FBI’s counter‑terrorism inquiry into Mr Farahi—not its status at trial—as sufficient proof of pending work.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2. Do you need an indictment, trial docket or conviction?

No.  
The standard looks at whether there is *current investigative activity*. In *Farahi* the court underscores this point when it says,

> “Therefore…the record‑holders may show such proceedings simply by attesting to their existence.”【999】

An immigration‑removal proceeding falls squarely within § 552(b)(7)(A)’s definition of enforcement actions because it is a federal civil action intended to compel compliance with federal law (*such actions are routinely protected wherever they serve law‑enforcement ends*, see *CNSS*).

### "Category” Identification Under § 552(b)– Why Must Agencies Use Specific Distinct Categories?
Question:
*Farahi* raised that excluded categories appeared overbroad because he never saw particular definitions.

I noted *CREW*, *Gallant*, and *Khine v. DHS*’s utility lists stating categories must enable courts identify applicable exemptions.

My confusions:

* What minimum level of detail distinguishes one category from another?

* If every page has both exempt information (evidence) and potential public interest facts, can any single category suffice? Or must agencies split these individually before affording partial release?

* How does failure to meet categorize expectations affect withdrawal credibility?
Answer:
**Short Answer**

The “category” test arises under § 552(b)(7)(A)’s requirement that any exempt portion “must be *reasonably segregable*” from non‑exempt material (§ 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)).  
To satisfy that standard an agency *must* divide a requested file into logically distinct groups whose boundaries let a court decide whether Exemption 7(A) applies separately to each group (*CREW*, 746 F.​3d 1088).

If every page contains both exempt evidence and potentially public‑interest data then a single broad category cannot satisfy this test unless the agency shows—by definitive line‑by‑line markup—that non‑exempt portions are truly intermixed and cannot be pulled out without loss of meaning (*Meads*, 566 F.​2d 262).  
In *Farahi*, Washington’s RIDS concluded that no “meaningful information was reasonably segregable” because “non‑exempt material was randomly dispersed” throughout each CD (*Farahi* Kt͟athas J., ¶ 139). Thus the FBI was required—and ultimately did—withhold whole CDs rather than offer a pseudo‑release built around an ambiguous aggregate grouping.

When an agency’s categorization does **not** meet this level of distinction, its denial becomes unreliable under § 552(a)(8). A court consequently treats any asserted segregation document or declaration skeptically (*Khine v DHS*, 943 F.​3d 959), potentially requiring further review or even reversal of an initial deny order if the missing detail shows non‑exempt portions could in fact have been released.



---

### What constitutes “sufficiently distinct” categorizations?

> “When an agency withholds documents **on a category‑by‑category basis**, it must use **categories…that are ‘sufficiently distinct’** …to allow a court …to determine whether …the specific claimed exemptions …are properly applied” (*CREW*, 746 F.​3d 1088).

For Exemption 7(A) this means producing groupings in which each bundle contains only material gathered **for** enforcement purposes and does **not** mix unrelated administrative or private sections.  
The “minimum level of detail” is thus enough resolution so a reviewer could isolate any potentially excluded fragment without needing additional internal processing—a standard implied wherever the court mentions “reasonably segregable portion.”  

In *Farahi* the FBI’s Bender declaration clarified two initiative layers:

| Category | Description   |
|----------|---------------|
| **Investigative Records** | Evidence directly supporting active investigations into terrorism ties |
| **Administrative/Case Documentation** | Forms and correspondences unrelated to investigative activity |

By parsing records into those two buckets—and then confirming which subset contained Post‑interrogation notes or fingerprint logs—the FBI demonstrated compliance with § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii).

### "Ex Parte" Declarations—When Are They Acceptable?
Question:
*Farahi* protested reliance upon sealed Hardy/Bender affidavits.

Is it legitimate for plaintiffs to challenge Secretary‐issued sealed submissions once a motion was approved without prejudice?

Answer:
### Quick Takeaway  
In **Foad Farahi v. FBI**, the D.C.~Circuit held that an agency may submit **sealed executive‑summaries** – i.e., “ex‑parte” affidavits – when a statutory exemption hinges on sensitive information whose release would probably **interfere with existing or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings** (*See §​7(A)*).  
Plaintiffs may contest those sealed submissions **only insofar as they show failure on three fronts**:

| Point | What must be proved | Citation |
|-------|--------------------|----------|
| **(1)** That a product was actually **compiled for law‑enforcement purposes** | The agency bears *burden* (“U.S.C.§ 552(a)(4)(B)”); partial support normally affords deference | *F»Farahi*, J.A.»4 |
| **(2)** That release would **reasonably be expected** to **interfere** with proceedings currently **pending or reasonably anticipated** | Pendency requires an “existing investigation” plus a plausible threat of harm | *COPE*’s ••“[We] have held….” &#x2026; cf.*CREW*, 746 F.R.L.^1 |
| **(3)** That non‑exempt material **cannot be meaningfully segregated** | If exempt portions mix so tightly with non‑exempt ones that removal would either leave them incomplete or impossible, total withdrawal is warranted | *	F»Farahi*, §III ⁹‑10 |

If any one element fails, courts will either demand more detail, order segregation/partial release (U.S.C.§ 552(a)(8)(A)), or deny blanket exemption protection altogether ***without prejudice***.

The central issue is whether FOIA Exemption 7(A) applies to records the FBI withheld from Farahi’s request and, if it does, whether the court correctly applied its deference standards and dealt properly with the “time‑passage” objection that Farahi raises.

---

### 1.  What Exemption 7(A) requires

Exemption 7(A) bars disclosure only when

1. **(i)** the records were **compiled for law‑enforcement purposes**; and  
2. **(ii)** their release could **reasonably be expected to interfere** with **pending or reasonably anticipated** enforcement proceedings (§552(b)(7)(A)).

The FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, so its judgment on point (i) receives “a fair measure of deference” (*Clemente* v. FBI; *Campbell* *et al.*).  
To satisfy point (ii) a court must accept any claim that an investigation is continuing or may be continued and that disclosure would jeopardize it (*Crews for Resp.* v. DOJ; *Juarez* v. DOJ).

---

### 2.  Evidence that proceedings are still “pending or reasonably anticipated”

Farahi contests that the very passage of time undermines these claims. The court rejected this argument on several grounds:

| Evidence | Why it satisfies pendency / anticipation | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| **Hardy public declaration** (dated Oct 2019) | Declared that the investigative files were “compiled … in furtherance of investigations … pending law‑enforcement proceedings.” | “Public Hardy declaration explained ... pending law‑enforcement proceedings” (J.A. 49) |
| **Hardy ex‑parte declaration** | Provided substantive detail on scope and status of the investigations (“ongoing”). | “Hardy’s ex‑parte declaration supplied considerable detail” (J.A. 49) |
| **Bender public declaration** (Mar 2023) | Confirmed that investigations remained *ongoing* at that date – an update rather than a new claim of pendency.| “Bender’s declaration confirmed the investigation remained ongoing as of March 2023” (J.A. 131–32) |

The district court already recognized in November 2022 that “the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes … seemingly met its burden to show that enforcement proceedings were pending or reasonably anticipated” (*J.A.* 122). Bender’s statement merely reinforced this conclusion rather than creating a new pendency claim. In light of this cumulative evidence and given FOIA’s policy emphasis on moving cases forward without undue delay – even over long horizons – the court found a sufficient factual basis for pendency/anticipation on **the merits** outweighing Farahi’s concern about vague assertions.

---

### 3.  Deference to governmental determinations

Because FOIA interprets statutory exemptions rather than private rulemaking, courts give deference to agencies’ factual findings when those determinations involve technical expertise (*Campbell*; *CNSS*). In this case:

- The FBI was presumed to have accurate knowledge of its own investigations (*Campbell*, “Given Deference”).  
- Both public and ex‑parte affidavits detailed investigative activities and prospective prosecutions involving Near‑term terrorism risks (*Crews*, “nonexempt portions ‘are inextricably intertwined’”).  

Hence the appellate court concluded correct deference was given: “the *. . . determination on this point is entitled to ‘deference.’”

---

### 4.  What does "time passage" mean in FOIA?

FOIA does not have a statute‑of‑limitations-like mechanism squashing claims merely because several years have elapsed. The statute speaks only of pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings at *the time* when disclosure would occur (*crews*, citing *Cremona*). A long‑running counterterrorism effort—particularly one still gathering evidence—may well remain “pending” even after many years if:

- The FBI still intends to prosecute based on information gathered recently or under pours presumption; and  
- Disclosure would risk informing targets or undermine investigative continuity (as broadly accepted under Exemption 7(A)) (*Juarez*, § 59).

In Farahi’s case, both Hardy and Bender attest—individually—not only that investigations continue but also that they remain connected to actionable terrorism allegations reported in his immigration proceedings six years earlier (e.g., alleged recruiter status, links to known terrorists). Those claims give credence to agency assertions that new evidence could still emerge during an ongoing pursuit—a condition satisfying our statutory trigger (“remains ongoing”).

---

### 5.  Segregability Objection

Even if some nonexempt material exists within a record set marked exempt under §552(b)(7)(A), FOIA mandates removal (“reasonable steps”) before refusal (*J.A.*, marshalled facts from Bender’s ex‑parte submission). Farahi argued little was segregable because relevant material was scattered linearly through otherwise sensitive files (“randomly interspersed line by line…”). The court held that:

- Where unexempt language is only marginally embedded within documents whose purpose centers on securing prosecutable evidence *and* where accessing indicated nonexempt material would also expose exempt portions (“intertangled”), segregation isn’t feasible (*Mead*, id).*  
- The ex‑parte Bender affidavit explained why no functionally meaningful subset could be trimmed away without losing essential context – consistent with *Juarez*’s principle that derivatives can mean acquisition processes themselves cannot be separated.*

Thus no meaningful segregable portion survived examination under §552(a)(8)(A)(ii), matching agency burden satisfaction per statutory requirement.

---

### 6.  Bottom line

Taking all elements together:

1️⃣ The FBI demonstrated—through multiple affidavits—that contrary information was continually being compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and contributed directly to imminent prosecutions involving terrorism allegations against Farahi (§552(b)(7)(A)).  

2️⃣ Even after six years since a statutory deadline had been missed and additional materials were added in March 2023, those affidavits speak convincingly enough about continued investigations such that courts naturally regard such claims as pending/anticipated offenses (§552(b)(7)(A)).  

3️⃣ Good-faith deference applied here aligns with prevailing precedent: agencies may reliably assert national‑security concerns when their findings rely upon internal knowledge crucial for ongoing counterterrorism operations (*CNSS*, *Campeoll Low*).  

4️⃣ Where records are intertwined with very sensitive inquiries—especially when they contain contradictory accounts—separating exclusively nonexempt portions collapses into impracticality (`Mead`; `Perioperative Services`).  

So farthest parties agree: Exemption 7(A) bar stands; time alone does not defeat its requirements once continuing investigative activity can be shown basally by agency experts’ affidavits; segregation failed because nonexempt elements are inseparable from exempt context.

**Verdict:** affirmative; your appeal fails on substantive grounds reflected precisely in Axios case order above—unless additional evidence shows actual termination of all investigatory activity unreasonably outstripping mere lapse since March 2023.
