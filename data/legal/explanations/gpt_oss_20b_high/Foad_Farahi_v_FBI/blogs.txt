# Past Action Persists – How Farahi Clarifies Pending Enforcement Proceedings Under Foia’s §552(b)(7)(A)

## Why this matters

In its September 30 ruling, Judge Katsas reinforced a principle long buried beneath a decade‑long gap between S‑B‑FBI operations and the request it sought to satisfy. By affirming that a “pending or reasonably anticipated” proceeding can survive even when an investigation has barely advanced since its inception, the D.C. Circuit gives government agents more latitude to cloak counter‑terrorism indictments behind Exemption 7(A). For FOIA litigants—and for those who rely on open records to hold national‑security authorities accountable—this means plaintiffs can now expect an agency’s bland declaration that an investigation remains “ongoing” to carry far greater weight than before.

The case also clarifies how deference is applied when courts are forced to rely on ex parte affidavits that potentially expose classified information they wish to keep secret—a common provision in modern FOIA battles over closed surveillance files and terror‑watch lists. By treating Jordan‑shaped affidavits from Robert Bender as an update rather than an independent argument, Katsas signals that one sliver of apparently innocuous testimony may suffice if it sits atop a broader dossier evidencing continual investigative work against alleged terrorists such as Foad Farahi.

Because Public Interest commentators have routinely debated whether “ongoing” really equals “pending,” this decision is consequential for both parties who must shape future filings—units seeking disclosure now know they will have more flexibility to present evidence that investigations still exist even if no obvious filing or charge has yet materialized into action against White House vetted victims or suspects whose association dates back nearly two decades.

# Deference Amid Secrecy  

A handful of Supreme‑Court‑style decisions paint a picture—and sometimes a paradox—of how courts trawling through intelligence files see themselves standing between President Biden’s preferred agents and free‑wheeling journalists seeking every digit written down about a former Iranian activist who spent his post‑college years working toward periods embroiled in the country's clandestine state apparatuses.



In **Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation**, the United States Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit affirmed an injunction that barred disclosure—by way of **FOIA Exemption 7(A)**—of nearly eleven thousand pages and eighty CDs assembled during a decade‑long counterterrorism investigation overseen by the FBI’s Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Its core question was whether an agency could claim that releasing its own investigation files would jeopardize ongoing or “reasonably anticipated” enforcement proceedings when it made those files public through sealed affidavits, and whether courts should afford it sweeping ripeness protection even when those attestations revealed very little detail beyond confirmation that “the investigations remain ongoing.”

# When Nothing Seeks Release — Segregation Challenges within Massified Confidential Files  

## Why This Matters  
The D.C. Circuit’s Seventh‑Turned‑Eighth Decision in *Foad Farahi v. FBI* sends a stark signal throughout the federal agency compliance community: when a single document is an amalgam of law‑enforcement data and innocuous administrative notes and those pieces are so tightly mixed together that no “meaningful” unit can be surgically extracted, the entire file can lawfully stay sealed under FOIA Exemption 7(A). In an era where courts increasingly confront requests for sprawling datasets—think marked-up UML maps, vetting interval logs, or intelligence‑grade spreadsheets—this ruling congeals the doctrine on “reasonable segregability” into a near‑legal blacklist for defendants wishing to reclaim public interest data en masse without first splintering inferential contours across machine‑generated tables or indentations of metadata flags rising over each filing line.

## The Case in Brief  
Fard “Farahi,” an Iranian immigrant whose U.S. immigration record included multiple attempts at asylum and appeals before removal proceedings were finally discontinued in his favor also drew federal investigative interest because DHS claimed he had ties to terrorist organizations listed by State Department designation or later convicted under federal law (notably DOJ–treason charges).
