Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Selling Secrets Is One Thing — Why Nothing Gets Divided Out

## Why This Case Matters  

On September 30, 2025 the D.C. Circuit affirmed an unfurling argument by the Federal Bureau of Investigation that its voluminous file on *Foad Farahi* cannot be churned out frame‑by‑frame under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). In a nutshell, the Court said that because most of those documents are considered integral parts of ongoing counter‑terrorist investigations – and because intermingled exempt material cannot be cleanly excised – everything in the file remains withheld under Exemption 7(A). The decision both re‑confirms law‑enforcement deference in exposure‑rights litigation and sharpens a very practical question for practitioners: when can a FOIA requester get a slice of an otherwise secret file?

The stakes are far from abstract. For years there have been dozens of filings aimed at unmasking government surveillance practices amid immigration cases or counter‑terrorism probes. What this decision does is crystallize a key threshold—“reasonable segregation”—in secret law‑enforcement files whose contents may mean for all their mundane sections that anyone reading them could learn something more or less critical about a crime investigation or an intelligence operation. Thinking outside this narrative is risky, because much of what we rely on in modern FOIA practice today comes from cases like *CREW* (2014), *Juarez* (2008), and *Mapother* (1993). That deference never ends abruptly once an indictment has been announced or a warrant has been executed; it merely follows its path across secret files until public disclosure would actually compromise enforcement proceedings—an outcome far less common than imagined by many defendants who seek transparency simply to expose any abuse or error in their files.

## The Factual Landscape  

Farahi is an Iranian national who has lived in Washington state since 1993 and has been subject to federal removal proceedings since 2002 as a result of an overstayed visa. Through his petitions he argued he had no substantive national‑security interest and complied with every regulation throughout his immigration track record as an “individual I love” – luckily his bashing means that he filed yet another request under FOIA seeking his full FBI dossier—10 750 pages plus 80 CDs—in order to see whether anyone ever actually “spelled” his name beside most plausible terrorist–linked activity.  

The FBI missed its statutory response deadline (the statutory due date after first inquiry failure) long enough that Farahi sued in federal court. Its first attempt – based on a declaration by David Hardy, RIDS chief – simply alleged that all of its material had been compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations…of national security…and international terrorism” – no picking‑apart whether any parts were actually relevant or safely separable from confidential information. In 2022 after extra expression from Joseph Bender (then acting RIDS chief), which confirmed “ongoing investigations,” the district court granted summary judgment without prejudice under Exemption 7(A). Since then Farahi appeals for two main reasons: (i) that exemption depends upon whether proceedings remain *pending* or *reasonably anticipated*, which may no longer be true given the elapsed time; (ii) that secrecy was overbroad because he could have gotten at least some useful nonexempt information if certain content could be neatly segregated from what was truly exempt.

## Deference to Law Enforcement

Under § 552(b)(7)(A), Exemption 7(A) shields any record compiled “for law‑enforcement purposes” if its disclosure could “reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” To determine “law‑enforcement purposes,” courts look for evidence that needs arose through legitimate investigation functions—a standard not meant to be arbitrarily tweaked by plaintiffs after they have filed their request. Thus when Judge Katsas said “because the FBI specializes…its decision...is entitled to deference,” she relied on longstanding jurisprudence (*Campbell*, *Clemente*, *Mapother*) that recognizes-specificity over speculation about jurisdictional roles.   

The Board’s discretion manifested even after almost three years’ silence when it accepted Hardy’s ex parte declaration along with Bender’s relatively brief sealing statements confirming continuous activity—both corroborated Farahi’s own admissions during his own hearings where he had appeared before attorneys alleging his link “to Khaled Sheikh Mohammed”–a known terrorist proceeded into criminal timing boards or immigration authorities’ complainery docket/green‑light queue via “charities such as Holy Land Foundation.” In short:

1️⃣ Exemption covers any document necessary for such investigations.
2️⃣ Biden had no immediate public argument meaning FBI legitimately assumed jurisdiction because **“compiled for law-enforcement purposes.”**

All else falls under deference arguments; contesting them would rarely succeed unless you could pin down a statutory exception or prove criminal use beyond propriety concerns — thus leaning sharply toward higher levels [NORs]. As far as policy goes—with antiterrorism priorities looming—law enforcement agencies preserve negotiated accommodations toward preventing premature leakage before evidence becomes publicly assignable.

## The Segregation Quandary

Real debate begins when Section 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) calibrates whether *parts* can patch out while still maintaining privacy compliance—the crux at stake here is “reasonable segregation.” Historically courts defined "reasonable segregation" against two test conditions:

1️⃣ The implicated non-exempt data can physically/sequentially extracted without thawing fully submerged critical evidence.
2️⃣ Segregation must not intentionally waive key context embedded within exempt sections lest it becomes "interwoven."
