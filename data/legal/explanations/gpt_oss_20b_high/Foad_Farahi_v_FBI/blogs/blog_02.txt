# Sealed Petitions Hide Nothing — Understanding Evasive “Ex Parte” Usage  

## Why This Matters  

In September 2025 the D.C. Circuit upheld an FBI refusal to disclose most of an almost 11‑million‑page file requested by Foad Farahi under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). Farahi was an Iranian national who had lived in the United States since 1993 and who had been held up in removal proceedings that included allegations linking him to foreign terrorist organizations—an allegation he repeatedly denied in voluntary deportation petitions before immigration judges dismissed his claims in all rounds of proceedings (§ II). After years of painstaking discovery work by the FBI—bringing forward several thousand pages unqualified for public release—the agency’s own paperwork arguably blinded anyone seeking knowledge of its evidence against Farahi by placing it behind neatly worded “ex parte” affidavits and sealing them outside public view.*

This decision reminds plaintiffs every time they practice “FOI‑booth” litigation that a single executive declaration can become an ironclad shield against disclosure – even when governments appear years away from any criminal action against an individual whose name appears in public court filings much later than when his files were produced.*

The case sits squarely at the intersection between transparency laws and national‑security exemptions. It serves both as precedent affirming broad governmental discretion under § 552(b)(7)(A), now tracked across hundreds more FOIOtifications involving alleged terrorist contacts, and as cautionary evidence that hindsight no longer allows courts to compel agencies re‑examine stale claims about “ongoing investigations.”*

Ultimately Farahi has lost one fight over record access while winning a narrow victory over intolerable plenary secrecy afforded by ex parte declarations supplanting a required public transparency reason.*

## Setting the Record Straight  

At root lies *Freedom*’s own balancing doctrine, codified in § 552(b)(7)(A):  
“*Compiled”* means classification dedicated to *law‑enforcement purposes.*   
“*Interference*” requires showing that disclosure would *reasonably be expected* to hamper either *pending* or *reasonable anticipatory* enforcement proceedings.*  

In *Foad Farahi v Federal Bureau of Investigation,* Judge Katsas wrote:

> “The government bears the burden…of showing that downloadable materials were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes...and that their disclosure could reasonably be expected…to interfere…with enforcement proceedings that are pending or reasonably anticipated.” *(See Federal Bureau…J.A.*101.*

Because Washington’s primary law‑enforcement organ is federal police work – national security included – any assertion it makes carries clear deference ‑ a point reinforced over two decades young.\*

Earlier judgments delimited this deference explicitly:\*

- *CREW v Department of Justice,* 746 F.^3d 1082 (*“pending”* status constituting sufficient standard);  
- *Juarez v DOJ,* 518 F.^3d 54 (*“inextricably intertwined”/entrenched conspiracies warrant total withholding.*\)
