## Why Jiménez v.*Bondi* Matters  

The tenth circuit’s decision on October 7 2025 is less about international diplomacy than about how U.S. courts demarcate crime‑less “political hostility” from an asylum‑eligible reality. In *Jiménez*, Colombian political activists who allegedly received repeated threats were denied relief entirely because every element of the federal asylum framework fell short – physical injury did not exist, politic affiliation was only tangentially invoked as motivation, and there was no documented protection failure by Bogotá’s state apparatus. Ultimately, the appellate discussion confirms that weak “claims except catastrophically damaging perceptions” cannot survive under statutory scrutiny; it resurrects weight placed on allegations around tangible harm versus faceless intimidation across this circuit’s broader jurisprudence – an issue now more pressing than ever amid heightened cartel–political entanglements in Latin America and rising concerns over arbitrary detention inside U.S.–aligned corridors such as Mexico and Central America​(Singh v.*Bondi*, 130 F.4th at 857).

Several defenders of umbrella protection systems argue that focusing on “physical” indicators implicitly places too high a bar for people seeking refuge via credible yet non‑physical grievances frequently encountered abroad‑country security devotees face daily––the blurb-of‑the day cartel intimidation denouncements scholars have tentatively linked to grantable *political opinion*. By reinforcing thresholds already tightened by *Karki* v.*Holder*, *Dallakoti* v.*Holder*, and *Yuk* v.*Ashcroft*, *Jiménez* essentially walks into an uncomfortable space where historical ruling constellations still demand root causality scrutiny that many’ll see as an impediment rather than necessary gatekeeping​(Karki v.*Holder*, 715 F.3d at 805).
