Answer:
**Answer**

The Ninth‑Circuit opinion in *Jimenez v. Bondi* (Tenth Circuit case 24‑9573) treats **internal‑relocation**—not a “safe‑location” test—as the relevant standard for determining whether petitioners can avoid future persecution by moving somewhere else in Colombia.

---

### 1.  What is “internal‑relocation” under INA and § 1208?

Under 8 C.F.R. § 1208(b)(1)(i) (and its predecessor statutes), an asylum claim is presumptively valid unless an applicant can show that he/she can “adequately protect himself or herself from future persecution” by *relocating* elsewhere in his/her country of nationality:

> *“The government may rebut an applicant’s presumption of a well‑founded fear … if it shows … (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of his or her country.”*  
> — *Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d 641, 646 (10th Cir. 2012) (quoting § 1208(b)(1)(i))

This is called “internal‑relocation” because it does **not** involve leaving one’s country altogether; it simply asks whether moving within national borders would keep one outside harm.

---

### 2.  How does *Jimenez* apply this rule?

In § III.F., Judge Tymkovich writes:

> “Petitioners have **not demonstrated a fear of future persecution** … and even if they had ... the record supports the finding that they could **reasonably relocate**…inside Colombia.”  
> — *Jimenez*, § III.F.

Key facts used were:

| Evidence | Interpretation |
|----------|----------------|
| Petitioners stayed with Jimenez’s mother in Bogotá’s countryside for **three weeks** before deciding to leave permanently | Shows *private* evidence that a safer place exists inside Colombia |
| No reports were made beyond two local police officers who were unhelpful | Shows lack of governmental action – implying custody / protection would not necessitate leaving Colombian borders |
| Men who threatened them were tied to Don Rafa but did **not** threaten them with death abroad | Indicates risk remains only local/intracity |

The Court notes explicitly that “the men who threatened Petitioners told them **to move out of the neighborhood**; they did not threaten them **to move out of the country**.” Therefore, there is no basis for a *categorical* danger warranting escape beyond Colombia’s borders – only an internal move would suffice.

---

### 3. Why isn’t a “safe‑location” test used here?

“Safe location” refers instead to category‑based nonentity tests — e.g., when national legislation declares a particular region inherently unsafe for certain recipients (like communities targeted by organized crime). Those tests are found in § 1208(b)(1)(ii) and allow removal if no *safe* locale exists outside one’s country rather than merely anywhere inside it.

Since Jimenez’s threats were tied specifically to political activity (and later personal confrontation with an organized criminal), they do **not** fall into those categorical exclusions, whose grounds include e.g., abuse committed by an employer or organized crime groups acting directly against a protected class independent of any national policy choice. Therefore, only the *internal-relocation* standard applies here, and the Court finds it available.

---

### 4. Bottom line

In *Jimenez v. Bondi* judgment:

- **Internal-relocation** is invoked as the correct framework for assessing whether petitioners’ fear could be mitigated by moving inside Colombia.
- Because Petitioners repeatedly stayed elsewhere inside Bogotá without ill effect, and because all threats were localized and tied back to Don Rafa’s influence rather than direct state action, Congress’ presumption of returnability wasn't rebutted.
- A categorical “safe location” analysis was unnecessary—it simply never applied in this case's facts.

Hence, the Court appropriately applies **internal‑relocation**, not “safe location,” to evaluate petitions under asylum/withholding standards.``
