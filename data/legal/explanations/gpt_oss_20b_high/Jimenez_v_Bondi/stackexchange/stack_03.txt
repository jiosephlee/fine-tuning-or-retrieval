Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### "Central Reason" Test – How Do Courts Decide Whether Political Opinion Was Motive?
Question:
"Rivera‑Barrientos" explains that perceived politics must be “at least one central reason” in persecutors’ conduct, not merely incidental or subordinate. Jiménez argues Don Rafael attacked him because he worked against Petro while defenders say his attack stemmed mainly from robberies tied burglary at him rented property. How do judges weigh partial motives against overall intent?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
In asylum practice a politician’s *opinion* can support an award only if it is **at least one central reason** why the persecutors harmed him—or will harm him—after all other possible causes are considered. When several motives are present, judges look for evidence that shows which motive dominated the fight/attack and then determine whether that dominant motive is also a protected ground. If politics played only an *incidental* or *subordinate* role behind another motive (whether money, revenge over a property dispute or “being a snitch”), the “central‑reason” test rejects a claim based on politics alone. That’s exactly what we see in **Jiménez v. Bondi** – the court concluded that Don Rafael’s intimidation was driven mainly by Jiménez’s professional quarrel over the rental house rather than his Liberal‑Party campaign work, so Jiménez’s political opinion could not be treated as a central cause.



------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1.  Where does this requirement come from?

The Ninth–circulating standard comes directly from ***Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder***:

> *“To show persecution on account of a political opinion…the applicant’s political opinion must be ‘at least one central reason’ for past persecution.”*  
> (*citations omitted*).

Like every asylum standard it goes further:

> *“The protected ground cannot be merely incidental…or subordinate…’”*          (*cf.* ***Dallakoti v. Holder***).

And when more than one motive exists—political vs non‑political—a *mixed‑motive* analysis applies:

> *“Even so … when persecutors have multiple motives … they must be motivated by at least one protected ground **that is a central reason** for Indonesia…the inflicted harm.”*        (*○ O.C.V.* v Bondi)*



------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2.  How do judges compare competing motives?

| Step | What judges do | Why |
|------|----------------|-----|
| **Collect all relevant allegations** | Threats, statements made by perpetrators, witnesses’ testimony | Gives full picture of possible causes |
| **Find every possible protected `ground` cited by persecutor** | Police notes blaming Jiménez as “a snitch,” campaign mentions <br> Notable words like “Libertario” or references to Petro | Determines if any protected‐ground claim exists |
| **Identify which causes are most strongly supported** | Corroborated detail > unclear speculation | Helps rank cause strength |
| **See whether any protected ground dominates** | A persistent claim that Don Rf was ordered by Pablo <br> Confrontations happen only after the landlord dispute | Establish dominance |
| **Apply “substantial evidence” standard** | Do reasonable adjudicators find politician’s role as dominant? | Upholds decisions already made by lower bureaus |

Judges do **not** up‑weight one piece over another arbitrarily—they content themselves with “substantial evidence” supporting whichever cause appears most credible.



------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3.  What happened in ***Jiménez v.\ Bondi***?

In this decision the Board recorded two sets of hostile incidents:

1. **Early phone calls & random farm threats against Liberal Party activists**, including Jiménez himself.
2. **Later physical intimidation tied explicitly to allegations about Don Rafa’s rented Alpine house**, where Jiménez had walked into an altercation while proving ownership documents belonged to Jane cross‑handed past crimes—an investigation he did *because he was their lawyer*, not because he spoke out against Petro.



The Board and Immigration Judge focused on those later incidents (which were clearly tied back to Jiménez’s *property transaction*), noting several facts indicating crime‑related motivation:

* Men identified themselves as agents acting under instruction from Don Rafa and blamed Jiménez as “responsible for searches and seizures” at the suspect house.
* Periodic police talk News aides told him he should stay away off flight polishment & compared him w/ previous proprietor ∗—& Feigning greed infers criminal propensity independent of Party alignment.
* Recurring references throughout Pertinent texts aka unpredictable men called him “snitch.”

After weighing these facts versus earlier threat messages containing no extra-party identifiers linking them clearly back to politics alone—the Board concluded ‘the predominant motive behind these attacks was Jiménez’s confrontation with Don Rafa…not his Liberal affiliation.’



------------------------------------------------------------------

### 4.  What does this mean for asylum applicants who face multi‑motive tugs?

– If you’re claimed hit because you’re *politically aligned*, you must produce hard proof—e.g., explicit threat stating your opposition to Petro or identifying you as party volunteer—as part of every violent offense logged against you.



– If violence comes out of robbery or financial grievances—and politics only shows up briefly—then politics will fail the “central‐reason” hurdle.


The DOJ said here simply put: **“Political opinion played little more than an organium seed ("tangential") rather than \emph{stem}."** That means those who deny austerity may rely on separate reprecked categories instead.

2️⃣ Show unequivocal links between each hostility incident and policies/ideology;

3️⃣ Demonstrate your ideology drove an action / compelled state action rather than being an afterthought.

In ***Jiménez v.\ Bondi*** justice rested on distinguishing what actually moved exiled agitators—their reluctance came from confronting wealthy gang members dealing in narcotics—not merely from duelling Colombia’s opposing parties.



Remember – the chain ends where your policy idea transitions into tangible persecution; otherwise your claim stands empty amid other triggers.
