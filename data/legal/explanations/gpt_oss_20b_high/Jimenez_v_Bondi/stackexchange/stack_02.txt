Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### "Threats" Alone Are Insufficient – What Threshold Does Past Persecution Require?
Question:
"Kirks v Holder" says mere verbal intimidation isn’t enough for past persecution unless there has been inflicted harm or psychological trauma severe enough to qualify (“more than merely threatening”). In Jimenez v Bondi many phone calls threatened Jiménez so frequently that he feared harming himself or his family, yet those calls were ruled insufficientally violent. How does marshaling past violence versus ongoing terror shape admissibility standards?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The INA’s *persecution* prong is not satisfied by a series of verbal intimidations unless those insults – taken together – actually caused **tangible suffering** (physical injury, substantive psychological trauma reported by a qualified examiner) or led to an act that was *dangerous* enough by its very nature (e.g., an armed encounter). Both **Karki v. Holder** and **Jimenez v. Bondi** held that “mere threats” do not rise to this standard when there is no accompanying physical assault or loss, even when the victim feels great fear.”

Below is an outline of how “violence” versus “ongoing terror” shape the *admissibility* test adopted by modern asylum adjudicators.



--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 1.  The statutory framework

1. **Asylum requires a “refugee” showing** (at least on a pre‑ponderance basis under § 1158(b)(1)).
2. A refugee is proved through one of four ways (§ 1101(a)(42)(A)):
   * Persecution **in fact** – `present‑or‑past experience OR` future‑risk.
   * Well‑founded future fear only.
   * Protection under the Convention Against Torture.
3. **Past persecution** itself has three ear‑nearly elements (Niang v. Gonzales):

   | Element | Standard |
   |---------|----------|
   | 1 | “An incident or incidents” → must rise *to the level* of *persecution* |
   | 2 | On account of a protected ground |
   | 3 | By an agent the government cannot control |


---



### 2.  What “rise to the level” means

The test has been crystallized most clearly by these authorities cited in **Jimenez**:

| Authority | Core holding |
|-----------|--------------|
| **Wiransane v. Ashcroft** (10th Cir., 2004) | “A finding requires infliction of suffering…or harming...and must entail more than just restrictions or threats.” |
| **Karki v. Holder** (10th Cir., 2013) | “Threats alone do *not* constitute persecution.” |
| **Ritonga v. Holder** (10th Cir., 2011) | Cumulative effect analysis – multiple incidents may add up **only if each contributes some substantive harm** |
| **Chhetri v.\*\* Rosen** & **Carias‑Mejia v.\*\* Garland** (latest 2024/2025) | E.g., “four telephone threats” & “threats with machetes and being robbed” – again BL under torture because no physical injury nor documented serious psychological damage |

Thus the *“persecution”* proof hinges on tangible impact rather than fear per se.



--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 3.  How “violent incidents” satisfy this threshold

A violent act usually contains some element beyond mere words—for example:

| Incident type | Typical evidence satisfies (“offensive & more than mere restriction”) |
|---------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Physical assault / bodily injury | Medical records showing bruises/trauma |
| Use of weapons / firearms     | Witness testimony, police report showing standoff à la murder spree |
| Property destruction affecting livelihood | Receipt claims/ depreciated asset valuations |

In ruling cases these acts are treated \*\*establishedly\*\*. When an applicant presents any one act that includes these facts it normally satisfies § 1101(a)(42)(A)(α).

However \*\*violence is not automatically enough.\*\* For instance Chhetri held that even six mugging attempts fell short because there was no clear victim loss – illustrating that the material harm component must be substantial.



--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 4. ‑ How “ongoing terror / repetitive phone calls” fall short

The *continuous fears* held by victims do count only *if* they accumulate into something that levies tangible suffering:

* **“Injuries’ alleged absence.”**  
Jimenez conceded that he was never physically harmed (“not physically harmed”). The court therefore gave him no foothold under Wiransane’s proposition *“Suffering ... must entail more than…restraints.”*

> > “Although physical harm is not necessary …the suffered harm must be offensive …and more than mere restrictions.”  
> — _Jimenez_ §C‑I‑2

* **“Serious psychological trauma.”**  
Clients who report PTSD symptoms supported by clinicians have had their claims granted (*ee.* Ruppert ’19). Jimenez’s sister’s record only mentioned sleeplessness and panic attacks which were largely anecdotal, unaffiliated medical reports – **not meeting “offensive” pain** fact stated earlier.

> > “…we have rarely granted asylum based on threaten…emotional or psychological...“   
> — _Jimenez_ §C‑I‑3

Because the appeals court relied heavily on precedent where courts treated repeated intimidation as falling short unless paired with some other form of inflicted *danger,* it concluded these seven-monthly phone calls were inadequate.



--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 5. ‑ The distinction vis‑à‑vis admissibility

When an adjudicator wrestles with pleadings consisting principalement de:
1) Repeated aggravated phone calls + occasional mentionings ("kill…")  
2) No evidence of actual detention, injuries or medical records  

the decision falls reviewing how they weigh speculation against proven facts:

*   “A single threat … does not meet our ‘persecution’ test.” — _Karki_ p.A‑4  
    → **Hence** each instance is excluded from admissibility *unless part of a pattern leading proportional damage.*

*   Recall Ritonga’s idea—cumulative effects \(in aggregate\) - only count if each incident demonstrates at least some degree beyond minimal intimidation ("a continuation" therefore can produce adequate levels).  

In _Jimenez_, after compiling all dossiers together, the board decided all incidents were in factologically deficient—the cumulative shortfalls were too small for consideration as permissible legal proof.


--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 6. ‑ Practical takeaway for applicants

> • If you survived an actual shoot‑up at your door even once → acceptably proves destruction / other ``physical'' elements beneficial for asylum defense.
> • If you received telephone codes telling you ‘you’ll die tomorrow’ with no recorded police line response → usually considered insufficient under current jurisprudence.
> • To raise tollers further - commit yourself early to obtain professional documentation:
>    - Police reports
>    - Medical records confirming depression/ PTSD‐diagnoses
>    - Court filings showing official attempts at intervention  

These forthwith bring admissible material facts asked for by courts.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Bottom line

Both cases illustrate what circumstances each court will find present AMA<|reserved_200793|>ation-worthy proof (“admissible”: i.e., persuade ISTER valid remedial proofs).  

Winning occurs when either:
1️⃣ You provide a single demonstrable violent incident — evidenced physically / medically O*, OR  
2️⃣ A series whereby each adds significant demonstrable suffering ([Ritonga].   

Recurrent verbal intimidation—no injuries—does not satisfy current prejudice thresholds set out after *Karki,* nor does it automatically equate with ongoing terror strong enough to waive intolerable fear plus prove prior hardship required for US immigration relief.
