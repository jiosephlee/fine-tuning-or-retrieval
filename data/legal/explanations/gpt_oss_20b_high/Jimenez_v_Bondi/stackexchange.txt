Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### How does appellate court apply a ‘substantial‑evidence’ standard when reviewing a Board’s denial under § 1252(b)(4)(A)?
Question:
"In Jimenez v. Bondi" the court said it reviews BIA findings under a substantial‑evidence standard **only** insofar as the record supports those findings – it didn’t conduct a de novo rebalance.
I’m struggling with what counts as substantial evidence in an immigration‑doctrine setting versus simple administrative fact finding.
When looking at the IJ’s record versus what happened on appeal, how should I decide whether testimony supports “reasonable adjudicators” versus suppression based on judicial discretion?
Answer:
**Short Answer**

Under § 1252(b)(4)(A) an appellate court reviews a Board’s denial underlying its findings **only as far as the administrative record supports those findings**.  
The appellate court applies a *substantial‑evidence* test—not a de‑novo re‑balance—and treats all records that could lead a **reasonable adjudicator** to twist toward one side or another equivalently.  
In Jimenez v. Bondi the court found that none of the parties’ testimony gave rise to “reasonable” conclusions concerning past persecution, nexus, or lack‑of‑government protection and therefore held that it was unsupported (“substantial”) enough for BIA/ IJ findings.


--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1. How “Substantial Evidence’’ Is Defined For This Review  

> “Under this standard, … **the BIA’s findings of fact are conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude otherwise**.”  
> *(Jimenez v. Bondi*, ¶ C)

The court explicitly notes it **does not conduct a de‑novo reassessment** – it merely checks whether any *reasonable* adjudicator could see through the existing record omitting new balancing arguments ("journalistic crusades")^[1].

### 2. What Must Be Present in Immigration‑Doctrine Settings  

In asylum contexts each element has its own evidentiary threshold—

| Element | What constitutes *substantial* support |
|---------|----------------------------------------|
| **Past persecution** | An incident or incidents together showing actual harm *beyond mere threats*: physical injury, psychological distress directly tied to a protected ground,^2 or any attack carried out by public officials/ forces allied with them.^3 |
| **Nexus / protected ground** | Evidence that at least one protected ground was **a central reason** for treatment—no marginality is allowed.^4 |
| **Government protection / safe relocation** | Concrete proof that government agencies took *action* against persecutors; alternatively that such action would be futile—or showing multiple credible reports indicating systemic failure.^5 |

The court in Jimenez applied these expectations directly:

* **Past Persecution:** The petitioners only cited phone calls and menal threats—“threats alone are insufficient … more than mere restrictions” —which “did **not rise … past persecution.”^6  
* **Nexus:** The Board concluded *political affiliation* was merely tangential because Jan observes *Victim’s motives likely arose from their conflict over Don Rafa’s property*, famously tagged “competing motive,” thus failing \[*central‑reason* criteria\]^7  
* **Government Protection:** Petitioners’ minimal outreach – brief conversations with local police – failed to satisfy required proof that Colombia would have taken effectual action—or lacked information on broader systemic failures—they indeed did not show police attempts had stopped injustice.^8  

No new factual weight was introduced on appeal ever again; hence all *attorney‑adjudicated facts* rested solely upon what was already recorded.
