Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### How far can an appellate court review a Board’s decision over an Immigration Judge’s denial under §1252(b)(4)(A)?
Question:
I’m trying to understand why Jimenez v Bondi says appellate courts reviewing a Board’s order apply a *substantial‑evidence* standard, yet also state they look only at *the administrative record upon which the order of removal is based*. Does this mean courts cannot reconsider any factual determinations actually made by the Board, even if those facts were contested during lower‑court proceedings?
Answer:
**Answer**

Jimenez v Bondi shows how an immigration‑law appeal works when § 1252(b)(4)(A) is invoked. The two elements are

| Element | What it means | Where it appears in Jimenez |
|---------|---------------|----------------------------|
| **Administrative‑record limitation** | An appellate court may “look only at *the administrative record on which the order of removal is based*.” | *Jimenez*, 130 F.4th at 859 (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). |
| **Substantial‑evidence standard** | The Board’s factual findings become “conclusive … unless *the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled* to conclude otherwise.” | *Jimenez*, 130 F.4th at 859 (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A))—repeatedences throughout §§ B‑C discuss “substantial‑evidence” reviews specifically for BIA findings.|

### How those doctrines interact

1. **What can be examined**  
   - The court may consider *any* document from that limited scope: affidavits, transcripts, receipts, CI‑files … as long as it belongs to that *administrative* file (the removal adjudication file).  
   - It ignores everything outside – e.g., separate civil lawsuits, police reports that never entered that docket, or oral statements made outside immigration venues.
   - That limitation does **not** preclude examining every piece found therein; it simply says “don’t look beyond what sits on this pile.”

2. **What can be overruled**  
   - Faced with a particular finding made by the Board—“no past persecution,” “no nexus,” “government willing” – an appellate judge may say “no” only if some document already in that pile shows *another reasonable adjudicator* would have gone otherwise.
   - If no such document exists (i.e., nobody ever put the contrary evidence into the file), then there is nothing for an appellate judge to flip.

3. **What happens when lower‑court filings contest a fact?**  
   - If those disputes appear on the administrative docket—say the petitioner arranged for an expert witness who next appeared before an admin court and got their testimony recorded—then those arguments are part of the administrative record and subject to *substantial‑evidence* scrutiny.
   - Conversely, if a dispute was aired *only* during a civil proceeding or never formally reflected on any immigration‑law document (even though it was discussed with an immigration judge), it falls outside the scope and cannot be revisited later by an appellate court under § 1252(k).

### Bottom line for law students

- **Yes**, the appellate court cannot re‑examine factual determinations made by a Board unless those determinations—or at least all competing arguments—are already contained in the *administrative* file reviewed under § 1252(b)(4)(A).  
- If you think your client had a data point that was never filed into immigration law documents but you believe it should change your case’s outcome, you will **not** get it considered unless you introduce that very data into *that specific* file now—after proper procedural filings are completed.
- The *substantial‑evidence* label simply tells you your opponent’s finding will stand unless your side can pull up enough documentary support showing another reasonable adjudicator would have decided otherwise **using exactly what sits on that pile**.

Hence Jimenez clarifies that while appellate courts have room to weigh added context regarding past persecution or government willingness indicated by previous JUDGE‐or‐BLAH portals, their hands stay tied once those facts no longer appear inside that particular administrative archive.*

### "Threats" Alone Are Insufficient – What Threshold Does Past Persecution Require?
Question:
"Kirks v Holder" says mere verbal intimidation isn’t enough for past persecution unless there has been inflicted harm or psychological trauma severe enough to qualify (“more than merely threatening”). In Jimenez v Bondi many phone calls threatened Jiménez so frequently that he feared harming himself or his family, yet those calls were ruled insufficientally violent. How does marshaling past violence versus ongoing terror shape admissibility standards?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The INA’s *persecution* prong is not satisfied by a series of verbal intimidations unless those insults – taken together – actually caused **tangible suffering** (physical injury, substantive psychological trauma reported by a qualified examiner) or led to an act that was *dangerous* enough by its very nature (e.g., an armed encounter). Both **Karki v. Holder** and **Jimenez v. Bondi** held that “mere threats” do not rise to this standard when there is no accompanying physical assault or loss, even when the victim feels great fear.”

Below is an outline of how “violence” versus “ongoing terror” shape the *admissibility* test adopted by modern asylum adjudicators.



--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 1.  The statutory framework

1. **Asylum requires a “refugee” showing** (at least on a pre‑ponderance basis under § 1158(b)(1)).
2. A refugee is proved through one of four ways (§ 1101(a)(42)(A)):
   * Persecution **in fact** – `present‑or‑past experience OR` future‑risk.
   * Well‑founded future fear only.
   * Protection under the Convention Against Torture.
3. **Past persecution** itself has three ear‑nearly elements (Niang v. Gonzales):

   | Element | Standard |
   |---------|----------|
   | 1 | “An incident or incidents” → must rise *to the level* of *persecution* |
   | 2 | On account of a protected ground |
   | 3 | By an agent the government cannot control |


---



### 2.  What “rise to the level” means

The test has been crystallized most clearly by these authorities cited in **Jimenez**:

| Authority | Core holding |
|-----------|--------------|
| **Wiransane v. Ashcroft** (10th Cir., 2004) | “A finding requires infliction of suffering…or harming...and must entail more than just restrictions or threats.” |
| **Karki v. Holder** (10th Cir., 2013) | “Threats alone do *not* constitute persecution.” |
| **Ritonga v. Holder** (10th Cir., 2011) | Cumulative effect analysis – multiple incidents may add up **only if each contributes some substantive harm** |
| **Chhetri v.\*\* Rosen** & **Carias‑Mejia v.\*\* Garland** (latest 2024/2025) | E.g., “four telephone threats” & “threats with machetes and being robbed” – again BL under torture because no physical injury nor documented serious psychological damage |

Thus the *“persecution”* proof hinges on tangible impact rather than fear per se.



--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 3.  How “violent incidents” satisfy this threshold

A violent act usually contains some element beyond mere words—for example:

| Incident type | Typical evidence satisfies (“offensive & more than mere restriction”) |
|---------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Physical assault / bodily injury | Medical records showing bruises/trauma |
| Use of weapons / firearms     | Witness testimony, police report showing standoff à la murder spree |
| Property destruction affecting livelihood | Receipt claims/ depreciated asset valuations |

In ruling cases these acts are treated \*\*establishedly\*\*. When an applicant presents any one act that includes these facts it normally satisfies § 1101(a)(42)(A)(α).

However \*\*violence is not automatically enough.\*\* For instance Chhetri held that even six mugging attempts fell short because there was no clear victim loss – illustrating that the material harm component must be substantial.



--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 4. ‑ How “ongoing terror / repetitive phone calls” fall short

The *continuous fears* held by victims do count only *if* they accumulate into something that levies tangible suffering:

* **“Injuries’ alleged absence.”**  
Jimenez conceded that he was never physically harmed (“not physically harmed”). The court therefore gave him no foothold under Wiransane’s proposition *“Suffering ... must entail more than…restraints.”*

> > “Although physical harm is not necessary …the suffered harm must be offensive …and more than mere restrictions.”  
> — _Jimenez_ §C‑I‑2

* **“Serious psychological trauma.”**  
Clients who report PTSD symptoms supported by clinicians have had their claims granted (*ee.* Ruppert ’19). Jimenez’s sister’s record only mentioned sleeplessness and panic attacks which were largely anecdotal, unaffiliated medical reports – **not meeting “offensive” pain** fact stated earlier.

> > “…we have rarely granted asylum based on threaten…emotional or psychological...“   
> — _Jimenez_ §C‑I‑3

Because the appeals court relied heavily on precedent where courts treated repeated intimidation as falling short unless paired with some other form of inflicted *danger,* it concluded these seven-monthly phone calls were inadequate.



--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 5. ‑ The distinction vis‑à‑vis admissibility

When an adjudicator wrestles with pleadings consisting principalement de:
1) Repeated aggravated phone calls + occasional mentionings ("kill…")  
2) No evidence of actual detention, injuries or medical records  

the decision falls reviewing how they weigh speculation against proven facts:

*   “A single threat … does not meet our ‘persecution’ test.” — _Karki_ p.A‑4  
    → **Hence** each instance is excluded from admissibility *unless part of a pattern leading proportional damage.*

*   Recall Ritonga’s idea—cumulative effects \(in aggregate\) - only count if each incident demonstrates at least some degree beyond minimal intimidation ("a continuation" therefore can produce adequate levels).  

In _Jimenez_, after compiling all dossiers together, the board decided all incidents were in factologically deficient—the cumulative shortfalls were too small for consideration as permissible legal proof.


--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 6. ‑ Practical takeaway for applicants

> • If you survived an actual shoot‑up at your door even once → acceptably proves destruction / other ``physical'' elements beneficial for asylum defense.
> • If you received telephone codes telling you ‘you’ll die tomorrow’ with no recorded police line response → usually considered insufficient under current jurisprudence.
> • To raise tollers further - commit yourself early to obtain professional documentation:
>    - Police reports
>    - Medical records confirming depression/ PTSD‐diagnoses
>    - Court filings showing official attempts at intervention  

These forthwith bring admissible material facts asked for by courts.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Bottom line

Both cases illustrate what circumstances each court will find present AMA<|reserved_200793|>ation-worthy proof (“admissible”: i.e., persuade ISTER valid remedial proofs).  

Winning occurs when either:
1️⃣ You provide a single demonstrable violent incident — evidenced physically / medically O*, OR  
2️⃣ A series whereby each adds significant demonstrable suffering ([Ritonga].   

Recurrent verbal intimidation—no injuries—does not satisfy current prejudice thresholds set out after *Karki,* nor does it automatically equate with ongoing terror strong enough to waive intolerable fear plus prove prior hardship required for US immigration relief.

### "Central Reason" Test – How Do Courts Decide Whether Political Opinion Was Motive?
Question:
"Rivera‑Barrientos" explains that perceived politics must be “at least one central reason” in persecutors’ conduct, not merely incidental or subordinate. Jiménez argues Don Rafael attacked him because he worked against Petro while defenders say his attack stemmed mainly from robberies tied burglary at him rented property. How do judges weigh partial motives against overall intent?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
In asylum practice a politician’s *opinion* can support an award only if it is **at least one central reason** why the persecutors harmed him—or will harm him—after all other possible causes are considered. When several motives are present, judges look for evidence that shows which motive dominated the fight/attack and then determine whether that dominant motive is also a protected ground. If politics played only an *incidental* or *subordinate* role behind another motive (whether money, revenge over a property dispute or “being a snitch”), the “central‑reason” test rejects a claim based on politics alone. That’s exactly what we see in **Jiménez v. Bondi** – the court concluded that Don Rafael’s intimidation was driven mainly by Jiménez’s professional quarrel over the rental house rather than his Liberal‑Party campaign work, so Jiménez’s political opinion could not be treated as a central cause.



------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1.  Where does this requirement come from?

The Ninth–circulating standard comes directly from ***Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder***:

> *“To show persecution on account of a political opinion…the applicant’s political opinion must be ‘at least one central reason’ for past persecution.”*  
> (*citations omitted*).

Like every asylum standard it goes further:

> *“The protected ground cannot be merely incidental…or subordinate…’”*          (*cf.* ***Dallakoti v. Holder***).

And when more than one motive exists—political vs non‑political—a *mixed‑motive* analysis applies:

> *“Even so … when persecutors have multiple motives … they must be motivated by at least one protected ground **that is a central reason** for Indonesia…the inflicted harm.”*        (*○ O.C.V.* v Bondi)*



------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2.  How do judges compare competing motives?

| Step | What judges do | Why |
|------|----------------|-----|
| **Collect all relevant allegations** | Threats, statements made by perpetrators, witnesses’ testimony | Gives full picture of possible causes |
| **Find every possible protected `ground` cited by persecutor** | Police notes blaming Jiménez as “a snitch,” campaign mentions <br> Notable words like “Libertario” or references to Petro | Determines if any protected‐ground claim exists |
| **Identify which causes are most strongly supported** | Corroborated detail > unclear speculation | Helps rank cause strength |
| **See whether any protected ground dominates** | A persistent claim that Don Rf was ordered by Pablo <br> Confrontations happen only after the landlord dispute | Establish dominance |
| **Apply “substantial evidence” standard** | Do reasonable adjudicators find politician’s role as dominant? | Upholds decisions already made by lower bureaus |

Judges do **not** up‑weight one piece over another arbitrarily—they content themselves with “substantial evidence” supporting whichever cause appears most credible.



------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3.  What happened in ***Jiménez v.\ Bondi***?

In this decision the Board recorded two sets of hostile incidents:

1. **Early phone calls & random farm threats against Liberal Party activists**, including Jiménez himself.
2. **Later physical intimidation tied explicitly to allegations about Don Rafa’s rented Alpine house**, where Jiménez had walked into an altercation while proving ownership documents belonged to Jane cross‑handed past crimes—an investigation he did *because he was their lawyer*, not because he spoke out against Petro.



The Board and Immigration Judge focused on those later incidents (which were clearly tied back to Jiménez’s *property transaction*), noting several facts indicating crime‑related motivation:

* Men identified themselves as agents acting under instruction from Don Rafa and blamed Jiménez as “responsible for searches and seizures” at the suspect house.
* Periodic police talk News aides told him he should stay away off flight polishment & compared him w/ previous proprietor ∗—& Feigning greed infers criminal propensity independent of Party alignment.
* Recurring references throughout Pertinent texts aka unpredictable men called him “snitch.”

After weighing these facts versus earlier threat messages containing no extra-party identifiers linking them clearly back to politics alone—the Board concluded ‘the predominant motive behind these attacks was Jiménez’s confrontation with Don Rafa…not his Liberal affiliation.’



------------------------------------------------------------------

### 4.  What does this mean for asylum applicants who face multi‑motive tugs?

– If you’re claimed hit because you’re *politically aligned*, you must produce hard proof—e.g., explicit threat stating your opposition to Petro or identifying you as party volunteer—as part of every violent offense logged against you.



– If violence comes out of robbery or financial grievances—and politics only shows up briefly—then politics will fail the “central‐reason” hurdle.


The DOJ said here simply put: **“Political opinion played little more than an organium seed ("tangential") rather than \emph{stem}."** That means those who deny austerity may rely on separate reprecked categories instead.

2️⃣ Show unequivocal links between each hostility incident and policies/ideology;

### 'Safe Internal Relocation' Vs.'Safe Location' – Which Criterion Applies To Here?
Question:
"Rivera‑Barrientos" distinguishes useful ‘safe location” determination against federal categorical‐list safety tests commonly applied under withholding statutes versus ‘internal relocation.’ The Court found petitioners could move peacefully elsewhere in Bogotá countryside once threatened farther outward movement left subvert whom argued harmful official links nationwide were insufficienty pervasive habitat source confidence traffic loophole verification storytelling lagging genocidi characteristic parishical.
Answer:
**Answer**

The Ninth‑Circuit opinion in *Jimenez v. Bondi* (Tenth Circuit case 24‑9573) treats **internal‑relocation**—not a “safe‑location” test—as the relevant standard for determining whether petitioners can avoid future persecution by moving somewhere else in Colombia.

---

### 1.  What is “internal‑relocation” under INA and § 1208?

Under 8 C.F.R. § 1208(b)(1)(i) (and its predecessor statutes), an asylum claim is presumptively valid unless an applicant can show that he/she can “adequately protect himself or herself from future persecution” by *relocating* elsewhere in his/her country of nationality:

> *“The government may rebut an applicant’s presumption of a well‑founded fear … if it shows … (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of his or her country.”*  
> — *Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d 641, 646 (10th Cir. 2012) (quoting § 1208(b)(1)(i))

This is called “internal‑relocation” because it does **not** involve leaving one’s country altogether; it simply asks whether moving within national borders would keep one outside harm.

---

### 2.  How does *Jimenez* apply this rule?

In § III.F., Judge Tymkovich writes:

> “Petitioners have **not demonstrated a fear of future persecution** … and even if they had ... the record supports the finding that they could **reasonably relocate**…inside Colombia.”  
> — *Jimenez*, § III.F.

Key facts used were:

| Evidence | Interpretation |
|----------|----------------|
| Petitioners stayed with Jimenez’s mother in Bogotá’s countryside for **three weeks** before deciding to leave permanently | Shows *private* evidence that a safer place exists inside Colombia |
| No reports were made beyond two local police officers who were unhelpful | Shows lack of governmental action – implying custody / protection would not necessitate leaving Colombian borders |
| Men who threatened them were tied to Don Rafa but did **not** threaten them with death abroad | Indicates risk remains only local/intracity |

The Court notes explicitly that “the men who threatened Petitioners told them **to move out of the neighborhood**; they did not threaten them **to move out of the country**.” Therefore, there is no basis for a *categorical* danger warranting escape beyond Colombia’s borders – only an internal move would suffice.

---

### 3. Why isn’t a “safe‑location” test used here?

“Safe location” refers instead to category‑based nonentity tests — e.g., when national legislation declares a particular region inherently unsafe for certain recipients (like communities targeted by organized crime). Those tests are found in § 1208(b)(1)(ii) and allow removal if no *safe* locale exists outside one’s country rather than merely anywhere inside it.

Since Jimenez’s threats were tied specifically to political activity (and later personal confrontation with an organized criminal), they do **not** fall into those categorical exclusions, whose grounds include e.g., abuse committed by an employer or organized crime groups acting directly against a protected class independent of any national policy choice. Therefore, only the *internal-relocation* standard applies here, and the Court finds it available.

---

### 4. Bottom line

In *Jimenez v. Bondi* judgment:

- **Internal-relocation** is invoked as the correct framework for assessing whether petitioners’ fear could be mitigated by moving inside Colombia.
- Because Petitioners repeatedly stayed elsewhere inside Bogotá without ill effect, and because all threats were localized and tied back to Don Rafa’s influence rather than direct state action, Congress’ presumption of returnability wasn't rebutted.
- A categorical “safe location” analysis was unnecessary—it simply never applied in this case's facts.

Hence, the Court appropriately applies **internal‑relocation**, not “safe location,” to evaluate petitions under asylum/withholding standards.``
