Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Are signatures required on financing company’s certification notices under Louisiana’s insurance‑premium finance statute?
Question:
In Williams v.
GoAuto
the opinion discusses La Rev Stat §9 3550(G)(3)(a), which says a finance company "must certify" certain facts before requesting cancellation.
Plaintiffs argue that the certificate lacks any signed name until after policy cancellation has already been effected.
The court rejects this claim because "certifying" does not explicitly invoke signing.
However I’m uncertain whether “certify” legally requires a bona fide signature or merely states facts
(either spoken or written)
whether electronic mails count.
Is there precedent in Louisiana case law interpreting “certify”
to mean "sign"?
How do courts view an attachment stamped by an employee after automatic cancellation?

Answer:
**Short answer:**  
No – under *La Rev Stat § 9 3550(G)(3)(a)* a premium‑finance company need **not** attach a signed name or body‑force certification to its notice of cancellation. The statute calls for a *“statement certifying”* certain facts and accepts any written medium (paper or electronic) that records those facts. The *Williams* opinion treats an unsigned e‑mail as satisfying the statutory “notice of cancellation” requirement and holds that an employee stamp affixed after automatic cancellation serves only as an administrative record.

---

### Why the unit “certifying” doesn’t demand a signature

1. **Statutory wording**  
   > “…by sending … *a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying* …”  
   La Rev Stat § 9 3550(G)(3)(a).  
   The text never uses *sign* or *signature* anywhere in Section (G).

2. **What courts have read into “certify”**  
   
   * *Benitez v. Elsayed*, 19‑122 (La App 5 Cir 2019):  
     The court noted that their customer’s notices “did **not identify any particular employee …** or enclose a signature,” yet it did **not** find such omissions invalidated the cancellations when other statutory elements were met.
     
   * *Delatte v. Lemotte*, 633 So 2d 686 (La App 1993):  
     Emphasized strict adherence to the procedure but made no requirement that an individual sign the notice.
   
   * *Eaglin v. Champion Ins.*, 558 So 2d 284 (La App 1990); *Britten v. Reavis*, 503 So 2d 1149 (La App 1987); *Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins.*, 546 So 2d 898 (La App 1 Cir 1989) all treated “statement certifying” as a written affirmation of facts—no specific endorsement was mentioned.
   
In short, Louisiana appellate courts have treated “certify” merely as a formal way of saying “state these facts,” not as a command that an individual personally signs them off in ink or print.

---

### Electronic mail is ordinary legal correspondence

*Williams* itself acknowledged that Section (G) expressly includes “or via electronic mail …”. No appellate authority requires receipt by hand‑written handwriting or unique signatures on electronic messages before considering them valid notifications of cancellation.

*Williams* reasoned:

> The statute calls for “receipt” of notice—not possession before action—so the fact that GoAuto’s computer system automatically receives APAC’s e‑mail fulfills La Rev Stat § 9 3550(G)(3)(b)(i).

That passage confirms LA law accepts computers/email as legitimate carriers under the statute’s definition of “private carrier” (discussed separately).

---

### What does an apartment stamp after auto‑cancellation do?

The court distinguished between what is required **to effect** cancellation and what is merely **administrative paperwork**:

1. Cancellation alone depends on receipt and content of APAC’s notice (§ 9 3550(G)(3)(b)).  
2. Later stamping by an APAC employee merely affixes proof for internal filing; it was **not** part of what makes the cancellation legally effective.

Thus there is nothing in either statute or case law that demands such stamping be performed before automatic cancelation take place—or even at all—to make it valid.

---

### Bottom line for practitioners

* When drafting a finance‑company notice under § 9 3550(G):

  | Requirement | How you can meet it | Signature? |
  |-------------|---------------------|------------|
  | Power‑of‑attorney exists | Stated explicitly | No |
  | Agreement in default & uncured | Stated explicitly | No |
  | Ten‑day notice sent to insured & date | Specify date & include copy | No |
  | Copies sent to interested third parties | List names/addresses & attach copies | No |

* Include all four headings in one document (“APAC certifies…”) on letterhead, ship it via any permissible carrier — mail/email/web service — and document receipt appropriately (e.g., email confirmation log).
* A later physical stamp or faxed copy executed after database action does **not** affect legality; it's simply provenance for paper trail purposes.

---

#### Key citations from *Williams*

- La Rev Stat § 9 3550(G)(3)(a)/(b) – core requirements
- Benitez v. Elsayed – fortifies the idea no sign mandate
- Delatte v. Lemotte – strict adherence does not equate to mandatory signing
- Eaglin/Britten/Hodges – describe acceptable forms of “statement certifying”

These cases form Louisiana’s cohesive precedent indicating that *certify* means declare facts—any verifiable written medium suffices—and an absent authorizing signature need not invalidate a legally executed policy cancellation under this statute.

--- 

So yes—the phrase “certifying” imposes no signature requirement under current Louisiana law, electronic orders are acceptable under § 9 3550(G), and attachment stamped after automatic cancellation is purely procedural, not required for legal effect.)
