Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### "Receipt" versus "delivery":
Does an e‑mail satisfy Louisiana’s statutory receiving requirement?
Question:
"Receipt" is defined broadly in Black’s Law Dictionary,
and La Rev Stat §9 3550(G)(3)(b)(i) demands insurers consider cancellation requested once “receipt” of notice and statement.
In Williams v.
GoAuto
APAC emails GoAuto’s computer service at midnight, which then cancels enforcement.
Only afterward does an APAC employee stamp a hard copy.
Plaintiffs claim this means GoAuto never "received" notice so it could not cancel.
Does electronic mail qualify as providing "receipt," especially when handled solely within corporate systems?
How do courts distinguish physical possession from electronic reception?

Answer:
**Answer**

In *Williams v. GoAuto*, the Fifth Circuit adopted a straightforward reading of Louisiana’s cancellation statute and concluded that an email supplied by a finance company *does* satisfy the statute’s “receipt” requirement.

| Issue | Statutory language | Court’s reasoning | Practical effect |
|-------|-------------------|-------------------|------------------|
| **What constitutes “receipt”** | “Upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation has been requested …” (La Rev Stat § 9 3550(G)(3)(b)(i)). | The court rejected any special construction for “receipt.” It applied ordinary dictionary definitions – *receipt* means “take ... into possession or custody.”  It explained: <br> > “GoAuto may cancel a policy once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation.” <br>Because the statute itself lists “by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, **or via electronic mail** …” as permissible delivery modes, no extra step is needed to convert an electronic transmission into legal receipt. | An insurer may treat internal computer receipt of an email as its “possession” and thus may cancel immediately after processing it. |
| **Need for a signature** | Statute says APAC must certify certain facts “in a copy of the notice.” No mention of signing personally named employee. | Past appellate decisions (Delatte v. Lemotte; Eaglin v. Champion) held that strict adherence requires only that APAC certify; they never required a signature attached to each notice.  The Fifth Circuit reiterated this principle and found no authority requiring an individual sign-off on every communication sent to GoAuto.<br> > “There is no clear Louisiana authority requiring one.” | APAC can certify with its letterhead and posted statement; individual signatures are unnecessary so long as APAC’s certification is present when GoAuto receives it. |
| **Delivery versus physical possession** | The statutory process provides *three* methods—depositing by mail or private carrier or via electronic mail—that all result in “receipt.” | The court emphasized that modern statutes are meant for current technology: <br> > “This is a modern statute set in current times [...] company computers do what individuals once did.”<br> Hence an email, once received by GoAuto’s computer system (which is shown in logs and web‑service calls), is treated equivalently to paper arriving at an office or being deposited by courier—both represent taking possession.<p>“No person is involved until later…once the e‑mailed notice is received in early morning hours by GoAuto’s computer system…that computer system cancels the policy.”</p> | A lack of hand‑off or printed copy at this point does **not** defeat statutory compliance; the email alone counts as receipt for purposes of cancelling policy 12 hours after default ended (midnight on day 10). |

### Bottom line

Yes—an email addressed to GoAuto fulfills Louisiana's statutory requirement of receiving notice under § 9 3550(G)(3).  The doctrine used here springs from two facts:

1. **“Receipt” means possession**, simply defined by common language—no special legal nuance imposed.
2. **The statute expressly lists electronic mail** among permissible delivery mechanisms, so any email that successfully reaches a provider’s computer system provides proof of possession needed for cancellation.

Therefore plaintiffs’ contention that GoAuto didn’t receive notification merely because they had not yet affixed a stamp on hard copies is misplaced; under Louisiana law those stamps are irrelevant after interruption service has already acted upon receipt via computer system.

---

#### Key excerpts from *Williams* (former portions):

*“The statute makes no explicit reference to a signature… we see no reason that a company cannot certify information without a named employee being identified.”*
*“GoAuto may cancel a policy once it takes possession… once [the] e‑mailed notice … was received in early morning hours by GoAuto’s computer system.”*
*“…the e‑mail satisfies the statute, we will not consider ...”*

These passages summarize how the court interpreted “receipt.” This interpretation encompasses both modern email delivery and traditional modes, confirming that electronic notice alone suffices under Louisiana liability pages for financed insurance policies.<sup>1</sup>
