# Do You Need a Signature?  
Separating Certifying Language from Formal Execution  

## Why This Matters  

The Fifth Circuit’s affirmation in **Williams v. GoAuto Insurance** clarifies how Louisiana’s premium‑finance statute is interpreted when modern technology steps into the picture. By holding “certifying” does not require a signer’s hand—one element policed most skeptics—the court reaffirmed Louisiana’s historical preference for strict procedural adherence over procedural formality. For insurers who had already been wrestling between old‑school sheet‑fed cancellations and automated workflows, this decision gives legal certainty about what level of documentation suffices under La.’s Code § 9 § 3550(G)(3). Above all, it demonstrates how lawmakers’ plain‑language rules about *when* creditors can cancel can coexist with relatively informal contemporary transaction devices such as emails and web services without alienating the statute’s intent─to fix timing and cure windows,* rather than mandating printed signatures at every step.* 

A misinterpretation here could have rippled badly through Louisiana’s beloved *freedman* dominated insurance market where financing arrangements crop up almost daily. A broad legal requirement for signatures would have imposed onerous administrative costs without commensurate benefits—the policyholders would still owe premiums regardless whether they received stack-print notices or encouraged infinite dominance infiltration—but early ambiguity left many insurers uncertain whether their purely electronic cancellation journeys were lawful.



## The Statutory Context  

Louisiana’s finance statutes are clear-eyed: **§ 9 § 3550(G)(3)(a)** obliges an insurer’s premium‑finance partner to “send … a copy … together with a statement certifying” four facts — i.e., existence of an effective power of attorney authorized waiver segment (“^1”) , presence of default uncorrected (“^2”), dispatching the “ten‑day notice” exactly pursuant to section (**) § **(3)(b) , and notification sent via private carriers or electronically (**(iii**). The language stops short at “certifying”; it does nothing condescendently about autographs or manually stamped seals.*

The courts surrounding Louisiana have repeatedly spoken up about *strict adherence,* especially in **Delatte v.~Lemotte**, **Eaglin v.~Champion Ins.\ Co**, and **Britten v.~Reavis** which laid down two purposes behind that insistence: identifying precisely when policies terminate and affording defectors an opportunity to cure before failing irrevocably becomes essential infrastructure around settlement disputes wherein *cancellation* functions analogously to contract termination*.*

## Receipt Revisited: What It Means Under Louisiana Cancellation Law

The Fifth Circuit’s recent affirmation—*Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*—may at first blush appear routine: a fee‑based insurance agent simply obeys a fine‑printed statute. But for Louisiana’s sophisticated auctioneer‑style insurance market, where premiums are typically financed through a chain of intermediaries known as premium‑finance companies, the question “What does ‘receipt’ actually buy an insurer?” is far from trivial. In *Williams*, the court turned to plain‑English dictionary meanings and drawn analogies from *Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience* to convince itself that a computerized acknowledgment suffices under § 9 3550(G)(3)(b)(i). The decision walks a narrow course between stare decisis and modern technology—and its effects reverberate across any statute that merely demands “receipt” or “possession.”

### Why This Matter at All

Louisiana’s finance‑policy regime spells out precisely which parties may cancel a covered policy and under what conditions that cancellation is valid. A key hurdle is statutory compliance with § 9 3550(G)(3)(b)(i): *upon receipt of such notice of cancellation…the insurer shall consider that cancellation has been requested*. When an insured fails to pay its installment loan from American Premium Assistance Company (APAC), APAC spots the default and sends GoAuto an electronic notification—a load‑balancing routine that simultaneously appends the signed certificate printer stamp via APAC’s proprietary web service interface.

The plaintiffs insisted GoAuto was acting without “strict adherence” because **no physical signature preceded cancellation**—the document was sent automatically by software before any APAC employee stamped it offline later that day. Their argument rested on two premises:

1. **Receipt** means some tangible hand‑off.
2. **Certifying** means there must be a named individual’s signature affixed at the moment of delivery.

If true, GoAuto would pointlessly hit its compliance wall even though the law applies within seconds after database log entry—a reality check about whether old statutes correctly speak modern systems.

### How the Ninth Workshop Tolerates Digital Receipts

As Judge Southwick noted while affirming district‑court findings:

> The term *receipt* … has “the act of receiving something,”and *receive* … means “to come into possession.”

That definition alone dismantles an intuition that only a signed envelope counts as proof of reception alows insurers discretion more appropriate for contemporary practice modes than manuscript letters do give affidavits that wait until manually handled printed copies are delivered to a filing cabinet—and one after cancellation is committed!

While drafted decades ago—most legislature left vague the subtle distinction between *message transmitted* & *document received*—the real question was *when does possession/materialize.* In the case at hand, APAC transmitted its copy electronically into GoAuto’s computer system on the midnight terminus day ; incorporated within APAC’s print‐out workflow was also an address field forwarding ballots en masse to any party specified under § 9 3550(G)(3)(a). Judges reasoned electronic media equals a *private carrier*.
