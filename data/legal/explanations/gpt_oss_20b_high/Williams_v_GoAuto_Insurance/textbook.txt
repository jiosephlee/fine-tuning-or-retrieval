Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Factual Background & Procedural Posture  

The controversy in **Williams v. GoAuto Insurance** centers on whether GoAuto Insurance Company (GoAuto) correctly honored Louisiana’s statutory procedure for canceling auto‑insurance policies that are financed through a third‑party premium‑finance arrangement. In order to understand the Court’s reasoning it is essential first to identify the parties involved, explain how American Premium Assistance Company (APAC) structured its financing agreements with insurers and insureds, and outline the procedural path that led a class action originally filed in state court to reach federal appellate review focused on a single procedural question under Louisiana law.

## Who were the litigants – insureds vs insurers vs financier  

The plaintiffs were two former insureds of GoAuto—Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright—joined later by Nicholas Jenkins, who had a separate policy but was involved in a lawsuit that prompted this case to be consolidated as a class action at the state level. All three had financed their GoAuto auto‑insurance premiums through APAC’s installment scheme rather than paying GoAuto directly. APAC collected monthly payments from these insureds and forwarded those payments in full to GoAuto. In exchange APAC obtained a power‑of‑attorney (POA) from each insured’s financing agreement that authorized APAC “to cancel [go]a[GoAuto’s] insurance policy upon failure to pay.” Thus APAC functioned not only as the principal guarantor of premium payment but also as the agent empowered by contract to terminate coverage when defaults occurred.

On the other side were two corporate defendants: GoAuto Insurance Company—the insurer whose policy cancellations became contentious—and APAC itself—whose role as financier is central to the statutory question at issue. A third insured party was involved only insofar as his accident related liability was alleged in the original claim; he was not a defendant or plaintiff named in this appeal but his case contributed facts about an insurance claim that ultimately remained severed after dismissal by the district court.

## How APAC’s financing agreement authorized cancellation  

APAC’s financing agreements contained four critical clauses that relate directly to Louisiana’s §9:3550(G). First, each contract stipulated that APAC held a valid power‑of‑attorney allowing it to take enforcement actions against GoAuto when an insured defaulted on premium payment. Second, because these contracts required monthly installments be made within specified deadlines, any failure triggered an “uncured default.” Third, upon such default APAC was required by statute—and by its own paperwork—to issue a “Ten‑Day Notice of Cancellation” communicating imminent termination unless payment was received within ten days of notice delivery. Fourth, once default remained uncured after ten days, APAC had both an obligation and authority under §9:3550(G) **(G)(3)(a)(i)-(iv)** to send GoAuto an electronic copy of this notice together with stated certifying statements confirming that (i) it possessed a valid POA; (ii) default had not been cured; (iii) it had sent the ten‑day notice; and (iv) copies had been delivered to all parties listed in the finance agreement who hold an interest in any loss arising thereunder (such as insurers or security holders). These certifying statements were written on APAC letterhead and included an affidavit request for proof of mailing for compliance purposes.

Thus APAC’s contracts provided every element Libya requires for antidiocogorithmatically sound cancellation proceedings apart from any question about physical signatures or receipt traditionally associated with paper notices—a question ultimately examined by this Court after district court findings had already dismissed all claims rendered moot by summary judgment on statutory compliance considerations.

## Timeline from state trial filing → removal → summary judgment question  

1. **State‑court filing** – In Louisiana state court Kimberly Williams filed suit alleging that GoAuto did not effectively cancel her financed policy after nonpayment and also alleged misrepresentation and breach of good faith collaterally connected with dispute over policy terminations. A later amended complaint brought Felita Wright into suit alongside Williams; meanwhile Nicholas Jenkins—who owned another financed policy affected by similar circumstances—joined what became an unclassed lawsuit against GoAuto and APAC.
   
2. **Removal** – Because both defendants were corporations incorporated outside Louisiana (GoAuto being interstate while APAC operated nationally), Williams chose to remove all cases—including her colleagues’ filings—to federal court pursuant to § 28 U.S.C.§ 1446(c). The removal was executed cleanly—the basis being defamation or misrepresentation allegations which qualify under removal doctrine—and so jurisdictional concerns did not arise once the case faced federal forum.
   
3. **Summary–judgment focus** – After removal, both parties resolved all substantive claims except one narrow procedural query concerning whether GoAuto’s liquidation practices conformed strictly with La.’s §9:3550(G). Each party moved for *summary judgment*, not yet addressing damages or equitable remedies but simply asking whether statutorily mandated steps—specifically delivery of notice, receipt of such notice by insurer demonstrating “receipt,” presence or absence of signatures—had been met.
   
4. **District‑court ruling** – The U.S.D.C., Middle District of Louisiana granted GoAuto's motion for summary judgment on grounds that its automation procedure satisfied every statutory requirement enumerated above—including acquiring notice via email based on modern interpretation of “private carrier”—and therefore dismissed all claims against it with prejudice.

# Louisiana Law Governing Cancellation Of Financed Policies

This section explains how Louisiana’s statutes regulate the cancellation of insurance policies financed through third‑party payment arrangements. It focuses specifically on La Rev Stat § 9 3550(G), whose language prescribes both *what* must appear in an insurer’s notice request to cancel and *when* the insurer must act upon receipt thereof.

---

## Textual Requirements (§ 9 3550(G)(3)(a) (i)–(iv)

La Rev Stat § 9 3550(G)(3) obligates a premium‑finance company that exercises its power‑of‑attorney authority toward an insurer “to send…[the] notice…together with a statement certifying”:

1. **Validity of the Power‑of‑Attorney** – The finance contract contains a valid power‑of‑attorney giving APAC authority over policy cancellation.(La Rev Stat § 9 3550(G)[(1)]).

2. **Un‑cured Default** – The finance agreement is in default and has not been timely cured.(La Rev Stat § 9 3550(G)[(2)]).

3. **Notice Sent Upon Default** – Upon default an insured received notice specifying when APAC initially sent it.(La Rev Stat § 9 3550(G)[(3)]).

4. **Copies Sent To All Interested Parties** – Copies were made available to everyone specified in the finance agreement who might have an interest in loss under the policy.(La Rev Stat § 9 3550(G)[(4)]).

These declarative statements need not contain signatures or identifying staff names unless a separate statute imposes those particulars—as no such requirement exists here, courts have interpreted “certifying” quite broadly enough that printed statements suffice.[^1]

[^1]: See *Lopez v Mississippi Automobile Insurance Co.*, recognizing documented mass‑mail notices were sufficient despite lacking individual author signatures.

---

## Necessity for Insurer Receipt (§ 9 3550(G)(3)[b] (i))

Once the insurer receives APAC’s certified notice described above, La Rev Stat mandates “the insurer shall consider [the] cancellation … requested …” so long as no cure occurs.(La Rev Stat §\,9\,3550[G][b]\,(i)).  
In practice this means an insurer can act upon electronic delivery (email via an authorized host), physical mailings, or private carriers—terms coextensive with contemporary business practices following standard postal doctrine quoted in *Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience*.[^2]

[^2]: The Fifth Circuit figures e‑mail delivered through APAC’s Web service constitutes “receipt” because it places control duty squarely upon GoAuto’s system regarding future actions concerning coverage status.


---

## Doctrine Noted Under _Delatte_ v _Lemotte_ et al.

The courts consistently reiterate two intertwined doctrinal points originating largely in _Delatte_ v _Lemotte_:

1. **Strict Adherence Is Required** – Courts call out “strict adherence” when discussing cancelled financed policies because any procedural lapse undermines statutory certainty goals.(see *Delatte*, 633 So.\,^2 686).

2. **Any Defect Produces Ineffective Cancellation** – Failure in any homogenous element—invalid POA, improper default documentation, unsent copies—renders cancellation void until remedied.(see *Eaglin v Champion*, 558 So.\,^2 Id.; _Britten_ ,503 So.\,^2 Id.).

These principles ensure parties cannot rely merely on general contractual belief when tender abandonment occurs under state mandate.
