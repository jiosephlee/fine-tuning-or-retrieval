# Party Identification, Scheme Overview,\nand Procedural Posture  

The first portion of *United States v. Constantinescu* sets out who was charged and how the federal prosecution articulated its theory against them. It also sketches the early procedural moves that led a district judge to dismiss an otherwise wide‑ranging indictment before it reached trial.



## Defendants’ Identities And Social‑Media Platforms  

The indictment identifies eight principal conspirators:
Edward Constantinescu;
Perry “PJ” Matlock;
John Rybarczyk;
Gary Deel;
Stefan Hrvatin;
Tom Cooperman;
Mitchell Hennessey;
and Daniel Knight.
Each accused had built large followings on mainstream social‑media networks popular among retail investors (the indictment never specified particular accounts or platforms). The prosecution emphasized that these individuals presented themselves online as expert traders whose public posts included their own buy–sell activity.



## Mechanics Of The Alleged Pump‑and‑Dump Scheme  

According only to what was pleaded into evidence,
the conspirators acted together as follows:
1) **Acquisition** – One or more members purchased an inexpensive security.
2) **Promulgation** – Through brief posts (“texts,” “stories,” or “videos”) on their various feeds they claimed direct evidence that trading positions would lift its price substantially.
3) **Inflation** – Other followers encountered these statements on purposefully sensationalized content and were induced to buy en masse.
4) **Disposal** – As buying pressure raised market prices beyond realistic levels,
the conspirators sold (“dumped”) their holdings before prices fell back down,
realizing gains derived largely from followers’ losses.
The prosecution alleges this arrangement yielded at least $114 million in illicit profits.



## Indictment Counts Under § 1349,\§§ 1348 & § 2  

The indictment contains two categories of criminal charging points:

* **Conspiracy**  
Under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 the group faces nine conspiracy counts (one per named defendant). Each count alleges agreement among several actors—that partnership was expressly aimed at conducting fraudulent “pump‑and‑dump” activity.
* **Securities‑fraud Attacks**  
For crimes under 18 U.S.C.s §§ 1348 (“fraud in connection with any security”) and § 2 (“sale or offer of missiles…”) the complaint lists numerous individual allegations covering purchases through which defamatory misinformation was disseminated followed by subsequent sales designed strictly for financial benefit rather than legitimate investment activity.



## Knight’s Guilty Plea Versus Remaining Defendants’ Dismissal Motion  

Daniel Knight separately entered into a guilty plea before any challenge was presented against other conspirators.
After Knight’s resolution Saskatchewan had filed motions seeking dismissal against seven other named defendants.
The motions argued essentially that if one conspirator had reached a proper admission,
the remaining proclamations amounted merely to speculative political intrigue lacking ‘proper’
property injury ‑ “an attack only depleting followers’ “potentially valuable economic information.” 
Knight’s act isolated his interests while providing ammunition for dismissal petitions pursued by his co‑defendants.



## District Court Reasoning––Failing To Alleg Damages Beyond Economic Information  
When reviewing a federal indictment de novo,
the trial judge interrogated whether all offensive elements were properly alleged.
In this case he concluded
that while deception per se might exist,
the complaint did **not** convincingly establish a *traditional* protected property
interest upon which punishment could hinge.
Broadly speaking he viewed prosecution statements framing loss merely as eroded informational worth—rather than loss of tangible money—to be inadequate absent explicit theft or monetary forfeiture.
Therefore he dismissed the case on its face.



The ruling set up precisely what appellate brief writers have since taken up:
whether “deprivation of valuable economic information” falls beneath sliding doors opened by
Ciminelli (*U.S.* v.\ Ciminelli) or whether misdirected hype does touch an existing protected right
under fraudulent‑inducement theory upheld in *Kousisis* (*U.S.* v.\ Kousisis).
Downstream proceedings would test whether diminution attendant merely misinformation
but accompanied by an intent to acquire money or asset ultimately suffices under statutes
at issue—a question far removed from where Congress legislated .

# Indictment Sufficiency Review Under Fifth Circuit Doctrine

Federal appellate courts evaluate an indictment’s sufficiency under a de novo standard that treats every allegation made therein as true unless manifestly erroneous (*United States v. Rafoi*, 60 F.R.D 982) . The court then asks whether those allegedly true statements amount to a complete statement‑of‑fact sufficient “to give a defendant notice that he faces criminal liability” (*Fontenot*, 665 F.R.D 640) . Two doctrines anchor this inquiry—first, an indictment must lay out every ordinary element required by law (*Crow*, 164 F.R.D 229); second, it must afford defendants their constitutional defenses against downstream prosecution (*Gaytan*, 74 F.R.D 545).

These considerations crystallize into a tripartite test expanded upon in *Guzman‑Ocampo* (“an indictment must enumerate fist‑fac­iae elements, afford notice compatible with double‑jeopardy protections, and convey legal certainty”). This section explains that three‑prong structure before shedding its application to United States v Constantinescu.

# Statutory Framework: The Securities Fraud Statute (§§ 1348 & 2)

The Federal securities‑fraud statutes are narrowly tailored instruments designed so that conspirators cannot cheat their victims merely by “giving away” confidential information—or, more broadly, by withholding information necessary for an investor’s discretionary economic decisions—even if no direct transfer of money occurs at the time a deceit is committed. United States v. Greenlaw held that § 1348 “borrows key concepts” from the mail‑and‑wire‑fraud provisions, resulting in a doctrine where malicious conduct coupled with intent to obtain money or property governs liability under both statutes (84 F​.4th 325). In light of this construction, an offense under § 1348 or § 2 requires proof of **both** (a) *a scheme to defraud* and (b) *intent to defraud* as articulated in United States v. Evans (892 F​.3d 692).  

Both subsections are peculiar in that they dissolve customary presumptions about victim type or loss attributable consequences—a point crucially underscored by recent Supreme Court decisions cited here by their application rather than by legislative language alone (Kousisis v. United States, 145 S​.Ct.​1388).  

---

## I The “Scheme-to‑Defraud” Element

A *scheme* is defined as “a **material misrepresentation** **intended** to deceive others so that they give up something of value.” It should be understood within a broader context where deception is coupled with an actionable goal—namely acquiring money or property from the deceived party.^1

In United States v. Evans (892 F​.3d 692), decision makers rejected an argument that mere wrongful internal belief lacked crime‑worthiness unless this belief was coupled with purposeful deceit guaranteed all respondents even when no monetary value was lost by victims.^2 Likewise here, indicted defendants “purchased a security… ‘pump’[ped]… [post]ed false … so that other investors were induced...to purchase…[the] security” (indictment ¶ 13–14). These allegations go beyond stating provocation based solely on economic loss or convenient rumors; instead they plainly state *strategy*: manipulating follower expectations via public media posts about supposed insider trades so followers parted with money deemed worthwhile purchasing chosen securities—aligning squarely with the “material misstatement” doctrine codified across jurisdictional precedent.^3  

Because deception hinged upon *information* offered publicly—a hallmark maneuvers comparable under both §§ 1348 (fraudulent pretenses regarding purchase/sale) and 2 (deceptive conduct accessible through no lawful channel)—the indictments accord directly reflect required structural compliance requirements established throughout Section III before reaching suggested analysis.^4

# Fraudulent–Inducement Theory Versus Right‑to‑Control Theory  

The premise common to both federal fraud theories is an underlying *scheme*—a deliberate plan by a defendant that misleads a victim and tempts the victim into parting with something valuable. Distinguishing features arise once the Court discerns whether the core injury lies in deprivation *of a right* (the “right‑to‑control” approach) or deprivation *of money or property itself* (the “fraudulent‐inducement” approach).

**Right‑to‑Control Theory**

Historically, prosecutors followed an interpretation derived from § 1348’s reference to “any person’’ who could acquire “money or property’’ related to buying or selling securities while presenting themselves through false pretenses. Early circuit opinions treated “control” as an intangible protectorate against manipulation—a victim who could decide whether—and when—to transact was deemed its own protected *property*. Thus a scheme might criminalize deceiving someone into keeping a hold over an asset merely by withholding useful information [Ciminelli grounds]. Under this formulation, failure merely damaged a person’s ability *to make decisions* about their assets and didn’t directly impair a commodity owned by them.

In *United States v/Ciminelli*, however, the Supreme Court said such an approach exceeds statutory limits because the statutory purpose under §§ 1348/1349 is vertically aimed at *traditional* property rights—money or tangible property—not merely abstract control abilities that do not result in an identifiable loss of an existing asset (“the [...] right … is not a traditional property interest”). If no transfer or theft occurs, then § 1348/2 cannot apply under this model.\[see *Cim.* at 316\]. Defendants squaring off can therefore argue District Courts should dismiss allegations brandishing only evaded decision autonomy without tangible losses…  

**Fraudulent‑Inducement Theory**

Federal courts conclude instead that § 1348 imposes liability when the defendant’s deception results in *the victim voluntarily parting with* money or other property under contractual assurances obtained through persuasion alone. A broad notion subsumes any variety of schemes—from selling stolen goods through online classifieds up through complicated financial instruments—provided **intention** exists *to obtain* something valuable via deceitful statements [Kousisis grounds]. Crucially, those courts do not require demonstrable victim loss; they consider wrongdoing complete when a fraudster ensnares another into transferring money or value without having made quantitative proof that harm followed.\[see *Kou.* at 1392\]. This view harmonizes monolingiously with § 1348’s textual reading that scheming “to obtain … any money … in connection with … purchase… sale…” specifies an end goal expressly stated as acquisition—not punishment for ignorance borne from withheld knowledge.\[Section 1348\f]

# Intent Considerations and Economic Loss Requirements under Kousisis

In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit was asked whether an indictment alleging only a desire to enrich oneself through deceptive social‑media posts could satisfy the intent component of federal securities‑fraud statutes. This chapter focuses on how *United States v. Kousisis* distills federal reasoning regarding *intent to defraud* when a defendant’s sole goal appears to be self‑profit and when victims receive goods or services whose value equals what they give up. Through an almost immediate exposition of *Kousisis*, we see why mere profitability does not preclude a finding that a defendant “intended to defraud” his victims – even in the absence of any demonstrable net loss for those victims.

## Mirrored‑Image Doctrine Rejected by Kousisis

The Supreme Court’s holding in *Kousisis* makes explicit that criminal fraud statutes do **not** require proof that an accused party caused an actual financial deficit for the victim beyond any goods or services otherwise provided in exchange for that very form of payment or consideration. Under traditional civil tort analysis — which relies on “mirror‑image” proof — an alleged plaintiff would need concrete evidence that she suffered a monetary loss equal or greater than what was received in return for her wrongful acceptance under fraudlously induced terms. In contrast,*Kousisis* carved out a distinct standard expressly tied to “fraudulent–inducement,” wherein liability hinges on *obtaining* money or property through deceit irrespective of subsequent equivalence between payment received and goods exchanged or fees paid back by the accused (see *Ekaterinos* at 1388).  Accordingly:

1.  **Fraudulent Inducement**  
    The court clarified that “a defendant uses a material misstatement...to trick … into entering … a contract requiring hand‑over ...of money or property.”  This definition explicitly separates *intent* from *victim outcome*.  It demands only proof that with deceptive language the defendant realized his objective of acquiring another person’s funds or property — no proof that those persons were eventually left worse off owing monetary damages later borne by themselves or otherwise compensated.
2.  **No Mirror‑Image Requirement**  
    In rejecting any expectation of proving “the victim’s bottom line” was harmed (see *Kousisis* at 1389), the Court signified offenders who supply some factor roughly equivalent val­ue can nonetheless enjoy conviction if they had deceptively induced acceptance into relinquishing funds or assets (see *Kousisis* at 1392). The refusal rejects penal logic hinging on fiscal harm as necessary trigger points for liability under these statutes.

Applying these insights to *Constantinescu*, an indictment constituting pump‑and‑dump activity shows defendants posting false commentary intended specifically to incline followers into buying shares so their prices would inflate at their advantage (*indictment ¶ 13–14*). Therefore:

- The **misrepresentations** are material because they affect investor decision making about securities’ worth;
- Defendants **intentionally** sought benefit via fraudulent channels — their tactic deliberately outran legitimate signals available in market structures;
- Defendants’ profit aim does not excuse absence of adjudicated monetary loss among followers because *Kousisis* establishes such damage is extraneous while demonstrating intent sufficed once fraudulent inducement secured recipients’ payment – irrespective of post‑purchase market outcomes.

# Holding and Implications for Future Fraud Prosecutions

In _United States v. Constantinescu_, the Fifth Circuit reversed a district‑court dismissal and remanded after finding that defendants’ indictment satisfied both elements required under 18 U.S.C. § 1348—scheming “to defraud” an individual **and** doing so with an intent to defraud—to effectuate a “pump‑and‑dump” scheme carried out through social‑media posts. Because an indictment is reviewed de novo, the appellate court had first examined whether the indictment itself provided each material element before looking at any subsequent procedure or evidence admissibility concerns.  

The reversal hinged upon a clear, rule‑based assessment:

1. **Scheme‑to‑Defraud**  
   The Seventh Bristle’s standard continues that “a defendant commits securities fraud when he ‘engaged in deception’ and ‘obtaining [the victim’s] money or property’ was ‘an object’ of his fraud.” In this case, the indictment narrated defendants purchasing securities “to **pump** them,” posting false statements so followers would **buy** those same securities, thereby artificially inflating prices before “dumping” them at a profit (per Indictment ¶ 13–14). That description meets the traditional notion that a **material misrepresentation** was used **intentionally** so other parties would relinquish money or property—in this instance followers’ cash invested in recently “pumped” shares.\n\nCriticism arose because defendants were purportedly seeking simply “to deprive their followers of potentially valuable economic information,” echoing a *right‑to‑control* framework advanced in *Ciminelli*. However, the Fifth Circuit found no indication that defendants were litigating under that theory: instead they asserted *fraudulent‑inducement*. That theory—codified both by earlier case law (see 18 USC § 1348’s language) and solidified recently by *Kousisis*—holds that a decreed offense exists when someone uses materially false pretenses specifically **to cause another person’s contract** requiring surrendering money or property. Thus depriving followers merely of knowledge does *not* satisfy § 1348; it is nonrelevant if accompanied by an inducement resulting in transfer.\n\n2. **Intent to Defraud**  
   The defendant statement shows sufficient intent because it directly links posts designed to deceive (“false and misleading information”) with procurement of money (“other investors were induced”). Here intent was expressed through purposeful operations meant to obtain donors’ money via fabricated positions—a classic fraudulent‐inducement motive recognized by *Kousisis*, which rejected an argument requiring measurable victim harm as essential or even necessary under the statutes.\n\n3. **Holistic Sufficiency Test**  
   By referencing case law supplied within its own brief—including *Rafoi*, which obligates courts take each allegation at face value—and applying those standards fresh against statutory construction adopted from *Ciminelli* *and* *Kousisis*, the Fifth Circuit found no determinative factual deficiency preventing an offense from being stated.\n\nThus each element demanded by § 1348 was federally satisfied, rendering dismissal legally improper.\n\n## Reversal Basis—Indictment Satisfied Sufficient Scheme‑and‑Intent Question
