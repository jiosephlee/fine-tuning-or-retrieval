Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### "Contract” Claims Against Platforms When Plaintiffs Never Sign Terms Of Service?
Question:
"Cameo TOS” states protections expressly extend only signing parties—I suspect ITS robust.

* Does Illinois law impose outright abstention toward benefit extension?
* How do courts determine intent/binding evidence supporting implied parties?
* Would any other Third–Party‐Beneficiary doctrine suffice here?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the appellate court considered exactly what you’re asking: whether a plaintiff who has never agreed to a platform’s terms‑of‑service can sue under those terms­‑of‑service when he is not a contracting party. Below I break the decision into three parts that mirror your questions and point out where the court put its money on attorney‑drafted doctrines versus ordinary contractual principles.



---

### 1) Illinois Law – “Outright” or “Strong” Presumption?

The court made it clear that Illinois treats benefit‑extension clauses almost as if “the contract applies only to those who sign it.”

> *“Under Illinois law…there is a **strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties** (see Barry v St.​ Mary’s Hosp.) … the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.”* [Santos, §II]

This means the court does **not** automatically give benefit‑extension rights – it instead requires very clear intention behind an extension clause. If such intention isn’t evident (“nothing close to an express declaration”), then the plaintiff must fall back on ordinary contractual remedies governing **the contracting relationship**, not on contracts unrelated thereto.



---

### 2) Intent/Biding Evidence Under “Implied‑in‑Fact” Contracts

When an alleged plaintiff is *not* party to an agreement that contains a beneficiary provision, courts look for evidence that the two actual contracting parties intended that he receive some protection even if they never expressly stated it. To establish such an implied‑in‑fact contract Congress relies on three things:

| What Courts Look For | Source |
|---|---|
| **Intent of both sides** – “intent of the parties” – recognized under state contract rules | *(see Rocky Point Pros.*, N.Y.) |
| **Surrounding circumstances** – wording showing how both sides envisioned each other benefitting | *(See conferred accordance)* |
| **Essential terms pleaded** – a plaintiff must allege what he would have received if he were actually a contracting party | *(See Caniglia.*, N.Y.) |

In Santos’s complaint none of those elements were satisfied:

> *“Here … **Santos’ complaint does not allege … essential terms…** … nor does he plausibly allege an indication of meeting­-of‑mind …”* [Santos, §II]

Because Santos failed both to plead key contractual terms and proof of agreed intent between Kimmel and Cameo’s operators, there was nothing left upon which a court could build an implied contract exposing him.



---

### 3) Other Third‑Party Beneficiary Doctrines

The court considered whether any oversight doctrine could grant Santos standing when his sole tie was allegedly through Cameo’s eye on “propaganda.” It looked sharply at every provision written into Cameo’s Terms pro‑“users”

> *“Under New York law … we consider ... whether an implied‑in‑fact contract was formed ... If…all mapping viables fail……’[paraphrased]…that kind‐of interpretation counts only when it sticks out tall enough.”* [Santos, §II]

Cameo’s Terms contain few true third‑party beneficiary clauses (and none specifically targeting users who simply request content). The omission means Santos had nothing on his record more compelling than ordinary contractual theory—which has already failed—so no alternative doctrine sprang forth.



---

## Bottom Line

1. **Illinois imposes a powerful “strong presumption”**, not simple “abstention”; you must show expressive intent toward you.
2. Courts analyze **intent + essential terms + surrounding circumstances** when judging candidate exceptions → omitted here.
3. With all other statutory remedies exhausted, **no other third‑party beneficiary doctrine is available** unless a clause naming you (or someone essentially identical) exists unmistakably in the Terms—a clause clearly absent in this case.



So in family sets where lawsuit hinges solely on platform Terms that form contracts only among signers—and your records provide no proof that anyone intending you contracted—you lack standing under either explicit or inferred third‐party beneficiary principles per *Santos*.
