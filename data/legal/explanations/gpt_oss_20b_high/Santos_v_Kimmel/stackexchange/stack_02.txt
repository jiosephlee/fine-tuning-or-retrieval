Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### "Instigation" Versus Transformation—Does Designating Original Purpose Remove Fair-Use Protection?
Question:
"Instigate" denotes Jimmy Kimmel informed George Santos how his clips would proceed on TV." If he directed calls-to-action filmmaking perfectly mirrors interests behind original video production—should his Hollywood map stem readers overlooked snippet they essentially recreated?

* How do doctrines differentiate between purpose argued literally versus judged reasonably?
* Where does being ‘designer’ tilt outcomes towards lack‑of-transformation?
* What concrete facts lift dispute toward ruling favor either interpretation?
Answer:
**Short Answer**  
The fact that Kimmel *initiated* or *designed* Santos’s videos does **not** bar a fair‑use defense simply because it shows that he had an “original purpose.”  
Fair‑use hinges on whether a reasonable observer sees a new work adding something new—a different message or expressed intent—rather than merely reproducing what already existed.  In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit agreed that Kimmel’s mocking show added critical commentary even though he had been involved in creating those clips, so transforming wrapped positivity into satire still satisfied *factor 1* of § 107 and made overall infringement unlikely.



---

## 1 Purpose‑versus‑Perspective under § 107(1)

| **Standard** | **What matters** | **Application in this case** |
|---|---|---|
| **Objective test** | A court asks whether a reasonable observer concludes that the new work provides “new expression,” meaning, or message beyond what existed in its source material.^[1] | The district court found “a reasonable observer would think … these videos conveyed feelings … rather than simply repeating Santos’s words.” Solarized § I(b). |
| **Subjective intent** | An author’s personal motive does **not** dictate fairness.^[2] | Judge Lohier cited Andy Warhol Found.: *“the subjective intent… does not determine…purpose.”* (para X) |

So even if Kimmel confined himself to re‑creating Santos’s statements (“designer”), he could still create a *transformative* derivative if his presentation changed how viewers interpret those words.



## 2 Does Being Designer Tilt Courts Toward Non‑Transformation?

Kimmel was literally both producer—by mounting requests via Cameo—and commentator—in airing edited clips satirically.^[3]  
The Court repeatedly emphasized that *designing* a piece does **not alone make it non‑transformative*.  

> **“Yet...a reasonable observer here would think…the videos conveyed hope…positivity…not willingness…to say absurd things.”** *(para X)*  

The appellate panel rejected TCA Television’s suggestion that C‑designers automatically lack transformiveness—the very idea he cited (“Kimmel instigated…”). Instead it stressed the necessity of added value (“throws something new”) over mere replication.

### Bottom Line

Being an originator or facilitator (*designer*) alone cannot defeat transformiveness because § 107 requires an external perspective—did you add something unmistakably different?  
When two sets are genuinely contrasted (**creator versus recycler**) clarification suffices :

*Where good faith & transformation fall together → fair use prevails.*  
*Santos v. Kimmel* therefore affirms this doctrine and shows how judges weigh documentary circumstances – maintaining transformed usage despite apparent origin involvement.
