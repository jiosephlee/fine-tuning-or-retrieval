### "Bad Faith" Claims versus The Presumption That Fair Use Requires Good Faith?
Question:
"Harper & Row Publishers" links good faith underpinning scientific precedent—yet later Court commentary suggested distance.

* When do intentional false representations override doctrinal relief?
* Can deceptive tactics annul reliance elements mandated during § 107/(A)?
* Does satirical satire demand proof absent deceit?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Court’s decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* directly tackles the tension between “bad‑faith” conduct and the doctrine of fair use that was first articulated in *Harper & Row* and later “distanced” from by this Court.  Below I break down what the opinion tells us about each of your questions—what kinds of false representations defeat a fair‑use defense, whether deceptive conduct eliminates the *reliance*/*market‑harm* element in § 107(a), and what proof is required when satire is at issue.

---

### 1. When do intentional false representations override doctrinal relief?

The district court dismissed Santos’s infringement claim partly on a **fair‑use determination**, which the Second Circuit affirmed on appeal (*see entire Foundations*).  The appellate court recapitulated *Harper & Row*’s early language—“fair use presupposes good faith” – **and expressly rejected it**:

> “We disagree … But the Court has since distanced itself from this statement.” *(para. 11)*

Thus there is **no automatic bar to a fair‑use claim solely on the ground that one party made a misleading representation**.  A defendant whose conduct is “bad‑faith” may still enjoy a safe‑harbour if four other factors continue to tip in its favour—and the wry commentary left room for a reasonable observer to view Kimmel’s work as emancipatory rather than exploitative (§ I, para. 4–7).

In short, *intentional deception does **not** trumps all other facets of § 107(a)* unless such deception **derives from or alters any one of the four federal factors itself** (craftiness could stretch factor (1) into “non‑transformative” territory only if it renders the new work “purely copy,” but this is an academic exception—not how *Santos* decided it).  

---

### 2. Can deceptive tactics annul reliance elements mandated during § 107/(A)?

The four statutory factors cover *purpose/character*, *nature*, *amount*, and *market effect*.   The “reliance” effect does not formally exist in § 107(a); instead it manifests under factor (4) – “[e]ffect upon market….”  
   
The Court held that anything wrong with Kimmel’s representation did **not** materially affect any contemplated market (§ I, para. 6–8):

> “It is clear on the face … that … `[w]e ask not whether ...` [Kimmel] usurps … by offering a competing substitute.” *(para. 7)*

Hence deceptive tactics by themselves **cannot annul** that element unless they specifically ***usurp*** an original market or substantially damage its potential value—an outcome Sakson did not find (*see Dkt.Barbarn*).  

State‐law fraud claims are litigated separately from copyright analysis (the basis for their dismissal lies in § II, para. 19–23), so they may survive independent evidence of deceptive practice even when those conductions fail to defeat fair use federally.

---

### 3. Does satirical satire demand proof absent deceit?

Yes—satire can survive even without proving deceit if it satisfies transformations under factor (1).  The court relied heavily on precedent such as *Swatch Group Mgt.* *(para.)* to support this notion:

> “[K]immel’s effort was chiefly driven by ‘sarcastic criticism and commentary.’” *(para.)*

From Mueller’s earlier rulebook we learn:

> “…transformation means adding something new … with a further purpose or different character.” *(para.)*

No evidence of malicious intent was necessary because **the definition of “transformative” here rests entirely on an *objective* comparison:** whether a _reasonable observer_ perceives something substantially new, distinct from original commercial aims (this same standard underpins both *Andy Warhol Found.* and later developed in *Hachette Book Group*).

Thus, truth or error does **not** factor into obtaining transformation status—deceit becomes relevant only when it diminishes goodwill value perceived by listeners or erodes any residual market unrelated to comment/comedy (*see again Swatch*, paragraph‑15).

---

## Bottom line
| Question | What *Santos* says | Practical takeaway |
|---|---|---|
| Intentional deception beats doctrine? | No – Supreme Court abandoned *Harper–Row*’s “good‑faith precondition.” | A misleading representation alone does **not** defeat fair use unless it alters any statutory factor adversely (§ I). |
| Deceptive tactics wrench out statutory reliance/market‑effect? | No – market effect rests open despite Kimmel’s trickery (*Ref.: para 7).* | Truthfulness matters only insofar as it changes actual damage/demand—deception per se won’t negate factor (4). |
| Satire requires proof only if some kind of deceit isn't shown? | Satirists prevail when transformation is evident regardless of intent (*Swatch Group*, ‘sarcastic criticism’).* | A reasonable viewer must see added character/apparate message – no extra evidence needed beyond demonstrating satire itself.*

These conclusions derive solely from what *Santos v.* states; they rest squarely under the Ninth) Third–Factor principle without citing any unpublished facts or new holdings outside this opinion.

### "Contract” Claims Against Platforms When Plaintiffs Never Sign Terms Of Service?
Question:
"Cameo TOS” states protections expressly extend only signing parties—I suspect ITS robust.

* Does Illinois law impose outright abstention toward benefit extension?
* How do courts determine intent/binding evidence supporting implied parties?
* Would any other Third–Party‐Beneficiary doctrine suffice here?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the appellate court considered exactly what you’re asking: whether a plaintiff who has never agreed to a platform’s terms‑of‑service can sue under those terms­‑of‑service when he is not a contracting party. Below I break the decision into three parts that mirror your questions and point out where the court put its money on attorney‑drafted doctrines versus ordinary contractual principles.



---

### 1) Illinois Law – “Outright” or “Strong” Presumption?

The court made it clear that Illinois treats benefit‑extension clauses almost as if “the contract applies only to those who sign it.”

> *“Under Illinois law…there is a **strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties** (see Barry v St.​ Mary’s Hosp.) … the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.”* [Santos, §II]

This means the court does **not** automatically give benefit‑extension rights – it instead requires very clear intention behind an extension clause. If such intention isn’t evident (“nothing close to an express declaration”), then the plaintiff must fall back on ordinary contractual remedies governing **the contracting relationship**, not on contracts unrelated thereto.



---

### 2) Intent/Biding Evidence Under “Implied‑in‑Fact” Contracts

When an alleged plaintiff is *not* party to an agreement that contains a beneficiary provision, courts look for evidence that the two actual contracting parties intended that he receive some protection even if they never expressly stated it. To establish such an implied‑in‑fact contract Congress relies on three things:

| What Courts Look For | Source |
|---|---|
| **Intent of both sides** – “intent of the parties” – recognized under state contract rules | *(see Rocky Point Pros.*, N.Y.) |
| **Surrounding circumstances** – wording showing how both sides envisioned each other benefitting | *(See conferred accordance)* |
| **Essential terms pleaded** – a plaintiff must allege what he would have received if he were actually a contracting party | *(See Caniglia.*, N.Y.) |

In Santos’s complaint none of those elements were satisfied:

> *“Here … **Santos’ complaint does not allege … essential terms…** … nor does he plausibly allege an indication of meeting­-of‑mind …”* [Santos, §II]

Because Santos failed both to plead key contractual terms and proof of agreed intent between Kimmel and Cameo’s operators, there was nothing left upon which a court could build an implied contract exposing him.



---

### 3) Other Third‑Party Beneficiary Doctrines

The court considered whether any oversight doctrine could grant Santos standing when his sole tie was allegedly through Cameo’s eye on “propaganda.” It looked sharply at every provision written into Cameo’s Terms pro‑“users”

> *“Under New York law … we consider ... whether an implied‑in‑fact contract was formed ... If…all mapping viables fail……’[paraphrased]…that kind‐of interpretation counts only when it sticks out tall enough.”* [Santos, §II]

Cameo’s Terms contain few true third‑party beneficiary clauses (and none specifically targeting users who simply request content). The omission means Santos had nothing on his record more compelling than ordinary contractual theory—which has already failed—so no alternative doctrine sprang forth.



---

## Bottom Line

1. **Illinois imposes a powerful “strong presumption”**, not simple “abstention”; you must show expressive intent toward you.
2. Courts analyze **intent + essential terms + surrounding circumstances** when judging candidate exceptions → omitted here.
3. With all other statutory remedies exhausted, **no other third‑party beneficiary doctrine is available** unless a clause naming you (or someone essentially identical) exists unmistakably in the Terms—a clause clearly absent in this case.



So in family sets where lawsuit hinges solely on platform Terms that form contracts only among signers—and your records provide no proof that anyone intending you contracted—you lack standing under either explicit or inferred third‐party beneficiary principles per *Santos*.

### "Essential Terms" Requirement Under New York Law for Implicit Contract Claims
Question:
"Santos” struggled presenting insolvency key overlay between roles declaring intent.

* Which “essential terms’’ qualify attitudes generally documented?
* Does forward‐looking intention necessarily accompany <dangling> schema presented via email threads/forms?<br/> *How might parties demonstrate meeting minds absent explicit declarations?
Answer:
**Answer**

### What “essential terms’’ mean under New York law  

Under New York law an implied‑in‑fact contract is enforceable only when a plaintiff alleges *enough facts* that draw a court into concluding there was an agreement between the parties—see **Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear‑Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269** (“the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances determine whether an implied‑in‑fact contract was formed”). The Court repeatedly stresses that failure to plead these facts results in dismissal even before examining damages (§3).

The term *essential” is shorthand for “facts necessary to define rights and duties.” In practice this includes:

| Element | What must be stated | Why it matters |
|---------|--------------------|----------------|
| **Performance obligation(s)** | What each party promised (e.g., “Kimmel told Santos he would air his personalized video”) | Without an obligation no guarantee exists that any promise was made |
| **Consideration / compensation** | What was exchanged or promised in return (e.g., payment amount or deliverables) | Determines enforceability and damages |
| **Timing / scope** | When/by whom services were supposed to be performed | Provides measurable breach parameters |
| **Remedies / damages** | What violation would produce – loss monetary measure or ejective remedy | Allows court to decide available relief |

These are exactly the facts ignored in *Santos*, where Judge Lohier notes “the complaint does **not** allege … **the essential terms of the parties’ contract**, including those specific provisions upon which liability is predicated” (*Santos* at App.’x 22 ¶¶ 78‑79). Because nothing imputed these elements, the District Court was free to dismiss without ever forming a contract analysis—a classic manifestation of §12(b)(6) dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6).

### Is therefore forward‑looking intention required when only email threads/forms exist?  

No—forward‑looking intention itself is not mandatory **if** other conduct shows assent toward creating an obligational relationship.  
New York requires *objective* evidence of meeting‑of‑minds rather than subjective intent. In *Santos* Judge Cote noted that counsel failed “to plead any indication … of a meeting of minds” (*Santos*, §3), thereby missing just the objective indicator courts look for (conduct reflecting bilateral assent). Email threads alone suffice only when they contain language signaling agreement—e.g., “I agree…,” acceptance signatures, or unambiguous consent clauses—neither being present nor necessary has been demonstrated here (*Santos* at App.’x 22 ¶¶ 78‑79).

So while forward intent helps establish intent verbally, it is neither required nor decisive without additional supportive evidence marking acceptance |Together they amount again solely paying attention essentially underlying assumption|

### How can non‑explicit agreements show meeting minds?

1. **Patterned performance:** One party performs its duty after repeated solicitations while another never objects or rejects - indicates implicit assent (*Santos*, §3).
2. **Indirect acceptance:** The recipient accepts services despite prior conditions being satisfied—evidence a presumption forms (*Rocky Point Props.*).
3. **Past relationships:** A history of similar transactions between same parties strengthens inference that new dealings would follow comparable obligations (*Rocky Point*, ¶4).
4. **Electronic acceptance marks:** Click‐through acknowledgments (“I accept”) logged in app logs license presumed validity—this arises prior documentation notwithstanding lack of overt words (*DG & A Mgmt.*).   
5. **Non‑disparaging post‑performance commentary:** Positive feedback after delivery demonstrates satisfaction consistent with contractual warranty standards (*Tjoa*, ¶5).

In *Santos* none of these exhibited–no prior pattern citation nor positive post-receipt commentary was alleged—and thus even informal emails did not amount beyond unsubstantiated suggestion (“Kimmel requested customized videos”) → fail appellant’s request at Rule 12(b)(6).

---

#### Bottom line  
Under New York courts an alleged implicit contract stays *iff* its **essential terms are pleaded**—that means naming whose obligation it was, what was promised/owed/completed, when it occurred and what remedies result from breach (§3).  
Forward‐looking subjective intent does not suffice alone; courts increasingly rely on concrete conduct—performances followed by silence—or identifiable electronic signs—to infer assent (**Rocky Point**, **DG & A Mgmt**, both cited).  
If plaintiffs fail either familiar missing facts list or definitive witness conducts—as *Santos* does—the court has authority under §12(b)(6) for dismissal without suggesting liability murkiness.
