Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 2: # Rule 404(b) Intrinsic Evidence vs Propensity Evidence  

In United States v. Justin Cutbank the defense moved pre‑trial to exclude D.F.’s testimony that she had seen Cutbank with a sawed‑off Marlin .22‑caliber rifle on several occasions before the alleged violent conduct in question. The district court rejected that motion, holding that the testimony was “intrinsic” evidence relevant to proving an element of the charged offense—specifically, prior possession that establishes ownership or control—rather than “propensity” evidence showing a predisposition to commit violent acts. The court’s ruling rested on the precedent set by United States v. Battle, which clarified when firearms testimony may be admitted under Rule 404(b). This chapter expounds on that precedent and illustrates how Cutbank’s record fits within it, contrasting intrinsic with propensity evidence and explaining why the judge’s determination did not constitute an abuse of discretion.

## Battle PMTS Doctrine Summary  

United States v. Battle decided that prior observation by a witness of a defendant handling or possessing a firearm satisfies the “pre‑existing POSSESSION” exception within Rule 404(b)’s *intrinsic* category (Battle, 774 F.3d at 511). The Court held that such testimony is indispensable for proving a defendant’s later possession because it directly establishes control or ownership during an earlier period with which the subsequent conduct is linked (*intrinsic* test).
Key elements of Battle:
1. **Relevance** – Witness testimony supports an element of sentencing or conviction—here, ‘possession’ of a weapon at issue.
2. **Directness** – Observation focused on identifying weapons handled by defendants—not merely describing personality traits.
3. **No Propensity Showing** – The statement does not show motive or personal character beyond ownership facts.
Justice Lisk insisted on clear separation between “intrinsic” data used to meet an evidentiary element and “propensity” data used to paint moral character or intent—the latter disallowed by Rule 404(b)(3).  
Thus, whereas motive or risk statements are barred as “propensity,” Beat’s trial witness “hearsay” regarding previous possession remains permissible if it bears directly on whether anyone actually possessed firepowder at a time relevant to prosecution claims.

## Differentiating Intrinsic vs Propensity Evidence  

Rule 404(b) prohibits evidence of an individual’s character or propensity unless specific exceptions apply (*e.g.* immigration crime statutes). Nevertheless, courts permit certain factual recitations pertinent to an offense—collectively labeled *intrinsic* evidence—to help establish elements like *ownership*, *control*, *contact*, or *use*. Distinguishing corners:
- **Intrinsic Evidence**  
    - Serves “within” prove specific statutory facts required by prosecution  
    - Answers how a particular phenomenon relates causally or temporally (e.g., *prior weapon possession → current possession*).  
    - Requires plaintiff engagement only when it justifies an inquiry into linkage between past and present actions.
- **Propensity Evidence**  
    - Aims at proving a generalized tendency or character trait—not tied to proof of any one statutory element  
    - Applied solely when waivers exist *or* in cases where Rule 459 (criminal–civil overlap) permits disclosures relevant both ways (irrelevant here).

The Supreme Court clarified that two categories often overlap only if admissible under nerve-perpetual rules; otherwise, they remain separate destinies (see *United States v. Charboneau*, 613 F.3d at 861). In Cutbank’s context, D.F.’s testimony was strictly factual identification—she claimed direct observation on multiple nights before midnight violence occurred—a statement undeniably central to demonstrating how Cutbank possessed and controlled possession of said weapon throughout his alleged career in violence (§ 922(g)(1)). Because such content answers *who possessed* rather than *why* he possessed it, it falls squarely into flight *ownership control* and cannot be considered an expression of propensity toward violence at large akin to hearsay moral statements (*example*: “because he likes violence…”) barred by Case law (**Charboneau**, **Bennett**).
