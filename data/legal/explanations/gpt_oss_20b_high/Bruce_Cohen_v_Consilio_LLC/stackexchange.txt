Answer:
**Short answer**

Under Minnesota’s MPWA a private employee cannot *independently* sue for an “average‑daily‑wage” penalty in his own name. Section §181.101(a) expressly reserves enforcement of those penalties to the Commissioner after a demand has been served on the employer, and the Court emphasized this limitation in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio* (8th Cir., 2025). An employee may bring a *civil action* for unpaid wages or liquidated damages under §181.171, but any statutory **penalties** are imposed only through the Commissioner's administrative process and are paid directly back into that system—not into an individual plaintiff’s bank account unless the Commission orders it so as part of a settlement or award.


---

## Why an employee can’t file “for” its own average‑daily‑wage award

1. **Text of § 181.101(a)**  
   > “If payment of wages … is not made within ten days of service … *the Commissioner* may charge … a *penalty* … and *any money collected by the Commissioner must be paid* to **the employee concerned**.”  
   The language *withholds authority.* The statute makes no reference anywhere else that an aggrieved worker can direct such a payment at himself​—it specifically points this power at **the Commissioner** – see *Hull v.* ConvergeOne*, 570 F.Supp.\t3d 681–695 (D.Minn., 2021).

2. **Court’s de‑novo construction**  
   In *Cohen*, Judge Kelly explained that when “the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous,” legislation takes precedence:
   > “When the language of a statute is plain … it **must be given effect**.” (See *Allan v.* Rd.D.), then applied it literally:
   > “The ability *to enforce a penalty* is limited **to the [Commissioner]***.”  
   No part of § 181.* allows an individual plaintiff to elect this downstream pool himself or herself​—the entire remedial framework has been built around formal administrative demand, determination, collection and refund by statute.


## What rough tools courts use when probing this kind of language

| Tool | How it was used in *Cohen* | Why it matters |
|------|--------------------------|----------------|
| **Plain‑text analysis** | Judge Kelly read § 181.101(a) literally and concluded its sole remedy was “commissioner may charge…collect…pay.” | Because there was no dispute she could decide less literally without conflicting precedent like *Hull*. |
| **Legislative intent / policy context** | Referenced Montana legislations (§174–176) showing that lawmakers knew how performance mechanisms could work if *desired*. | Suggests intentional omission was deliberate equality—Minnesota chose not to give workers direct sanction power here. |
| **Consistency/specific vs general set­ups** | Highlighted that § 181.*¶171 offers civil remedies *in lieu of* average‑daily‑wage sanctions—but those sanctions remain confined where § 181.*¶1 says so.* | The Court confirmed there’s no internal conflict – both sections function together rather than clash.\**

These three approaches together produce the single holding: “Only the Commissioner may collect—and subsequently pay—a statutory average‑daily‑wage penalty.”


## How does species‐specific clause (a) coexist with general liability clauses?

The statute contains two layers:

1. **Specific enforcement route (§ 181 § 1)** – claims start with a demand by \*\*Commissioner**, who may then charge collection data + fixed daily‐penalty …
2. **General civil action route (§ 171)** – once an employer has been found in violation, he becomes liable for compensatory damages…and any *civil penalties* provided in structure § 171 bi vettve****

The Supreme Court repeatedly adopts “specific over general” when both provisions address essentially the same conduct—both compel payment based on hardness evidence—including standard interpretation that reduces broader claims when narrower ones occupy shared territory.

Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Under *Friends of the Earth* a plaintiff may recover civil‑penalty money from a private defendant even if the penalty is paid into an agency account, provided

1. **Standing** – the plaintiff (or class) shows that it suffered an actual or imminent injury that is *directly* caused by the defendant’s conduct, **and that this injury could not have been avoided** by any other means (i.e., no more general “interest” test).  
2. **Mootness/continuing interest** – there must still be an ongoing or reasonably foreseeable violation that justifies asking a court to order deterrence (and/or crediting damages). If the defendant has already ceased violating the statute or follows with a strict compliance program, courts can dismiss on “no more continuing interest” grounds just as they do with *Davis* in *Concentrated Phosphate*.
3. When those two tests are satisfied, a Pennsylvania FLSA‑style statute that designates penalties payable to the commissioner does **not bar** recovery by employees because courts view such claims under *Friends of the Earth* much like a private right‑to‑damages claim—it is an instrument meant to deter future wrongdoing, not merely compensate personal loss.*

---

### How § 27(a) of the MFLSA interacts with *Friends of the Earth*

In *Cohen v. Consilio*, 8th Cir. 2025 (the opinion you supplied), there were three intervening deadlines:

| Claim | Summary‑judgment outcome | Reason |
|-------|------------------------|--------|
| MPWA | Denied | Penalties require a commissioner demand; employer cannot sue for them independently |
| MFLSA | Vacated & remanded | Addressed only where there is **continued potential conduct** or ongoing violations |
| MWTA | Dismissed | No statutory authority for civil penalties |

Because **all statutory penalties under MFLSA accrue into the Department of Labor’s general fund** (not directly into an employee’s pocket), courts treat them “inexactly” like administrative fines—recoverable by plaintiffs if their **standing** survives because they suffer a direct injury from continued improper practice.*  

The court made clear that employees need not “show personal interest” beyond suffering damage from unpaid wages—just that those wages are their property—and *was simply impossible* for a private plaintiff who only claims “statutory penalty” without demanding the wages themselves.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1. Standing under *Friends of the Earth*  

The court’s discussion echoes *Town of Chester v. Laroe*, 581 U.S. 433 (2017).  
> “A plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks…The requisite personal interest…must continue throughout his existence.”  
*(See also· Sisney v._Kaemingk_, 15 F⁴th 1181.)*

The elements applied in *Cohen* were:

| Element | Court’s Application |
|---------|----------------------|
| **Actual/Injury‑in‑fact** | Cohen had been compensated for all unpaid overtime wages at trial‑time, yet he contended that because Consilio’s policy still existed he remained exposed to future wage violations that could be deterred by a penalty claim.* |
| **Direct injury** | The alleged continued policy directly threatened his hourly pay—an election from above Roche–Davis; salary protection was explicitly held in FLSA jurisprudence.* |
| **Caused by defendant’s conduct** | Consilio’s broad “base‑pay only” overtime policy was a concrete act.* |
| **Not speculative or conjectural** | Even after payment of wages and liquidated damages, Cohen argued non‑payment was possible unless policy was rescinded.* |

When these factors satisfied him on each specific relief sought (i.e., “statutory penalty”), *Friends of the Earth* let him proceed despite those penalties being payable into an agency account.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2. Continuity / Mootness

*Cohen* revived *Friends of the Earth*s rule against saying mortgages cannot be denied li after renouncement:
> “If subsequent events make it absolutely clear that allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably recur…a ‘defendant’s voluntary cessation…does not deprive a federal court of its power….”  
(*Friend*, 528 U.S. at 189.)

The remedy therefore hinges on whether an ongoing violation remains—a more factual inquiry than abstract injunctive prevention —and so:


* **If Consilio still employs its former overtime policy**, then _Cohen_ has continuing legal injury and can continue seeking statutory penalties.
* **If Consilio promptly eliminates or permanently alters its policy**, then Cohen’s specific claim – especially prima facie based on “potential future harm” – becomes moot and courts can dismiss it on mootness grounds.


--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3 What does this mean for ordinary employees?

* The statutory language **does not require employee plaintiffs** ("doctors," nurses, attorneys) to prove that any portion of their compensation would go directly into the treasury—they merely have to show they would have suffered actual unilateral loss if non‑payment continued.
  
* If an employer ceases violating but has pledged remedial steps (e.g., new overtime procedure), a private class member may still sue voluntarily because there remains ongoing interest—common underlying is preventing reoccurrence.
  
* Conversely, once both parties agree upon payment settlement and confirm permanent compliance, courts will—as Miller rightly pointed out—dismiss any pending penalty claim due to lack of “continuing legal interest.”

---

#### Bottom line

- **Yes**, employees may attempt recovery of **civil‑penalty money** even if it is programmed into a state/federal agency account – so long as (§1) they satisfy standing (direct injury + causation) AND (§2) there remains an ongoing/recurring infractions risk (“continuing interest”).  
- If either test fails—no genuine injury/undue risk remaining—the case collapses (vacated / dismissed).
- As illustrated by *Cohen*, denial or failure-to-satisfy underlying conditions is spent boilerplate — nobody requires you hold “personal interests” beyond your own wages because legislation designed penalty funds aimed at deterrence rather than individual compensation.

### 1.  What the opinion says

**A)   Statutory framework**

* The MFLSA penalizes “fraudulent payment practices” (§ 177‑27); a defendant who fails “to pay properly” may suffer “statutory penalties” when an “employee successfully proves an action.”
* Federal courts reject claims unless a plaintiff **shows standing** – i.e., **a concrete injury that will not disappear** (the court cites *Friends of Earth*).

**B)   Standing requirement**

> *“a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks…but…the requisite personal interest …must continue throughout its existence.”*  
> — Seventh Circuit quotation (“Town of Chester v Laroe Ests.” via *Friends of Earth*)

The opinion notes that *penalties under MFLSA are payable into a public account* (the commissioner’s), their purpose being deterrence rather than compensation for employees directly.  Because Cohen had already been **paid allowing overtime wages**—the very financial harm allegedly caused—his only remaining potential injury would stem from **future violations** (the continued existence of Consilio’s illegal overtime policy).

The court therefore treats **standing as central:**  
*If none exists—because the alleged conduct has ceased—the penalty claim cannot survive.*  

**C)   Moot‑ness analysis**

The opinion reminds us that courts do not “retain jurisdiction over cases … where one or both parties plainly lack a continuing interest” (*Friends of Earth* §189‑192).  
In particular:

> *“…if Consilio’s policy remains … Cohen’s claim for civil penalties may still be viable.”*  
> — Seventh Circuit discussion on §B‑4

> *“If Consilio’s policy is no longer in place … Cohen’s MFLSA claim … may actually be moot.”*  
> — same passage

Thus **ignorance over current employment practice stalls determination**—unless evidence shows either:
1. Consilio continues practising its unlawful overtime scheme (proofable by persistent non‑compliance), **or**
2. It has definitively terminated it and there’s no reasonable chance it would return near-term.

Only then can courts evaluate *whether Stephen can truly seek these statutory fines* or whether its mere admission by Coeeen that “he received all overtime wages” already eliminates any real‑dear‑added hurt.【I】

Hence the panel concluded:

> *“Because it could not yet decide whether Cohen had a continuing interest …the district court’s summary judgment was vacated.”*  
> – Seventh Circuit ruling section C
