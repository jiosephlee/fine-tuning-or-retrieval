Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Under *Friends of the Earth* a plaintiff may recover civil‑penalty money from a private defendant even if the penalty is paid into an agency account, provided

1. **Standing** – the plaintiff (or class) shows that it suffered an actual or imminent injury that is *directly* caused by the defendant’s conduct, **and that this injury could not have been avoided** by any other means (i.e., no more general “interest” test).  
2. **Mootness/continuing interest** – there must still be an ongoing or reasonably foreseeable violation that justifies asking a court to order deterrence (and/or crediting damages). If the defendant has already ceased violating the statute or follows with a strict compliance program, courts can dismiss on “no more continuing interest” grounds just as they do with *Davis* in *Concentrated Phosphate*.
3. When those two tests are satisfied, a Pennsylvania FLSA‑style statute that designates penalties payable to the commissioner does **not bar** recovery by employees because courts view such claims under *Friends of the Earth* much like a private right‑to‑damages claim—it is an instrument meant to deter future wrongdoing, not merely compensate personal loss.*

### How § 27(a) of the MFLSA interacts with *Friends of the Earth*

In *Cohen v. Consilio*, 8th Cir. 2025 (the opinion you supplied), there were three intervening deadlines:

| Claim | Summary‑judgment outcome | Reason |
|-------|------------------------|--------|
| MPWA | Denied | Penalties require a commissioner demand; employer cannot sue for them independently |
| MFLSA | Vacated & remanded | Addressed only where there is **continued potential conduct** or ongoing violations |
| MWTA | Dismissed | No statutory authority for civil penalties |

Because **all statutory penalties under MFLSA accrue into the Department of Labor’s general fund** (not directly into an employee’s pocket), courts treat them “inexactly” like administrative fines—recoverable by plaintiffs if their **standing** survives because they suffer a direct injury from continued improper practice.*  

The court made clear that employees need not “show personal interest” beyond suffering damage from unpaid wages—just that those wages are their property—and *was simply impossible* for a private plaintiff who only claims “statutory penalty” without demanding the wages themselves.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1. Standing under *Friends of the Earth*  

The court’s discussion echoes *Town of Chester v. Laroe*, 581 U.S. 433 (2017).  
> “A plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks…The requisite personal interest…must continue throughout his existence.”  
*(See also· Sisney v._Kaemingk_, 15 F⁴th 1181.)*

The elements applied in *Cohen* were:

| Element | Court’s Application |
|---------|----------------------|
| **Actual/Injury‑in‑fact** | Cohen had been compensated for all unpaid overtime wages at trial‑time, yet he contended that because Consilio’s policy still existed he remained exposed to future wage violations that could be deterred by a penalty claim.* |
| **Direct injury** | The alleged continued policy directly threatened his hourly pay—an election from above Roche–Davis; salary protection was explicitly held in FLSA jurisprudence.* |
| **Caused by defendant’s conduct** | Consilio’s broad “base‑pay only” overtime policy was a concrete act.* |
| **Not speculative or conjectural** | Even after payment of wages and liquidated damages, Cohen argued non‑payment was possible unless policy was rescinded.* |

When these factors satisfied him on each specific relief sought (i.e., “statutory penalty”), *Friends of the Earth* let him proceed despite those penalties being payable into an agency account.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2. Continuity / Mootness

*Cohen* revived *Friends of the Earth*s rule against saying mortgages cannot be denied li after renouncement:
> “If subsequent events make it absolutely clear that allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably recur…a ‘defendant’s voluntary cessation…does not deprive a federal court of its power….”  
(*Friend*, 528 U.S. at 189.)

The remedy therefore hinges on whether an ongoing violation remains—a more factual inquiry than abstract injunctive prevention —and so:


* **If Consilio still employs its former overtime policy**, then _Cohen_ has continuing legal injury and can continue seeking statutory penalties.
* **If Consilio promptly eliminates or permanently alters its policy**, then Cohen’s specific claim – especially prima facie based on “potential future harm” – becomes moot and courts can dismiss it on mootness grounds.


--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3 What does this mean for ordinary employees?

* The statutory language **does not require employee plaintiffs** ("doctors," nurses, attorneys) to prove that any portion of their compensation would go directly into the treasury—they merely have to show they would have suffered actual unilateral loss if non‑payment continued.
  
* If an employer ceases violating but has pledged remedial steps (e.g., new overtime procedure), a private class member may still sue voluntarily because there remains ongoing interest—common underlying is preventing reoccurrence.
  
* Conversely, once both parties agree upon payment settlement and confirm permanent compliance, courts will—as Miller rightly pointed out—dismiss any pending penalty claim due to lack of “continuing legal interest.”

---

#### Bottom line

- **Yes**, employees may attempt recovery of **civil‑penalty money** even if it is programmed into a state/federal agency account – so long as (§1) they satisfy standing (direct injury + causation) AND (§2) there remains an ongoing/recurring infractions risk (“continuing interest”).  
- If either test fails—no genuine injury/undue risk remaining—the case collapses (vacated / dismissed).
- As illustrated by *Cohen*, denial or failure-to-satisfy underlying conditions is spent boilerplate — nobody requires you hold “personal interests” beyond your own wages because legislation designed penalty funds aimed at deterrence rather than individual compensation.
