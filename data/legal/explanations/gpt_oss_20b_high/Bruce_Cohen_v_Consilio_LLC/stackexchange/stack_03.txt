**Bottom line:**  
Cohen’s civil‑penalty claim under § 177.27 (MFLSA) lacks *standing* unless he shows an ongoing threat that would give him a real‑dear‑added financial interest.  Even if he were entitled to seek such a penalty, *mootness* would still bar relief once it became obvious—or provably impossible—that Consilio’s unlawful overtime practice could recur.  The Seventh Circuit therefore vacated the district court’s grant of summary judgment on that claim and remanded only after it can determine whether a *continuing* injury exists (i.e., whether Consilio’s policy remains in force).

---

### 1.  What the opinion says

**A)   Statutory framework**

* The MFLSA penalizes “fraudulent payment practices” (§ 177‑27); a defendant who fails “to pay properly” may suffer “statutory penalties” when an “employee successfully proves an action.”
* Federal courts reject claims unless a plaintiff **shows standing** – i.e., **a concrete injury that will not disappear** (the court cites *Friends of Earth*).

**B)   Standing requirement**

> *“a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks…but…the requisite personal interest …must continue throughout its existence.”*  
> — Seventh Circuit quotation (“Town of Chester v Laroe Ests.” via *Friends of Earth*)

The opinion notes that *penalties under MFLSA are payable into a public account* (the commissioner’s), their purpose being deterrence rather than compensation for employees directly.  Because Cohen had already been **paid allowing overtime wages**—the very financial harm allegedly caused—his only remaining potential injury would stem from **future violations** (the continued existence of Consilio’s illegal overtime policy).

The court therefore treats **standing as central:**  
*If none exists—because the alleged conduct has ceased—the penalty claim cannot survive.*  

**C)   Moot‑ness analysis**

The opinion reminds us that courts do not “retain jurisdiction over cases … where one or both parties plainly lack a continuing interest” (*Friends of Earth* §189‑192).  
In particular:

> *“…if Consilio’s policy remains … Cohen’s claim for civil penalties may still be viable.”*  
> — Seventh Circuit discussion on §B‑4

> *“If Consilio’s policy is no longer in place … Cohen’s MFLSA claim … may actually be moot.”*  
> — same passage

Thus **ignorance over current employment practice stalls determination**—unless evidence shows either:
1. Consilio continues practising its unlawful overtime scheme (proofable by persistent non‑compliance), **or**
2. It has definitively terminated it and there’s no reasonable chance it would return near-term.

Only then can courts evaluate *whether Stephen can truly seek these statutory fines* or whether its mere admission by Coeeen that “he received all overtime wages” already eliminates any real‑dear‑added hurt.【I】

Hence the panel concluded:

> *“Because it could not yet decide whether Cohen had a continuing interest …the district court’s summary judgment was vacated.”*  
> – Seventh Circuit ruling section C

### 2.  Why it matters

| Issue | What possession means | What Judgments look like |
|-------|----------------------|--------------------------|
| **Standing** | Real, ongoing damage caused by present crooks | Without harmful injury today/future, courts dismiss |
| **Mootness** | Will future irritation stop inevitably? | If yes → case drops before you get ruled upon |

1. **Prove continued illegal behavior**

   • Record any internal communications confirming change was temporary
2. **Demonstrate your personal stake**  
   • Highlight actual time you’ve been paid extra due to current practice
   • Show future risk of non‑payment affecting you specifically

### TLDR

Lawyers MAY seek MFLSA penalties after payment equalization **only if** they still meet both requirements:
1️⃣ They possess *standing*—a continuing threat/harm,
2️⃣ That threat persists long enough that a *moot‑analysis* won’t hollow out their case,  

or else courts will simply dismiss such claims as unsupportable under Seventh‑Circuit precedent adopted in Pekely v.. For this particular case (Cohen v Consilio), short proof here kept petition chill until it reached either satisfied requirement; thus Kennedy vacated summary judgement pending analysis of viability/mootness【I】.
