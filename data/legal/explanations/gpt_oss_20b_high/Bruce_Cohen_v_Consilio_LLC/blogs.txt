# When Your Overtime Policy Doesn’t Even Email‑Themselves—MWTA Notice Requirements Under Scrutiny  

## Why This Case Matters

Merriam‑Webster’s definition of “policy” sometimes feels generous enough when you’re a large consultancy whose facilitation paperwork streams brighter than a late‑night fireworks show—but what happens when a company’s “policy” effectively vanishes *before* the mailbox spell‑checks it? In October 2025 the Eighth Circuit held that an employee who wanted compensation—specifically a statute‑imposed civil penalty—for failing “to give required notice” under Minnesota’s Wage Theft Act had insufficient ground.<sup>1</sup> 

At first glance this is a narrow technicality—a second‑tier provision often ignored by paralegals scribbling memos about overtime premiums—but **it signals a larger trend**. Employers are increasingly relying on terse electronic communications (“the team will start using X every month” emailed one shot) while workers look up statutory language online expecting those seven pounds up front indeed translate into enforceable remedies—even when Congress intentionally reserved enforcement powers with a specific agency rather than a personal lawsuit.<sup>2</sup> If courts are reading the statute hawk‑like and refusing plaintiff claims simply because they would have been paid out through an administrative trigger instead of a raw penalty awarded by a judge, businesses will find themselves operating in grey zones dangerous both financially and reputationally.</p>
