Answer:
**Short answer:**  
In *Coleman*, the Seventh Circuit found that Officer Kizer’s phrasing – “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside…” – did **not** give rise to coercive pressure sufficient to render Lisa’s consent involuntary.  The district court correctly held that her agreement was “free and voluntary,” and the ensuing search stayed within the scope granted by her consent.

---

### The legal framework

The courts look at *voluntariness* under a **total‑ity‑of‑circumstances** test (U.S. v. Han, 105 F.4th 986).  Relevant factors include:

1.  The person’s age/education/understanding  
2.  Whether he/she was advised of rights  
3.  How long he/she was detained before consenting  
4.  Whether consent was immediate or repeated  
5.  Whether any physical coercion or intimidation was used  
6.  Whether he/she was already in custody

(See U.S. v. Figueroa‑Espana 511 F.3d 705.)

If none of these factors point toward a lack of voluntariness, a court will affirm that consent was obtained freely (Bumper v. North Carolina).

### How those factors played out in *Coleman*

| Factor | *Coleman* findings |
|--------|--------------------|
| **Age/education/understanding** | None mentioned; absence of suggestion of demented capacity |
| **Advised rights** | Not required for a one‑time consent at an opening door |
| **Detention time** | No detention – police merely spoke to her on the porch |
| **Immediate vs repeated requests** | Two polite requests preceded her assent; no repeated urging after the second “okay” |
| **Physical coercion** | None – Officer Kizer is described as calmly asking for permission |
| **Custody** | Lisa was not in custody | 

In § 2B–C the court writes:

> “From the time Officer Kizer arrived at the Coleman residence … he spoke calmly and respectfully… At no point was Lisa in custody… While Officer Kizer did ask twice for permission… Their entire conversation lasted only a few minutes… Susan [sic] may have felt some degree of pressure … at no point did Officer Kizer exert coercion.”  

and later,

> “…“OKAY…I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside…” does not make his request coercive …”

The additional statutory language—“consent must be freely and voluntarily” (Bumper)—supports this reading: it is not simply about overt force but about apparent pressure imposed on an otherwise freely acting individual.

### Does “almost need” create coercive pressure?

The volitional decision rests on how *both* judge and *reasonable witness* would interpret officer‑speech without any additional contextual clues suggesting duress or threat.  
In this case, the officer’s words were embedded in a context where:

* He had already been questioned several times,
* She had just declined entry twice,
* He then offered an explanation (“to make sure the kids are okay”) and
* She answered “OKAY” after hearing those words.

The court thereby concluded:

> “…the district court could reasonably find that Officer Kizer’s use of the word ‘need’ did not make his request coercive…”.

Because none of the total‑ity factors point toward an absence of voluntariness—even with Reviewer View somewhat harsh‑voices such as ‘need’—the consistent reading is that Lisa chose whether or not to allow entry without undue pressure.

### Scope of consent

Once entry was accorded, officers stayed within the limits set by Lisa’s assertion (“to make sure the children are okay,” etc.).  They did not exceed those limits setting aside their subsequent warrant search—in line with objective reasonableness (Saucedo); see § 3C above.

---

## Bottom line

In *United States v. Jaison Coleman*, Ninth Circuit upheld that Officer Kizer’s phrasing (“we almost need”) introduced **no** conduct that constituted coercive pressure under the total‑ity test governing voluntary consent under § 1983 / Fourth Amendment analysis.  Thus his request did not invalidate Lisa’s consent, and there is no basis for suppression on those grounds with respect to entry and subsequent search inside the home.
