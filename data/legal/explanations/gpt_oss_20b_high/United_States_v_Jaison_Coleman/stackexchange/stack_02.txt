Answer:
**Answer**

The Fifth‑Amendment check is straightforward once you remember what Bumper *v.* North Carolina required:

> “Consent must be *freely* and *voluntarily* given.”  
> **Bumper**, 391 U.S. 543 (1968)

The Supreme Court explained that “freedom” is determined by looking at the **totality** of the circumstances at the time the person gives her assent (*Bumper*, 548).  
The Seventh Circuit has spelled out the concrete factors an appellate panel will weigh when it reviews a lower‑court finding of voluntariness (*United States v.* Jones*, 22 F.4th 667–76):

| Factor | What it looks for | What appeared in *Coleman* |
|--------|-------------------|---------------------------|
| **Person’s age / mental capacity** | Ability to understand rights and consequences | No indication that Lisa was a minor or otherwise incapacitated |
| **Intelligence / education** | Whether she could comprehend authority’s offer | Body‑cam footage shows her speaking clearly and responding decisively |
| **Counsel / constitutional rights‑advice** | Advisement can indicate comprehension | No mention that counsel was offered |
| **Length of detention before consent** | Prolonged detention can suggest pressure | Lisa was outside, moving back and forth; only ~3 min between refusal and acceptance |
| **Repetition / persistence by police** | Repeated requests can be coercive unless done calmly | Officer Kizer asked twice, once hesitant (“I’m sensing…”) then again after Lisa withdrew; no threat language |
| **Physical coercion** | Any forceful touch or intimidation | None shown on film |
| **Custody status** | Being in custody can negate voluntariness | Not in police custody at any point |

### How those facts feed into the “totality” analysis
1. **Capacity**  
   The videos show Lisa articulate her own position (“I’d prefer you not”), indicating she had full mental ability to refuse—fulfilling *Bumper*’s requirement that consent be given only if capable of “freely deciding.”

2. **No intimidation**  
   Officer Kizer’s tone—“I’m sensing…”—was cautious rather than aggressive (“our conversation began calmly...”).  His repeated requests followed an apparent decline of cooperation when Lisa initially refused (“Um…I’d prefer you not”).  The *Coleman* opinion stresses explicitly that “...there was no physical coercion or force used.”  

3. **Short detention/time frame**  
   Videos show less than three minutes elapsed between denial and eventual assent (“a break in conversation followed… three minutes passed before Officer Kizer re‑asked”).  Such brevity does not reach the length required for duress claims (*Han*, 105 F.4th 986).

Because every factor either shows freedom from physical danger or involves comfortable communication consistent with an open dialogue rather than intimidation, the Ninth‑Circuit appellate panel correctly found no error in its lower‑court determination that Lisa’s go‑ahead constituted proper volition (*Jones*, 22 F.4th at 676–77).  

### Key takeaways for students
* Apply Bumper’s “totality” criterion systematically—a checklistway.
* For each factor look at evidence embedded in video or testimony—not only explicit language.
* Even if consent was yielded after repeated asks,
  *the mere factuality isn’t enough*: judge optionally weighs whether requests were made while imposing any real psychological pressure.
* Courts are far more likely to reject voluntariness when one sees:
  - evident duress;
  - physical force;
  - long detention;
