Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 2: # Intrinsic Firearm-Possession Evidence vs. Rule 404(b)

This chapter explains why testimony that witnesses had observed the same sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle before the night of the charged offense was admissible at Justin Cutbank’s trial. The district court treated that testimony as intrinsic evidence probative of continuous possession and control of the weapon rather than as extrinsic “other-acts” evidence barred by Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The Eighth Circuit affirmed, applying settled law about when prior possession of the same weapon is relevant to later possession and reviewing the district court’s decision only for abuse of discretion.

## Defense motion in limine to exclude D.F. and C.F. testimony about prior sightings of the rifle

Before trial, Cutbank filed a motion to bar testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had seen him with the sawed-off rifle on prior occasions. The defense framed that testimony as impermissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b): evidence of other bad acts offered to show the defendant’s character and thus his likelihood to commit the charged offense. The motion sought to prevent the government from eliciting these prior-sighting statements at trial.

A motion in limine like this requires the court to decide whether the contested evidence is properly characterized as 404(b) “other acts” evidence (which is subject to exclusion unless it meets the Rule 404(b) exceptions) or instead is intrinsic to the charged crime and therefore admissible without invoking Rule 404(b) constraints. The district court concluded the witnesses’ prior sightings were intrinsic to the possession charge because they helped establish ownership or control of the very gun that was later found and linked to Cutbank.

## District court’s ruling: evidence is intrinsic and probative of continuous possession and control

The district court accepted the government’s argument that testimony that D.F. and C.F. had seen Cutbank with the specific sawed-off rifle on prior occasions was intrinsic evidence. The court reasoned that prior possession of the same firearm goes directly to elements and factual predicates of the charged offense—specifically, to proving that Cutbank possessed (owned or controlled) the weapon on the night of the offense and that the possession was not a one-off or mistaken identity. Because the testimony concerned the same instrumentality (the same sawed-off rifle) and bore directly on identity, possession, and continuity of control, the court allowed the witnesses to testify about their prior observations.

That ruling turns on the functional role of the evidence at trial. When evidence of earlier conduct is offered not to prove character or propensity but to explain the relationship among people, show the chain of events, establish identity of a weapon or object, or demonstrate continuous dominion or control over the very instrumentality at issue, courts routinely treat it as intrinsic and admissible. The district court applied that principle here and admitted the testimony subject to normal limits (e.g., relevance and Rule 403 balancing).

## Eighth Circuit authority: United States v. Battle—prior possession of the same weapon is directly relevant to later possession

The Eighth Circuit relied on United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014), to explain and support the district court’s ruling. Battle holds that prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of the same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control. That precedent draws the critical distinction between prior-act evidence that merely shows a defendant’s propensity to commit crimes (which Rule 404(b) generally excludes) and prior possession of the identical or clearly identifiable weapon, which tends to prove a material fact—identity, ownership, or continuous dominion—central to the prosecution’s case.

In short, Battle and similar Eighth Circuit decisions create a predictable pathway: testimony that a witness previously observed the defendant with the same gun is admissible to show that the gun found later belonged to or was controlled by the defendant, rather than being treated as impermissible character evidence.

## Trial testimony: D.F. saw rifle a “handful of times”; C.F. saw it “once or twice” before

At trial, both D.F. and C.F. testified consistently with the district court’s pretrial ruling. D.F. said she had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle a “handful of times” prior to the night of the incident. C.F., when called as a government witness, testified she had seen the gun used before “once or twice.” The government also introduced physical and forensic evidence linking the rifle to Cutbank (e.g., DNA on the gun and the jury’s finding of actual possession). Those corroborating facts reduced any risk that admitting the witnesses’ prior-sighting testimony would produce unfair prejudice or otherwise mislead the jury.

Cutbank renewed objections at trial, but the district court again allowed the testimony based on the same intrinsic-evidence rationale. The appellate court noted that Cutbank did not present contrary authority undermining the pretrial ruling and therefore affirmed admission under the applicable standards.

## Standard of review: abuse of discretion; affirmance of admission as intrinsic evidence

The Eighth Circuit reviewed the district court’s evidentiary decision for abuse of discretion, the standard applied to most trial-court rulings about admissibility. Under that deferential standard, the appellate court will reverse only if the district court’s decision was arbitrary, unreasonable, or based on an erroneous view of the law.

Applying this standard, the court found no abuse of discretion. The prior-sighting testimony was intrinsic because it directly tended to prove possession, control, or ownership of the gun later connected to Cutbank. Battle provided controlling guidance, the testimony was corroborated by physical and forensic evidence, and no significant Rule 403 unfair prejudice appeared. Even if some marginal error had occurred, the court noted the evidence against Cutbank was substantial and any evidentiary rulings were harmless.

Takeaway: when the same weapon is at issue, testimony that the defendant was seen with that weapon before the charged offense is often admissible as intrinsic evidence relevant to identity, ownership, and continuous possession. District courts have wide discretion in making that call, and appellate review will sustain the decision so long as it rests on a reasonable application of legal principles like those articulated in Battle and the evidence is not unduly prejudicial under Rule 403.
