Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 5: # Sentencing: § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Enhancement, ACCA Interaction, and Reasonableness Review

This section explains the Eighth Circuit’s treatment of the four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm “in connection with” another felony, how that enhancement interacts with the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) guideline calculations, and why the court affirmed Cutbank’s within-Guidelines sentence as both procedurally and substantively sound.

## PSR calculations and the contested enhancement

The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) supplied the factual and numerical foundation for sentencing. The PSR assigned a base offense level of 26 because the firearm at issue was a sawed-off weapon (triggering the statutory definition in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a)) and because Cutbank had at least two prior crimes of violence. The PSR then recommended additional adjustments: a +3 increase for an altered or obliterated serial number, +2 for recklessly creating a substantial risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing law enforcement, and +4 under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing the firearm “in connection with” another felony offense. Those adjustments produced a total offense level of 35. With a Criminal History Category of VI, the advisory Guidelines range was 292–365 months.

Cutbank challenged the application of the +4 enhancement on appeal, arguing that its inclusion produced a sentence inconsistent with the § 3553(a) parsimony principle. The court’s evaluation begins with whether the enhancement was properly applied under the Guidelines.

## Application Note 14(C) and the meaning of “in connection with”

The Sentencing Guidelines commentary relevant here—Application Note 14(C) to § 2K2.1—states that the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement applies where a firearm was used or possessed “in connection with” another felony, and that this enhancement may apply “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” The court relied on that text: the guideline does not require a separate charge or conviction for the other felony conduct so long as the conduct supporting the enhancement is proven at sentencing by the applicable standard.

In Cutbank’s case, the PSR described facts tying the firearm possession to other violent conduct (the home confinement, threats, and flight that led to the later encounter with police), which the district court adopted at sentencing. Under the Guideline language and commentary, the factual showing in the PSR was sufficient to support the +4 enhancement absent a successful objection.

## Adoption of PSR facts; review for clear error

A key procedural point is that the district court adopted the factual statements in the PSR without objection. When a district court adopts PSR facts at sentencing and no objection is made, the appellate standard of review is deferential: factual findings are reviewed for clear error. The opinion cites United States v. Dunn for that standard. Because the district court incorporated the PSR and Cutbank did not object to those facts at the time, the Eighth Circuit found no clear error in the underlying factual determinations that supported the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement.

The consequence is practical: when the PSR is adopted and unchallenged, the appellate court will not overturn a district court’s factual findings unless they are clearly erroneous. Cutbank’s failure to preserve meaningful factual objections at sentencing made it difficult to prevail on an appellate challenge to the enhancement.

## Interaction with ACCA and USSG § 4B1.4(b)

Cutbank also pointed out that the jury’s ACCA finding (three or more prior violent felonies) would, under the career-offender/ACCA framework, lead to an offense level calculation of 34 rather than 35. The Guidelines themselves resolve this kind of conflict: USSG § 4B1.4(b) provides that, where two Guidelines provisions yield different offense levels, the greater offense level controls. The court applied that rule and explained that the existence of a slightly lower offense level under the ACCA-related calculation does not render the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement improper; the Guidelines direct sentencing courts to use the higher applicable offense level.

Thus, the interplay between § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) and the ACCA-related calculation did not require striking the enhancement—under the Guidelines’ own tie-breaking rule, the higher level remained controlling.

## Procedural obligations before substantive review: Gall and Rosales‑Mireles

Before a court of appeals may evaluate the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, it must ensure the district court committed no significant procedural error—most centrally, that the court correctly calculated the Guidelines range. The opinion rests this point on Gall v. United States (district courts must correctly calculate the Guidelines range and consider the Guidelines) and Rosales‑Mireles v. United States (an appellate court must confirm no significant procedural error before reviewing substantive reasonableness). Because the district court adopted the PSR and applied the Guidelines adjustments, the Eighth Circuit found no procedural error in the calculation of the advisory range.

The implication for defendants is practical: unsuccessful or unpreserved challenges to PSR facts make it difficult to show that a district court miscalculated the Guidelines range in a way that would trigger reversal or remand for resentencing.

## Substantive-reasonableness review and the presumption for within‑Guidelines sentences

Once procedural correctness is established, the appellate court assesses substantive reasonableness. The Eighth Circuit applied its precedent that a within-Guidelines sentence is presumed reasonable on appeal (citing Goodhouse). The court also observed that the district court explicitly reviewed the PSR, considered sentencing memoranda, and discussed § 3553(a) factors that could justify a variance. The court found those steps sufficient to show the district court considered the relevant statutory factors; it is not required to recite each factor mechanically so long as the record makes clear that the court considered them (citing Masood and Maluoth).

Cutbank claimed the +4 enhancement produced a sentence greater than necessary under § 3553(a). The appellate court rejected that argument because the district court (1) correctly calculated the Guidelines range, (2) was aware of and considered the defendant’s arguments and the § 3553(a) factors, and (3) imposed a within-Guidelines sentence. Given the presumption of reasonableness for within-Guidelines sentences and the factual record, the court concluded the sentence was not substantively unreasonable.

## Aggravating facts supporting the enhancement and the within‑Guidelines sentence

The opinion emphasizes the factual context that supports both the enhancement and the ultimate sentence. Cutbank had an extensive criminal history—nearly a dozen felony convictions, many violent and firearm-related. The charged conduct involved effectively imprisoning his girlfriend at gunpoint in her home in front of her children, threats, physical assault with the gun, flight, and injury to an officer during resistance. Those facts supply the kind of aggravating conduct that the Guidelines and a district court may permissibly consider when deciding whether a within-Guidelines sentence is appropriate and when applying enhancements tied to related felonious behavior.

The court pointed out that application of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) was consistent with the PSR’s factual description of how the firearm was used in connection with the other felony conduct (the domestic-violence-related restraint and subsequent flight), and therefore not arbitrary or unreasonable.

## Holding: no procedural error, no abuse of discretion, sentence affirmed

Bringing these threads together, the Eighth Circuit held that (1) the district court did not commit procedural error in applying the +4 enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), because the PSR facts supported the enhancement and the court adopted those facts without objection; (2) the Guidelines’ commentary permits application of the enhancement even without a separate charge or conviction for the related felony; (3) any apparent tension between the enhancement and the ACCA-related offense level is resolved by the Guidelines’ rule to apply the greater offense level; and (4) because the district court correctly calculated the Guidelines and considered § 3553(a) factors, the within-Guidelines 292-month sentence was presumptively reasonable and was not substantively unreasonable given Cutbank’s violent history and the circumstances of the offense. The judgment and sentence were therefore affirmed.

Practical takeaway for law students: a sentencing challenge to a Guidelines enhancement often turns on (a) whether the district court’s factual findings were preserved and adequately supported by the PSR or other evidence, (b) the specific language of the relevant Guidelines and their commentary (here, Application Note 14(C)), and (c) whether the district court correctly calculated the range and adequately considered § 3553(a). Failure to object to PSR facts or to preserve a detailed factual record at sentencing significantly weakens appellate review.
