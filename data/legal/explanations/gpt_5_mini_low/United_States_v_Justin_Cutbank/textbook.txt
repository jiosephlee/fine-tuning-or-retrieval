Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Case Overview and Procedural Posture

This opinion arises from a violent, late‑night confrontation and the federal prosecution that followed. The chapter summarizes the key facts from the district‑court trial, the federal charges, the jury’s findings, the sentencing calculation and result, and the discrete issues Cutbank raised on appeal. These elements set the factual and procedural frame the Eighth Circuit used in reviewing the district court’s evidentiary rulings and the challenged sentencing enhancement.

November 19–20, 2020 incident
On the night of November 19–20, 2020, Justin Cutbank, who had been drinking and using methamphetamine, awakened his girlfriend, D.F., and pointed a firearm at her face. According to D.F.’s trial testimony, Cutbank ordered her into a closet, made paranoid claims about “listening devices” and people waiting to harm him, threatened to kill her, and struck her with the gun. He also ordered D.F. to wake other occupants of the residence, including her two teenage sons and a housemate, C.F. After detaining D.F. at gunpoint for what she described as hours, Cutbank fled the home with D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm. D.F. contacted police after he left.

Flight and standoff with police
Shortly afterward, the police received a separate call from a residence about a man refusing to leave a garage about a half mile away. When officers arrived, Cutbank barricaded himself in the garage and over several hours made erratic statements, claimed to possess a weapon, and resisted attempts to detain him. During the struggle an officer was bitten; Cutbank also grabbed an officer’s gun during the altercation. The sequence of flight, barricading, and physical resistance formed an important factual backdrop at trial and at sentencing.

Recovery of physical evidence and identifications
Police investigators searched the most likely route between D.F.’s house and the garage and recovered several items: D.F.’s cell phone, a butcher‑style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle that had a sawed‑off barrel and stock. A DNA analysis linked material on the rifle to Cutbank. At trial, both D.F. and C.F. identified that same sawed‑off Marlin rifle as the weapon they had observed Cutbank with prior to the incident. D.F. testified she had seen the rifle a “handful of times” before the night of the offense; C.F. indicated she had seen it “once or twice” before. Those identifications and the DNA match were among the government’s core pieces of proof tying Cutbank to possession of the firearm.

Federal charges and statutory framework
Cutbank was charged in federal court with being a felon in possession of a firearm, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). The indictment also alleged that he had at least three prior violent‑felony convictions, which, if found by the jury, would invoke the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e), exposing him to an enhanced statutory penalty range. The ACCA increases the statutory minimum and maximum penalties for a § 922(g) conviction when the defendant has three prior convictions for violent felonies or serious drug offenses.

Trial, verdicts, and ACCA finding
A jury convicted Cutbank of the charged felon‑in‑possession offense. The jury also found the separate allegation that Cutbank had been previously convicted of three or more violent felonies, a finding that triggered the ACCA’s enhanced statutory penalties. The ACCA finding was outcome‑determinative of the statutory regime that governed sentencing.

Sentencing calculation and the imposed sentence
At sentencing the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) recommended application of several guideline adjustments that together produced a total offense level of 35 and a Criminal History Category (CHC) of VI. The PSR’s calculation began with a base offense level of 26 because the firearm here was a sawed‑off rifle (an NFA firearm under 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a)). The PSR then included enhancements: a three‑level increase for an altered or obliterated serial number; a two‑level increase for recklessly creating a substantial risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing from police; and a four‑level increase under U.S.S.G. § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm “in connection with another felony offense.” The resulting guidelines range for offense level 35, CHC VI, was 292 to 365 months’ imprisonment. The district court adopted the PSR’s factual findings, calculated the guideline range accordingly, and imposed a within‑range sentence of 292 months’ imprisonment.

Issues raised on appeal
Cutbank appealed both his conviction and his sentence. On the evidentiary and procedural side, he argued that multiple district‑court rulings before and during trial—primarily admitting testimony that witnesses had seen him with the sawed‑off rifle before the charged incident, the district court’s handling of alleged hearsay and Confrontation Clause concerns, certain testimony about D.F.’s sister’s death by a gun, and limitations on reading portions of a police report during impeachment—deprived him of a fair trial. The Eighth Circuit treated these contentions together, noting that Cutbank offered little legal analysis and that the district court’s rulings fell within its broad evidentiary discretion. The court emphasized that prior possession evidence is intrinsic and directly relevant to proving later possession and that the Confrontation Clause was satisfied once declarants testified at trial.

On sentencing, Cutbank specifically challenged the district court’s application of the four‑level enhancement under U.S.S.G. § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which applies when a firearm is possessed in connection with another felony offense (and applies “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained,” per the guideline commentary). Cutbank argued the enhancement produced an advisory Guideline sentence inconsistent with the § 3553(a) parsimony principle that a sentence be “sufficient, but not greater than necessary.” The district court had adopted the PSR factual statements and considered § 3553(a) factors before imposing a within‑range sentence. The Eighth Circuit affirmed, applying the standard presumptions about within‑Guidelines sentences and recognizing that the district court had considered the statutory sentencing factors.

How the posture frames the court’s review
The factual findings and the sequence of pretrial rulings are important because they shaped the appellate standard of review and the remedies available. Evidentiary rulings made by the district court are reviewed for abuse of discretion; the Eighth Circuit repeatedly framed Cutbank’s evidentiary claims within that deferential standard and applied harmless‑error principles where applicable. Sentencing calculations, when adopted from the PSR and unobjected to at sentencing, carry a strong presumption of correctness and are reviewed for clear error at the factual level and for abuse of discretion at the sentence level; the court thus focused on whether the district court properly calculated the Guidelines range and reasonably considered § 3553(a) factors before imposing a within‑range sentence. The conviction and sentence were therefore evaluated in light of (1) the substantial physical and testimonial evidence tying Cutbank to the sawed‑off rifle and the flight/confrontation conduct, (2) the district court’s evidentiary rulings admitting prior‑possession testimony as intrinsic, and (3) the court’s sentencing calculus that incorporated the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement and the ACCA framework. The Eighth Circuit concluded that the district court acted within its discretion on both evidentiary and sentencing matters and affirmed.

# Intrinsic Firearm-Possession Evidence vs. Rule 404(b)

This chapter explains why testimony that witnesses had observed the same sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle before the night of the charged offense was admissible at Justin Cutbank’s trial. The district court treated that testimony as intrinsic evidence probative of continuous possession and control of the weapon rather than as extrinsic “other-acts” evidence barred by Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The Eighth Circuit affirmed, applying settled law about when prior possession of the same weapon is relevant to later possession and reviewing the district court’s decision only for abuse of discretion.

## Defense motion in limine to exclude D.F. and C.F. testimony about prior sightings of the rifle

Before trial, Cutbank filed a motion to bar testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had seen him with the sawed-off rifle on prior occasions. The defense framed that testimony as impermissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b): evidence of other bad acts offered to show the defendant’s character and thus his likelihood to commit the charged offense. The motion sought to prevent the government from eliciting these prior-sighting statements at trial.

A motion in limine like this requires the court to decide whether the contested evidence is properly characterized as 404(b) “other acts” evidence (which is subject to exclusion unless it meets the Rule 404(b) exceptions) or instead is intrinsic to the charged crime and therefore admissible without invoking Rule 404(b) constraints. The district court concluded the witnesses’ prior sightings were intrinsic to the possession charge because they helped establish ownership or control of the very gun that was later found and linked to Cutbank.

## District court’s ruling: evidence is intrinsic and probative of continuous possession and control

The district court accepted the government’s argument that testimony that D.F. and C.F. had seen Cutbank with the specific sawed-off rifle on prior occasions was intrinsic evidence. The court reasoned that prior possession of the same firearm goes directly to elements and factual predicates of the charged offense—specifically, to proving that Cutbank possessed (owned or controlled) the weapon on the night of the offense and that the possession was not a one-off or mistaken identity. Because the testimony concerned the same instrumentality (the same sawed-off rifle) and bore directly on identity, possession, and continuity of control, the court allowed the witnesses to testify about their prior observations.

That ruling turns on the functional role of the evidence at trial. When evidence of earlier conduct is offered not to prove character or propensity but to explain the relationship among people, show the chain of events, establish identity of a weapon or object, or demonstrate continuous dominion or control over the very instrumentality at issue, courts routinely treat it as intrinsic and admissible. The district court applied that principle here and admitted the testimony subject to normal limits (e.g., relevance and Rule 403 balancing).

## Eighth Circuit authority: United States v. Battle—prior possession of the same weapon is directly relevant to later possession

The Eighth Circuit relied on United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014), to explain and support the district court’s ruling. Battle holds that prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of the same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control. That precedent draws the critical distinction between prior-act evidence that merely shows a defendant’s propensity to commit crimes (which Rule 404(b) generally excludes) and prior possession of the identical or clearly identifiable weapon, which tends to prove a material fact—identity, ownership, or continuous dominion—central to the prosecution’s case.

In short, Battle and similar Eighth Circuit decisions create a predictable pathway: testimony that a witness previously observed the defendant with the same gun is admissible to show that the gun found later belonged to or was controlled by the defendant, rather than being treated as impermissible character evidence.

## Trial testimony: D.F. saw rifle a “handful of times”; C.F. saw it “once or twice” before

At trial, both D.F. and C.F. testified consistently with the district court’s pretrial ruling. D.F. said she had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle a “handful of times” prior to the night of the incident. C.F., when called as a government witness, testified she had seen the gun used before “once or twice.” The government also introduced physical and forensic evidence linking the rifle to Cutbank (e.g., DNA on the gun and the jury’s finding of actual possession). Those corroborating facts reduced any risk that admitting the witnesses’ prior-sighting testimony would produce unfair prejudice or otherwise mislead the jury.

Cutbank renewed objections at trial, but the district court again allowed the testimony based on the same intrinsic-evidence rationale. The appellate court noted that Cutbank did not present contrary authority undermining the pretrial ruling and therefore affirmed admission under the applicable standards.

## Standard of review: abuse of discretion; affirmance of admission as intrinsic evidence

The Eighth Circuit reviewed the district court’s evidentiary decision for abuse of discretion, the standard applied to most trial-court rulings about admissibility. Under that deferential standard, the appellate court will reverse only if the district court’s decision was arbitrary, unreasonable, or based on an erroneous view of the law.

Applying this standard, the court found no abuse of discretion. The prior-sighting testimony was intrinsic because it directly tended to prove possession, control, or ownership of the gun later connected to Cutbank. Battle provided controlling guidance, the testimony was corroborated by physical and forensic evidence, and no significant Rule 403 unfair prejudice appeared. Even if some marginal error had occurred, the court noted the evidence against Cutbank was substantial and any evidentiary rulings were harmless.

Takeaway: when the same weapon is at issue, testimony that the defendant was seen with that weapon before the charged offense is often admissible as intrinsic evidence relevant to identity, ownership, and continuous possession. District courts have wide discretion in making that call, and appellate review will sustain the decision so long as it rests on a reasonable application of legal principles like those articulated in Battle and the evidence is not unduly prejudicial under Rule 403.

# Hearsay, Confrontation, and Limiting Instructions

This section examines Cutbank’s request that the district court give a pretrial, prophylactic jury instruction telling jurors they could not consider D.F.’s out-of-court statements to police as substantive evidence until D.F. herself testified. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s refusal, and the court’s reasoning turns on (1) the nature and admissibility of the statements actually introduced at trial, (2) the proper role and timing of limiting instructions, and (3) Confrontation Clause principles when the declarant later testifies.

Defense request and government response. Before trial, Cutbank sought a preemptive limiting instruction aimed at avoiding both hearsay problems and Confrontation Clause implications from D.F.’s statements to police. The government opposed such an instruction on two principal grounds: first, it said the officers’ testimony about D.F.’s statements would be offered not as substantive proof of the facts asserted but to explain the officers’ conduct and state of mind (for example, why officers went to particular locations or took certain precautions); second, the government noted that if any issue arose at trial, a bench instruction at the close of evidence would be the customary way to address jury confusion. In short, the government argued that a prophylactic, front‑end jury instruction was unnecessary and that trial practice allowed for correction later if needed.

Legal standards governing limiting instructions and review. The appellate court reviewed the district court’s decision under the familiar abuse‑of‑discretion standard. The court relied on United States v. Bennett, which clarifies that a district court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted” and that courts may give an appropriate instruction at the close of trial rather than contemporaneously with each piece of evidence. This standard reflects practical trial management: judges have discretion about when and whether a limiting instruction is necessary, depending on how the evidence actually comes in and whether jurors might be misled.

What the record showed. Two facts from the record were decisive. First, the court determined that no hearsay was improperly admitted during trial. The government’s anticipated use of the officers’ testimony as context for their actions did not, in the district court’s view, convert those statements into substantive hearsay. Second, and independently dispositive for Confrontation Clause concerns, D.F. later testified live at trial. Because she appeared and was subject to cross‑examination, any testimonial statements she had made to police were not excluded under the Confrontation Clause when the jury also heard her live testimony.

Confrontation Clause principle. The court relied on United States v. Charboneau for the proposition that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” In other words, an out‑of‑court, potentially testimonial statement does not raise a Confrontation Clause bar if the declarant later testifies at trial and is available for cross‑examination about those statements. That principle removes the categorical need for a pretrial limiting instruction designed to prevent jurors from treating earlier police testimony as substantive proof when the declarant later takes the stand.

Why denial was not an abuse of discretion. Applying these principles, the Eighth Circuit concluded the district court did not abuse its discretion in declining the requested preemptive instruction. The court emphasized that the district court need not give contemporaneous limiting instructions for each item of evidence and that it may wait to see how evidence is used in context. Here, there was no improper hearsay admitted, and the Confrontation Clause concerns were resolved by D.F.’s in‑court testimony. Under Bennett and related precedent, the district court’s managerial choice about instructions—declining a pretrial limiting instruction and addressing any potential confusion later—fell well within its discretionary authority.

Takeaway for reading opinions. When a defendant seeks a pretrial limiting instruction about out‑of‑court statements, focus on three things the appellate court will examine: (1) how the statements were actually used at trial (substantive evidence versus context for police conduct), (2) whether the declarant testified at trial (which largely resolves Confrontation Clause objections), and (3) whether the trial court gave appropriate instructions at an appropriate time (with courts having discretion to give a single, comprehensive instruction at the close of evidence rather than repeated contemporaneous admonitions). The Cutbank opinion illustrates that denial of a prophylactic instruction is not reversible error when those conditions are met.

# Cross-Examination Boundaries, Rule 403, Authentication, and Harmless Error

This section explains the Eighth Circuit’s treatment of several interrelated evidentiary issues that arose during cross-examination at Justin Cutbank’s trial: (1) a witness’s on-the-stand explanation for prior inconsistent statements that referenced personal trauma, (2) limits on using a police report to impeach a witness, (3) the authentication requirement for extrinsic impeachment evidence, and (4) the appellate harmless-error and cumulative-error principles the court applied in assessing any asserted trial errors. The court affirmed the district court’s rulings, emphasizing the district court’s broad discretion and the overwhelming strength of the government’s case.

D.F.’s explanation for inconsistent statements
During cross-examination the defense confronted D.F. with prior inconsistent statements about whether she had seen the gun that night. In response she explained that some inconsistencies arose because she was “not familiar with guns” and that she “did not like guns . . . [a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun.” Cutbank moved to strike the portion of this testimony that mentioned the sister’s death. The district court denied the motion.

The Eighth Circuit approved that ruling. Two principles inform the court’s analysis. First, a witness may be asked about—and may explain—prior inconsistent statements. When a witness offers such an explanation on direct or cross-examination, it is testimony responsive to the questioning and not automatically barred simply because the explanation touches on a sensitive matter. Second, the district court must police prejudicial or confusing testimony under Federal Rule of Evidence 403, but that rule requires a showing that probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice, confusion of the issues, or misleading the jury. The appellate court found no showing of unfair prejudice or confusion here. The mention of the sister’s death was directly responsive to the defense’s impeachment attempts and helped explain why D.F.’s prior statements might have been inconsistent; it therefore had probative value and did not warrant exclusion under Rule 403.

The court also observed that C.F., another government witness, mentioned the sister’s death without a defense objection. That fact weighs against the claim of prejudice: an unobjected reference by a government witness signals the topic was not so inflammatory as to require exclusion and reinforces the district court’s broad evidentiary discretion.

Limits on using police reports to impeach; authentication requirement
The defense also sought to impeach C.F. by using statements she had made to a police investigator. The district court allowed defense counsel to ask C.F. about what she told the police (oral or recall-based questioning), but it sustained government objections when defense counsel attempted to read portions of the police report into evidence without foundation. The court required proper authentication before admitting portions of the report as extrinsic impeachment evidence.

The appellate court upheld this ruling and explained why. Federal impeachment law permits extrinsic evidence—including prior inconsistent statements—to be used to impeach a witness, but that extrinsic evidence must be authenticated. United States v. Cumbie, cited by the panel, states that extrinsic impeachment evidence requires a foundational showing linking the evidence to the witness (authentication) before it may be introduced. A police report is often a document prepared by an investigator and may recount a witness’s statements, but the proponent must lay a foundation that the portion read into evidence actually reflects the witness’s statement (for example, by showing the witness made or adopted the statement or by other means of authentication). Absent that foundation, the court did not abuse its discretion by barring reading the report into the record.

The opinion also notes (in somewhat admonitory language) that the government’s briefing expanded the issues on appeal by suggesting the court had “thwarted” cross-examination; the appellate panel declined to entertain unnecessary expansions of the record but resolved the authentication and foundation question on the merits in the government’s favor.

Rule 403 and district court discretion
Underlying both the decision to allow the witnesses’ explanatory testimony and the decision to require authentication for the police report is the principle that district courts enjoy broad discretion in evidentiary rulings. The Supreme Court’s decision in Sprint/United Management Co. v. Mendelsohn, cited by the panel, underscores that appellate review of such rulings is deferential. A district court balancing probative value against unfair prejudice under Rule 403 is not required to strike marginally probative testimony; it need only exclude evidence where prejudice substantially outweighs probative value. The Eighth Circuit concluded no such imbalance existed here.

Harmless error and cumulative-error standards
Even if an evidentiary ruling is erroneous, reversal is not automatic. The court applied the familiar harmless-error framework: an appellate court will reverse only if the erroneously admitted evidence may have substantially swayed the jury. United States v. DeMarce, which the panel cited, articulates that only errors that could have influenced the jury’s verdict require reversal—errors that had “only a slight influence” are harmless. The court also noted that cumulative-error reversal requires showing substantial prejudice from the aggregate of errors; isolated, harmless mistakes that do not combine to produce prejudice will not overturn a conviction (citing United States v. Keys).

Applying those standards here, the Eighth Circuit found any asserted evidentiary missteps to be harmless. The government’s case included strong testimonial identifications (D.F. and C.F. identifying the sawed-off rifle and connecting it to Cutbank) and physical evidence—D.F.’s phone and the rifle found along the likely path from the victim’s home to where Cutbank barricaded himself, DNA on the gun matching Cutbank, and the violent facts at the scene. Given that weight of evidence, the court concluded the district court’s rulings “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict,” and any errors did not produce substantial prejudice. The court therefore affirmed Cutbank’s conviction.

Conclusion: what to take away
This portion of the opinion illustrates several practical rules for trial and appellate practice:

- Witness explanations for prior inconsistent statements are admissible as responsive testimony; the district court should exclude such testimony only if Rule 403’s substantial prejudice or confusion standard is met.
- A party may impeach a witness with prior statements made to police, but reading portions of a police report into the record requires authentication/foundation linking the report contents to the witness.
- Appellate review of these evidentiary decisions is highly deferential; the district court’s balancing is reversed only for abuse of discretion.
- Even an erroneously admitted item will not warrant reversal absent a showing that the error substantially swayed the jury; cumulative harmless-error review requires a showing of substantial prejudice from the aggregate errors.

In Cutbank’s case, the district court’s exercise of discretion on each contested point fell within those bounds, and any asserted errors were harmless in light of the case’s robust testimonial and physical evidence.

# Sentencing: § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Enhancement, ACCA Interaction, and Reasonableness Review

This section explains the Eighth Circuit’s treatment of the four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm “in connection with” another felony, how that enhancement interacts with the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) guideline calculations, and why the court affirmed Cutbank’s within-Guidelines sentence as both procedurally and substantively sound.

## PSR calculations and the contested enhancement

The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) supplied the factual and numerical foundation for sentencing. The PSR assigned a base offense level of 26 because the firearm at issue was a sawed-off weapon (triggering the statutory definition in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a)) and because Cutbank had at least two prior crimes of violence. The PSR then recommended additional adjustments: a +3 increase for an altered or obliterated serial number, +2 for recklessly creating a substantial risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing law enforcement, and +4 under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing the firearm “in connection with” another felony offense. Those adjustments produced a total offense level of 35. With a Criminal History Category of VI, the advisory Guidelines range was 292–365 months.

Cutbank challenged the application of the +4 enhancement on appeal, arguing that its inclusion produced a sentence inconsistent with the § 3553(a) parsimony principle. The court’s evaluation begins with whether the enhancement was properly applied under the Guidelines.

## Application Note 14(C) and the meaning of “in connection with”

The Sentencing Guidelines commentary relevant here—Application Note 14(C) to § 2K2.1—states that the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement applies where a firearm was used or possessed “in connection with” another felony, and that this enhancement may apply “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” The court relied on that text: the guideline does not require a separate charge or conviction for the other felony conduct so long as the conduct supporting the enhancement is proven at sentencing by the applicable standard.

In Cutbank’s case, the PSR described facts tying the firearm possession to other violent conduct (the home confinement, threats, and flight that led to the later encounter with police), which the district court adopted at sentencing. Under the Guideline language and commentary, the factual showing in the PSR was sufficient to support the +4 enhancement absent a successful objection.

## Adoption of PSR facts; review for clear error

A key procedural point is that the district court adopted the factual statements in the PSR without objection. When a district court adopts PSR facts at sentencing and no objection is made, the appellate standard of review is deferential: factual findings are reviewed for clear error. The opinion cites United States v. Dunn for that standard. Because the district court incorporated the PSR and Cutbank did not object to those facts at the time, the Eighth Circuit found no clear error in the underlying factual determinations that supported the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement.

The consequence is practical: when the PSR is adopted and unchallenged, the appellate court will not overturn a district court’s factual findings unless they are clearly erroneous. Cutbank’s failure to preserve meaningful factual objections at sentencing made it difficult to prevail on an appellate challenge to the enhancement.

## Interaction with ACCA and USSG § 4B1.4(b)

Cutbank also pointed out that the jury’s ACCA finding (three or more prior violent felonies) would, under the career-offender/ACCA framework, lead to an offense level calculation of 34 rather than 35. The Guidelines themselves resolve this kind of conflict: USSG § 4B1.4(b) provides that, where two Guidelines provisions yield different offense levels, the greater offense level controls. The court applied that rule and explained that the existence of a slightly lower offense level under the ACCA-related calculation does not render the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement improper; the Guidelines direct sentencing courts to use the higher applicable offense level.

Thus, the interplay between § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) and the ACCA-related calculation did not require striking the enhancement—under the Guidelines’ own tie-breaking rule, the higher level remained controlling.

## Procedural obligations before substantive review: Gall and Rosales‑Mireles

Before a court of appeals may evaluate the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, it must ensure the district court committed no significant procedural error—most centrally, that the court correctly calculated the Guidelines range. The opinion rests this point on Gall v. United States (district courts must correctly calculate the Guidelines range and consider the Guidelines) and Rosales‑Mireles v. United States (an appellate court must confirm no significant procedural error before reviewing substantive reasonableness). Because the district court adopted the PSR and applied the Guidelines adjustments, the Eighth Circuit found no procedural error in the calculation of the advisory range.

The implication for defendants is practical: unsuccessful or unpreserved challenges to PSR facts make it difficult to show that a district court miscalculated the Guidelines range in a way that would trigger reversal or remand for resentencing.

## Substantive-reasonableness review and the presumption for within‑Guidelines sentences

Once procedural correctness is established, the appellate court assesses substantive reasonableness. The Eighth Circuit applied its precedent that a within-Guidelines sentence is presumed reasonable on appeal (citing Goodhouse). The court also observed that the district court explicitly reviewed the PSR, considered sentencing memoranda, and discussed § 3553(a) factors that could justify a variance. The court found those steps sufficient to show the district court considered the relevant statutory factors; it is not required to recite each factor mechanically so long as the record makes clear that the court considered them (citing Masood and Maluoth).

Cutbank claimed the +4 enhancement produced a sentence greater than necessary under § 3553(a). The appellate court rejected that argument because the district court (1) correctly calculated the Guidelines range, (2) was aware of and considered the defendant’s arguments and the § 3553(a) factors, and (3) imposed a within-Guidelines sentence. Given the presumption of reasonableness for within-Guidelines sentences and the factual record, the court concluded the sentence was not substantively unreasonable.

## Aggravating facts supporting the enhancement and the within‑Guidelines sentence

The opinion emphasizes the factual context that supports both the enhancement and the ultimate sentence. Cutbank had an extensive criminal history—nearly a dozen felony convictions, many violent and firearm-related. The charged conduct involved effectively imprisoning his girlfriend at gunpoint in her home in front of her children, threats, physical assault with the gun, flight, and injury to an officer during resistance. Those facts supply the kind of aggravating conduct that the Guidelines and a district court may permissibly consider when deciding whether a within-Guidelines sentence is appropriate and when applying enhancements tied to related felonious behavior.

The court pointed out that application of § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) was consistent with the PSR’s factual description of how the firearm was used in connection with the other felony conduct (the domestic-violence-related restraint and subsequent flight), and therefore not arbitrary or unreasonable.

## Holding: no procedural error, no abuse of discretion, sentence affirmed

Bringing these threads together, the Eighth Circuit held that (1) the district court did not commit procedural error in applying the +4 enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), because the PSR facts supported the enhancement and the court adopted those facts without objection; (2) the Guidelines’ commentary permits application of the enhancement even without a separate charge or conviction for the related felony; (3) any apparent tension between the enhancement and the ACCA-related offense level is resolved by the Guidelines’ rule to apply the greater offense level; and (4) because the district court correctly calculated the Guidelines and considered § 3553(a) factors, the within-Guidelines 292-month sentence was presumptively reasonable and was not substantively unreasonable given Cutbank’s violent history and the circumstances of the offense. The judgment and sentence were therefore affirmed.

Practical takeaway for law students: a sentencing challenge to a Guidelines enhancement often turns on (a) whether the district court’s factual findings were preserved and adequately supported by the PSR or other evidence, (b) the specific language of the relevant Guidelines and their commentary (here, Application Note 14(C)), and (c) whether the district court correctly calculated the range and adequately considered § 3553(a). Failure to object to PSR facts or to preserve a detailed factual record at sentencing significantly weakens appellate review.
