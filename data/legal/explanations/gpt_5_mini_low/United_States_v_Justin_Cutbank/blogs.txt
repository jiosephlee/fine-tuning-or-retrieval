Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

## Intrinsic or 404(b)? The Eighth Circuit’s Elastic Approach to Prior Firearm Possession

Why this matters: At the intersection of evidence law and firearm prosecutions lies a recurring question — when may the government introduce testimony that a witness saw a defendant with the very weapon at issue before the charged offense? In United States v. Justin Cutbank, an Eighth Circuit panel answered that question broadly in favor of the prosecution. Relying on United States v. Battle and the court’s longstanding “intrinsic evidence” approach, the panel treated prior-observation testimony about the sawed‑off Marlin .22 as direct proof of ownership, control, and continuity of possession rather than as Rule 404(b) propensity evidence. The decision is important for two reasons: it makes it easier for prosecutors to put on lay witnesses who knew the defendant and the weapon, and it highlights continuing doctrinal tensions about how far the intrinsic-evidence exception to Rule 404(b) should reach before it swallows the rule against propensity proof.

What the Eighth Circuit held and why: Cutbank was convicted of being a felon in possession of a firearm after testimony and physical evidence connected him to a sawed‑off rifle found along the path he likely used when fleeing a victim’s home. Pretrial, Cutbank moved to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had seen him with that rifle on prior occasions, arguing the testimony was impermissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b). The district court admitted the testimony, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed.

The court’s reasoning is straightforward and doctrinally familiar within the circuit. It treated the prior observations as intrinsic, not extrinsic, evidence: testimony that the witnesses had seen Cutbank with that same rifle "a handful of times" bore directly on ownership, control, and continuous possession — facts that make it more likely the defendant possessed the same weapon when the charged offense occurred. The panel quoted Battle to the effect that prior possession is “directly relevant” to later possession, and it stressed the district court’s broad discretion in balancing relevance and prejudice. The court also disposed of Cutbank’s other evidentiary complaints and rejected his sentencing argument; for our purposes the key takeaway is the Eighth Circuit’s expansive application of the intrinsic-evidence doctrine.

How this fits into the Rule 404(b) landscape: Rule 404(b) is framed as a backstop — it keeps out evidence of other crimes or wrongs when offered to prove a defendant’s propensity, while allowing such evidence for non‑propensity purposes (motive, intent, identity, absence of mistake, etc.). Courts have long recognized, however, that certain prior acts are not “other crimes” at all for Rule 404(b) purposes when they are inextricably intertwined with the charged conduct. The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Cutbank follows that tradition, emphasizing a pragmatic, context-driven inquiry: if the prior observation of the weapon helps establish identity, possession, or continuity, it is intrinsic and admissible.

That pragmatic posture has advantages. In many felon‑in‑possession cases, there is no direct contemporaneous admission of ownership; lay witness testimony that a defendant was known to have a particular firearm may be the most probative evidence of control. Treating such evidence as intrinsic prevents prosecutors from jumping through formalistic hoops and allows the jury to see the narrative arc that links defendant, weapon, and crime.

But there is a flip side: propensity creep. The more elastic the intrinsic exception becomes, the greater the risk that judges will admit prior-act evidence that essentially says only “the defendant is the sort of person who carried guns,” or that invites jurors to convict because of bad character rather than proof of the charged elements. The danger grows when prior acts are temporally remote, materially different in kind, or relevant only to the defendant’s general dangerousness rather than to identity or continuity with the specific weapon or episode.

Comparative circuit practice and doctrinal guardrails: The Eighth Circuit’s approach is not unique, but it is on the more permissive end of the spectrum. Other circuits have pressed back by imposing firmer limits on intrinsic evidence, particularly after the 2006 amendments to Rule 404(b) and subsequent caselaw that stressed careful articulation of non‑propensity purposes and Rule 403 balancing. In those jurisdictions, courts have required a closer temporal or modal relationship between the prior act and the charged offense, sometimes demanding that the prior act be necessary to make the charged behavior intelligible (for example, where the prior conduct explains the context of a co‑conspirator’s behavior or provides the only plausible explanation for identity).

What that means in practice is that admissibility often turns on fine facts: Is the prior observation of the weapon the same model, distinctive, or personally associated with the defendant? Is the witness describing repeated, recent possession or a single, remote encounter? Would exclusion unduly fragment the story the government must tell, or is the proffered evidence merely cumulative and prejudicial? The Eighth Circuit’s reliance on Battle suggests that when the prior possession is of the same weapon and probative of ownership or control, the balance tips toward admissibility.

Trial practice implications — for prosecutors and defense counsel: For prosecutors, Cutbank is an endorsement of a common-sense strategy: establish the continuity between the defendant and the seized weapon early and concretely. Lay testimony that a defendant habitually carried or stored a particular firearm, particularly one with distinguishing features (a sawed‑off stock, altered serial number, etc.), will often survive 404(b) challenge in the Eighth Circuit.

For defenders, the decision underscores the need to frame 404(b) challenges with particularity. Generic objections that prior‑act testimony is “propensity” evidence will not suffice. Instead, defense motions in limine should attack admissibility along multiple lines: challenge the nexus (is it truly the same weapon or a similar kind of weapon?), press for limiting instructions narrowly tailored to any permissible non‑propensity purpose, and force the government to articulate with specificity why the prior observations are necessary to prove identity, ownership, or continuity. Preserve objections on the record and separately raise Rule 403 prejudice concerns — the Eighth Circuit was explicit that harmless‑error review and the weight of other evidence will often defeat appellate reversal.

Downstream doctrinal effects and open questions: Cutbank reaffirms an Eighth Circuit pattern that will predictably keep the door open to prior‑possession testimony in many felon‑in‑possession prosecutions. That is likely to increase the evidentiary burdens on defense counsel to exclude or limit such testimony at the district-court level and to sharpen appellate litigation about the contours of “intrinsic” evidence. The decision also leaves unresolved questions that will energize future litigation: how distinct must a prior act be to count as propensity if offered without a clear ownership nexus? How should courts evaluate remoteness and cumulative prejudice when the government stitches together a narrative from multiple, scattered prior sightings?

Finally, Cutbank illustrates the continuing tension between evidentiary realism and protection against character‑based inference. The Eighth Circuit’s flexible test favors narrative completeness and prosecutorial efficiency; critics will say it risks diluting Rule 404(b)’s core safeguard against convicting for who a defendant is rather than what he did. Whether that balance will be sustained by the Supreme Court or recalibrated by other circuits remains to be seen.

Conclusion: United States v. Cutbank is a useful, if unsurprising, restatement of the Eighth Circuit’s view that prior observations of the same firearm are intrinsic and therefore admissible to prove possession, ownership, or continuity. Practitioners in the circuit should plan accordingly: prosecutors can confidently seek to place such testimony before juries, while defense counsel must make specific, contextualized evidentiary arguments and press for limiting instructions and rigorous Rule 403 scrutiny to blunt potential prejudice. For scholars and advocates attentive to the Rule 404(b) regime, the decision spotlights the ongoing struggle to draw principled lines between legitimate contextual proof and impermissible propensity evidence — a struggle that will shape the conduct and outcome of firearm prosecutions for years to come.



## ‘Context’ Testimony, Limiting Instructions, and the Confrontation Clause: A Quiet Win for Prosecutors

Why this matters: United States v. Cutbank (8th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025) is not a blockbuster confrontation-clause or hearsay decision, but it is a practical one. It reaffirms a routine — and important — prosecutorial tool: allowing police witnesses to recount a victim’s out-of-court statements as background to explain why officers acted as they did, while leaving resolution of any hearsay or confrontation concerns to the declarant’s later, in-court testimony. For defense counsel, Cutbank is a reminder that timing and preservation matter, and that courts have substantial discretion in whether and when to give limiting instructions. For practitioners focused on the line between “context” and substantive hearsay, the opinion is a useful, fairly defendant-friendly roadmap of what will — and what will not — get you reversal.

What the Eighth Circuit held, in short
Cutbank asked the district court, before trial, to instruct jurors not to treat the victim D.F.’s statements to police as substantive evidence of the defendant’s conduct unless and until D.F. herself testified. The district court declined to give a contemporaneous limiting instruction, opting instead to allow police testimony to describe the calls and officers’ observations and to rely on a customary instruction at the close of trial if needed. On appeal the Eighth Circuit affirmed, holding (1) a district court need not give a limiting instruction each time evidence is admitted if it provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial; (2) much of the officers’ testimony fell into non-hearsay “background” or “context” material explaining police conduct; and (3) because D.F. later testified at trial, Confrontation Clause concerns were satisfied under the court’s reading of United States v. Charboneau and related precedent.

Why the court’s reasoning is defensible (and familiar)
Two doctrinal strands run through the opinion. The first is federal evidentiary practice: courts routinely permit witnesses to provide the “context” of police action — what the officer heard on the 911 call, how the scene appeared, and why the officer did what he did — so long as the statements are not offered for the truth of the matter asserted. The Eighth Circuit leaned on its own precedent (Battle) about prior possession as intrinsic evidence and on the general proposition that contextual background is often non-hearsay. That approach is orthodox: admitting background statements can be essential for jurors to understand why an officer took certain investigative steps without using the statements to prove guilt.

The second strand is Confrontation Clause doctrine. The court relied on Charboneau (and the familiar rule that the Clause is satisfied when the declarant actually appears and testifies at trial) to reject any independent Sixth Amendment problem with officers’ recounting of the victim’s earlier statements. That is also orthodox: the problem the Clause protects against is untested testimonial hearsay offered for its truth at trial when the declarant cannot be cross-examined. When the declarant testifies, prior statements lose their Sixth Amendment sting — though they may still be hearsay if offered for their truth.

The Eighth Circuit’s practical posture — deference to trial judges on instructions — follows Bennett and its progeny. The court emphasized that a judge need not interrupt the flow of trial to give limiting instructions each time an item of evidence is admitted; a single, appropriate instruction at the close of trial will often do. The appellate court reviewed for abuse of discretion and found none.

Where the line between context and hearsay can be sharp
Cutbank confirms the generally permissive approach to “context” testimony, but it leaves the familiar, hard fact pattern unresolved: when will an officer’s recounting of a declarant’s statement cross from context into substantive hearsay? The answer remains fact-bound. Courts look at how the prosecutor uses the testimony and whether the statement was offered for the truth of the matter asserted (e.g., “the victim told me the defendant shot him” used to prove the shooting) versus to explain police conduct (e.g., “the officer went to the house because he received an anonymous report and heard commotion on arrival”).

Two practical points follow. First, prosecutors should draft direct examination to emphasize non-truth purposes when recounting out-of-court statements — to explain why police were on the scene or what the officers perceived — and avoid introducing the content in a way that invites use for its truth. Second, defense counsel should be prepared to object promptly when an officer’s narrative shifts toward substantive assertions (or when the statement is clearly testimonial and the declarant will not testify).

Charboneau and the Confrontation Clause: no surprise, but an important reminder
Cutbank’s Confrontation Clause claim failed because D.F. testified, thereby restoring the “confrontation” the Clause demands. This is a straightforward application of Charboneau: a live-witness appearance cures the Sixth Amendment problem raised by an officer’s earlier recounting of that witness’s statements. But appellate practice often hinges on the record: if the declarant is not called or the court permits an officer to read in testimonial content before any live testimony and the defendant is deprived of effective cross-examination, the analysis could tilt the other way. Cutbank is a reminder that once the declarant testifies, Confrontation Clause arguments become mainly about whether the jury was allowed to rely improperly on prior statements without proper limiting instruction — an evidentiary, not a constitutional, issue.

Harmless error and the real-world appellate hurdle
Even when a district court’s decision about limiting instructions or background testimony is arguably incorrect, Cutbank underscores the practical appellate reality: harmless-error rules are hard for appellants to overcome where the government’s case is strong. The Eighth Circuit pointed to substantial independent evidence — DNA on the rifle, eyewitness identifications, and the volatile facts at the residence and garage — to conclude any evidentiary blemishes did not substantially sway the jury. That is an important caveat for defense attorneys: preserving issues is essential, but even preserved errors may be insulated by the harmless-error standard if the record contains compelling independent evidence.

What defense counsel should do to preserve and sharpen these issues
Cutbank highlights several steps defense counsel should take to maximize the chances of appellate relief (or at least to narrow the scope of admitted background testimony):

- Make specific pretrial motions identifying the precise statements you want excluded and the exact grounds (Rule 403, Rule 404(b), hearsay, or Confrontation Clause) rather than broad, boilerplate requests. That gives the district court a focused record and gives an appellate court something concrete to review.

- Object contemporaneously and with specificity at trial to any officer testimony that shifts from context to substantive assertion, and request a limiting instruction on the record if the court declines. A contemporaneous objection is often necessary to preserve the claim and frames an appellate issue.

- If the declarant will not testify, press forcefully for a pretrial ruling on admissibility of testimonial statements and preserve argument that their admission would violate the Sixth Amendment. The live appearance of the declarant was dispositive in Cutbank; without it the outcome could differ.

- When the court denies a limiting instruction, ask for a contemporaneous curative instruction to reduce any chance of prejudice and to create a clearer appellate record.

- Where possible, isolate and litigate whether the officer’s proffered purpose for recounting the statement is genuine (explaining police conduct) rather than pretextual (using the officer as a conduit for testimonial hearsay).

Downstream effects and final takeaways
Cutbank will not alter evidentiary or confrontation doctrine, but it is a useful practitioner’s decision. It reaffirms that (1) district courts have broad discretion about when to give limiting instructions; (2) officers may recount victims’ statements as background for why they acted without creating per se hearsay or confrontation problems; and (3) a victim’s subsequent in-court testimony will generally cure confrontation concerns tied to those statements.

For prosecutors, the opinion is a green light for careful use of background testimony, provided the testimony is not offered for the truth of the matter asserted and the declarant can or will testify. For defense lawyers, the ruling is a reminder that success on appeal often requires a carefully preserved record, precise objections, and an eye toward harmless-error analysis. Cutbank is not a sweeping endorsement of unfettered testimonial repetition by officers, but it does confirm that courts will tolerate reasonable, context-driven testimony and will defer to trial judges’ judgment about the timing and wording of limiting instructions.



# Stacking Enhancements in an ACCA Case: §2K2.1(b)(6)(B), §4B1.4, and the Presumption of Reasonableness

Why this case matters
United States v. Cutbank is a useful, if unsurprising, reminder about three things that often determine outcomes in violent-firearm sentences: (1) the Sentencing Guidelines regime still prizes an accurate numerical calculation of offense level, (2) the Guidelines themselves tell sentencing courts which enhancement wins when multiple rules could apply, and (3) a within-Guidelines sentence enjoys a strong presumption of reasonableness on appeal. Cutbank—an ACCA case arising from a violent episode in which the defendant held a sawed-off .22 at gunpoint and later resisted arrest—presents a textbook contest between a § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) four-level enhancement (possession “in connection with another felony”), the separate ACCA enhancement via § 4B1.4, and a defense argument that applying both (or the higher numerical result) produces an unjust sentence even when the underlying conduct is brutal.

The Eighth Circuit affirmed. Its reasoning is straightforward: the district court adopted the PSR’s facts, applied the applicable Guidelines, chose the greater offense level as § 4B1.4 directs, and sentenced within the resulting advisory range. Because the calculation was correct and the court considered § 3553(a) factors, the within-Guidelines sentence is presumed reasonable and survives substantive-review challenge.

What the court did (and why)
The factual record the PSR adopted was strong: witness identifications linking Cutbank to the sawed-off rifle, DNA on the weapon, and violent conduct in the victim’s home culminating in a flight and struggle with police. The PSR applied several increases under USSG § 2K2.1: altered serial number (+3), reckless creation of risk while fleeing (+2), and the contested +4 for possession “in connection with” another felony under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). Separately, ACCA’s treatment produced a slightly lower offense level under USSG § 4B1.4(b). The Guidelines themselves resolve that tension: where multiple offense levels could apply, the Guidelines instruct the court to use the greater offense level. The district court did so; the Eighth Circuit treated the calculation as procedural correctness and therefore the prerequisite to appellate review of substantive reasonableness.

On appeal, Cutbank argued both that the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement was improper and that, even if the Guidelines calculation was “correct,” applying it produced a sentence inconsistent with the parsimony directive of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). The panel rejected both points. It viewed the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) finding as supported by the PSR and admissible facts adopted at sentencing, and it declined to do more than presume the district court considered the § 3553(a) factors when imposing a within-Guidelines sentence.

Analytical perspective: double-counting, uncharged conduct, and nexus
Three doctrinal flashpoints arise from Cutbank.

First, double-counting concerns. Defendants frequently raise the specter that applying multiple enhancements “double-counts” the same criminal behavior. The Eighth Circuit’s treatment follows prevailing practice: so long as each guideline provision targets a different aspect of culpability or the Guidelines themselves resolve which provision governs (as § 4B1.4(b) does here), the court will not disturb the calculation. Cutbank does not, and could not, present an argument that the Guidelines’ internal ordering is arbitrary; the panel accepts the Guidelines’ command to apply the higher offense level.

Second, the permissibility of considering uncharged conduct. Application Note 14(C) to § 2K2.1 expressly permits the enhancement when a firearm is possessed “in connection with another felony offense, regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” The Court of Appeals reaffirms that sentencing fact-finding may rest on the PSR’s adopted facts and that sentencing courts can rely on uncharged conduct—so long as it is supported in the record and the defendant had a chance to contest it. That doctrine has been settled for years, but Cutbank is another illustration: the DNA and witness identifications created a solid factual predicate for the district court’s finding that the weapon was tied to broader criminality beyond mere possession.

Third, the nexus requirement. § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) requires a connection between the firearm and “another felony offense.” The most vulnerable defense strategy in cases like Cutbank is to challenge that causal or temporal nexus: argue the firearm was merely present and unrelated to an independent felony, or that the PSR’s recitation conflates separate conduct. Cutbank’s record—witnesses who saw the defendant with the gun before the incident, the gun found along the path between crime scenes, and DNA on the weapon—made such a challenge difficult, and the Eighth Circuit deferred to the district court’s factual finding.

The role of the presumption of reasonableness
Cutbank underscores how the presumption of reasonableness for Guidelines-range sentences operates in practice. The panel treated accurate guideline calculation as a threshold procedural requirement; once that is met, the appellate inquiry into substantive reasonableness is deferential. The defendant argued that stacking the § 2K2.1 enhancement atop ACCA’s framework produced a sentence incompatible with § 3553(a)’s “no greater than necessary” mandate. The court was unmoved: the district court reviewed the PSR and expressly considered § 3553(a) arguments, and because the sentence fell within the correctly calculated Guidelines range, appellate review starts from a position of deference.

Doctrinal and practical implications
Cutbank will not reshape major doctrinal contours, but it sends practical signals to defense counsel and sentencing courts.

- For prosecutors: Cutbank is a green light to pursue § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancements where the PSR and record show a firearm was connected to other felony conduct, even if prosecutors do not—or cannot—bring separate charges. Application Note 14(C) remains an effective hook for increasing offense levels.

- For defense lawyers: the decision highlights the importance of contesting factual predicates early and with specificity. Because appellate courts give weight to adopted PSR facts and defer on factual findings at sentencing, evidentiary fights over nexus and authentication need to be waged before sentencing or preserved with clear objections. Also, it demonstrates that urging § 3553(a) parsimony after a correct guideline calculation is a high bar; appellate courts will presume the within-Guidelines result reasonable absent a clear sign the district court failed to consider relevant factors or committed procedural error.

- For judges: Cutbank is a reminder to make a record that shows active consideration of § 3553(a) factors. The Eighth Circuit reiterates that a “mechanical recitation” is unnecessary, but a visible engagement with mitigation arguments improves the durability of a sentence on appeal.

Downstream effects and open questions
Cutbank fits into a broader line of cases in which courts accept sentencing increases based on uncharged conduct and adhere to Guidelines’ internal hierarchies. It does not resolve tougher issues that sometimes arise with § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)—for example, close cases where the alleged “other felony” is attenuated from the firearm conduct, or situations where multiple enhancements arguably punish the same element of criminality. Nor does it alter the continuing debate over the strength of the presumption of reasonableness; it simply applies the existing framework that Gall and its progeny established, showing that deference persists when the procedural box of correct guideline calculation is checked.

Strategic takeaways
If you represent defendants in firearm cases with messy factual records, Cutbank suggests four practical steps that matter:

1. Fight the PSR facts early. Make targeted objections and submit contrary evidence before sentencing.
2. Challenge nexus with specificity. Demonstrate temporal or causal disconnection between the firearm and the alleged “other felony.”
3. Preserve confrontation and authentication arguments at trial and at sentencing so appellate courts must entertain them.
4. Build a record on § 3553(a) considerations—emphasize mitigating circumstances, rehabilitation prospects, and disproportionate effects of stacked enhancements to give the district court concrete reasons to vary.

Conclusion
United States v. Cutbank is a predictable but clarifying decision: where the record supports a PSR’s factual findings and the Guidelines direct selection of the higher offense level, stacking a § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement atop an ACCA-driven calculation will survive review so long as the district court properly calculates the Guidelines and considers § 3553(a). The real battle in these cases remains at the fact-finding and sentencing stages: contest the facts, press nexus, and force a clear § 3553(a) record if you hope to overcome the powerful presumption that a within-Guidelines sentence is reasonable.



# Impeachment by Police Reports and Appellate Discipline: Authentication Matters

## Why this decision matters

United States v. Cutbank (8th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025) is a compact but useful decision for trial practitioners about two recurring—but sometimes overlooked—areas of criminal practice: the mechanics of using third‑party police reports to impeach witness testimony, and appellate etiquette (and preservation). The panel affirmed a district court that refused to let defense counsel read portions of a police report into evidence to impeach a witness because the report lacked the necessary foundation and authentication. The court relied on its recent line of authority (including United States v. Cumbie) requiring authentication for extrinsic impeachment evidence. And, in a moment of admonishment, the panel chided the government for broadening the issues on appeal—an instructive reminder about appellate framing and preservation. Cutbank also touches, briefly, on the limits of Rule 403 when emotionally freighted testimony is offered in response to cross‑examination.

The lesson for trial lawyers is practical: you cannot expect to treat a police report as a self‑authenticating slab of impeachment material. If you want to use someone else’s report to impeach, you must lay the foundation for admissibility—or else risk the judge sustaining an objection and losing a tactical opportunity. On appeal, you should also keep your question-set tight and respect preservation rules; courts do not reward litigants who expand the record or press new claims post hoc.

## The ruling and the court’s reasoning

At trial, defense counsel attempted to impeach government witness C.F. by reading portions of a police report that contained C.F.’s prior statements. The district court allowed questions about what C.F. told police but sustained government objections to counsel’s effort to read the police report itself into the record because the report was not authored by C.F. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit affirmed. It relied on United States v. Cumbie, which the panel cites for the proposition that extrinsic impeachment evidence—such as a written report containing a prior inconsistent statement—must be authenticated. Because the defense did not authenticate the police report (and did not secure an appropriate foundation for its admission as an adoptive or recorded recollection, or otherwise meet a hearsay exception or the requirements of Rule 613/801(d)(1)(A) for prior consistent/inconsistent statements), the district court did not abuse its discretion by excluding the reading of the report.

The court also noted an appellate‑practice point: the government, in its brief, argued a related claim about the district court “thwart[ing]” the defense’s cross‑examination, and the panel rebuked appellees who expand issues on appeal—implicitly reminding litigants to brief only the preserved claims. Finally, although unrelated to the impeachment ruling, the panel affirmed a district court’s refusal to strike testimony about the victim’s sister having been killed by a gun, concluding the testimony was responsive to cross‑examination and not unduly prejudicial under Rule 403.

## Practical takeaways for trial counsel

1. Authenticate before you impeach with a third‑party report. If you intend to use a police report (or any third‑party record that contains a witness’s prior statements) as extrinsic impeachment, build a foundation during cross: get the witness to confirm she made the statements contained in the report, identify the report, and either produce the witness’s statement in a form that satisfies Rule 801(d)(1)(A) (prior inconsistent statement given under oath at a prior proceeding or otherwise adopted by the witness) or authenticate the document under Fed. R. Evid. 901. If the report is not the witness’s own writing or a recorded recollection, authentication can be accomplished by establishing the report’s origin and linking its contents to the declarant.

2. Use the declarant, not the author, when possible. The safer route is to have the declarant (the person whose out‑of‑court statement appears in the report) testify and adopt, deny, or explain the prior statement. Once the witness acknowledges making the statement, the contents are easier to offer as impeachment (and, depending on circumstances, may be admissible substantively under Rule 801(d)(1)(A)). If the declarant cannot remember or denies making the statement, be prepared to lay the foundation for extrinsic evidence under Rule 613(b): show that the matter is truly inconsistent and that the witness was given an opportunity to explain or deny the prior statement.

3. Consider hearsay exceptions and form. If the police report contains the witness’s own assertions but is itself not a statement of the witness (e.g., the officer’s narrative of what others said), then the report may be double hearsay. You should consider alternate routes: redact the parts that are hearsay‑within‑hearsay, offer only those portions that quote the witness verbatim and can be authenticated, or seek admission as a recorded recollection (Fed. R. Evid. 803(5)) if the witness once had knowledge and cannot now recall sufficiently well.

4. Be ready to authenticate under Rule 901. Authentication can be simple (testimony that the document is what it purports to be) or more elaborate (certificates, chain of custody, testimony from the report’s author). Don’t wait until the eleventh hour—prepare a witness (or a sponsor) who can authenticate the report if you plan to use it.

5. If you lose the bid at trial, preserve the precise grounds of your objection and the theory of admissibility for appeal. Cutbank’s brief indicates the defense questioned preservation; courts expect a clear objection and an articulated theory to preserve claims for appellate review.

## Mitigating prejudicial but context‑linked testimony

The court in Cutbank also affirmed the district court’s decision not to strike testimony that D.F. (the victim) mentioned her sister had been killed by a gun. The Eighth Circuit treated that testimony as responsive to cross‑examination about why D.F. was “not familiar with guns” and did not view it as unduly prejudicial under Rule 403. Trial judges have broad discretion under Rule 403, and appellate courts will not lightly disturb rulings that emotionally charged testimony was properly admitted when it bears on credibility or motive.

Defense counsel should anticipate these dynamics. If a witness offers emotionally laden testimony in response to inquiry, consider seeking a limiting instruction that confines the jury’s use of the testimony to credibility or context. If the testimony is unfairly prejudicial and not probative, move to strike or move for a mistrial if the impact is severe. But be mindful that courts will tolerate probative emotional context, especially when it rebuts a credibility attack or explains a witness’s behavior.

## On appellate practice: do not expand the battlefield

Cutbank includes a pointed line about appellees who “unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal.” The government argued a related—but not precisely preserved—claim about cross‑examination and the district court’s rulings; the panel disapproved. That rebuke is a useful reminder: appellate panels expect disciplined briefs that confine themselves to preserved errors and the arguments the parties actually litigated below. Raising new theories on appeal, or enlarging the scope of an opponent’s arguments, risks dismissal of the point and, as here, an admonition in the opinion. Doctrinally it doesn’t change the law, but practically it signals that appellate goodwill is partially a function of clear, focused advocacy.

## Bottom line

Cutbank reinforces a straightforward but sometimes forgotten truth: extrinsic impeachment evidence that comes in the form of third‑party reports must be authenticated before you may read it into the record. Use the declarant where possible, build authentication in advance, and be prepared to show how the evidence fits an admissible impeachment route. At the same time, litigants should respect the contours of appellate advocacy: preserve your theory below and limit your appellate arguments to the preserved, clearly presented issues. Finally, emotional testimony tied to credibility or context will usually survive a Rule 403 challenge; plan for limiting instructions, but don’t assume a strike will be automatic.
