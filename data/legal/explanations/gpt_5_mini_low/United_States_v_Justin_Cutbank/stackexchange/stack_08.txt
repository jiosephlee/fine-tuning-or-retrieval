Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Can an appellee broaden the issues on appeal, and how does that affect standards of review and party presentation?
Question:
The panel criticizes the government for 'unnecessarily expand[ing] the issues on appeal' by framing a claim about thwarted cross-examination that appellant didn’t clearly brief. What is the Eighth Circuit’s approach to the party presentation principle in criminal appeals? If the government raises an additional framing or justification, will the court consider it, disregard it, or resolve it under plain error? How does this interact with forfeiture vs. waiver of objections made (or not) in the district court and in the opening brief?
Answer:
Short answer
- The Eighth Circuit follows the party-presentation principle: parties frame the issues, and the court normally decides the questions the parties actually present. An appellee who tries to broaden or recast the appellant’s claims on appeal risks criticism and the court may decline to rely on that new framing.
- That said, the court will not automatically ignore an argument the appellee presses. If the appellant preserved an issue below, the court will decide it under the ordinary standard (e.g., abuse of discretion for most evidentiary rulings). If the appellant did not preserve the issue, the court will treat it as unpreserved and resolve it under the appropriate unpreserved-error standard (in criminal cases, plain‑error review) and/or apply the harmless‑error analysis where applicable.
- In Cutbank the court both admonished the government for expanding the issues and nonetheless addressed the substance: it applied ordinary abuse‑of‑discretion review to the evidentiary rulings and explained that, even if the claim were not preserved, it would fail (including harmlessness analysis).

Explanation, grounded in the opinion
1) Party-presentation principle and the court’s reaction
- The panel expressly reproved the government for “unnecessarily expand[ing] the issues on appeal,” signaling that an appellee should not recast the appellant’s argument or press a new theory that the appellant did not clearly brief. That language reflects the court’s expectation that the parties control which issues the court decides.

2) How the court treats an appellee’s additional framing
- The opinion shows two things: (a) the court will call out an appellee that broadens the record of issues, and (b) the court may nevertheless address the point on the merits if needed. In the C‑F cross‑examination dispute, the government’s brief characterized a “next claim” (that the defense was “thwarted” in cross‑examining C.F.), which the panel said it “d[id] not appreciate.” The court then went on to decide the point on evidentiary/authentication grounds. So an appellee’s new framing may be criticized and is at risk of being disregarded, but it will not automatically keep the court from resolving the underlying question.

3) Preservation, standards of review, and harmlessness
- If the appellant preserved the objection in district court, the court uses the ordinary standard for that kind of ruling (the opinion applies “we review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion,” and likewise treats evidentiary authentication rulings as within the district court’s discretion). Cutbank’s preserved evidentiary objections were reviewed under abuse‑of‑discretion and rejected.
- If the appellant did not preserve the issue, the court makes clear it can still consider it: the opinion explicitly says “whether preserved or not, the ‘claim’ is without merit.” In criminal cases, that posture implicates the ordinary unpreserved‑error frameworks (including plain‑error review under Rule 52(b)), and the court also applies harmless‑error analysis where appropriate. The opinion applied harmlessness language: “we will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless…we will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed…Cutbank cannot clear this hurdle.”
- Thus, unpreserved objections may be considered but are subject to the stricter plain‑error/harmlessness tests the court describes.

4) Forfeiture versus waiver (as shown in the opinion)
- The opinion does not use the technical labels “forfeiture” or “waiver” in a doctrinal holding here, but its practice aligns with the usual distinction: an objection actually made and preserved invites ordinary review; an objection not raised is forfeited/forgone for purposes of ordinary review and therefore faces plain‑error/harmlessness if the appellant later presses it on appeal. The panel’s “whether preserved or not” gloss confirms the court will address unpreserved claims, but only under the tighter standards and harmlessness inquiry.

Takeaway for advocates
- Appellees should not broaden or recast an appellant’s claims on appeal; the court may criticize or disregard such widening of the issues.
- Appellants ought to preserve and clearly present the claims they want the court to decide; preserved claims get the ordinary standard of review, whereas unpreserved claims will be subject to plain‑error/harmlessness review.
- Even when an appellee advances a new framing, the court may nonetheless resolve the issue on the law and facts, but the appellee’s approach is frowned upon and may carry less persuasive force.
