Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### How does the Eighth Circuit distinguish intrinsic evidence from Rule 404(b) in prior possession-of-the-same-gun cases?
Question:
The opinion treats D.F. and C.F.'s testimony that they saw Cutbank with the same sawed-off rifle previously as 'intrinsic' rather than 404(b) propensity evidence, citing United States v. Battle. I'm confused about the line the Eighth Circuit draws here: when is prior possession of the same firearm considered intrinsic (and thus admissible without 404(b) analysis) versus extrinsic 404(b) evidence? What facts typically make prior sightings of the same weapon 'directly relevant' to proving later possession rather than impermissible character evidence?
Answer:
Short answer
The Eighth Circuit treats testimony that a witness previously saw the defendant with the very same, identifiable gun as “intrinsic” when that testimony is directly probative of an element of the charged offense (ownership, control, or continuous possession of that particular weapon), not as Rule 404(b) propensity evidence. United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014) supplies the governing principle: prior possession of a firearm can be “directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon.” United States v. Cutbank, No. 24‑1225, slip op. at § II.A–C (8th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025).

Legal doctrine / standard
- Intrinsic vs. extrinsic (404(b)). Evidence is intrinsic (not subject to 404(b) restriction) when it is (1) inextricably intertwined with the charged offense or (2) directly probative of a fact that is an element of the charged offense (identity, intent, motive, ownership, control, continuity of possession, etc.). If the evidence does only what propensity evidence does—showing bad character or conviction-prone behavior—then it is extrinsic and must satisfy the 404(b) framework.
- The Eighth Circuit applies Battle: prior possession of the same firearm is intrinsic when it helps establish ownership or control of that weapon at the time of the charged offense because it connects the defendant to the specific weapon later recovered.

How Cutbank applies those principles (what made the sightings intrinsic)
The opinion shows the concrete facts that moved the prior sightings into the “intrinsic” bin:

- The witnesses identified the recovered weapon as the same distinctive sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle they had seen Cutbank possess before. Distinctiveness of the gun makes the prior sightings directly probative of identity/possession.
- Physical evidence linked the same weapon to the defendant (DNA on the gun and the gun was recovered along the path between the victim’s home and where Cutbank was found). The prior sightings therefore corroborated a chain tying Cutbank to that specific firearm.
- The testimony of prior sightings bore on an element of the offense (possession/control of the particular firearm) and on continuity of possession before the charged incident (D.F.: “a handful of times”; C.F.: “once or twice” before). Thus the testimony was offered to prove something other than general bad character.
- Because the testimony directly established a factual link between the defendant and the particular weapon—rather than simply saying “he’s violent” or “he carries guns”—the court treated it as intrinsic and not subject to 404(b) exclusion.

Practical factors that typically make prior sightings intrinsic rather than impermissible propensity evidence
When evaluating similar cases, look for these indicators that prior sightings are properly intrinsic:
- The weapon is identifiable or distinctive (model plus unusual alteration, sawed‑off configuration, serial-number alteration, markings) so prior sightings are probative that it was the same gun.
- There is independent physical or circumstantial evidence connecting that same recovered weapon to the defendant at or near the charged event (DNA, fingerprints, recovery location matching the defendant’s route, stolen/unique features).
- The testimony goes to identity, ownership, control, or continuity of possession of the specific gun (an element or directly relevant factual predicate), not to the defendant’s general propensity for violence.
- Temporal and spatial proximity that makes prior possession relevant to proving possession at the charged time (e.g., saw him with it repeatedly before, and it was later recovered where he fled).
- The prior sightings are limited in scope (who saw it, how often) and are used to prove a discrete, material fact—not to show the defendant is a bad person.

When the evidence becomes extrinsic/404(b)
- If the prior weapon sightings are too remote, the weapon not identifiable as the same, or there is no independent link between defendant and the recovered gun, the testimony risks being only character propensity evidence.
- If the proffered testimony’s only logical relevance is to show the defendant’s criminal propensity (e.g., “he often carries guns, so he must have had it now”), the court should treat it as extrinsic and require 404(b) analysis (purpose, relevance independent of propensity, Rule 403 balancing).

Bottom line
Cutbank follows Battle: prior sightings of the same, identifiable firearm are intrinsic when they directly help prove possession/ownership/control of that specific weapon at the time of the charged offense. Distinctiveness of the gun, corroborating physical/circumstantial links, and the testimony’s focus on identity/continuity of possession (not character) are the usual facts that justify treating such testimony as intrinsic rather than 404(b) evidence.
