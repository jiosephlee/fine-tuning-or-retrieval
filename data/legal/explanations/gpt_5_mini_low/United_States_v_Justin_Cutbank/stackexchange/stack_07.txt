Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### What level of explanation must a district judge give when imposing a within-Guidelines sentence to show consideration of § 3553(a)?
Question:
The panel presumes reasonableness for a within-range sentence and says no 'mechanical recitation' is required so long as the record shows the court considered § 3553(a). How much engagement with defense mitigation is required in the Eighth Circuit? If the court references reviewing the PSR and memoranda and mentions some § 3553(a) factors, is that sufficient even when the defense argues the range is inflated by enhancements? Are there cases where a within-Guidelines sentence was reversed for inadequate explanation?
Answer:
Short answer
- In the Eighth Circuit a district court imposing a within‑Guidelines sentence must correctly calculate the Guidelines and must show (through the record) that it considered the § 3553(a) factors, but it need not give a “mechanical recitation” or engage in detailed, factor‑by‑factor analysis. United States v. Cutbank applies the presumption of reasonableness for a within‑range sentence and holds that a statement that the court reviewed the PSR and sentencing memoranda and discussed § 3553(a) factors is sufficient where the record shows the court was aware of the defendant’s mitigation arguments. (Cutbank, slip op. § III.)

What the doctrine requires (doctrinal roadmap)
1. Correct Guidelines calculation first. The district court must “begin all sentencing proceedings by correctly calculating the applicable Guidelines range.” Gall v. United States, quoted in Cutbank. If the Guidelines are miscalculated, procedural error follows and appellate review is triggered. (Cutbank § III.)
2. Consideration of § 3553(a). After computing the range, the court must consider the § 3553(a) factors and then may impose a within‑range sentence; such a sentence is presumed reasonable on appeal. Cutbank cites that presumption (Goodhouse) and explains that the appellate court will uphold a within‑range sentence if the record makes plain the district court considered § 3553(a). (Cutbank § III.)
3. No formulaic recitation required. The Eighth Circuit does not demand a rote, factor‑by‑factor explanation. It is enough that the record shows the court actually considered the factors; the court may accomplish this by indicating it reviewed the PSR, memoranda, and that it weighed relevant § 3553(a) considerations. Cutbank relies on Masood and Maluoth for that point. (Cutbank § III; citing Maluoth, 121 F.4th at 1164; Masood, 133 F.4th at 809.)

How much engagement with defense mitigation (including attacks on enhancements) is required
- The district court must be aware of the defendant’s mitigation arguments and consider them, but need not write a detailed rebuttal to every point. In Cutbank the court expressly reviewed the PSR and the parties’ sentencing memoranda and reviewed § 3553(a) factors; the panel concluded that, given the court’s awareness of Cutbank’s arguments (including his challenge to a four‑level enhancement), the presumption applied and the sentence was reasonable. The court framed this as: “Thus, the district court was aware of [the defendant’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” (Cutbank § III.)
- Put another way: if the record shows the court knew the defense contention (for example, that the Guidelines range was inflated by an enhancement) and the court indicates it considered the PSR/memos and § 3553(a), that will generally be sufficient in the Eighth Circuit to uphold a within‑Guidelines sentence.

Were there cases reversing a within‑Guidelines sentence for inadequate explanation?
- Cutbank does not identify a reversal for mere failure to recite § 3553(a) factors mechanically; instead it emphasizes that procedural errors like failing to calculate the Guidelines properly (Rosales‑Mireles) or other significant procedural errors can trigger reversal. Cutbank cites Maluoth and Masood for the proposition that a non‑mechanical, record‑based showing of consideration is adequate. The opinion therefore treats inadequate explanation in the sense of a missing record as a reversible procedural error, but does not point to a case in which a within‑range sentence was reversed solely because the court did not engage at length with mitigation—rather, the appellate rule is to look for a record showing consideration of § 3553(a).

Practical takeaways for practitioners
- Preserve the record. Make sure defense arguments about why an enhancement inflates the range and about mitigating § 3553(a) factors are in the sentencing filings (memo, objections to PSR) and stated on the record at sentencing.
- Obtain an on‑the‑record acknowledgment. If the district judge does not explicitly address an important mitigation argument, ask the court to state on the record that it has considered the PSR, submissions, and the specific § 3553(a) arguments—this avoids reliance on a bare, conclusory sentence that could be attacked as not clearly showing consideration.
- Don’t expect a written factor‑by‑factor analysis. In the Eighth Circuit a concise statement that the court reviewed the PSR/memoranda and considered § 3553(a) will ordinarily suffice for a within‑Guidelines sentence so long as the record makes the court’s awareness of and consideration of the defendant’s contentions clear. (Cutbank § III.)

References from the opinion
- Cutbank § III (presumption of reasonableness for within‑range sentences; court noted it “reviewed the PSR and sentencing memoranda and reviewed § 3553(a) factors”; reliance on Masood and Maluoth)
- Gall v. United States (sentencing framework)
- Rosales‑Mireles v. United States (need to calculate Guidelines before appellate substantive‑reasonableness review)
- United States v. Goodhouse; United States v. Masood; United States v. Maluoth (as cited in Cutbank)
