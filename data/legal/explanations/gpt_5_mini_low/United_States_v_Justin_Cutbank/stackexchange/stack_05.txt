Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Can the court rely on uncharged conduct in the PSR to apply § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), and are there any Apprendi/Confrontation concerns?
Question:
The four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm 'in connection with another felony offense' was applied based on PSR facts that were adopted without objection. What evidentiary standard applies at federal sentencing for such uncharged conduct (preponderance?), and is the court permitted to rely solely on the PSR absent live testimony if the defendant doesn't object? Also, does Apprendi/Booker require jury findings for this enhancement when it increases the advisory range but not the statutory maximum, and are there any Sixth Amendment or Confrontation Clause limits on using hearsay within the PSR?
Answer:
Short answer
- Sentencing findings about uncharged conduct are judged by the preponderance standard and the district court may rely on facts in an adopted PSR (even if those facts describe uncharged conduct), provided the defendant does not timely object. United States v. Cutbank treats the PSR’s adopted facts as a proper basis for applying USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).
- Apprendi/Booker do not require a jury finding here because the enhancement changed only the advisory Guidelines range, not the statutory maximum. The Sixth Amendment jury-trial rule applies only when a judge’s factfinding increases the statutory maximum sentence.
- Confrontation/hearsay concerns are limited where the declarant testifies (Confrontation satisfied) and, more generally at sentencing, courts may rely on hearsay in the PSR unless the defendant preserves a timely objection.

Explanation and authority from the opinion

1) Evidentiary standard and use of uncharged conduct
Cutbank confirms that a district court may apply USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) based on conduct “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained” (quoting the guideline comment). The court approved the district court’s use of PSR facts that were “adopted, without objection,” to find the firearm was possessed “in connection with another felony.” Op. § III (sentencing issue) (noting comment n.14(C) and that the district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements without objection).

Although Cutbank does not recite the standard formula in the opinion, the court’s citation to United States v. Dunn for the standard of review (and the usual Sentencing Guideline practice) is consistent with the established rule that sentencing courts make factual findings by a preponderance of the evidence when determining guideline enhancements based on conduct not charged or proven to a jury. Cutbank treats the PSR adoption as a sufficient factual basis, and affirms that adopting the PSR without objection defeats a later attack on those factual findings.

Practical takeaway from Cutbank:
- If the PSR’s facts are adopted by the district court and the defendant does not object, the court may rely on them to apply § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).
- If the defendant objects, the district court must resolve the dispute (typically under the preponderance standard).

2) Apprendi / Booker / Sixth Amendment
Cutbank’s sentencing enhancement altered only the advisory Guidelines range; the district court imposed a within-Guidelines sentence. The opinion explains the proper sentencing sequence (calculate Guidelines, consider § 3553(a)) and treats the Guidelines calculation and enhancement as matters the judge can resolve in sentencing. The court does not treat this enhancement as requiring a jury trial because it did not increase the statutory maximum; instead it affected only the advisory range. See Op. § III (discussing Guidelines calculation and that the district court properly applied the enhancement and imposed a within-range sentence).

That follows Apprendi/Booker principles: Apprendi requires jury findings only when judge-made facts increase the statutory maximum. Where a finding only changes the advisory Guidelines range (and not the statutory maximum), Booker left the Guidelines advisory and judge factfinding at sentencing is permissible.

3) Confrontation Clause and hearsay in the PSR
Cutbank directly addresses Confrontation concerns earlier: the court held the Confrontation Clause was satisfied “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” Op. II.B. The opinion also notes the district court “did not admit hearsay during the trial” and that D.F. testified, so Confrontation was satisfied. Applied to sentencing, the opinion’s approach supports two points:
- If a declarant appears and testifies at trial, Confrontation Clause objections regarding that testimony are resolved.
- More generally, the opinion treats reliance on PSR material adopted without objection as permissible. Sentencing practice routinely allows judges to consider hearsay in PSRs; Cutbank illustrates that absent a timely objection, the court may adopt and rely on PSR statements that describe uncharged conduct.

Bottom line
Cutbank affirms the standard and practical rules you summarized: (1) courts may find uncharged conduct by a preponderance at sentencing and rely on an adopted PSR; (2) Apprendi/Booker do not require jury findings for an enhancement that only raises the advisory Guidelines range; and (3) Confrontation problems are avoided when declarants testify and, generally, a court may rely on PSR hearsay unless the defendant preserves an objection. The opinion explicitly rests the enhancement on PSR facts “adopted, without objection,” and affirms the sentence.
