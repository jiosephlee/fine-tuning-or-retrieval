Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Did admitting testimony that the victim’s sister was killed by a gun risk Rule 403 unfair prejudice?
Question:
On cross, defense impeached D.F. with prior inconsistent statements about the gun; in explaining her discomfort with guns, she said her sister was killed by a gun. The court denied a motion to strike, and the panel found no unfair prejudice. Under Rule 403, how do courts weigh probative value (explaining inconsistencies/bias) against the risk the jury will be inflamed by a sympathetic, unrelated death? Are there Eighth Circuit cases addressing similar 'context-of-witness-bias' facts that still warranted exclusion or a limiting instruction?
Answer:
Short answer
No reversible Rule 403 error. The Eighth Circuit held that D.F.’s statement that her sister “was killed by a gun” was responsive to cross‑examination about prior inconsistent statements and probative of D.F.’s unfamiliarity/bias about guns; the court found no unfair prejudice and declined to reverse. The district court’s ruling was within its broad discretion and any alleged error would be harmless in light of the other strong evidence.

Why — the governing balancing and how it was applied here
- Rule 403 requires excluding evidence only when its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusion, or misleading the jury. The inquiry is fact‑specific and reviewed for abuse of discretion. (See the opinion’s reliance on Fed. R. Evid. 403 and the deferential standard.)
- Context matters: testimony that explains why a witness made inconsistent statements or possessed a particular bias is highly probative of credibility. The panel treated D.F.’s remark as exactly that—an explanation for why she was “not familiar with guns” and therefore gave inconsistent answers—so its probative value on impeachment/credibility was significant.
- The opinion notes procedural facts that reduce the danger of unfair prejudice here: the testimony was responsive to cross; defense did not point to any specific prejudice or confusion beyond a blanket objection; C.F. later mentioned the same fact without objection; and the court’s broad evidentiary discretion governs. Given those features, the panel found “none apparent” under Rule 403 and affirmed. (Op. Part II.D.)

What the opinion cites on reviewability and harmlessness
- The court applied the usual deferential abuse‑of‑discretion standard for evidentiary rulings (citing Bennett) and emphasized that even a mistaken ruling will not require reversal unless the defendant shows the jury was substantially swayed by the evidence. (Op. at II.D & concluding paragraph citing DeMarce and Keys.)
- Here, the record contained substantial testimonial and physical evidence (DNA on the rifle, eyewitness ID, phone found on Cutbank’s likely path, etc.), so any alleged prejudice did not meet the threshold for reversal as not substantially influencing the verdict. (Op. at II, concluding paragraphs.)

Are there Eighth Circuit cases that required exclusion in similar “context of witness bias” situations?
- The Cutbank opinion does not cite an Eighth Circuit decision excluding similar testimony as unduly prejudicial when the testimony was offered to explain inconsistent statements or bias. Instead, the opinion applies precedent favoring admissibility of evidence intrinsic to credibility and reiterates the trial court’s broad discretion.
- The opinion does cite Battle (on intrinsic evidence of possession) and Cumbie (on authentication for extrinsic impeachment), and relies on Bennett for the standard of review; it does not identify a contrary Eighth Circuit holding where a factually similar, responsive statement about a relative’s death was excluded under Rule 403.

Practical takeaways for trial practice
- If a witness offers a brief, directly responsive explanation for prior inconsistencies (including emotionally charged background that bears on bias or unfamiliarity), courts in this circuit are likely to find probative value outweighs prejudice and admit it.
- To attack such testimony you should: (1) articulate specific unfair‑prejudice risks (sympathy that would overwhelm the jury, confusion on an element, or collateral narrative unrelated to impeachment), (2) seek a limiting instruction at the time of admission, and (3) preserve the issue and show how the evidence could have substantially swayed the jury given the rest of the record.

Bottom line
Cutbank treats the sister’s death statement as probative impeachment/context and finds no Rule 403 problem, stressing deference to the trial court and harmless‑error principles. The opinion does not point to an Eighth Circuit decision compelling exclusion of analogous testimony under Rule 403.
