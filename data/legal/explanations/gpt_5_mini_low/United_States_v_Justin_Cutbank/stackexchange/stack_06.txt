Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### How do ACCA’s § 4B1.4 offense levels interact with § 2K2.1 and specific offense characteristics without double counting?
Question:
The court notes the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would produce an offense level of 34, but the court used a higher total offense level (35) after applying § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) and other enhancements, because the Guidelines say use the greater offense level. Is it standard to calculate both paths (ACCA § 4B1.4 vs. § 2K2.1 with SOCs) and choose the higher? When do enhancements like altered/obliterated serial number or reckless endangerment during flight risk impermissible double counting with § 4B1.4? Any Eighth Circuit authority clarifying the boundaries?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. Under the Guidelines you calculate the §4B1.4 (Armed Career Criminal Guideline) offense level and you also calculate the §2K2.1 base level plus any applicable specific‑offense characteristics (SOCs); USSG §4B1.4(b) then directs you to use the greater of the two offense levels. The Eighth Circuit in Cutbank applied that rule: the court noted the ACCA guideline would have produced level 34 but the §2K2.1 path with SOCs produced level 35, and the district court properly used the greater level. See Cutbank, slip op. §III (discussing USSG §4B1.4(b)).

Legal doctrine and how it operates
- Two parallel calculations. When a defendant qualifies as an armed career criminal, the Guidelines require the district court to do both computations: (1) calculate the Chapter Four career‑offender/ACCA guideline offense level under §4B1.4, and (2) calculate the offense level under the substantive guideline (here §2K2.1) including any applicable SOCs. USSG §4B1.4(b) controls and the court uses the greater offense level. Cutbank explicitly follows that rule (opinion §III).
- No automatic exclusion of SOCs by the ACCA guideline. The ACCA guideline does not itself say you ignore the §2K2.1 enhancements; it says you compare and take the higher. That is why the district court in Cutbank applied the §2K2.1 SOCs (altered serial number, reckless endangerment in flight, possession in connection with another felony) and reached a higher total offense level than the §4B1.4 calculation, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed (Cutbank §III).
- Double‑counting concern. The constitutional/Guidelines principle is that a district court should not impermissibly punish the same conduct twice. Whether a particular SOC “double counts” depends on whether it is effectively duplicative of the conduct already accounted for in the career‑offender/ACCA calculation. The Eighth Circuit reviews application of contested sentencing enhancements for clear error to the facts and for abuse of discretion as to legal questions. In Cutbank, the court accepted the PSR’s factual findings (adopted without objection) that supported the §2K2.1 SOCs and did not find impermissible double counting; it upheld the added levels and explained that the Guidelines themselves instruct using the greater level if the §2K2.1 path yields a higher number (Cutbank §III).

Eighth Circuit authority and limits (as reflected in Cutbank)
- Cutbank: district court adopted PSR facts, applied §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) and other SOCs to reach level 35, noted §4B1.4(b) requires choosing the greater offense level, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed. The court did not conclude that those SOCs were impermissibly duplicative of the ACCA enhancement; rather, it treated them as separate guideline adjustments supported by the PSR (Cutbank §III).
- Related Eighth Circuit guidance cited in the opinion: Dunn (standard of review for fact findings at sentencing), and general sentencing principles from Gall and Rosales‑Mireles about correct Guidelines calculation and consideration of §3553(a) factors (Cutbank §III).

Practical takeaways for sentencing practice
- Preserve objections and develop the record. If you believe a SOC duplicates the same conduct accounted for by §4B1.4 (or by the ACCA predicate findings), develop factual distinctions in the PSR and at sentencing and object concretely so the district court can rule—Cutbank shows courts will rely on the PSR when adopted without objection.
- Frame double‑counting challenges around distinct factual bases. To succeed, show the SOC punishes precisely the same conduct or element already incorporated in the career‑offender calculation rather than a separate manner, means, or harm. General disagreement about severity is unlikely to carry the day.
- If the §2K2.1 path yields a higher number, remind the court that USSG §4B1.4(b) requires using the greater offense level (as the Cutbank court did).

Bottom line
It is standard and required by the Guidelines to compute both paths and apply the higher offense level (USSG §4B1.4(b)); double‑counting challenges must show the SOC is truly duplicative of the career‑offender/ACCA calculation, and the Eighth Circuit in Cutbank affirmed application of §2K2.1 SOCs where the PSR supported distinct factual findings and the Guidelines’ “use the greater” rule applied.
