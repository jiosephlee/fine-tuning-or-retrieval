Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Impeachment by Police Reports and Appellate Discipline: Authentication Matters

## Why this decision matters

United States v. Cutbank (8th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025) is a compact but useful decision for trial practitioners about two recurring—but sometimes overlooked—areas of criminal practice: the mechanics of using third‑party police reports to impeach witness testimony, and appellate etiquette (and preservation). The panel affirmed a district court that refused to let defense counsel read portions of a police report into evidence to impeach a witness because the report lacked the necessary foundation and authentication. The court relied on its recent line of authority (including United States v. Cumbie) requiring authentication for extrinsic impeachment evidence. And, in a moment of admonishment, the panel chided the government for broadening the issues on appeal—an instructive reminder about appellate framing and preservation. Cutbank also touches, briefly, on the limits of Rule 403 when emotionally freighted testimony is offered in response to cross‑examination.

The lesson for trial lawyers is practical: you cannot expect to treat a police report as a self‑authenticating slab of impeachment material. If you want to use someone else’s report to impeach, you must lay the foundation for admissibility—or else risk the judge sustaining an objection and losing a tactical opportunity. On appeal, you should also keep your question-set tight and respect preservation rules; courts do not reward litigants who expand the record or press new claims post hoc.

## The ruling and the court’s reasoning

At trial, defense counsel attempted to impeach government witness C.F. by reading portions of a police report that contained C.F.’s prior statements. The district court allowed questions about what C.F. told police but sustained government objections to counsel’s effort to read the police report itself into the record because the report was not authored by C.F. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit affirmed. It relied on United States v. Cumbie, which the panel cites for the proposition that extrinsic impeachment evidence—such as a written report containing a prior inconsistent statement—must be authenticated. Because the defense did not authenticate the police report (and did not secure an appropriate foundation for its admission as an adoptive or recorded recollection, or otherwise meet a hearsay exception or the requirements of Rule 613/801(d)(1)(A) for prior consistent/inconsistent statements), the district court did not abuse its discretion by excluding the reading of the report.

The court also noted an appellate‑practice point: the government, in its brief, argued a related claim about the district court “thwart[ing]” the defense’s cross‑examination, and the panel rebuked appellees who expand issues on appeal—implicitly reminding litigants to brief only the preserved claims. Finally, although unrelated to the impeachment ruling, the panel affirmed a district court’s refusal to strike testimony about the victim’s sister having been killed by a gun, concluding the testimony was responsive to cross‑examination and not unduly prejudicial under Rule 403.

## Practical takeaways for trial counsel

1. Authenticate before you impeach with a third‑party report. If you intend to use a police report (or any third‑party record that contains a witness’s prior statements) as extrinsic impeachment, build a foundation during cross: get the witness to confirm she made the statements contained in the report, identify the report, and either produce the witness’s statement in a form that satisfies Rule 801(d)(1)(A) (prior inconsistent statement given under oath at a prior proceeding or otherwise adopted by the witness) or authenticate the document under Fed. R. Evid. 901. If the report is not the witness’s own writing or a recorded recollection, authentication can be accomplished by establishing the report’s origin and linking its contents to the declarant.

2. Use the declarant, not the author, when possible. The safer route is to have the declarant (the person whose out‑of‑court statement appears in the report) testify and adopt, deny, or explain the prior statement. Once the witness acknowledges making the statement, the contents are easier to offer as impeachment (and, depending on circumstances, may be admissible substantively under Rule 801(d)(1)(A)). If the declarant cannot remember or denies making the statement, be prepared to lay the foundation for extrinsic evidence under Rule 613(b): show that the matter is truly inconsistent and that the witness was given an opportunity to explain or deny the prior statement.

3. Consider hearsay exceptions and form. If the police report contains the witness’s own assertions but is itself not a statement of the witness (e.g., the officer’s narrative of what others said), then the report may be double hearsay. You should consider alternate routes: redact the parts that are hearsay‑within‑hearsay, offer only those portions that quote the witness verbatim and can be authenticated, or seek admission as a recorded recollection (Fed. R. Evid. 803(5)) if the witness once had knowledge and cannot now recall sufficiently well.

4. Be ready to authenticate under Rule 901. Authentication can be simple (testimony that the document is what it purports to be) or more elaborate (certificates, chain of custody, testimony from the report’s author). Don’t wait until the eleventh hour—prepare a witness (or a sponsor) who can authenticate the report if you plan to use it.

5. If you lose the bid at trial, preserve the precise grounds of your objection and the theory of admissibility for appeal. Cutbank’s brief indicates the defense questioned preservation; courts expect a clear objection and an articulated theory to preserve claims for appellate review.

## Mitigating prejudicial but context‑linked testimony

The court in Cutbank also affirmed the district court’s decision not to strike testimony that D.F. (the victim) mentioned her sister had been killed by a gun. The Eighth Circuit treated that testimony as responsive to cross‑examination about why D.F. was “not familiar with guns” and did not view it as unduly prejudicial under Rule 403. Trial judges have broad discretion under Rule 403, and appellate courts will not lightly disturb rulings that emotionally charged testimony was properly admitted when it bears on credibility or motive.

Defense counsel should anticipate these dynamics. If a witness offers emotionally laden testimony in response to inquiry, consider seeking a limiting instruction that confines the jury’s use of the testimony to credibility or context. If the testimony is unfairly prejudicial and not probative, move to strike or move for a mistrial if the impact is severe. But be mindful that courts will tolerate probative emotional context, especially when it rebuts a credibility attack or explains a witness’s behavior.

## On appellate practice: do not expand the battlefield

Cutbank includes a pointed line about appellees who “unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal.” The government argued a related—but not precisely preserved—claim about cross‑examination and the district court’s rulings; the panel disapproved. That rebuke is a useful reminder: appellate panels expect disciplined briefs that confine themselves to preserved errors and the arguments the parties actually litigated below. Raising new theories on appeal, or enlarging the scope of an opponent’s arguments, risks dismissal of the point and, as here, an admonition in the opinion. Doctrinally it doesn’t change the law, but practically it signals that appellate goodwill is partially a function of clear, focused advocacy.

## Bottom line

Cutbank reinforces a straightforward but sometimes forgotten truth: extrinsic impeachment evidence that comes in the form of third‑party reports must be authenticated before you may read it into the record. Use the declarant where possible, build authentication in advance, and be prepared to show how the evidence fits an admissible impeachment route. At the same time, litigants should respect the contours of appellate advocacy: preserve your theory below and limit your appellate arguments to the preserved, clearly presented issues. Finally, emotional testimony tied to credibility or context will usually survive a Rule 403 challenge; plan for limiting instructions, but don’t assume a strike will be automatic.
