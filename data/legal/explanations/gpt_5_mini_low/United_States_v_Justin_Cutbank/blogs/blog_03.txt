Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Stacking Enhancements in an ACCA Case: §2K2.1(b)(6)(B), §4B1.4, and the Presumption of Reasonableness

Why this case matters
United States v. Cutbank is a useful, if unsurprising, reminder about three things that often determine outcomes in violent-firearm sentences: (1) the Sentencing Guidelines regime still prizes an accurate numerical calculation of offense level, (2) the Guidelines themselves tell sentencing courts which enhancement wins when multiple rules could apply, and (3) a within-Guidelines sentence enjoys a strong presumption of reasonableness on appeal. Cutbank—an ACCA case arising from a violent episode in which the defendant held a sawed-off .22 at gunpoint and later resisted arrest—presents a textbook contest between a § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) four-level enhancement (possession “in connection with another felony”), the separate ACCA enhancement via § 4B1.4, and a defense argument that applying both (or the higher numerical result) produces an unjust sentence even when the underlying conduct is brutal.

The Eighth Circuit affirmed. Its reasoning is straightforward: the district court adopted the PSR’s facts, applied the applicable Guidelines, chose the greater offense level as § 4B1.4 directs, and sentenced within the resulting advisory range. Because the calculation was correct and the court considered § 3553(a) factors, the within-Guidelines sentence is presumed reasonable and survives substantive-review challenge.

What the court did (and why)
The factual record the PSR adopted was strong: witness identifications linking Cutbank to the sawed-off rifle, DNA on the weapon, and violent conduct in the victim’s home culminating in a flight and struggle with police. The PSR applied several increases under USSG § 2K2.1: altered serial number (+3), reckless creation of risk while fleeing (+2), and the contested +4 for possession “in connection with” another felony under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). Separately, ACCA’s treatment produced a slightly lower offense level under USSG § 4B1.4(b). The Guidelines themselves resolve that tension: where multiple offense levels could apply, the Guidelines instruct the court to use the greater offense level. The district court did so; the Eighth Circuit treated the calculation as procedural correctness and therefore the prerequisite to appellate review of substantive reasonableness.

On appeal, Cutbank argued both that the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement was improper and that, even if the Guidelines calculation was “correct,” applying it produced a sentence inconsistent with the parsimony directive of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). The panel rejected both points. It viewed the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) finding as supported by the PSR and admissible facts adopted at sentencing, and it declined to do more than presume the district court considered the § 3553(a) factors when imposing a within-Guidelines sentence.

Analytical perspective: double-counting, uncharged conduct, and nexus
Three doctrinal flashpoints arise from Cutbank.

First, double-counting concerns. Defendants frequently raise the specter that applying multiple enhancements “double-counts” the same criminal behavior. The Eighth Circuit’s treatment follows prevailing practice: so long as each guideline provision targets a different aspect of culpability or the Guidelines themselves resolve which provision governs (as § 4B1.4(b) does here), the court will not disturb the calculation. Cutbank does not, and could not, present an argument that the Guidelines’ internal ordering is arbitrary; the panel accepts the Guidelines’ command to apply the higher offense level.

Second, the permissibility of considering uncharged conduct. Application Note 14(C) to § 2K2.1 expressly permits the enhancement when a firearm is possessed “in connection with another felony offense, regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” The Court of Appeals reaffirms that sentencing fact-finding may rest on the PSR’s adopted facts and that sentencing courts can rely on uncharged conduct—so long as it is supported in the record and the defendant had a chance to contest it. That doctrine has been settled for years, but Cutbank is another illustration: the DNA and witness identifications created a solid factual predicate for the district court’s finding that the weapon was tied to broader criminality beyond mere possession.

Third, the nexus requirement. § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) requires a connection between the firearm and “another felony offense.” The most vulnerable defense strategy in cases like Cutbank is to challenge that causal or temporal nexus: argue the firearm was merely present and unrelated to an independent felony, or that the PSR’s recitation conflates separate conduct. Cutbank’s record—witnesses who saw the defendant with the gun before the incident, the gun found along the path between crime scenes, and DNA on the weapon—made such a challenge difficult, and the Eighth Circuit deferred to the district court’s factual finding.

The role of the presumption of reasonableness
Cutbank underscores how the presumption of reasonableness for Guidelines-range sentences operates in practice. The panel treated accurate guideline calculation as a threshold procedural requirement; once that is met, the appellate inquiry into substantive reasonableness is deferential. The defendant argued that stacking the § 2K2.1 enhancement atop ACCA’s framework produced a sentence incompatible with § 3553(a)’s “no greater than necessary” mandate. The court was unmoved: the district court reviewed the PSR and expressly considered § 3553(a) arguments, and because the sentence fell within the correctly calculated Guidelines range, appellate review starts from a position of deference.

Doctrinal and practical implications
Cutbank will not reshape major doctrinal contours, but it sends practical signals to defense counsel and sentencing courts.

- For prosecutors: Cutbank is a green light to pursue § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancements where the PSR and record show a firearm was connected to other felony conduct, even if prosecutors do not—or cannot—bring separate charges. Application Note 14(C) remains an effective hook for increasing offense levels.

- For defense lawyers: the decision highlights the importance of contesting factual predicates early and with specificity. Because appellate courts give weight to adopted PSR facts and defer on factual findings at sentencing, evidentiary fights over nexus and authentication need to be waged before sentencing or preserved with clear objections. Also, it demonstrates that urging § 3553(a) parsimony after a correct guideline calculation is a high bar; appellate courts will presume the within-Guidelines result reasonable absent a clear sign the district court failed to consider relevant factors or committed procedural error.

- For judges: Cutbank is a reminder to make a record that shows active consideration of § 3553(a) factors. The Eighth Circuit reiterates that a “mechanical recitation” is unnecessary, but a visible engagement with mitigation arguments improves the durability of a sentence on appeal.

Downstream effects and open questions
Cutbank fits into a broader line of cases in which courts accept sentencing increases based on uncharged conduct and adhere to Guidelines’ internal hierarchies. It does not resolve tougher issues that sometimes arise with § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)—for example, close cases where the alleged “other felony” is attenuated from the firearm conduct, or situations where multiple enhancements arguably punish the same element of criminality. Nor does it alter the continuing debate over the strength of the presumption of reasonableness; it simply applies the existing framework that Gall and its progeny established, showing that deference persists when the procedural box of correct guideline calculation is checked.

Strategic takeaways
If you represent defendants in firearm cases with messy factual records, Cutbank suggests four practical steps that matter:

1. Fight the PSR facts early. Make targeted objections and submit contrary evidence before sentencing.
2. Challenge nexus with specificity. Demonstrate temporal or causal disconnection between the firearm and the alleged “other felony.”
3. Preserve confrontation and authentication arguments at trial and at sentencing so appellate courts must entertain them.
4. Build a record on § 3553(a) considerations—emphasize mitigating circumstances, rehabilitation prospects, and disproportionate effects of stacked enhancements to give the district court concrete reasons to vary.

Conclusion
United States v. Cutbank is a predictable but clarifying decision: where the record supports a PSR’s factual findings and the Guidelines direct selection of the higher offense level, stacking a § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement atop an ACCA-driven calculation will survive review so long as the district court properly calculates the Guidelines and considers § 3553(a). The real battle in these cases remains at the fact-finding and sentencing stages: contest the facts, press nexus, and force a clear § 3553(a) record if you hope to overcome the powerful presumption that a within-Guidelines sentence is reasonable.
