Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

## ‘Context’ Testimony, Limiting Instructions, and the Confrontation Clause: A Quiet Win for Prosecutors

Why this matters: United States v. Cutbank (8th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025) is not a blockbuster confrontation-clause or hearsay decision, but it is a practical one. It reaffirms a routine — and important — prosecutorial tool: allowing police witnesses to recount a victim’s out-of-court statements as background to explain why officers acted as they did, while leaving resolution of any hearsay or confrontation concerns to the declarant’s later, in-court testimony. For defense counsel, Cutbank is a reminder that timing and preservation matter, and that courts have substantial discretion in whether and when to give limiting instructions. For practitioners focused on the line between “context” and substantive hearsay, the opinion is a useful, fairly defendant-friendly roadmap of what will — and what will not — get you reversal.

What the Eighth Circuit held, in short
Cutbank asked the district court, before trial, to instruct jurors not to treat the victim D.F.’s statements to police as substantive evidence of the defendant’s conduct unless and until D.F. herself testified. The district court declined to give a contemporaneous limiting instruction, opting instead to allow police testimony to describe the calls and officers’ observations and to rely on a customary instruction at the close of trial if needed. On appeal the Eighth Circuit affirmed, holding (1) a district court need not give a limiting instruction each time evidence is admitted if it provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial; (2) much of the officers’ testimony fell into non-hearsay “background” or “context” material explaining police conduct; and (3) because D.F. later testified at trial, Confrontation Clause concerns were satisfied under the court’s reading of United States v. Charboneau and related precedent.

Why the court’s reasoning is defensible (and familiar)
Two doctrinal strands run through the opinion. The first is federal evidentiary practice: courts routinely permit witnesses to provide the “context” of police action — what the officer heard on the 911 call, how the scene appeared, and why the officer did what he did — so long as the statements are not offered for the truth of the matter asserted. The Eighth Circuit leaned on its own precedent (Battle) about prior possession as intrinsic evidence and on the general proposition that contextual background is often non-hearsay. That approach is orthodox: admitting background statements can be essential for jurors to understand why an officer took certain investigative steps without using the statements to prove guilt.

The second strand is Confrontation Clause doctrine. The court relied on Charboneau (and the familiar rule that the Clause is satisfied when the declarant actually appears and testifies at trial) to reject any independent Sixth Amendment problem with officers’ recounting of the victim’s earlier statements. That is also orthodox: the problem the Clause protects against is untested testimonial hearsay offered for its truth at trial when the declarant cannot be cross-examined. When the declarant testifies, prior statements lose their Sixth Amendment sting — though they may still be hearsay if offered for their truth.

The Eighth Circuit’s practical posture — deference to trial judges on instructions — follows Bennett and its progeny. The court emphasized that a judge need not interrupt the flow of trial to give limiting instructions each time an item of evidence is admitted; a single, appropriate instruction at the close of trial will often do. The appellate court reviewed for abuse of discretion and found none.

Where the line between context and hearsay can be sharp
Cutbank confirms the generally permissive approach to “context” testimony, but it leaves the familiar, hard fact pattern unresolved: when will an officer’s recounting of a declarant’s statement cross from context into substantive hearsay? The answer remains fact-bound. Courts look at how the prosecutor uses the testimony and whether the statement was offered for the truth of the matter asserted (e.g., “the victim told me the defendant shot him” used to prove the shooting) versus to explain police conduct (e.g., “the officer went to the house because he received an anonymous report and heard commotion on arrival”).

Two practical points follow. First, prosecutors should draft direct examination to emphasize non-truth purposes when recounting out-of-court statements — to explain why police were on the scene or what the officers perceived — and avoid introducing the content in a way that invites use for its truth. Second, defense counsel should be prepared to object promptly when an officer’s narrative shifts toward substantive assertions (or when the statement is clearly testimonial and the declarant will not testify).

Charboneau and the Confrontation Clause: no surprise, but an important reminder
Cutbank’s Confrontation Clause claim failed because D.F. testified, thereby restoring the “confrontation” the Clause demands. This is a straightforward application of Charboneau: a live-witness appearance cures the Sixth Amendment problem raised by an officer’s earlier recounting of that witness’s statements. But appellate practice often hinges on the record: if the declarant is not called or the court permits an officer to read in testimonial content before any live testimony and the defendant is deprived of effective cross-examination, the analysis could tilt the other way. Cutbank is a reminder that once the declarant testifies, Confrontation Clause arguments become mainly about whether the jury was allowed to rely improperly on prior statements without proper limiting instruction — an evidentiary, not a constitutional, issue.

Harmless error and the real-world appellate hurdle
Even when a district court’s decision about limiting instructions or background testimony is arguably incorrect, Cutbank underscores the practical appellate reality: harmless-error rules are hard for appellants to overcome where the government’s case is strong. The Eighth Circuit pointed to substantial independent evidence — DNA on the rifle, eyewitness identifications, and the volatile facts at the residence and garage — to conclude any evidentiary blemishes did not substantially sway the jury. That is an important caveat for defense attorneys: preserving issues is essential, but even preserved errors may be insulated by the harmless-error standard if the record contains compelling independent evidence.

What defense counsel should do to preserve and sharpen these issues
Cutbank highlights several steps defense counsel should take to maximize the chances of appellate relief (or at least to narrow the scope of admitted background testimony):

- Make specific pretrial motions identifying the precise statements you want excluded and the exact grounds (Rule 403, Rule 404(b), hearsay, or Confrontation Clause) rather than broad, boilerplate requests. That gives the district court a focused record and gives an appellate court something concrete to review.

- Object contemporaneously and with specificity at trial to any officer testimony that shifts from context to substantive assertion, and request a limiting instruction on the record if the court declines. A contemporaneous objection is often necessary to preserve the claim and frames an appellate issue.

- If the declarant will not testify, press forcefully for a pretrial ruling on admissibility of testimonial statements and preserve argument that their admission would violate the Sixth Amendment. The live appearance of the declarant was dispositive in Cutbank; without it the outcome could differ.

- When the court denies a limiting instruction, ask for a contemporaneous curative instruction to reduce any chance of prejudice and to create a clearer appellate record.

- Where possible, isolate and litigate whether the officer’s proffered purpose for recounting the statement is genuine (explaining police conduct) rather than pretextual (using the officer as a conduit for testimonial hearsay).

Downstream effects and final takeaways
Cutbank will not alter evidentiary or confrontation doctrine, but it is a useful practitioner’s decision. It reaffirms that (1) district courts have broad discretion about when to give limiting instructions; (2) officers may recount victims’ statements as background for why they acted without creating per se hearsay or confrontation problems; and (3) a victim’s subsequent in-court testimony will generally cure confrontation concerns tied to those statements.

For prosecutors, the opinion is a green light for careful use of background testimony, provided the testimony is not offered for the truth of the matter asserted and the declarant can or will testify. For defense lawyers, the ruling is a reminder that success on appeal often requires a carefully preserved record, precise objections, and an eye toward harmless-error analysis. Cutbank is not a sweeping endorsement of unfettered testimonial repetition by officers, but it does confirm that courts will tolerate reasonable, context-driven testimony and will defer to trial judges’ judgment about the timing and wording of limiting instructions.
