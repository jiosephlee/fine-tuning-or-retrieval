Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

## Intrinsic or 404(b)? The Eighth Circuit’s Elastic Approach to Prior Firearm Possession

Why this matters: At the intersection of evidence law and firearm prosecutions lies a recurring question — when may the government introduce testimony that a witness saw a defendant with the very weapon at issue before the charged offense? In United States v. Justin Cutbank, an Eighth Circuit panel answered that question broadly in favor of the prosecution. Relying on United States v. Battle and the court’s longstanding “intrinsic evidence” approach, the panel treated prior-observation testimony about the sawed‑off Marlin .22 as direct proof of ownership, control, and continuity of possession rather than as Rule 404(b) propensity evidence. The decision is important for two reasons: it makes it easier for prosecutors to put on lay witnesses who knew the defendant and the weapon, and it highlights continuing doctrinal tensions about how far the intrinsic-evidence exception to Rule 404(b) should reach before it swallows the rule against propensity proof.

What the Eighth Circuit held and why: Cutbank was convicted of being a felon in possession of a firearm after testimony and physical evidence connected him to a sawed‑off rifle found along the path he likely used when fleeing a victim’s home. Pretrial, Cutbank moved to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had seen him with that rifle on prior occasions, arguing the testimony was impermissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b). The district court admitted the testimony, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed.

The court’s reasoning is straightforward and doctrinally familiar within the circuit. It treated the prior observations as intrinsic, not extrinsic, evidence: testimony that the witnesses had seen Cutbank with that same rifle "a handful of times" bore directly on ownership, control, and continuous possession — facts that make it more likely the defendant possessed the same weapon when the charged offense occurred. The panel quoted Battle to the effect that prior possession is “directly relevant” to later possession, and it stressed the district court’s broad discretion in balancing relevance and prejudice. The court also disposed of Cutbank’s other evidentiary complaints and rejected his sentencing argument; for our purposes the key takeaway is the Eighth Circuit’s expansive application of the intrinsic-evidence doctrine.

How this fits into the Rule 404(b) landscape: Rule 404(b) is framed as a backstop — it keeps out evidence of other crimes or wrongs when offered to prove a defendant’s propensity, while allowing such evidence for non‑propensity purposes (motive, intent, identity, absence of mistake, etc.). Courts have long recognized, however, that certain prior acts are not “other crimes” at all for Rule 404(b) purposes when they are inextricably intertwined with the charged conduct. The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Cutbank follows that tradition, emphasizing a pragmatic, context-driven inquiry: if the prior observation of the weapon helps establish identity, possession, or continuity, it is intrinsic and admissible.

That pragmatic posture has advantages. In many felon‑in‑possession cases, there is no direct contemporaneous admission of ownership; lay witness testimony that a defendant was known to have a particular firearm may be the most probative evidence of control. Treating such evidence as intrinsic prevents prosecutors from jumping through formalistic hoops and allows the jury to see the narrative arc that links defendant, weapon, and crime.

But there is a flip side: propensity creep. The more elastic the intrinsic exception becomes, the greater the risk that judges will admit prior-act evidence that essentially says only “the defendant is the sort of person who carried guns,” or that invites jurors to convict because of bad character rather than proof of the charged elements. The danger grows when prior acts are temporally remote, materially different in kind, or relevant only to the defendant’s general dangerousness rather than to identity or continuity with the specific weapon or episode.

Comparative circuit practice and doctrinal guardrails: The Eighth Circuit’s approach is not unique, but it is on the more permissive end of the spectrum. Other circuits have pressed back by imposing firmer limits on intrinsic evidence, particularly after the 2006 amendments to Rule 404(b) and subsequent caselaw that stressed careful articulation of non‑propensity purposes and Rule 403 balancing. In those jurisdictions, courts have required a closer temporal or modal relationship between the prior act and the charged offense, sometimes demanding that the prior act be necessary to make the charged behavior intelligible (for example, where the prior conduct explains the context of a co‑conspirator’s behavior or provides the only plausible explanation for identity).

What that means in practice is that admissibility often turns on fine facts: Is the prior observation of the weapon the same model, distinctive, or personally associated with the defendant? Is the witness describing repeated, recent possession or a single, remote encounter? Would exclusion unduly fragment the story the government must tell, or is the proffered evidence merely cumulative and prejudicial? The Eighth Circuit’s reliance on Battle suggests that when the prior possession is of the same weapon and probative of ownership or control, the balance tips toward admissibility.

Trial practice implications — for prosecutors and defense counsel: For prosecutors, Cutbank is an endorsement of a common-sense strategy: establish the continuity between the defendant and the seized weapon early and concretely. Lay testimony that a defendant habitually carried or stored a particular firearm, particularly one with distinguishing features (a sawed‑off stock, altered serial number, etc.), will often survive 404(b) challenge in the Eighth Circuit.

For defenders, the decision underscores the need to frame 404(b) challenges with particularity. Generic objections that prior‑act testimony is “propensity” evidence will not suffice. Instead, defense motions in limine should attack admissibility along multiple lines: challenge the nexus (is it truly the same weapon or a similar kind of weapon?), press for limiting instructions narrowly tailored to any permissible non‑propensity purpose, and force the government to articulate with specificity why the prior observations are necessary to prove identity, ownership, or continuity. Preserve objections on the record and separately raise Rule 403 prejudice concerns — the Eighth Circuit was explicit that harmless‑error review and the weight of other evidence will often defeat appellate reversal.

Downstream doctrinal effects and open questions: Cutbank reaffirms an Eighth Circuit pattern that will predictably keep the door open to prior‑possession testimony in many felon‑in‑possession prosecutions. That is likely to increase the evidentiary burdens on defense counsel to exclude or limit such testimony at the district-court level and to sharpen appellate litigation about the contours of “intrinsic” evidence. The decision also leaves unresolved questions that will energize future litigation: how distinct must a prior act be to count as propensity if offered without a clear ownership nexus? How should courts evaluate remoteness and cumulative prejudice when the government stitches together a narrative from multiple, scattered prior sightings?

Finally, Cutbank illustrates the continuing tension between evidentiary realism and protection against character‑based inference. The Eighth Circuit’s flexible test favors narrative completeness and prosecutorial efficiency; critics will say it risks diluting Rule 404(b)’s core safeguard against convicting for who a defendant is rather than what he did. Whether that balance will be sustained by the Supreme Court or recalibrated by other circuits remains to be seen.

Conclusion: United States v. Cutbank is a useful, if unsurprising, restatement of the Eighth Circuit’s view that prior observations of the same firearm are intrinsic and therefore admissible to prove possession, ownership, or continuity. Practitioners in the circuit should plan accordingly: prosecutors can confidently seek to place such testimony before juries, while defense counsel must make specific, contextualized evidentiary arguments and press for limiting instructions and rigorous Rule 403 scrutiny to blunt potential prejudice. For scholars and advocates attentive to the Rule 404(b) regime, the decision spotlights the ongoing struggle to draw principled lines between legitimate contextual proof and impermissible propensity evidence — a struggle that will shape the conduct and outcome of firearm prosecutions for years to come.
