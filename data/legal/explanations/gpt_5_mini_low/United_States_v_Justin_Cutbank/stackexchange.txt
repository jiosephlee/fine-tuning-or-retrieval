Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### How does the Eighth Circuit distinguish intrinsic evidence from Rule 404(b) in prior possession-of-the-same-gun cases?
Question:
The opinion treats D.F. and C.F.'s testimony that they saw Cutbank with the same sawed-off rifle previously as 'intrinsic' rather than 404(b) propensity evidence, citing United States v. Battle. I'm confused about the line the Eighth Circuit draws here: when is prior possession of the same firearm considered intrinsic (and thus admissible without 404(b) analysis) versus extrinsic 404(b) evidence? What facts typically make prior sightings of the same weapon 'directly relevant' to proving later possession rather than impermissible character evidence?
Answer:
Short answer
The Eighth Circuit treats testimony that a witness previously saw the defendant with the very same, identifiable gun as “intrinsic” when that testimony is directly probative of an element of the charged offense (ownership, control, or continuous possession of that particular weapon), not as Rule 404(b) propensity evidence. United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014) supplies the governing principle: prior possession of a firearm can be “directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon.” United States v. Cutbank, No. 24‑1225, slip op. at § II.A–C (8th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025).

Legal doctrine / standard
- Intrinsic vs. extrinsic (404(b)). Evidence is intrinsic (not subject to 404(b) restriction) when it is (1) inextricably intertwined with the charged offense or (2) directly probative of a fact that is an element of the charged offense (identity, intent, motive, ownership, control, continuity of possession, etc.). If the evidence does only what propensity evidence does—showing bad character or conviction-prone behavior—then it is extrinsic and must satisfy the 404(b) framework.
- The Eighth Circuit applies Battle: prior possession of the same firearm is intrinsic when it helps establish ownership or control of that weapon at the time of the charged offense because it connects the defendant to the specific weapon later recovered.

How Cutbank applies those principles (what made the sightings intrinsic)
The opinion shows the concrete facts that moved the prior sightings into the “intrinsic” bin:

- The witnesses identified the recovered weapon as the same distinctive sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle they had seen Cutbank possess before. Distinctiveness of the gun makes the prior sightings directly probative of identity/possession.
- Physical evidence linked the same weapon to the defendant (DNA on the gun and the gun was recovered along the path between the victim’s home and where Cutbank was found). The prior sightings therefore corroborated a chain tying Cutbank to that specific firearm.
- The testimony of prior sightings bore on an element of the offense (possession/control of the particular firearm) and on continuity of possession before the charged incident (D.F.: “a handful of times”; C.F.: “once or twice” before). Thus the testimony was offered to prove something other than general bad character.
- Because the testimony directly established a factual link between the defendant and the particular weapon—rather than simply saying “he’s violent” or “he carries guns”—the court treated it as intrinsic and not subject to 404(b) exclusion.

Practical factors that typically make prior sightings intrinsic rather than impermissible propensity evidence
When evaluating similar cases, look for these indicators that prior sightings are properly intrinsic:
- The weapon is identifiable or distinctive (model plus unusual alteration, sawed‑off configuration, serial-number alteration, markings) so prior sightings are probative that it was the same gun.
- There is independent physical or circumstantial evidence connecting that same recovered weapon to the defendant at or near the charged event (DNA, fingerprints, recovery location matching the defendant’s route, stolen/unique features).
- The testimony goes to identity, ownership, control, or continuity of possession of the specific gun (an element or directly relevant factual predicate), not to the defendant’s general propensity for violence.
- Temporal and spatial proximity that makes prior possession relevant to proving possession at the charged time (e.g., saw him with it repeatedly before, and it was later recovered where he fled).
- The prior sightings are limited in scope (who saw it, how often) and are used to prove a discrete, material fact—not to show the defendant is a bad person.

When the evidence becomes extrinsic/404(b)
- If the prior weapon sightings are too remote, the weapon not identifiable as the same, or there is no independent link between defendant and the recovered gun, the testimony risks being only character propensity evidence.
- If the proffered testimony’s only logical relevance is to show the defendant’s criminal propensity (e.g., “he often carries guns, so he must have had it now”), the court should treat it as extrinsic and require 404(b) analysis (purpose, relevance independent of propensity, Rule 403 balancing).

Bottom line
Cutbank follows Battle: prior sightings of the same, identifiable firearm are intrinsic when they directly help prove possession/ownership/control of that specific weapon at the time of the charged offense. Distinctiveness of the gun, corroborating physical/circumstantial links, and the testimony’s focus on identity/continuity of possession (not character) are the usual facts that justify treating such testimony as intrinsic rather than 404(b) evidence.

### Are victim statements admitted to explain police conduct non-hearsay, and when is a limiting instruction required?
Question:
The defendant asked for a preemptive instruction telling the jury not to consider D.F.'s statements to police for their truth until she testified. The court declined, and the panel says no hearsay was admitted and the Confrontation Clause was satisfied because D.F. testified. In the Eighth Circuit, when officers testify about out-of-court statements for 'context' or to explain their actions, is that categorically non-hearsay? How do courts police the line between 'background' and backdoor hearsay, and under what circumstances must a limiting instruction be given at the time of admission versus at the close of trial?
Answer:
Short answer
- No — Eighth Circuit law does not treat every officer testimony recounting a witness’s out‑of‑court statement as categorically non‑hearsay. Such testimony is non‑hearsay when (and only when) it is actually offered for a non‑truth purpose (e.g., to explain why officers did what they did or what they knew at the time).  
- Courts police the line by examining the purpose of the evidence, the context in which it was elicited, and whether the jury is likely to use the statement for its truth; if the risk of “backdoor” hearsay is real, the district court must take steps (redaction, contemporaneous limiting instruction, exclusion) to prevent misuse.  
- As to timing of limiting instructions: the district court has discretion. It need not give a preemptive instruction at the moment each item is admitted and may instead give an appropriate limiting instruction at the close of trial (Bennett). But the court should give a contemporaneous instruction or otherwise act immediately when the probative use is ambiguous or the potential for prejudice is substantial.

Explanation with reference to United States v. Cutbank
1) Non‑hearsay depends on the offered purpose, not a categorical rule
- Cutbank shows the Eighth Circuit follows the ordinary hearsay analysis: officer testimony recounting a victim’s statements can be non‑hearsay where the government offers it to explain police conduct or show the officer’s state of mind (i.e., why officers acted as they did), rather than to prove the truth of the victim’s assertions. The government in Cutbank argued the victim’s statements “would establish the context for police officer actions and their knowledge at the time,” and the court accepted that approach. The court concluded “the record demonstrates the court did not admit hearsay during the trial.” That is consistent with the standard hearsay rule: admissibility turns on the purpose for which the statement is admitted.

2) How courts police the “background” vs. backdoor‑hearsay line
- The district court must look beyond labels and assess whether the jury could reasonably treat the out‑of‑court statement as substantive proof of the facts asserted in it. In Cutbank the court found (and the panel agreed) that no hearsay was admitted and that the Confrontation Clause was satisfied because D.F. testified in person (citing Charboneau: Confrontation Clause satisfied “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person”).  
- Practical tools courts use to police the line:
  - Evaluate the proffered purpose: ask whether the evidence is truly for context/state of mind or is a veiled attempt to prove the truth of the matter asserted.  
  - Limit testimony or redact parts that are likely to be used for their truth.  
  - Allow cross‑examination of the declarant (or insist the declarant testify) when the statement is central. Cutbank reinforces that when the declarant testifies, Confrontation concerns are addressed.  
  - Exclude or strike portions that are unfairly prejudicial or likely to confuse the jury under Rule 403. Cutbank relied on the district court’s broad discretion under such evidentiary rules.

3) When a limiting instruction is required and when it may be given
- Timing and necessity are discretionary. Cutbank cites United States v. Bennett for the proposition that a district court “need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial.” The Eighth Circuit reviews that decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion.  
- But that discretion has limits. If an officer’s testimony clearly risks communicating hearsay as substantive proof, or if admission without immediate clarification would be substantially prejudicial or confusing, the better practice (and sometimes required exercise of discretion) is to give a contemporaneous limiting instruction or to exclude/redact the testimony. If the risk is less acute, a later instruction at the close of trial can suffice. Cutbank shows the court accepted a pretrial and contemporaneous approach by declining the defendant’s preemptive instruction request because (a) no hearsay was admitted in the record and (b) the declarant testified, so Confrontation concerns were satisfied.

Practical takeaways for practice or briefing
- Don’t assume officer recounting = hearsay. Frame objections around the actual purpose and the realistic risk the jury will use the statement for its truth.  
- If the statement is ambiguous in purpose or is highly probative on a contested matter, move to exclude or ask for a contemporaneous limiting instruction — courts may decline preemptive instructions, but will be less deferential if the record shows likely prejudice.  
- If the declarant testifies, emphasize Confrontation Clause cure; if not, emphasize confrontation and hearsay risk and press for redaction/exclusion or immediate instruction.

Bottom line: Eighth Circuit law treats officer‑recounted statements as non‑hearsay only when offered for a non‑truth purpose and when the record supports that limiting purpose; courts police misuse by examining purpose, context, redaction/cross‑examination and, where necessary, by giving limiting instructions — which the district court may give either contemporaneously or at the close of trial unless immediate prejudice or ambiguity makes a prompt instruction necessary. Cutbank applied those principles, found no hearsay was admitted, and held that a later instruction (or no instruction beyond the standard charge) was not an abuse of discretion where the declarant testified.

### Did admitting testimony that the victim’s sister was killed by a gun risk Rule 403 unfair prejudice?
Question:
On cross, defense impeached D.F. with prior inconsistent statements about the gun; in explaining her discomfort with guns, she said her sister was killed by a gun. The court denied a motion to strike, and the panel found no unfair prejudice. Under Rule 403, how do courts weigh probative value (explaining inconsistencies/bias) against the risk the jury will be inflamed by a sympathetic, unrelated death? Are there Eighth Circuit cases addressing similar 'context-of-witness-bias' facts that still warranted exclusion or a limiting instruction?
Answer:
Short answer
No reversible Rule 403 error. The Eighth Circuit held that D.F.’s statement that her sister “was killed by a gun” was responsive to cross‑examination about prior inconsistent statements and probative of D.F.’s unfamiliarity/bias about guns; the court found no unfair prejudice and declined to reverse. The district court’s ruling was within its broad discretion and any alleged error would be harmless in light of the other strong evidence.

Why — the governing balancing and how it was applied here
- Rule 403 requires excluding evidence only when its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusion, or misleading the jury. The inquiry is fact‑specific and reviewed for abuse of discretion. (See the opinion’s reliance on Fed. R. Evid. 403 and the deferential standard.)
- Context matters: testimony that explains why a witness made inconsistent statements or possessed a particular bias is highly probative of credibility. The panel treated D.F.’s remark as exactly that—an explanation for why she was “not familiar with guns” and therefore gave inconsistent answers—so its probative value on impeachment/credibility was significant.
- The opinion notes procedural facts that reduce the danger of unfair prejudice here: the testimony was responsive to cross; defense did not point to any specific prejudice or confusion beyond a blanket objection; C.F. later mentioned the same fact without objection; and the court’s broad evidentiary discretion governs. Given those features, the panel found “none apparent” under Rule 403 and affirmed. (Op. Part II.D.)

What the opinion cites on reviewability and harmlessness
- The court applied the usual deferential abuse‑of‑discretion standard for evidentiary rulings (citing Bennett) and emphasized that even a mistaken ruling will not require reversal unless the defendant shows the jury was substantially swayed by the evidence. (Op. at II.D & concluding paragraph citing DeMarce and Keys.)
- Here, the record contained substantial testimonial and physical evidence (DNA on the rifle, eyewitness ID, phone found on Cutbank’s likely path, etc.), so any alleged prejudice did not meet the threshold for reversal as not substantially influencing the verdict. (Op. at II, concluding paragraphs.)

Are there Eighth Circuit cases that required exclusion in similar “context of witness bias” situations?
- The Cutbank opinion does not cite an Eighth Circuit decision excluding similar testimony as unduly prejudicial when the testimony was offered to explain inconsistent statements or bias. Instead, the opinion applies precedent favoring admissibility of evidence intrinsic to credibility and reiterates the trial court’s broad discretion.
- The opinion does cite Battle (on intrinsic evidence of possession) and Cumbie (on authentication for extrinsic impeachment), and relies on Bennett for the standard of review; it does not identify a contrary Eighth Circuit holding where a factually similar, responsive statement about a relative’s death was excluded under Rule 403.

Practical takeaways for trial practice
- If a witness offers a brief, directly responsive explanation for prior inconsistencies (including emotionally charged background that bears on bias or unfamiliarity), courts in this circuit are likely to find probative value outweighs prejudice and admit it.
- To attack such testimony you should: (1) articulate specific unfair‑prejudice risks (sympathy that would overwhelm the jury, confusion on an element, or collateral narrative unrelated to impeachment), (2) seek a limiting instruction at the time of admission, and (3) preserve the issue and show how the evidence could have substantially swayed the jury given the rest of the record.

Bottom line
Cutbank treats the sister’s death statement as probative impeachment/context and finds no Rule 403 problem, stressing deference to the trial court and harmless‑error principles. The opinion does not point to an Eighth Circuit decision compelling exclusion of analogous testimony under Rule 403.

### How can a defendant impeach a witness with statements reflected only in a police report the witness did not author?
Question:
Defense tried to read from the investigator's report to impeach C.F., but the court barred it for lack of authentication since C.F. didn't write it. What are the proper routes to get these inconsistencies before the jury: (1) laying a Rule 613(b) foundation and asking the witness about the specific statement without introducing the report; (2) calling the officer/report author to authenticate; (3) using recorded recollection or past recollection refreshed; or (4) prior statements adopted by the witness? In practice, what foundation is required in the Eighth Circuit to use a police report for impeachment when the witness didn't write or sign it?
Answer:
Short answer
The Eighth Circuit in Cutbank made clear: you cannot simply read aloud an investigator’s report that the witness did not write and treat it as impeachment without laying the proper foundation and authenticating it. See Cutbank § II.E (citing United States v. Cumbie, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022)). The proper routes, and the practical foundation required in this circuit, are:

1) Confront the witness on cross (Rule 613(b) foundation)
- Ask the witness on the stand about the specific statement(s) reflected in the report. Rule 613(b) permits impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, and the easiest, preferred route is to get the witness to admit she said (or at least recall) the statement reflected in the report. If the witness acknowledges or cannot explain the inconsistency, the jury hears the inconsistency without introducing the officer’s report into evidence.
- Cutbank: the court allowed questions about what C.F. said to police but sustained reading the report itself without foundation. The opinion implies that questioning the witness about prior statements is acceptable; simply reading the unauthenticated report is not.

2) Have the witness adopt or acknowledge the report (Rule 801(d)(1)(A) / adoption)
- If the witness adopts the contents of the report (expressly or by clear conduct/words)—for example, “Yes, that’s what I told the officer”—the statement can be treated as a prior out-of-court statement of the witness (non-hearsay under Rule 801(d)(1)(A)) or at least as admissible impeachment evidence. That avoids needing the officer to authenticate the writing as the witness’s own words.

3) Call the officer/report author to authenticate and testify (extrinsic evidence)
- If the witness denies making the statement (or will not adopt it), and you want to introduce the report or its contents, you must authenticate the document and lay the foundation that the report records the witness’s statement. That normally means calling the officer who prepared the report (or another competent witness) to testify that (a) the officer wrote/compiled the report, (b) the report accurately reflects what the witness told the officer, and (c) the report was prepared contemporaneously/fresh so the content is reliable.
- Cutbank states extrinsic impeachment evidence “must still be authenticated.” See Cumbie, 28 F.4th at 914.

4) Refreshing memory / past recollection recorded (Rules 612 and 803(5))
- If the witness has forgotten, you can use the report to refresh her recollection on the stand (Rule 612). A document used to refresh memory need not be admitted into evidence or authored by the witness; you show it to the witness, attempt to refresh, then elicit the witness’s testimony. If the witness still cannot recall, you may attempt to admit the writing as a recorded recollection (Rule 803(5)), but recorded recollection has a specific foundation: the witness once had personal knowledge, made or adopted the memorandum when the matter was fresh, and the memorandum accurately reflects the witness’s knowledge. A police report not authored or adopted by the witness usually fails that foundation for admitting it as the witness’s recorded recollection unless you can show adoption by the witness or other circumstances meeting the Rule 803(5) requirements.

Practical,Eighth-Circuit foundation required (summary)
- The circuit requires authentication before extrinsic documentary impeachment by a police report that the witness did not author. Practically, that means one of these foundations must be established:
  a) The witness is confronted with the specific prior statement on the stand and adopts it or is shown to have made it (so the prior statement is the witness’s own and may be non-hearsay); or
  b) The document is authenticated through the officer who created it (or another witness with firsthand knowledge that the report records what the witness said), so the court can admit it for impeachment (subject to hearsay limits and other objections); or
  c) For recorded recollection, show the witness once had firsthand knowledge, made or adopted the writing when the matter was fresh, and it accurately reflects the witness’s memory—otherwise, the report cannot be admitted as the witness’s recorded recollection.

How to proceed at trial (practical checklist)
- First try cross-examining the witness and get her to confirm the specific words or facts she allegedly told police (Rule 613(b)). If she admits, use that admission.
- If she denies, either (i) call the officer to authenticate the report and testify to the witness’s statements, or (ii) use the report to refresh the witness’s memory on the stand and then elicit testimony, or (iii) attempt to show the witness adopted the report.
- Avoid attempting to read portions of an unauthenticated police report into the record over objection; Cutbank confirms courts will require authentication and may bar such reading absent it.

Bottom line
Cutbank reiterates that impeachment with a police report the witness did not author requires authentication or witness adoption. The most reliable Eighth-Circuit approaches are (1) confront the witness with the alleged prior statement on cross so she can adopt or be impeached by it, or (2) call the officer who prepared the report to authenticate it (or show the report is a recorded recollection with the required foundation). Without one of those foundations, the district court was correct in excluding the reading of the unauthenticated report.

### Can the court rely on uncharged conduct in the PSR to apply § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), and are there any Apprendi/Confrontation concerns?
Question:
The four-level enhancement for possessing a firearm 'in connection with another felony offense' was applied based on PSR facts that were adopted without objection. What evidentiary standard applies at federal sentencing for such uncharged conduct (preponderance?), and is the court permitted to rely solely on the PSR absent live testimony if the defendant doesn't object? Also, does Apprendi/Booker require jury findings for this enhancement when it increases the advisory range but not the statutory maximum, and are there any Sixth Amendment or Confrontation Clause limits on using hearsay within the PSR?
Answer:
Short answer
- Sentencing findings about uncharged conduct are judged by the preponderance standard and the district court may rely on facts in an adopted PSR (even if those facts describe uncharged conduct), provided the defendant does not timely object. United States v. Cutbank treats the PSR’s adopted facts as a proper basis for applying USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).
- Apprendi/Booker do not require a jury finding here because the enhancement changed only the advisory Guidelines range, not the statutory maximum. The Sixth Amendment jury-trial rule applies only when a judge’s factfinding increases the statutory maximum sentence.
- Confrontation/hearsay concerns are limited where the declarant testifies (Confrontation satisfied) and, more generally at sentencing, courts may rely on hearsay in the PSR unless the defendant preserves a timely objection.

Explanation and authority from the opinion

1) Evidentiary standard and use of uncharged conduct
Cutbank confirms that a district court may apply USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) based on conduct “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained” (quoting the guideline comment). The court approved the district court’s use of PSR facts that were “adopted, without objection,” to find the firearm was possessed “in connection with another felony.” Op. § III (sentencing issue) (noting comment n.14(C) and that the district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements without objection).

Although Cutbank does not recite the standard formula in the opinion, the court’s citation to United States v. Dunn for the standard of review (and the usual Sentencing Guideline practice) is consistent with the established rule that sentencing courts make factual findings by a preponderance of the evidence when determining guideline enhancements based on conduct not charged or proven to a jury. Cutbank treats the PSR adoption as a sufficient factual basis, and affirms that adopting the PSR without objection defeats a later attack on those factual findings.

Practical takeaway from Cutbank:
- If the PSR’s facts are adopted by the district court and the defendant does not object, the court may rely on them to apply § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B).
- If the defendant objects, the district court must resolve the dispute (typically under the preponderance standard).

2) Apprendi / Booker / Sixth Amendment
Cutbank’s sentencing enhancement altered only the advisory Guidelines range; the district court imposed a within-Guidelines sentence. The opinion explains the proper sentencing sequence (calculate Guidelines, consider § 3553(a)) and treats the Guidelines calculation and enhancement as matters the judge can resolve in sentencing. The court does not treat this enhancement as requiring a jury trial because it did not increase the statutory maximum; instead it affected only the advisory range. See Op. § III (discussing Guidelines calculation and that the district court properly applied the enhancement and imposed a within-range sentence).

That follows Apprendi/Booker principles: Apprendi requires jury findings only when judge-made facts increase the statutory maximum. Where a finding only changes the advisory Guidelines range (and not the statutory maximum), Booker left the Guidelines advisory and judge factfinding at sentencing is permissible.

3) Confrontation Clause and hearsay in the PSR
Cutbank directly addresses Confrontation concerns earlier: the court held the Confrontation Clause was satisfied “when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person.” Op. II.B. The opinion also notes the district court “did not admit hearsay during the trial” and that D.F. testified, so Confrontation was satisfied. Applied to sentencing, the opinion’s approach supports two points:
- If a declarant appears and testifies at trial, Confrontation Clause objections regarding that testimony are resolved.
- More generally, the opinion treats reliance on PSR material adopted without objection as permissible. Sentencing practice routinely allows judges to consider hearsay in PSRs; Cutbank illustrates that absent a timely objection, the court may adopt and rely on PSR statements that describe uncharged conduct.

Bottom line
Cutbank affirms the standard and practical rules you summarized: (1) courts may find uncharged conduct by a preponderance at sentencing and rely on an adopted PSR; (2) Apprendi/Booker do not require jury findings for an enhancement that only raises the advisory Guidelines range; and (3) Confrontation problems are avoided when declarants testify and, generally, a court may rely on PSR hearsay unless the defendant preserves an objection. The opinion explicitly rests the enhancement on PSR facts “adopted, without objection,” and affirms the sentence.

### How do ACCA’s § 4B1.4 offense levels interact with § 2K2.1 and specific offense characteristics without double counting?
Question:
The court notes the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would produce an offense level of 34, but the court used a higher total offense level (35) after applying § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) and other enhancements, because the Guidelines say use the greater offense level. Is it standard to calculate both paths (ACCA § 4B1.4 vs. § 2K2.1 with SOCs) and choose the higher? When do enhancements like altered/obliterated serial number or reckless endangerment during flight risk impermissible double counting with § 4B1.4? Any Eighth Circuit authority clarifying the boundaries?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. Under the Guidelines you calculate the §4B1.4 (Armed Career Criminal Guideline) offense level and you also calculate the §2K2.1 base level plus any applicable specific‑offense characteristics (SOCs); USSG §4B1.4(b) then directs you to use the greater of the two offense levels. The Eighth Circuit in Cutbank applied that rule: the court noted the ACCA guideline would have produced level 34 but the §2K2.1 path with SOCs produced level 35, and the district court properly used the greater level. See Cutbank, slip op. §III (discussing USSG §4B1.4(b)).

Legal doctrine and how it operates
- Two parallel calculations. When a defendant qualifies as an armed career criminal, the Guidelines require the district court to do both computations: (1) calculate the Chapter Four career‑offender/ACCA guideline offense level under §4B1.4, and (2) calculate the offense level under the substantive guideline (here §2K2.1) including any applicable SOCs. USSG §4B1.4(b) controls and the court uses the greater offense level. Cutbank explicitly follows that rule (opinion §III).
- No automatic exclusion of SOCs by the ACCA guideline. The ACCA guideline does not itself say you ignore the §2K2.1 enhancements; it says you compare and take the higher. That is why the district court in Cutbank applied the §2K2.1 SOCs (altered serial number, reckless endangerment in flight, possession in connection with another felony) and reached a higher total offense level than the §4B1.4 calculation, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed (Cutbank §III).
- Double‑counting concern. The constitutional/Guidelines principle is that a district court should not impermissibly punish the same conduct twice. Whether a particular SOC “double counts” depends on whether it is effectively duplicative of the conduct already accounted for in the career‑offender/ACCA calculation. The Eighth Circuit reviews application of contested sentencing enhancements for clear error to the facts and for abuse of discretion as to legal questions. In Cutbank, the court accepted the PSR’s factual findings (adopted without objection) that supported the §2K2.1 SOCs and did not find impermissible double counting; it upheld the added levels and explained that the Guidelines themselves instruct using the greater level if the §2K2.1 path yields a higher number (Cutbank §III).

Eighth Circuit authority and limits (as reflected in Cutbank)
- Cutbank: district court adopted PSR facts, applied §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) and other SOCs to reach level 35, noted §4B1.4(b) requires choosing the greater offense level, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed. The court did not conclude that those SOCs were impermissibly duplicative of the ACCA enhancement; rather, it treated them as separate guideline adjustments supported by the PSR (Cutbank §III).
- Related Eighth Circuit guidance cited in the opinion: Dunn (standard of review for fact findings at sentencing), and general sentencing principles from Gall and Rosales‑Mireles about correct Guidelines calculation and consideration of §3553(a) factors (Cutbank §III).

Practical takeaways for sentencing practice
- Preserve objections and develop the record. If you believe a SOC duplicates the same conduct accounted for by §4B1.4 (or by the ACCA predicate findings), develop factual distinctions in the PSR and at sentencing and object concretely so the district court can rule—Cutbank shows courts will rely on the PSR when adopted without objection.
- Frame double‑counting challenges around distinct factual bases. To succeed, show the SOC punishes precisely the same conduct or element already incorporated in the career‑offender calculation rather than a separate manner, means, or harm. General disagreement about severity is unlikely to carry the day.
- If the §2K2.1 path yields a higher number, remind the court that USSG §4B1.4(b) requires using the greater offense level (as the Cutbank court did).

Bottom line
It is standard and required by the Guidelines to compute both paths and apply the higher offense level (USSG §4B1.4(b)); double‑counting challenges must show the SOC is truly duplicative of the career‑offender/ACCA calculation, and the Eighth Circuit in Cutbank affirmed application of §2K2.1 SOCs where the PSR supported distinct factual findings and the Guidelines’ “use the greater” rule applied.

### What level of explanation must a district judge give when imposing a within-Guidelines sentence to show consideration of § 3553(a)?
Question:
The panel presumes reasonableness for a within-range sentence and says no 'mechanical recitation' is required so long as the record shows the court considered § 3553(a). How much engagement with defense mitigation is required in the Eighth Circuit? If the court references reviewing the PSR and memoranda and mentions some § 3553(a) factors, is that sufficient even when the defense argues the range is inflated by enhancements? Are there cases where a within-Guidelines sentence was reversed for inadequate explanation?
Answer:
Short answer
- In the Eighth Circuit a district court imposing a within‑Guidelines sentence must correctly calculate the Guidelines and must show (through the record) that it considered the § 3553(a) factors, but it need not give a “mechanical recitation” or engage in detailed, factor‑by‑factor analysis. United States v. Cutbank applies the presumption of reasonableness for a within‑range sentence and holds that a statement that the court reviewed the PSR and sentencing memoranda and discussed § 3553(a) factors is sufficient where the record shows the court was aware of the defendant’s mitigation arguments. (Cutbank, slip op. § III.)

What the doctrine requires (doctrinal roadmap)
1. Correct Guidelines calculation first. The district court must “begin all sentencing proceedings by correctly calculating the applicable Guidelines range.” Gall v. United States, quoted in Cutbank. If the Guidelines are miscalculated, procedural error follows and appellate review is triggered. (Cutbank § III.)
2. Consideration of § 3553(a). After computing the range, the court must consider the § 3553(a) factors and then may impose a within‑range sentence; such a sentence is presumed reasonable on appeal. Cutbank cites that presumption (Goodhouse) and explains that the appellate court will uphold a within‑range sentence if the record makes plain the district court considered § 3553(a). (Cutbank § III.)
3. No formulaic recitation required. The Eighth Circuit does not demand a rote, factor‑by‑factor explanation. It is enough that the record shows the court actually considered the factors; the court may accomplish this by indicating it reviewed the PSR, memoranda, and that it weighed relevant § 3553(a) considerations. Cutbank relies on Masood and Maluoth for that point. (Cutbank § III; citing Maluoth, 121 F.4th at 1164; Masood, 133 F.4th at 809.)

How much engagement with defense mitigation (including attacks on enhancements) is required
- The district court must be aware of the defendant’s mitigation arguments and consider them, but need not write a detailed rebuttal to every point. In Cutbank the court expressly reviewed the PSR and the parties’ sentencing memoranda and reviewed § 3553(a) factors; the panel concluded that, given the court’s awareness of Cutbank’s arguments (including his challenge to a four‑level enhancement), the presumption applied and the sentence was reasonable. The court framed this as: “Thus, the district court was aware of [the defendant’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” (Cutbank § III.)
- Put another way: if the record shows the court knew the defense contention (for example, that the Guidelines range was inflated by an enhancement) and the court indicates it considered the PSR/memos and § 3553(a), that will generally be sufficient in the Eighth Circuit to uphold a within‑Guidelines sentence.

Were there cases reversing a within‑Guidelines sentence for inadequate explanation?
- Cutbank does not identify a reversal for mere failure to recite § 3553(a) factors mechanically; instead it emphasizes that procedural errors like failing to calculate the Guidelines properly (Rosales‑Mireles) or other significant procedural errors can trigger reversal. Cutbank cites Maluoth and Masood for the proposition that a non‑mechanical, record‑based showing of consideration is adequate. The opinion therefore treats inadequate explanation in the sense of a missing record as a reversible procedural error, but does not point to a case in which a within‑range sentence was reversed solely because the court did not engage at length with mitigation—rather, the appellate rule is to look for a record showing consideration of § 3553(a).

Practical takeaways for practitioners
- Preserve the record. Make sure defense arguments about why an enhancement inflates the range and about mitigating § 3553(a) factors are in the sentencing filings (memo, objections to PSR) and stated on the record at sentencing.
- Obtain an on‑the‑record acknowledgment. If the district judge does not explicitly address an important mitigation argument, ask the court to state on the record that it has considered the PSR, submissions, and the specific § 3553(a) arguments—this avoids reliance on a bare, conclusory sentence that could be attacked as not clearly showing consideration.
- Don’t expect a written factor‑by‑factor analysis. In the Eighth Circuit a concise statement that the court reviewed the PSR/memoranda and considered § 3553(a) will ordinarily suffice for a within‑Guidelines sentence so long as the record makes the court’s awareness of and consideration of the defendant’s contentions clear. (Cutbank § III.)

References from the opinion
- Cutbank § III (presumption of reasonableness for within‑range sentences; court noted it “reviewed the PSR and sentencing memoranda and reviewed § 3553(a) factors”; reliance on Masood and Maluoth)
- Gall v. United States (sentencing framework)
- Rosales‑Mireles v. United States (need to calculate Guidelines before appellate substantive‑reasonableness review)

### Can an appellee broaden the issues on appeal, and how does that affect standards of review and party presentation?
Question:
The panel criticizes the government for 'unnecessarily expand[ing] the issues on appeal' by framing a claim about thwarted cross-examination that appellant didn’t clearly brief. What is the Eighth Circuit’s approach to the party presentation principle in criminal appeals? If the government raises an additional framing or justification, will the court consider it, disregard it, or resolve it under plain error? How does this interact with forfeiture vs. waiver of objections made (or not) in the district court and in the opening brief?
Answer:
Short answer
- The Eighth Circuit follows the party-presentation principle: parties frame the issues, and the court normally decides the questions the parties actually present. An appellee who tries to broaden or recast the appellant’s claims on appeal risks criticism and the court may decline to rely on that new framing.
- That said, the court will not automatically ignore an argument the appellee presses. If the appellant preserved an issue below, the court will decide it under the ordinary standard (e.g., abuse of discretion for most evidentiary rulings). If the appellant did not preserve the issue, the court will treat it as unpreserved and resolve it under the appropriate unpreserved-error standard (in criminal cases, plain‑error review) and/or apply the harmless‑error analysis where applicable.
- In Cutbank the court both admonished the government for expanding the issues and nonetheless addressed the substance: it applied ordinary abuse‑of‑discretion review to the evidentiary rulings and explained that, even if the claim were not preserved, it would fail (including harmlessness analysis).

Explanation, grounded in the opinion
1) Party-presentation principle and the court’s reaction
- The panel expressly reproved the government for “unnecessarily expand[ing] the issues on appeal,” signaling that an appellee should not recast the appellant’s argument or press a new theory that the appellant did not clearly brief. That language reflects the court’s expectation that the parties control which issues the court decides.

2) How the court treats an appellee’s additional framing
- The opinion shows two things: (a) the court will call out an appellee that broadens the record of issues, and (b) the court may nevertheless address the point on the merits if needed. In the C‑F cross‑examination dispute, the government’s brief characterized a “next claim” (that the defense was “thwarted” in cross‑examining C.F.), which the panel said it “d[id] not appreciate.” The court then went on to decide the point on evidentiary/authentication grounds. So an appellee’s new framing may be criticized and is at risk of being disregarded, but it will not automatically keep the court from resolving the underlying question.

3) Preservation, standards of review, and harmlessness
- If the appellant preserved the objection in district court, the court uses the ordinary standard for that kind of ruling (the opinion applies “we review a district court’s decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion,” and likewise treats evidentiary authentication rulings as within the district court’s discretion). Cutbank’s preserved evidentiary objections were reviewed under abuse‑of‑discretion and rejected.
- If the appellant did not preserve the issue, the court makes clear it can still consider it: the opinion explicitly says “whether preserved or not, the ‘claim’ is without merit.” In criminal cases, that posture implicates the ordinary unpreserved‑error frameworks (including plain‑error review under Rule 52(b)), and the court also applies harmless‑error analysis where appropriate. The opinion applied harmlessness language: “we will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless…we will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed…Cutbank cannot clear this hurdle.”
- Thus, unpreserved objections may be considered but are subject to the stricter plain‑error/harmlessness tests the court describes.

4) Forfeiture versus waiver (as shown in the opinion)
- The opinion does not use the technical labels “forfeiture” or “waiver” in a doctrinal holding here, but its practice aligns with the usual distinction: an objection actually made and preserved invites ordinary review; an objection not raised is forfeited/forgone for purposes of ordinary review and therefore faces plain‑error/harmlessness if the appellant later presses it on appeal. The panel’s “whether preserved or not” gloss confirms the court will address unpreserved claims, but only under the tighter standards and harmlessness inquiry.

Takeaway for advocates
- Appellees should not broaden or recast an appellant’s claims on appeal; the court may criticize or disregard such widening of the issues.
- Appellants ought to preserve and clearly present the claims they want the court to decide; preserved claims get the ordinary standard of review, whereas unpreserved claims will be subject to plain‑error/harmlessness review.
- Even when an appellee advances a new framing, the court may nonetheless resolve the issue on the law and facts, but the appellee’s approach is frowned upon and may carry less persuasive force.
