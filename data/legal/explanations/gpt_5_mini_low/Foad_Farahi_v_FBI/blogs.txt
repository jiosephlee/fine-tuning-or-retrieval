Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# D.C. Circuit Doubles Down on Exemption 7(A) Deference in National-Security Files

## Why Farahi matters
The D.C. Circuit’s decision in Farahi v. FBI (Sept. 30, 2025) is a consequential reaffirmation of the court’s repeated willingness to defer to the FBI when Exemption 7(A) is invoked in the national-security and counterterrorism context. For FOIA requesters who seek files touching on counterterrorism, foreign terrorist organizations, or investigations tied to national security, Farahi signals that courts in this circuit will accept agency attestations that (1) records were compiled for law enforcement purposes, (2) disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings, and (3) meaningful segregability is often not practicable—so long as the agency provides some combination of public and classified ex parte detail. The opinion is important not because it breaks new doctrinal ground, but because it consolidates and applies a string of precedents—CREW, Juarez, CNSS, Campbell, and Mead—to sustain broad protection for terrorism-related files against FOIA disclosure.

## The court’s reasoning in brief
The FBI located more than 10,000 pages responsive to Farahi’s request and withheld most of them under Exemption 7(A). After an initial summary-judgment submission, the district court—concerned about passage of time—required the FBI to update its declarations. The FBI then filed public and ex parte declarations confirming that investigations related to Farahi remained “ongoing” and explaining why nonexempt material could not meaningfully be segregated. The district court granted summary judgment to the FBI; the D.C. Circuit affirmed.

The panel’s analysis follows familiar lines. First, because the FBI is a law-enforcement agency, its view that the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” is owed a “fair measure of deference” (Campbell; Clemente) and was easily satisfied here given the terrorism-linked allegations in Farahi’s immigration record. Second, Exemption 7(A)’s interference prong requires that the enforcement proceedings be “pending or reasonably anticipated” (CREW), and courts in this circuit accept agency attestations of ongoing investigations—particularly where, as here, the agency supplemented a public declaration with classified ex parte material that provided substantive detail (Juarez; CNSS). The court rejected Farahi’s staleness argument largely because the agency supplied an “update” confirming the continuing existence and scope of the investigation; the Bender declaration was short, but it was not the only evidence. Finally, on segregability, the court relied on the FBI’s ex parte showing that nonexempt fragments were so interspersed as to be not “reasonably segregable” (Mead; Perioperative).

## What the opinion reinforces about Exemption 7(A)
Three doctrinal points stand out from Farahi.

First, evidentiary burden and deference. The government’s burden under Exemption 7(A) is not trivial, but the D.C. Circuit continues to give the FBI significant breathing room. Where an agency with national-security responsibilities provides a public declaration attesting to the law-enforcement purpose of records and supplements it with classified ex parte material that describes the nature and scope of an investigation, courts will typically accept the agency’s showing that disclosure could interfere with investigations. Farahi reiterates that courts assess these showings holistically; a terse update can suffice when it supplements earlier, more detailed classified submissions.

Second, “pending or reasonably anticipated” remains a low threshold when an investigation continues to gather evidence. Juarez and CREW already held that ongoing criminal investigations count as “proceedings” and that the government may establish pendency by attesting to existence. Farahi leans on those precedents to make clear that a broad counterterrorism investigation—especially one tied to foreign terrorist organizations and known violent actors—creates a plausible, concrete prospective law-enforcement proceeding that disclosure could jeopardize.

Third, segregability doctrine in national-security cases remains cabined by Mead-style principles: courts will credit agency explanations—again, especially when supported by classified submissions—that nonexempt material is inextricably intertwined with exempt information such that meaningful release would itself reveal sensitive content. The court reiterates that FOIA’s aim is information, not mere words on a page; juiced-up word searches or piecemeal disclosures that would still convey operative intelligence are not required.

## Practical consequences and downstream effects
For FOIA litigants, Farahi has immediate practical implications. Staleness challenges—arguing that an investigation has become so aged that Exemption 7(A) no longer applies—face an uphill climb absent contemporaneous public evidence that the investigation has been closed. The D.C. Circuit gives agencies an opportunity to refresh their assertions; a short, contemporaneous attestation that an investigation “remains ongoing” can, when buttressed by prior ex parte detail, be decisive.

The decision also makes it harder to litigate segregability piecemeal. When core facts are national-security sensitive, courts will accept agency attestations that releasable material is either so scattered that disclosure would reveal the exempt substance or would itself facilitate inference about the substance of the investigation. That dynamic preserves a durable wall against disclosure for voluminous terrorism-related files.

For agencies, Farahi affirms that submitting classified ex parte declarations remains a powerful tool—one that can be decisive on both the pendency and segregability questions. For requesters, however, ex parte submissions create procedural and substantive asymmetry: the district court and the appellate panel may consider information that the requester never sees, making appeals and public rebuttal harder.

## Strategic takeaways for FOIA challengers
Farahi does not mean that all hope is lost for FOIA requesters seeking terrorism-related files, but it does suggest best practices that will be increasingly necessary in this circuit.

First, litigants should press early to identify and contest any vagueness in the agency’s public declarations. CREW cautions against accepting conclusory “ongoing” claims in a vacuum; the more detailed and concrete the public showing, the less that ex parte classified proof will be necessary to sustain the agency.

Second, challenge the categorization and the Vaughn-style treatment where possible. The D.C. Circuit in Farahi rejected an overbreadth challenge as forfeited. Requesters should therefore press district courts to require precise category definitions, a robust Vaughn index (to the extent compatible with classification restrictions), and clear contemporaneous statements addressing whether investigations are active, their scope, and particularized reasons why specific categories would reveal information harmful to ongoing proceedings.

Third, where ex parte submissions are used, litigants should seek in camera review by the court and move for summaries that explain—as much as possible on the public record—the bases for withheld categories. While courts may not be able to disclose classified detail, they can and should explain why the agency prevailed without simply reciting deference.

Finally, strategic settlement may be more attractive: agencies sometimes will produce limited redactions or summaries in the face of litigation risk and public pressure, and negotiating a tailored release (perhaps a sanitized summary) may be preferable to protracted litigation that will likely be decided on classified affidavits.

## Broader doctrinal and policy questions
Farahi underscores a persistent tension in FOIA law: balancing transparency with operational secrecy. The D.C. Circuit’s posture favors operational secrecy in the counterterrorism arena, reflecting the practical difficulty courts face in evaluating national-security risk without seeing classified material. That posture is defensible as a matter of institutional competence—courts cannot responsibly second-guess certain national-security judgments without access to sensitive information—but it also means FOIA’s promise of accountability is empirically narrowed when investigations implicate terrorism or national-security actors.

The opinion may also nudge litigants toward statutory or policy routes outside FOIA when accountability is the goal—e.g., congressional oversight, Inspector General reviews, or carefully tailored Freedom of Information Act litigation strategies that pressure agencies to release non-sensitive metadata or process-oriented documents (e.g., timelines, policies) that can be disclosed without revealing operational secrets.

## Conclusion
Farahi is a reminder that, in the D.C. Circuit, Exemption 7(A) remains a robust shield for FBI files touching national security. The decision does not announce novel law but synthesizes CREW, Juarez, CNSS, and Mead into a clear message: when the FBI couples public attestation with classified ex parte detail—and supplies contemporaneous updates—courts will generally accept that disclosure could interfere with ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings and that meaningful segregability is not feasible. For FOIA practitioners, Farahi demands careful front-end challenges to public declarations, meticulous work on categorical and Vaughn issues, and creative litigation strategies to obtain at least some transparency when national-security concerns loom.



## ‘Stale’ No More: Managing Time in FOIA Through Updated Declarations

Why this case matters

Foad Farahi v. FBI answers a recurring procedural question that has bedeviled FOIA litigation for years: when an agency relies on an earlier, detailed declaration to justify withholding records under an exemption, how fresh must that showing be after protracted litigation? The D.C. Circuit’s decision—affirming the district court’s insistence on an updated showing and its acceptance of a terse “confirmation” declaration as an adequate update to an earlier, detailed submission—clarifies the interplay between (1) the judicial concern that factual showings not grow stale over litigation, and (2) the practical limits on what agencies may (or should) disclose when national-security or investigative sensitivity is implicated.

At bottom, the court stakes out a middle path. It reiterates that agencies must refresh their factual basis for withholding when time has passed, but it also permits an agency to supply a short, contemporaneous “update” that confirms the continuing existence of the underlying circumstances described in a prior, more fulsome declaration — so long as that update is read in context with the earlier material (including any ex parte filings).

What the court did — the reasoning in brief

The panel affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the FBI under FOIA Exemption 7(A) (records compiled for law enforcement purposes that could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings). Two linked procedural questions governed the outcome.

First, did the FBI’s materials show that the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes? The court accepted the FBI’s public declaration (Hardy) and its sealed ex parte declaration as an adequate factual showing, given the FBI’s law-enforcement role and the plaintiff’s own concessions in related immigration litigation about alleged ties to terrorism. The court emphasized the usual deference given to FBI affidavits, especially in national-security and counterterrorism contexts.

Second, and the point that drives the opinion’s practical import: because years had elapsed since the FBI’s initial declaration, did the government need to update that showing to prove that enforcement proceedings were still “pending or reasonably anticipated”? The district court required a refreshed assessment; the FBI supplied a contemporaneous, comparatively terse public declaration from Bender stating that the investigations “remain ongoing,” together with Bender’s sealed submission and the earlier Hardy declarations. The D.C. Circuit approved this approach, holding that the Bender declaration could legitimately be viewed as an update — not a standalone replacement — of the earlier record. Context mattered: the court refused to treat the brief update in isolation because the government had already made a detailed showing and because the district court had previously found the initial showing to “seemingly” satisfy the regulatory elements. The court also relied on the breadth of the underlying investigation as making it plausible that proceedings remained ongoing.

The court likewise upheld the district court’s segregability analysis: the FBI’s ex parte showing explained why any nonexempt material was inextricably intertwined with exempt material or was otherwise not “reasonably segregable,” and the court accepted that justification despite the voluminous nature of the records.

Analytical perspective — what the opinion permits and limits

Farahi does two important things simultaneously: it reinforces courts’ vigilance against stale factual showings, while recognizing pragmatic ways agencies can comply without re-litigating sensitive substance in open court.

First, the opinion reiterates that courts have power — and responsibility — to require agencies to refresh evidentiary showings when factual predicates may have changed during protracted litigation. That is consistent with CREW and other decisions cautioning against blind reliance on old declarations. Courts will not accept a declaration that is plausibly stale simply because the agency once said the investigation was ongoing.

Second, the decision gives agencies a workable procedure to meet that renewed burden: a short, contemporaneous “confirmation” can suffice if the earlier, detailed submissions remain in the record and the update is plainly an update rather than a new, standalone assertion. In other words, freshness can be demonstrated by a combination of (a) earlier detailed factual materials, (b) a contemporaneous, brief attestation that nothing material has changed, and (c) — when necessary — sealed, ex parte materials that provide the district court the factual specificity it needs to evaluate withheld materials without public disclosure.

But this is a contextual decision, and several important limits remain. The court explicitly warns—following CREW—that a conclusory “investigation remains ongoing” assertion cannot always carry the day when considered in isolation. Here, the update was admissible only because it was explicitly tied back to substantial earlier declarations (including ex parte material) and because the district court had already determined that the government had “seemingly” met its burden. Thus, requesters still have traction when an update is both conclusory and untethered to a prior detailed showing. Courts will not allow agencies to warehouse old, potentially stale affidavits and then prop them up with a perfunctory, post hoc stamp of “still ongoing” unless the earlier, fuller evidence is adequate and remains credible.

Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

The decision shapes doctrine principally in two areas: (1) the temporal dimensions of an agency’s burden under Exemption 7(A), and (2) practical handling of segregability when responsive records are massive and interspersed with sensitive material.

On timing and “freshness,” Farahi confirms that courts will police the chronological integrity of an agency’s showing. Agencies should not expect a single, early affidavit to survive for years of delay without some refreshed attestation. At the same time, the ruling formally recognizes that courts can accept a modest update as sufficient when it explicitly supplements an earlier, more fulsome showing. So the doctrine evolves toward a functional, holistic assessment: courts will consider the entire evidentiary mosaic — initial declarations, contemporaneous updates, and ex parte materials — rather than insisting on a single document meeting a rigid checklist.

On segregability, the panel reasserts the established line: small nonexempt material randomly scattered through highly sensitive records may be “not reasonably segregable” where extraction would itself reveal sensitive context. That reasoning reinforces precedent holding that FOIA’s focus is disclosure of meaningfully informative material, not the mechanical release of isolated words or phrases.

Practical playbook — for agencies and requesters

For agencies:
- Treat initial declarations as live files that require maintenance. If litigation stretches for years, plan to file a short, timely update that (1) links explicitly to the initial declaration(s), (2) confirms whether the operative investigative facts remain the same, and (3) says whether any material has changed since the earlier submission. When secrecy demands it, supplement public updates with sealed ex parte submissions to provide the court needed specificity.
- When crafting category-by-category withholdings and segregability rationales, make the record robust and sufficiently granular up front; doing so will ease later reliance on terse updates because the court can read the update in context of a detailed foundation.

For requesters:
- Challenge staleness early and specifically. Broad “it’s been too long” objections are weaker than pointed arguments showing how the initial factual predicate could have changed.
- Insist that the court require an updated showing when initial declarations are several years old. If the agency provides only a terse update, press the court to evaluate the update against the earlier detailed materials and to demand more detail if the earlier materials themselves were thin.
- Preserve arguments about timing and sufficiency: Farahi rejected certain challenges as forfeited or inadequately developed; litigants shouldn’t give agencies uncontested procedural advantages.

Concluding thought

Farahi does not radically change FOIA law, but it clarifies the operating rules for a problem that arises in many FOIA cases: how to keep the agency’s factual showing current without forcing public disclosure of sensitive investigative detail. The decision requires agencies to refresh their evidence when time has passed, but it sensibly allows a short, contemporaneous confirmation to bridge an earlier, detailed affidavit — provided the update is contextualized and the underlying showing remains persuasive. For litigants and courts, the lesson is pragmatic: FOIA fights are not frozen in time, but neither must they result in public re-litigation of sensitive specifics every few years.



# Ex Parte and Inextricably Intertwined: Segregability in the Shadow of National Security

Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in Farahi v. FBI (Sept. 30, 2025) is a compact but important ruling about the interplay of two perennial FOIA fault lines: segregability and the government’s use of ex parte, sealed submissions in national-security–sensitive cases. At stake is the practical ability of requesters to obtain nonexempt information from voluminous law‑enforcement files when the agency insists that any releasable material is “interspersed line‑by‑line” with genuinely sensitive content. The panel affirmed the district court’s reliance on sealed declarations and its conclusion that the records were not reasonably segregable — a result that reinforces existing doctrine favoring agency deference in national‑security contexts and highlights the uphill climb FOIA plaintiffs face when the government files ex parte affidavits.

What the court did

The court affirmed denial of disclosure under Exemption 7(A) and, crucial here, accepted the FBI’s segregability showing. The FBI had produced public and sealed declarations from senior RIDS officials (Hardy and Bender). The district court initially required updated declarations because of the passage of time; the FBI supplemented, including an ex parte submission addressing both the pendency of investigations and segregability. On appeal, the D.C. Circuit found three points dispositive.

First, the court credited the government’s showing that the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings — a showing buttressed by both public and sealed declarations and by the broader record of Farahi’s alleged ties to terrorism‑related individuals and organizations.

Second, the panel underscored the settled principle that the FBI’s invocation of Exemption 7 is entitled to a “fair measure of deference,” especially where national‑security concerns are implicated; that posture informed its willingness to accept the agency’s factual attestations.

Third, and the focus of the opinion’s practical bite, the court accepted the district court’s conclusion — grounded in the ex parte material — that any nonexempt material was either trivial (isolated words or phrases) or so tightly interwoven with exempt content that meaningful segregation was infeasible. The panel explicitly cited Mead and more recent decisions (including Perioperative Services) for the proposition that FOIA’s object is information, not isolated words, and that where nonexempt content is woven line‑by‑line into exempt material, it may not be “reasonably segregable.”

Why the court’s reasoning is defensible

The opinion stays within settled D.C. Circuit doctrine. For decades the court has allowed agencies to file in‑camera, ex parte affidavits in FOIA litigation when disclosure of the affidavits’ specifics would itself reveal sensitive material. It has also repeatedly recognized that courts may accept agency affidavits that “show with reasonable specificity” why documents cannot be further segregated. Given the national‑security and counterterrorism context here, the court’s deference tracks precedent such as CNSS, Campbell, Juarez, and Mead: the FBI is plausibly in the best position to explain how the requested records fit into an ongoing, complex investigation and why carving out fragments would reveal more than it conceals.

The panel also attended to process: the district court had already required an update to avoid stale assertions, and the FBI did supply updated declarations. The D.C. Circuit treated the March 2023 declaration (Bender) as an update to the earlier, more detailed Hardy material rather than a freestanding, conclusory declaration. That contextual approach is sensible: assessing whether an investigation remains ongoing — and whether segregability is realistic — often requires layered submissions over time.

Where the approach raises concerns

Even so, the decision highlights tensions that persist in FOIA jurisprudence.

First, ex parte, sealed submissions create asymmetry. They shield the court’s view of sensitive facts from the requester and the public, which is often necessary, but they also deprive plaintiffs of the information needed to mount targeted challenges to an agency’s labels, categories, and segregability methodology. Farahi did not oppose the FBI’s ex parte practice, and the panel relied on that procedural posture to reject his later complaint about secrecy. But many requesters do oppose or cannot effectively challenge ex parte submissions without counsel cleared for classified or law‑enforcement‑sensitive material. The result is practical: when the agency’s declarations remain sealed, judicial review is inherently limited, and plaintiffs face a steep burden to show that the agency’s categorical descriptions are overbroad.

Second, the court’s reliance on the “inextricably intertwined” rationale combined with the Mead/Perioperative gloss risks an outcome in which agencies can defeat segregability claims by producing massive files in which bits of mundane content are scattered among sensitive material. The court made clear that trivial extractable words or phrases — even if technically nonexempt — do not compel disclosure if they lack “meaningful information content” or if extracting them would reveal the exempt context. But that standard is malleable. Agencies with resources can produce enormous document collections and plausibly assert that isolating nonexempt snippets would be functionally impossible or would reveal the surrounding exempt content, thereby insulating large swaths of records from release.

Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Farahi does not rewrite FOIA law, but it cements a particular equilibrium: in national‑security and counterterrorism investigations, courts will generally accept agency affidavits (including sealed ones) that explain why release would interfere with investigations and why segregability is impractical. Two downstream consequences seem likely.

One, plaintiffs will have stronger incentives to litigate early and to attack procedural choices — for example, objecting to ex parte submissions, pushing for narrowed redaction or for appointment of special counsel with clearance to review sealed material, or demanding a Vaughn index that is as granular as possible on the public record. Where a requester fails to contest ex parte practice, the court will be less sympathetic later.

Two, agencies may be reinforced in a practice of submitting sealed, detailed segregability showings when files implicate ongoing investigations. That will preserve government latitude to withhold in full where the exempt material permeates the file. Practically, this raises the cost of FOIA litigation for requesters and may reduce the number of successful partial disclosures in high‑sensitivity cases.

Strategic guidance for plaintiffs

Farahi is a reminder that litigating segregability against the FBI in counterterrorism contexts requires a proactive and forensic approach.

First, press for detail on the public record from the outset. Where possible, demand a sufficiently particularized Vaughn index and a public declaration that explains the categories of documents and the grounds for withholding them. The more granular the public record, the less weight courts will place on later sealed submissions.

Second, challenge ex parte practice early (and expressly). If the requester opposes sealed filings, the court must weigh the need for secrecy against fairness; a timely, reasoned objection preserves the record for appeal and may incentivize the agency to provide more public particulars. If portions must remain sealed, request appointment of a special advocate or counsel with appropriate clearances to review ex parte declarations and challenge them to the extent possible.

Third, when feasible, litigate pressure points other than segregability: temporal limits on asserted pendency, narrower definitions of categories, and any public facts inconsistent with the agency’s claims. Showing publicly that the government’s categorical descriptions are overbroad or untethered to the actual document content increases the chance a court will scrutinize sealed claims more closely.

Fourth, where the records are voluminous, consider proposing a pragmatic sequencing: a phased disclosure process that tests whether limited redactions or sampling mechanisms can release genuinely nonexempt material without material risk. Sampling can force the agency to make hard calls about redaction rather than sweepingly asserting inextricability.

Conclusion

Farahi reinforces the D.C. Circuit’s preexisting approach: in national‑security–tainted FOIA disputes, courts will often defer to agency affidavits — including sealed ones — that explain both the harm of disclosure and the practical infeasibility of segregating releasable information. That doctrine is defensible on the ground that some details can only be litigated safely in camera. But it also entrenches an asymmetry that favors agencies with sensitive investigatory materials. For requesters, the lesson is practical and procedural: contest ex parte practice where possible, insist on as much public specificity as you can, and use procedural levers (sampling, phased production, special advocates) to avoid being shut out of meaningful review by sealed declarations.



## Category Definitions, Forfeiture, and the Vaughn Playbook After Farahi

Why this case matters: Farahi v. FBI is another reminder from the D.C. Circuit that the battles over FOIA exemptions increasingly occur at the threshold of how agencies define categories of withheld records — and that plaintiffs who fail to press precise, record-specific objections risk forfeiting their challenges. The court’s terse treatment of the category-definition issue — invoking CREW and Gallant, finding forfeiture because Farahi “provides no specific explanation” of overbreadth — should sharpen litigators’ attention to two linked tasks in FOIA litigation: (1) preserving a granular, document-level record about why a particular categorical withholding sweeps too broadly; and (2) anticipating what an agency’s Vaughn-style submissions (public declarations, ex parte affidavits, line-by-line logs, and category descriptions) must contain to survive judicial scrutiny in cases involving law‑enforcement and national‑security material.

What the court did (and did not) hold
The D.C. Circuit affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the FBI under Exemption 7(A), endorsing the agency’s showings that the records were compiled for law enforcement and that disclosure could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings. On the category-definition point the court said relatively little: it recited the familiar rule that when an agency withholds on a category-by-category basis the categories must be “sufficiently distinct” to permit judicial review (citing Gallant and CREW), but it disposed of Farahi’s challenge because he did not offer “specific explanation[s]” for why the FBI’s categories were overbroad and thus forfeited the argument.

That disposition is straightforward, but important. It confirms that the D.C. Circuit expects plaintiffs to do more than lodge generalized complaints about category breadth — and that courts will not parse the adequacy of category definitions unaided by adversarial briefing focused on particular categories and particular records.

Why granular preservation matters
Two features of FOIA litigation make specificity essential. First, courts often defer to agency expertise (particularly the FBI’s) about the law‑enforcement character of records and the risk that disclosure would interfere with investigations. That deference shifts pressure onto plaintiffs to demonstrate — with concrete, document-linked argument — why a claimed exemption does not apply. Second, category‑based withholdings are a practical necessity in voluminous productions; courts therefore require categories that are intelligible and tethered to identifiable bases for exemption so the court can test the agency’s claim without being forced into extensive in camera review.

“Sufficiently distinct” categories, per Gallant, mean more than labels. They must be definitional: each category should explain what the withheld documents are (who, when, type of document, subject matter) and why that class of material is covered by the asserted exemption. A plaintiff’s job, if contesting the categories, is to show in specific terms (and ideally citing to individual documents or representative examples) that the scope is untethered to the claimed exemption — e.g., that a category sweeps in purely administrative or historical material, or that the definition is so amorphous that the court cannot determine whether the exemption plausibly applies.

What Farahi tells litigators to do (plaintiff side)
1. Develop the record early. When you receive an agency’s declaration and category descriptions, map them against the responsive inventory (page counts, dates, document types). Identify categories that seem overbroad on their face, and flag representative documents or small subsets that illustrate the problem.

2. Make specific, document-linked arguments. Don’t say “categories are overbroad.” Say: “Category 4, described as ‘All correspondence relating to X,’ appears to include purely ministerial calendar entries and advisory emails from non‑investigative units (see CD 12, files A3–A7, which include email headers and non-substantive scheduling emails). Those materials are not compiled for law enforcement and do not risk interference.” When possible, cite page numbers, file names, Bates ranges, or exhibit identifiers the agency produced to you (or that the agency’s Vaughn submission cites).

3. Use proposed redactions and sampling. When documents might contain a mix of exempt and nonexempt material, propose a sampling protocol or suggested redactions that would isolate nonexempt information. That both assists the court in assessing segregability and signals diligence on the FOIA plaintiff’s part.

4. Preserve objections to ex parte reliance. If the government asks to submit ex parte or sealed declarations, object or at least contemporaneously request that the court provide a limited in camera review with a public summary that isolates the legal bases for withholding. Farahi was not heard to object to the ex parte process, and the court emphasized that point.

What Farahi suggests agencies will do (defendant side)
Agencies will continue to rely on the “Vaughn playbook” — category descriptions plus public and sealed declarations — but will be mindful that categories must be described in a way that lets a court (and an adversary in the briefs) assess their distinctness. Expect future Vaughn-style submissions to incorporate the following refinements to withstand D.C. Circuit scrutiny:

- More granular category labels: identify the custodial source, document type, date ranges, and the nexus to specific investigative activities or alleged wrongdoing.
- Representative exemplars: where practicable, produce redacted examples (in the public record) that show the sort of material encompassed in a category; where exemplar production is impossible for safety reasons, provide a detailed sealed description explaining why production would itself reveal sensitive investigative details.
- Segregability explanations tailored to categories: explain why nonexempt material is inextricably intertwined with exempt content or why line-by-line redaction would itself reveal the exempt material’s substance.
- Temporal updates: where the initial declaration is old, agencies will supplement with concise updates confirming ongoing activity or explaining continued risk (as the FBI did with its Bender declaration, albeit the court stressed that it must be considered in context with the more fulsome Hardy declarations).

Doctrinal implications and downstream effects
Farahi reaffirms two settled but consequential points in FOIA law. First, the Gallant/CREW requirement that categories be “sufficiently distinct” is procedural as much as substantive: it is a vehicle for meaningful judicial review. But second, and critically, that requirement places a corresponding burden on plaintiffs to mount focused, record-specific challenges. The upshot: FOIA plaintiffs who simply assert overbreadth at a high level risk procedural dismissal or forfeiture.

This dynamic will likely have two downstream effects. On the one hand, agencies will feel more secure in asserting categorical withholdings so long as they provide defensible category descriptions and robust (and when needed sealed) factual affidavits. On the other, well-resourced plaintiffs—especially organizations with access to technical review teams—can still win by doing the painstaking, document-by-document work early and pressing the court with concrete examples.

Practical litigation takeaways (brief)
For plaintiffs, Farahi is a practical warning: specificity wins. Preserve objections, tie them to pages or file identifiers, propose narrow redactions or sampling, and challenge any attempt by the agency to rely exclusively on ex parte submissions without adequate public justification. For agencies, the opinion signals that careful category architecture and contemporaneous factual updates matter; vagueness in category definitions invites targeted attacks, even if those attacks must themselves be specific.

Conclusion
Farahi is not a dramatic doctrinal shift. It is, instead, an enforcement of existing norms: categories must be intelligible, and challengers must be specific. The real lesson is tactical. FOIA fights over categories are won or lost in the weeds — in the exhibits, in the exhibit references in briefs, and in the pre‑motion work that links an allegedly overbroad category to actual pages in the record. Lawyers on both sides would be wise to treat category-definition disputes as ones-of-right rather than afterthoughts.
