Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 3: # Law-Enforcement Purpose: Satisfying Exemption 7’s Threshold

A threshold requirement for invoking FOIA Exemption 7 is that the records at issue were “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” The court’s treatment of that element in Farahi v. FBI illustrates both the legal standard and the kind of evidentiary showing that will satisfy it when the agency is the FBI and the subject matter implicates national-security investigations.

The legal test. We treat the “compiled for law enforcement purposes” inquiry as a factual one, but courts apply a legal standard shaped by precedents such as Pratt and Campbell. An agency must show a rational nexus between the investigation that produced the records and “one of the agency’s law enforcement duties.” Pratt v. Webster and Campbell v. DOJ make clear that this is not a hyper-technical test: the agency need not show a pending prosecution or a formal criminal charge, but it must connect the records to an identifiable law-enforcement function and to an individual or incident that poses a possible security risk or a potential violation of federal law. Put differently, the court asks whether it is reasonable to conclude that the records were created, maintained, or used in the course of the agency’s efforts to investigate, prevent, or prosecute unlawful activity—not whether they were generated for some wholly administrative or benign purpose.

Deference to the FBI. The opinion emphasizes that the FBI’s conclusion is entitled to a “fair measure of deference.” Because the FBI’s core mission is law enforcement—especially counterterrorism and national-security work—courts give weight to its characterization of records as law-enforcement materials. That deference does not replace the need for a factual showing, but it means courts will not require the same level of particularized proof they might demand from a non-law-enforcement agency.

What the Hardy declaration said publicly. In this case the FBI’s public (Hardy) declaration performed the initial work of tying the records to law-enforcement duties. The public declaration explained that the withheld records were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism,” and that those records related to pending proceedings and to the subject’s associations with individuals “directly involved in such violations.” This statement addressed both elements of the Pratt/Campbell test: it linked the records to the FBI’s law-enforcement responsibilities (investigating national-security and terrorism violations) and it linked the records to individuals and conduct that could pose security risks or involve federal crimes.

What the ex parte Hardy declaration supplied. The opinion highlights that the ex parte (sealed) Hardy declaration added significant, nonpublic detail about the investigation’s nature and scope. Courts often accept sealed, in camera submissions where national-security or law-enforcement sensitivities are at stake; such material can supply the factual specificity the public record cannot reveal without compromising ongoing work. Here, Hardy’s ex parte declaration elaborated on investigative facts—details the court could not publicly recite but that enabled it to assess whether a legitimate investigative nexus existed. The combination of the public declaration’s general attestations and the ex parte declaration’s specifics met the court’s need for a reasoned, evidentiary basis showing the records were law-enforcement materials.

Context supplied by the immigration proceedings. The opinion also treats the litigation background as corroborative context. In earlier immigration proceedings before other tribunals, the government had introduced evidence and testimony alleging that Farahi had connections to high-level terrorists and various organizations implicated in terrorism financing and support. The court cites specific allegations in the immigration record—ties to organizations designated as foreign terrorist organizations, connections to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other Al Qaeda affiliates, links to convicted or indicted terrorists such as Jose Padilla, and support for charities later tied to terrorism financing (e.g., the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation). Those prior allegations, which Farahi himself acknowledged in the district-court filings, reinforced the reasonableness of the FBI’s characterization of its file as law-enforcement material focused on national-security threats.

Why that combination sufficed. The opinion explains that the public Hardy declaration established the existence of an investigation tied to national-security law-enforcement duties, while the sealed Hardy declaration provided the necessary factual specificity about the investigation’s contours. The immigration-record allegations supplied independent context that made the agency’s assertions plausible. Under the Pratt/Campbell framework, and given the deference ordinarily afforded the FBI in national-security matters, this mix of public attestation, sealed elaboration, and corroborating context met the agency’s burden to show a rational nexus between the FBI’s law-enforcement responsibilities and the records compiled about Farahi.

Holding on the threshold. The court therefore held that the FBI satisfied Exemption 7’s threshold: the Farahi file was compiled for law-enforcement purposes. The decision illustrates a few practical lessons for reading FOIA opinions: (1) a public declaration that links records to an identifiable enforcement function is important; (2) sealed, ex parte declarations are often the vehicle for the granular factual detail courts need when national security or sensitive investigations are involved; and (3) corroborating factual context from parallel proceedings can strengthen the agency’s showing without substituting for the formal declaration requirement.
