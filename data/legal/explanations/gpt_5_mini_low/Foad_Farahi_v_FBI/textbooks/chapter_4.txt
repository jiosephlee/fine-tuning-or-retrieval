Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 4: # Interference with Pending or Anticipated Proceedings under Exemption 7(A)

The court’s Exemption 7(A) analysis turns on two linked points: (1) whether disclosure of the requested records “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings,” and (2) whether those proceedings were “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The opinion applies a familiar, fact-specific test: the FBI must show a rational connection between its investigatory files and law‑enforcement duties and then demonstrate that disclosure would pose a concrete risk to ongoing or foreseeable proceedings. That showing can be made through agency declarations, including classified or ex parte submissions when necessary, but those submissions must be sufficient to establish that the proceedings are more than speculative.

Interference rationale: tipping off targets to scope and direction of investigation
A central, well-established justification for Exemption 7(A) invoked here is the risk that disclosure will “tip off” investigation targets about the scope, direction, or evidence-gathering techniques of an investigation. The Hardy declaration explicitly relied on that rationale: revealing information contained in Farahi’s FBI file would be likely to alert targets and thereby frustrate evidence collection, witness cooperation, or future prosecutorial strategy. The court treats this as a paradigmatic 7(A) harm—one that courts have repeatedly recognized in the context of criminal and national‑security investigations. In short, the asserted interference is not a nebulous claim of embarrassment or inconvenience but a concrete policing concern: premature disclosure would materially impair the government’s ability to pursue or perfect enforcement actions.

Evidence of pendency: Hardy’s public attestation and ex parte elaboration
To invoke 7(A) the government must show that enforcement proceedings are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The court relied first on the public Hardy declaration, which affirmatively stated that the withheld records were compiled in furtherance of investigations into national‑security and terrorism matters and that those investigations remained ongoing. That public attestation establishes the “existence” of an investigation—sufficient, under our precedents, to show a proceeding for 7(A) purposes when coupled with a showing that disclosure would risk interference. The government supplemented the public declaration with an ex parte (sealed) Hardy declaration that provided further factual detail about the nature, scope, and sensitivity of the investigation. Although the court did not disclose the sealed contents, it credited them as supplying the specificity necessary to explain why the FBI reasonably anticipated continued enforcement activity and how disclosure would interfere.

Bender’s declarations as updates confirming continued pendency as of March 2023
When the district court initially hesitated because of the elapsed time between the FBI’s first declaration and its decision, the government filed updated declarations from Joseph Bender. The public Bender declaration succinctly stated that the agent supervising Farahi’s file had confirmed that investigations remained ongoing; the Bender submission also included an ex parte elaboration. The court treated these materials not as freestanding proofs of pendency but as contemporaneous updates that confirmed nothing material had changed since the Hardy declarations. In that role, Bender’s statements performed the necessary evidentiary function: they bridged the temporal gap and supported the continued applicability of 7(A) as of March 2023.

Addressing CREW’s caution about vagueness: context matters
Farahi relied on CREW, which warns that conclusory or vague assertions that an investigation is “ongoing” may not suffice to sustain Exemption 7(A), particularly when a long time has elapsed. The court recognized that Bender’s public declaration, taken alone, was terse and could look conclusory. But CREW does not require that every update be a stand-alone mosaic of facts; rather, it requires that the overall record—public and ex parte declarations taken together—provide an adequate factual basis. Here the court emphasized context: the detailed ex parte Hardy declaration and the district court’s earlier finding that the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden together supply the specificity CREW demands. Thus, Bender’s brief confirmation functions as a permissible update to an already detailed factual record rather than an insufficient, isolated claim of continued investigation.

Role of national‑security deference and breadth of the counterterrorism investigation
The opinion also underscores institutional and contextual deference to the FBI’s judgment in national‑security and counterterrorism matters. The court repeatedly notes precedent that grants the FBI a “fair measure of deference” when it invokes law‑enforcement exemptions, and it treats such deference as particularly appropriate where disclosure would implicate counterterrorism work. That deference operates at two levels: first, in evaluating whether the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes; and second, in assessing whether disclosure would reasonably be expected to interfere with ongoing enforcement activity. The court also considered the breadth of the underlying investigation—public materials in related immigration proceedings and the sealed declarations describing ties to known terrorist actors—to conclude that ongoing investigatory work was plausible and that the government’s non‑speculative fear of interference was reasonable.

Conclusion: existence of a concrete prospective law‑enforcement proceeding established; Exemption 7(A) satisfied
Weighing the public and sealed evidence together, and giving appropriate deference in a national‑security context, the court concluded that the FBI met its burden under Exemption 7(A). The record showed a concrete, prospective law‑enforcement proceeding that was pending or reasonably anticipated; it showed a plausible, concrete risk that disclosure would tip off targets and impede evidence-gathering; and it supplied adequate explanation—including ex parte detail—for why nonexempt material could not be meaningfully segregated. For those reasons, the court upheld the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the FBI under Exemption 7(A).
