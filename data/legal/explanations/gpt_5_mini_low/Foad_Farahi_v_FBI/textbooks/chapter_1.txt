Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural Posture

This chapter explains who the parties are, what records Foad Farahi sought from the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), how the FBI processed his Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request, and the sequence of filings and court rulings that produced the appeal to the D.C. Circuit. The account focuses on the factual and procedural steps that the district court and the court of appeals relied on when evaluating the government’s use of FOIA Exemption 7(A) and the separate question of segregability.

Farahi’s immigration history and DHS’s reliance on FBI evidence in removal proceedings
Foad Farahi is an Iranian national who has lived in the United States since 1993. In 2002 the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) initiated removal proceedings against him based on visa overstay. Throughout those immigration proceedings Farahi sought relief by applying for asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. In opposing his claims, DHS relied in part on materials and live testimony produced by the FBI: an FBI agent testified and presented documents that, according to DHS, documented Farahi’s connections to high‑level terrorists. Those allegations included ties to individuals later detained as enemy combatants and to organizations designated as foreign terrorist organizations, and specific references in the record linked Farahi to people and entities involved in terrorism prosecutions. Farahi’s immigration litigation with DHS therefore provides substantive context for the FBI’s investigation and the sensitivity of the records he later sought under FOIA.

FOIA request scope: approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs located
Farahi submitted a FOIA request seeking his FBI file. The agency’s search located a very large volume of potentially responsive material: roughly 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. That scope meant the request implicated a substantial repository of investigative records, many of which the FBI treated as potentially sensitive because they were compiled in the course of national‑security and counterterrorism investigations. The sheer quantity of material also framed later disputes about segregability: whether reasonably nonexempt portions could be produced without revealing exempt material.

Initial disclosures and withholdings; FBI’s 2019 summary‑judgment motion
After the FBI located the records, it conducted a document review and produced some materials to Farahi but withheld the bulk of the records. Farahi sued after the FBI failed to meet FOIA’s statutory deadline for responding. The litigation proceeded to summary judgment practice. In October 2019 the FBI moved for summary judgment, submitting a public declaration from David Hardy, then Section Chief of the FBI’s Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Hardy’s public declaration stated that the withheld records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and asserted that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that were pending or reasonably anticipated. Because the declaration contained classified or otherwise sensitive detail, the FBI also sought — and obtained without opposition from Farahi — leave to file a supplemental declaration under seal and ex parte. That sealed declaration provided additional factual specifics about the relevant investigation that were not placed on the public record.

Public and ex parte Hardy declarations; district court’s 2022 order seeking updates due to staleness and segregability concerns
The district court considered the FBI’s initial summary judgment submission in November 2022. It accepted that the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” and it observed that, at least seemingly, the FBI had met its burden under Exemption 7(A) to show that disclosure could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. Because a significant amount of time had elapsed between the FBI’s initial Hardy declaration and the court’s consideration of summary judgment, the district court was concerned that the factual basis for claiming interference might have become stale. The court therefore ordered the FBI to update its assessment — to confirm whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or were still reasonably anticipated. The court also concluded it needed additional information to resolve segregability (whether any reasonably nonexempt information could be released), but explained that it could not state the basis for those segregability concerns on the public record. Consequently, the district court denied the FBI’s first summary judgment motion without prejudice and directed the government to supply updated declarations addressing both pendency and segregability.

Renewed motion with public and ex parte Bender declarations; district court’s grant of summary judgment
The FBI complied by submitting supplemental declarations, this time including both a public and an ex parte declaration from Joseph Bender, who was then Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In the public Bender declaration the FBI reported that RIDS had contacted the FBI agent responsible for overseeing Farahi’s file and that the agent confirmed the investigation remained ongoing. The government again filed an ex parte declaration with classified or otherwise sensitive details describing the nature and scope of the investigation and setting out why segregable nonexempt material could not be meaningfully released. After reviewing the public and sealed submissions, the district court granted the renewed motion for summary judgment. The court credited the FBI’s confirmation that the relevant enforcement proceedings remained ongoing and found that the sealed ex parte submission adequately explained why the withheld material was not reasonably segregable. The district court therefore concluded Exemption 7(A) applied and that no meaningful nonexempt material could be segregated for release.

Appeal to the D.C. Circuit
Farahi appealed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the United States Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit. His appeal attacked several aspects of the district court’s disposition: he argued the FBI had not carried its burden to show that enforcement proceedings were “pending or reasonably anticipated” given the passage of time; he challenged the sufficiency and specificity of the categories the FBI used to justify categorical withholdings; and he contended the agency had not taken adequate steps to segregate and release nonexempt information and that the district court improperly relied on ex parte submissions to resolve segregability. The D.C. Circuit reviewed the grant of summary judgment de novo, evaluated the sufficiency of the FBI’s public and sealed Hardy and Bender declarations (considering the district court’s earlier findings and the context of the sealed materials), and affirmed the district court’s decision that Exemption 7(A) protected the withheld records and that the agency satisfied its segregability obligations under FOIA.
