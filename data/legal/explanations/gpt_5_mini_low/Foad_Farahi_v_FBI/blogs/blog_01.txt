Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# D.C. Circuit Doubles Down on Exemption 7(A) Deference in National-Security Files

## Why Farahi matters
The D.C. Circuit’s decision in Farahi v. FBI (Sept. 30, 2025) is a consequential reaffirmation of the court’s repeated willingness to defer to the FBI when Exemption 7(A) is invoked in the national-security and counterterrorism context. For FOIA requesters who seek files touching on counterterrorism, foreign terrorist organizations, or investigations tied to national security, Farahi signals that courts in this circuit will accept agency attestations that (1) records were compiled for law enforcement purposes, (2) disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings, and (3) meaningful segregability is often not practicable—so long as the agency provides some combination of public and classified ex parte detail. The opinion is important not because it breaks new doctrinal ground, but because it consolidates and applies a string of precedents—CREW, Juarez, CNSS, Campbell, and Mead—to sustain broad protection for terrorism-related files against FOIA disclosure.

## The court’s reasoning in brief
The FBI located more than 10,000 pages responsive to Farahi’s request and withheld most of them under Exemption 7(A). After an initial summary-judgment submission, the district court—concerned about passage of time—required the FBI to update its declarations. The FBI then filed public and ex parte declarations confirming that investigations related to Farahi remained “ongoing” and explaining why nonexempt material could not meaningfully be segregated. The district court granted summary judgment to the FBI; the D.C. Circuit affirmed.

The panel’s analysis follows familiar lines. First, because the FBI is a law-enforcement agency, its view that the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” is owed a “fair measure of deference” (Campbell; Clemente) and was easily satisfied here given the terrorism-linked allegations in Farahi’s immigration record. Second, Exemption 7(A)’s interference prong requires that the enforcement proceedings be “pending or reasonably anticipated” (CREW), and courts in this circuit accept agency attestations of ongoing investigations—particularly where, as here, the agency supplemented a public declaration with classified ex parte material that provided substantive detail (Juarez; CNSS). The court rejected Farahi’s staleness argument largely because the agency supplied an “update” confirming the continuing existence and scope of the investigation; the Bender declaration was short, but it was not the only evidence. Finally, on segregability, the court relied on the FBI’s ex parte showing that nonexempt fragments were so interspersed as to be not “reasonably segregable” (Mead; Perioperative).

## What the opinion reinforces about Exemption 7(A)
Three doctrinal points stand out from Farahi.

First, evidentiary burden and deference. The government’s burden under Exemption 7(A) is not trivial, but the D.C. Circuit continues to give the FBI significant breathing room. Where an agency with national-security responsibilities provides a public declaration attesting to the law-enforcement purpose of records and supplements it with classified ex parte material that describes the nature and scope of an investigation, courts will typically accept the agency’s showing that disclosure could interfere with investigations. Farahi reiterates that courts assess these showings holistically; a terse update can suffice when it supplements earlier, more detailed classified submissions.

Second, “pending or reasonably anticipated” remains a low threshold when an investigation continues to gather evidence. Juarez and CREW already held that ongoing criminal investigations count as “proceedings” and that the government may establish pendency by attesting to existence. Farahi leans on those precedents to make clear that a broad counterterrorism investigation—especially one tied to foreign terrorist organizations and known violent actors—creates a plausible, concrete prospective law-enforcement proceeding that disclosure could jeopardize.

Third, segregability doctrine in national-security cases remains cabined by Mead-style principles: courts will credit agency explanations—again, especially when supported by classified submissions—that nonexempt material is inextricably intertwined with exempt information such that meaningful release would itself reveal sensitive content. The court reiterates that FOIA’s aim is information, not mere words on a page; juiced-up word searches or piecemeal disclosures that would still convey operative intelligence are not required.

## Practical consequences and downstream effects
For FOIA litigants, Farahi has immediate practical implications. Staleness challenges—arguing that an investigation has become so aged that Exemption 7(A) no longer applies—face an uphill climb absent contemporaneous public evidence that the investigation has been closed. The D.C. Circuit gives agencies an opportunity to refresh their assertions; a short, contemporaneous attestation that an investigation “remains ongoing” can, when buttressed by prior ex parte detail, be decisive.

The decision also makes it harder to litigate segregability piecemeal. When core facts are national-security sensitive, courts will accept agency attestations that releasable material is either so scattered that disclosure would reveal the exempt substance or would itself facilitate inference about the substance of the investigation. That dynamic preserves a durable wall against disclosure for voluminous terrorism-related files.

For agencies, Farahi affirms that submitting classified ex parte declarations remains a powerful tool—one that can be decisive on both the pendency and segregability questions. For requesters, however, ex parte submissions create procedural and substantive asymmetry: the district court and the appellate panel may consider information that the requester never sees, making appeals and public rebuttal harder.

## Strategic takeaways for FOIA challengers
Farahi does not mean that all hope is lost for FOIA requesters seeking terrorism-related files, but it does suggest best practices that will be increasingly necessary in this circuit.

First, litigants should press early to identify and contest any vagueness in the agency’s public declarations. CREW cautions against accepting conclusory “ongoing” claims in a vacuum; the more detailed and concrete the public showing, the less that ex parte classified proof will be necessary to sustain the agency.

Second, challenge the categorization and the Vaughn-style treatment where possible. The D.C. Circuit in Farahi rejected an overbreadth challenge as forfeited. Requesters should therefore press district courts to require precise category definitions, a robust Vaughn index (to the extent compatible with classification restrictions), and clear contemporaneous statements addressing whether investigations are active, their scope, and particularized reasons why specific categories would reveal information harmful to ongoing proceedings.

Third, where ex parte submissions are used, litigants should seek in camera review by the court and move for summaries that explain—as much as possible on the public record—the bases for withheld categories. While courts may not be able to disclose classified detail, they can and should explain why the agency prevailed without simply reciting deference.

Finally, strategic settlement may be more attractive: agencies sometimes will produce limited redactions or summaries in the face of litigation risk and public pressure, and negotiating a tailored release (perhaps a sanitized summary) may be preferable to protracted litigation that will likely be decided on classified affidavits.

## Broader doctrinal and policy questions
Farahi underscores a persistent tension in FOIA law: balancing transparency with operational secrecy. The D.C. Circuit’s posture favors operational secrecy in the counterterrorism arena, reflecting the practical difficulty courts face in evaluating national-security risk without seeing classified material. That posture is defensible as a matter of institutional competence—courts cannot responsibly second-guess certain national-security judgments without access to sensitive information—but it also means FOIA’s promise of accountability is empirically narrowed when investigations implicate terrorism or national-security actors.

The opinion may also nudge litigants toward statutory or policy routes outside FOIA when accountability is the goal—e.g., congressional oversight, Inspector General reviews, or carefully tailored Freedom of Information Act litigation strategies that pressure agencies to release non-sensitive metadata or process-oriented documents (e.g., timelines, policies) that can be disclosed without revealing operational secrets.

## Conclusion
Farahi is a reminder that, in the D.C. Circuit, Exemption 7(A) remains a robust shield for FBI files touching national security. The decision does not announce novel law but synthesizes CREW, Juarez, CNSS, and Mead into a clear message: when the FBI couples public attestation with classified ex parte detail—and supplies contemporaneous updates—courts will generally accept that disclosure could interfere with ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings and that meaningful segregability is not feasible. For FOIA practitioners, Farahi demands careful front-end challenges to public declarations, meticulous work on categorical and Vaughn issues, and creative litigation strategies to obtain at least some transparency when national-security concerns loom.
