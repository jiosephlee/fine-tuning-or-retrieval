Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

## Category Definitions, Forfeiture, and the Vaughn Playbook After Farahi

Why this case matters: Farahi v. FBI is another reminder from the D.C. Circuit that the battles over FOIA exemptions increasingly occur at the threshold of how agencies define categories of withheld records — and that plaintiffs who fail to press precise, record-specific objections risk forfeiting their challenges. The court’s terse treatment of the category-definition issue — invoking CREW and Gallant, finding forfeiture because Farahi “provides no specific explanation” of overbreadth — should sharpen litigators’ attention to two linked tasks in FOIA litigation: (1) preserving a granular, document-level record about why a particular categorical withholding sweeps too broadly; and (2) anticipating what an agency’s Vaughn-style submissions (public declarations, ex parte affidavits, line-by-line logs, and category descriptions) must contain to survive judicial scrutiny in cases involving law‑enforcement and national‑security material.

What the court did (and did not) hold
The D.C. Circuit affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the FBI under Exemption 7(A), endorsing the agency’s showings that the records were compiled for law enforcement and that disclosure could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings. On the category-definition point the court said relatively little: it recited the familiar rule that when an agency withholds on a category-by-category basis the categories must be “sufficiently distinct” to permit judicial review (citing Gallant and CREW), but it disposed of Farahi’s challenge because he did not offer “specific explanation[s]” for why the FBI’s categories were overbroad and thus forfeited the argument.

That disposition is straightforward, but important. It confirms that the D.C. Circuit expects plaintiffs to do more than lodge generalized complaints about category breadth — and that courts will not parse the adequacy of category definitions unaided by adversarial briefing focused on particular categories and particular records.

Why granular preservation matters
Two features of FOIA litigation make specificity essential. First, courts often defer to agency expertise (particularly the FBI’s) about the law‑enforcement character of records and the risk that disclosure would interfere with investigations. That deference shifts pressure onto plaintiffs to demonstrate — with concrete, document-linked argument — why a claimed exemption does not apply. Second, category‑based withholdings are a practical necessity in voluminous productions; courts therefore require categories that are intelligible and tethered to identifiable bases for exemption so the court can test the agency’s claim without being forced into extensive in camera review.

“Sufficiently distinct” categories, per Gallant, mean more than labels. They must be definitional: each category should explain what the withheld documents are (who, when, type of document, subject matter) and why that class of material is covered by the asserted exemption. A plaintiff’s job, if contesting the categories, is to show in specific terms (and ideally citing to individual documents or representative examples) that the scope is untethered to the claimed exemption — e.g., that a category sweeps in purely administrative or historical material, or that the definition is so amorphous that the court cannot determine whether the exemption plausibly applies.

What Farahi tells litigators to do (plaintiff side)
1. Develop the record early. When you receive an agency’s declaration and category descriptions, map them against the responsive inventory (page counts, dates, document types). Identify categories that seem overbroad on their face, and flag representative documents or small subsets that illustrate the problem.

2. Make specific, document-linked arguments. Don’t say “categories are overbroad.” Say: “Category 4, described as ‘All correspondence relating to X,’ appears to include purely ministerial calendar entries and advisory emails from non‑investigative units (see CD 12, files A3–A7, which include email headers and non-substantive scheduling emails). Those materials are not compiled for law enforcement and do not risk interference.” When possible, cite page numbers, file names, Bates ranges, or exhibit identifiers the agency produced to you (or that the agency’s Vaughn submission cites).

3. Use proposed redactions and sampling. When documents might contain a mix of exempt and nonexempt material, propose a sampling protocol or suggested redactions that would isolate nonexempt information. That both assists the court in assessing segregability and signals diligence on the FOIA plaintiff’s part.

4. Preserve objections to ex parte reliance. If the government asks to submit ex parte or sealed declarations, object or at least contemporaneously request that the court provide a limited in camera review with a public summary that isolates the legal bases for withholding. Farahi was not heard to object to the ex parte process, and the court emphasized that point.

What Farahi suggests agencies will do (defendant side)
Agencies will continue to rely on the “Vaughn playbook” — category descriptions plus public and sealed declarations — but will be mindful that categories must be described in a way that lets a court (and an adversary in the briefs) assess their distinctness. Expect future Vaughn-style submissions to incorporate the following refinements to withstand D.C. Circuit scrutiny:

- More granular category labels: identify the custodial source, document type, date ranges, and the nexus to specific investigative activities or alleged wrongdoing.
- Representative exemplars: where practicable, produce redacted examples (in the public record) that show the sort of material encompassed in a category; where exemplar production is impossible for safety reasons, provide a detailed sealed description explaining why production would itself reveal sensitive investigative details.
- Segregability explanations tailored to categories: explain why nonexempt material is inextricably intertwined with exempt content or why line-by-line redaction would itself reveal the exempt material’s substance.
- Temporal updates: where the initial declaration is old, agencies will supplement with concise updates confirming ongoing activity or explaining continued risk (as the FBI did with its Bender declaration, albeit the court stressed that it must be considered in context with the more fulsome Hardy declarations).

Doctrinal implications and downstream effects
Farahi reaffirms two settled but consequential points in FOIA law. First, the Gallant/CREW requirement that categories be “sufficiently distinct” is procedural as much as substantive: it is a vehicle for meaningful judicial review. But second, and critically, that requirement places a corresponding burden on plaintiffs to mount focused, record-specific challenges. The upshot: FOIA plaintiffs who simply assert overbreadth at a high level risk procedural dismissal or forfeiture.

This dynamic will likely have two downstream effects. On the one hand, agencies will feel more secure in asserting categorical withholdings so long as they provide defensible category descriptions and robust (and when needed sealed) factual affidavits. On the other, well-resourced plaintiffs—especially organizations with access to technical review teams—can still win by doing the painstaking, document-by-document work early and pressing the court with concrete examples.

Practical litigation takeaways (brief)
For plaintiffs, Farahi is a practical warning: specificity wins. Preserve objections, tie them to pages or file identifiers, propose narrow redactions or sampling, and challenge any attempt by the agency to rely exclusively on ex parte submissions without adequate public justification. For agencies, the opinion signals that careful category architecture and contemporaneous factual updates matter; vagueness in category definitions invites targeted attacks, even if those attacks must themselves be specific.

Conclusion
Farahi is not a dramatic doctrinal shift. It is, instead, an enforcement of existing norms: categories must be intelligible, and challengers must be specific. The real lesson is tactical. FOIA fights over categories are won or lost in the weeds — in the exhibits, in the exhibit references in briefs, and in the pre‑motion work that links an allegedly overbroad category to actual pages in the record. Lawyers on both sides would be wise to treat category-definition disputes as ones-of-right rather than afterthoughts.
