Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

## ‘Stale’ No More: Managing Time in FOIA Through Updated Declarations

Why this case matters

Foad Farahi v. FBI answers a recurring procedural question that has bedeviled FOIA litigation for years: when an agency relies on an earlier, detailed declaration to justify withholding records under an exemption, how fresh must that showing be after protracted litigation? The D.C. Circuit’s decision—affirming the district court’s insistence on an updated showing and its acceptance of a terse “confirmation” declaration as an adequate update to an earlier, detailed submission—clarifies the interplay between (1) the judicial concern that factual showings not grow stale over litigation, and (2) the practical limits on what agencies may (or should) disclose when national-security or investigative sensitivity is implicated.

At bottom, the court stakes out a middle path. It reiterates that agencies must refresh their factual basis for withholding when time has passed, but it also permits an agency to supply a short, contemporaneous “update” that confirms the continuing existence of the underlying circumstances described in a prior, more fulsome declaration — so long as that update is read in context with the earlier material (including any ex parte filings).

What the court did — the reasoning in brief

The panel affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the FBI under FOIA Exemption 7(A) (records compiled for law enforcement purposes that could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings). Two linked procedural questions governed the outcome.

First, did the FBI’s materials show that the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes? The court accepted the FBI’s public declaration (Hardy) and its sealed ex parte declaration as an adequate factual showing, given the FBI’s law-enforcement role and the plaintiff’s own concessions in related immigration litigation about alleged ties to terrorism. The court emphasized the usual deference given to FBI affidavits, especially in national-security and counterterrorism contexts.

Second, and the point that drives the opinion’s practical import: because years had elapsed since the FBI’s initial declaration, did the government need to update that showing to prove that enforcement proceedings were still “pending or reasonably anticipated”? The district court required a refreshed assessment; the FBI supplied a contemporaneous, comparatively terse public declaration from Bender stating that the investigations “remain ongoing,” together with Bender’s sealed submission and the earlier Hardy declarations. The D.C. Circuit approved this approach, holding that the Bender declaration could legitimately be viewed as an update — not a standalone replacement — of the earlier record. Context mattered: the court refused to treat the brief update in isolation because the government had already made a detailed showing and because the district court had previously found the initial showing to “seemingly” satisfy the regulatory elements. The court also relied on the breadth of the underlying investigation as making it plausible that proceedings remained ongoing.

The court likewise upheld the district court’s segregability analysis: the FBI’s ex parte showing explained why any nonexempt material was inextricably intertwined with exempt material or was otherwise not “reasonably segregable,” and the court accepted that justification despite the voluminous nature of the records.

Analytical perspective — what the opinion permits and limits

Farahi does two important things simultaneously: it reinforces courts’ vigilance against stale factual showings, while recognizing pragmatic ways agencies can comply without re-litigating sensitive substance in open court.

First, the opinion reiterates that courts have power — and responsibility — to require agencies to refresh evidentiary showings when factual predicates may have changed during protracted litigation. That is consistent with CREW and other decisions cautioning against blind reliance on old declarations. Courts will not accept a declaration that is plausibly stale simply because the agency once said the investigation was ongoing.

Second, the decision gives agencies a workable procedure to meet that renewed burden: a short, contemporaneous “confirmation” can suffice if the earlier, detailed submissions remain in the record and the update is plainly an update rather than a new, standalone assertion. In other words, freshness can be demonstrated by a combination of (a) earlier detailed factual materials, (b) a contemporaneous, brief attestation that nothing material has changed, and (c) — when necessary — sealed, ex parte materials that provide the district court the factual specificity it needs to evaluate withheld materials without public disclosure.

But this is a contextual decision, and several important limits remain. The court explicitly warns—following CREW—that a conclusory “investigation remains ongoing” assertion cannot always carry the day when considered in isolation. Here, the update was admissible only because it was explicitly tied back to substantial earlier declarations (including ex parte material) and because the district court had already determined that the government had “seemingly” met its burden. Thus, requesters still have traction when an update is both conclusory and untethered to a prior detailed showing. Courts will not allow agencies to warehouse old, potentially stale affidavits and then prop them up with a perfunctory, post hoc stamp of “still ongoing” unless the earlier, fuller evidence is adequate and remains credible.

Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

The decision shapes doctrine principally in two areas: (1) the temporal dimensions of an agency’s burden under Exemption 7(A), and (2) practical handling of segregability when responsive records are massive and interspersed with sensitive material.

On timing and “freshness,” Farahi confirms that courts will police the chronological integrity of an agency’s showing. Agencies should not expect a single, early affidavit to survive for years of delay without some refreshed attestation. At the same time, the ruling formally recognizes that courts can accept a modest update as sufficient when it explicitly supplements an earlier, more fulsome showing. So the doctrine evolves toward a functional, holistic assessment: courts will consider the entire evidentiary mosaic — initial declarations, contemporaneous updates, and ex parte materials — rather than insisting on a single document meeting a rigid checklist.

On segregability, the panel reasserts the established line: small nonexempt material randomly scattered through highly sensitive records may be “not reasonably segregable” where extraction would itself reveal sensitive context. That reasoning reinforces precedent holding that FOIA’s focus is disclosure of meaningfully informative material, not the mechanical release of isolated words or phrases.

Practical playbook — for agencies and requesters

For agencies:
- Treat initial declarations as live files that require maintenance. If litigation stretches for years, plan to file a short, timely update that (1) links explicitly to the initial declaration(s), (2) confirms whether the operative investigative facts remain the same, and (3) says whether any material has changed since the earlier submission. When secrecy demands it, supplement public updates with sealed ex parte submissions to provide the court needed specificity.
- When crafting category-by-category withholdings and segregability rationales, make the record robust and sufficiently granular up front; doing so will ease later reliance on terse updates because the court can read the update in context of a detailed foundation.

For requesters:
- Challenge staleness early and specifically. Broad “it’s been too long” objections are weaker than pointed arguments showing how the initial factual predicate could have changed.
- Insist that the court require an updated showing when initial declarations are several years old. If the agency provides only a terse update, press the court to evaluate the update against the earlier detailed materials and to demand more detail if the earlier materials themselves were thin.
- Preserve arguments about timing and sufficiency: Farahi rejected certain challenges as forfeited or inadequately developed; litigants shouldn’t give agencies uncontested procedural advantages.

Concluding thought

Farahi does not radically change FOIA law, but it clarifies the operating rules for a problem that arises in many FOIA cases: how to keep the agency’s factual showing current without forcing public disclosure of sensitive investigative detail. The decision requires agencies to refresh their evidence when time has passed, but it sensibly allows a short, contemporaneous confirmation to bridge an earlier, detailed affidavit — provided the update is contextualized and the underlying showing remains persuasive. For litigants and courts, the lesson is pragmatic: FOIA fights are not frozen in time, but neither must they result in public re-litigation of sensitive specifics every few years.
