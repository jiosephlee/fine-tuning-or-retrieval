Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

This chapter explains who the parties are, what records Foad Farahi sought from the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), how the FBI processed his Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request, and the sequence of filings and court rulings that produced the appeal to the D.C. Circuit. The account focuses on the factual and procedural steps that the district court and the court of appeals relied on when evaluating the government’s use of FOIA Exemption 7(A) and the separate question of segregability.

Farahi’s immigration history and DHS’s reliance on FBI evidence in removal proceedings
Foad Farahi is an Iranian national who has lived in the United States since 1993. In 2002 the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) initiated removal proceedings against him based on visa overstay. Throughout those immigration proceedings Farahi sought relief by applying for asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. In opposing his claims, DHS relied in part on materials and live testimony produced by the FBI: an FBI agent testified and presented documents that, according to DHS, documented Farahi’s connections to high‑level terrorists. Those allegations included ties to individuals later detained as enemy combatants and to organizations designated as foreign terrorist organizations, and specific references in the record linked Farahi to people and entities involved in terrorism prosecutions. Farahi’s immigration litigation with DHS therefore provides substantive context for the FBI’s investigation and the sensitivity of the records he later sought under FOIA.

FOIA request scope: approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs located
Farahi submitted a FOIA request seeking his FBI file. The agency’s search located a very large volume of potentially responsive material: roughly 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. That scope meant the request implicated a substantial repository of investigative records, many of which the FBI treated as potentially sensitive because they were compiled in the course of national‑security and counterterrorism investigations. The sheer quantity of material also framed later disputes about segregability: whether reasonably nonexempt portions could be produced without revealing exempt material.

Initial disclosures and withholdings; FBI’s 2019 summary‑judgment motion
After the FBI located the records, it conducted a document review and produced some materials to Farahi but withheld the bulk of the records. Farahi sued after the FBI failed to meet FOIA’s statutory deadline for responding. The litigation proceeded to summary judgment practice. In October 2019 the FBI moved for summary judgment, submitting a public declaration from David Hardy, then Section Chief of the FBI’s Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Hardy’s public declaration stated that the withheld records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and asserted that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that were pending or reasonably anticipated. Because the declaration contained classified or otherwise sensitive detail, the FBI also sought — and obtained without opposition from Farahi — leave to file a supplemental declaration under seal and ex parte. That sealed declaration provided additional factual specifics about the relevant investigation that were not placed on the public record.

Public and ex parte Hardy declarations; district court’s 2022 order seeking updates due to staleness and segregability concerns
The district court considered the FBI’s initial summary judgment submission in November 2022. It accepted that the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” and it observed that, at least seemingly, the FBI had met its burden under Exemption 7(A) to show that disclosure could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. Because a significant amount of time had elapsed between the FBI’s initial Hardy declaration and the court’s consideration of summary judgment, the district court was concerned that the factual basis for claiming interference might have become stale. The court therefore ordered the FBI to update its assessment — to confirm whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or were still reasonably anticipated. The court also concluded it needed additional information to resolve segregability (whether any reasonably nonexempt information could be released), but explained that it could not state the basis for those segregability concerns on the public record. Consequently, the district court denied the FBI’s first summary judgment motion without prejudice and directed the government to supply updated declarations addressing both pendency and segregability.

Renewed motion with public and ex parte Bender declarations; district court’s grant of summary judgment
The FBI complied by submitting supplemental declarations, this time including both a public and an ex parte declaration from Joseph Bender, who was then Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In the public Bender declaration the FBI reported that RIDS had contacted the FBI agent responsible for overseeing Farahi’s file and that the agent confirmed the investigation remained ongoing. The government again filed an ex parte declaration with classified or otherwise sensitive details describing the nature and scope of the investigation and setting out why segregable nonexempt material could not be meaningfully released. After reviewing the public and sealed submissions, the district court granted the renewed motion for summary judgment. The court credited the FBI’s confirmation that the relevant enforcement proceedings remained ongoing and found that the sealed ex parte submission adequately explained why the withheld material was not reasonably segregable. The district court therefore concluded Exemption 7(A) applied and that no meaningful nonexempt material could be segregated for release.

Appeal to the D.C. Circuit
Farahi appealed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the United States Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit. His appeal attacked several aspects of the district court’s disposition: he argued the FBI had not carried its burden to show that enforcement proceedings were “pending or reasonably anticipated” given the passage of time; he challenged the sufficiency and specificity of the categories the FBI used to justify categorical withholdings; and he contended the agency had not taken adequate steps to segregate and release nonexempt information and that the district court improperly relied on ex parte submissions to resolve segregability. The D.C. Circuit reviewed the grant of summary judgment de novo, evaluated the sufficiency of the FBI’s public and sealed Hardy and Bender declarations (considering the district court’s earlier findings and the context of the sealed materials), and affirmed the district court’s decision that Exemption 7(A) protected the withheld records and that the agency satisfied its segregability obligations under FOIA.

# FOIA Framework, Exemption 7(A), and Standards of Review

This chapter explains the statutory framework and judicial standards the D.C. Circuit applied in Farahi v. FBI to decide whether the FBI properly withheld records under FOIA’s Exemption 7(A). The discussion is aimed at a student learning to read the opinion: it identifies the legal elements the FBI had to prove, the precedents the court relied on to interpret those elements, how courts treat agency representations (especially from law‑enforcement agencies), and the practical duties that remain even when an exemption applies.

## FOIA’s disclosure mandate and the government’s burden

FOIA establishes a broad disclosure obligation: federal agencies must make records available to the public on request unless a statutory exemption applies (5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(3); see opinion I). Crucially, the government bears the burden of proving that an exemption authorizes withholding. The court in Farahi repeatedly frames the inquiry by reference to that burden: to prevail on summary judgment, the FBI had to show both that the disputed records fall within the language of Exemption 7(A) and that disclosure would present the specific harm the exemption protects against (citing 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B) and the court’s FOIA jurisprudence). FOIA cases proceed like other civil actions on summary judgment, and the agency’s evidentiary showing must suffice to sustain judgment for the government.

Put simply for this case: the FBI had to (1) show the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and (2) show that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” The court treated those as distinct elements that the FBI needed to establish.

## Exemption 7(A): two elements and how they operate here

Exemption 7(A) covers records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if their release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” (5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A)). The opinion breaks the exemption into two inquiries, following controlling precedent.

First, the threshold “compiled for law enforcement purposes” inquiry asks whether the records have a rational nexus to one of the agency’s law‑enforcement duties and to an identifiable investigation or potential violation of law. Because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, courts give its determination a “fair measure of deference,” but the agency’s declarations still must establish the necessary nexus and connection (see Campbell v. DOJ; Pratt as quoted in Campbell). In Farahi, the court accepted the FBI’s public and ex parte declarations (and contextual facts about Farahi’s immigration proceedings and the allegations of terrorist ties) as satisfying the threshold element.

Second, the “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” requirement is a harm‑based showing. The critical interpretive gloss, drawn from CREW and Juarez and applied in Farahi, is that the enforcement “proceedings” that disclosure might interfere with must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” An ongoing criminal investigation qualifies as a “proceeding” for Exemption 7(A) purposes; an agency may show such an investigation exists by attesting to its existence, and courts will sustain that showing when the investigation remains engaged in evidence gathering that would be jeopardized by premature disclosure. In Farahi, the court accepted the FBI’s statements that the investigation remained ongoing and explained why disclosure could tip off targets about the scope of the investigation—an oft‑cited and accepted rationale for invoking 7(A).

The two elements therefore require (1) factual linkage of the records to law‑enforcement functions and (2) a specific, plausible explanation of how disclosure would harm pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement activity.

## CREW and Juarez: “pending or reasonably anticipated” and the evidentiary posture

Two decisions the opinion relies on are CREW (Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington v. DOJ) and Juarez v. DOJ. Those cases refine what counts as a “proceeding” and what an agency must show:

- CREW emphasizes that the proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated,” rejecting a broad reading that would allow stale or merely theoretical investigations to justify withholding. CREW also warns that conclusory assertions that an investigation is ongoing may be insufficient if the government provides no concrete basis for that claim.
- Juarez explains that continuing investigative activity — particularly evidence gathering for a possible future criminal case — suffices as a “proceeding.” Juarez also affirms that an agency may show an ongoing investigation by attestation to its existence, provided there is enough context to render the assertion plausible.

In Farahi the court reconciled those principles: a terse or conclusory contemporaneous declaration (Bender’s) would not be enough standing alone, but viewed in context with earlier, detailed Hardy declarations (including a sealed ex parte declaration) and the district court’s earlier finding that the FBI “seemingly” met its burden, the update served as an adequate confirmation that investigations remained pending. Thus, courts evaluate the sufficiency of an “ongoing” allegation in context, asking whether the record as a whole provides concrete support for the government’s assertion that enforcement proceedings exist and would be harmed by disclosure.

## Agency deference, especially in national security and counterterrorism contexts

Farahi reiterates the settled principle that courts afford a measure of deference to agencies like the FBI in FOIA Exemption 7 cases because they specialize in law enforcement (citing Campbell). That deference is heightened where national security or counterterrorism concerns are implicated; the opinion cites CNSS for the rule that courts consistently defer to the executive in FOIA cases involving national security. Deference does not mean no review: the agency must still make a record-level and harm-specific showing, but courts give weight to agency expertise and consider classified or ex parte submissions when necessary to avoid revealing the material that is assertedly damaging.

In practice in this case, the court accepted the FBI’s public declarations and its sealed ex parte submission in combination. The court emphasized that the FBI’s subject matter (terrorism-related allegations, links to designated organizations and convicted terrorists) made its representation of ongoing investigative activity plausible and entitled to deference.

## Segregability duty and the role of detailed affidavits

Even when an exemption applies, FOIA requires agencies to disclose any “reasonably segregable” nonexempt portions of records (5 U.S.C. § 552(b); see also § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) quoted in the opinion). The agency must therefore take “reasonable steps” to segregate and release nonexempt information.

Farahi illustrates how courts treat segregability claims: a court may rely on agency affidavits or declarations that explain, with reasonable specificity, why nonexempt material cannot be meaningfully segregated without harming the government’s interests. Courts have recognized that where nonexempt material is interspersed line‑by‑line among exempt, classified, or sensitive material so that isolating it would reveal the exempt material or produce only trivial “words on a page” rather than meaningful information, the nonexempt portions need not be released (Mead Data; Perioperative Services cited in the opinion). In Farahi the district court — after reviewing Bender’s ex parte submission — found that the FBI provided a detailed justification that nonexempt material was not reasonably segregable. The D.C. Circuit affirmed that approach, recognizing both the permissibility of relying on ex parte submissions in sensitive cases and the reasonableness of concluding that randomly interspersed nonexempt bits are not meaningfully segregable.

Two practical points follow from the opinion: (1) segregability is a factual showing; (2) where disclosure of segregated material itself would reveal exempt content or provide negligible informational value, an agency can justify non‑release with detailed, specific explanations in declarations — including sealed ones when necessary.

## Category‑by‑category withholdings standard

When an agency withholds records on a category‑by‑category basis, the categories must be sufficiently distinct to permit judicial review of the claimed exemptions (Gallant; CREW). This requirement means the court needs enough specificity to determine whether a particular exemption applies to each category. In Farahi, the court noted this standard but found that Farahi had forfeited any challenge to the FBI’s categories because he did not meaningfully explain why they were overbroad. Thus, challenges to the propriety of categorical descriptions must be preserved and supported with specifics, or they risk forfeiture.

## Standard of review: de novo review of summary judgment

Finally, the opinion reiterates the standard of appellate review: FOIA summary-judgment rulings are reviewed de novo. Although courts afford deference to agency expertise in appropriate respects (especially in national security and law‑enforcement contexts), appellate review remains plenary over whether the statutory exemption and segregability requirements have been met. The government retains the burden of persuasion, and the trial court’s grant of summary judgment for the agency must rest on an adequate administrative record and reasoned declarations that allow the court to conclude exemptions apply and that nonexempt material is not reasonably segregable.

Taken together, these standards explain the path the D.C. Circuit followed in Farahi: it assessed whether the FBI’s declarations — public and ex parte — established the law‑enforcement nexus and the existence of pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings, credited the agency’s expertise given the terrorism context, and accepted the district court’s segregability finding because the agency provided detailed, specific reasons (including sealed evidence) for treating the files as nonsegregable.

# Law-Enforcement Purpose: Satisfying Exemption 7’s Threshold

A threshold requirement for invoking FOIA Exemption 7 is that the records at issue were “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” The court’s treatment of that element in Farahi v. FBI illustrates both the legal standard and the kind of evidentiary showing that will satisfy it when the agency is the FBI and the subject matter implicates national-security investigations.

The legal test. We treat the “compiled for law enforcement purposes” inquiry as a factual one, but courts apply a legal standard shaped by precedents such as Pratt and Campbell. An agency must show a rational nexus between the investigation that produced the records and “one of the agency’s law enforcement duties.” Pratt v. Webster and Campbell v. DOJ make clear that this is not a hyper-technical test: the agency need not show a pending prosecution or a formal criminal charge, but it must connect the records to an identifiable law-enforcement function and to an individual or incident that poses a possible security risk or a potential violation of federal law. Put differently, the court asks whether it is reasonable to conclude that the records were created, maintained, or used in the course of the agency’s efforts to investigate, prevent, or prosecute unlawful activity—not whether they were generated for some wholly administrative or benign purpose.

Deference to the FBI. The opinion emphasizes that the FBI’s conclusion is entitled to a “fair measure of deference.” Because the FBI’s core mission is law enforcement—especially counterterrorism and national-security work—courts give weight to its characterization of records as law-enforcement materials. That deference does not replace the need for a factual showing, but it means courts will not require the same level of particularized proof they might demand from a non-law-enforcement agency.

What the Hardy declaration said publicly. In this case the FBI’s public (Hardy) declaration performed the initial work of tying the records to law-enforcement duties. The public declaration explained that the withheld records were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism,” and that those records related to pending proceedings and to the subject’s associations with individuals “directly involved in such violations.” This statement addressed both elements of the Pratt/Campbell test: it linked the records to the FBI’s law-enforcement responsibilities (investigating national-security and terrorism violations) and it linked the records to individuals and conduct that could pose security risks or involve federal crimes.

What the ex parte Hardy declaration supplied. The opinion highlights that the ex parte (sealed) Hardy declaration added significant, nonpublic detail about the investigation’s nature and scope. Courts often accept sealed, in camera submissions where national-security or law-enforcement sensitivities are at stake; such material can supply the factual specificity the public record cannot reveal without compromising ongoing work. Here, Hardy’s ex parte declaration elaborated on investigative facts—details the court could not publicly recite but that enabled it to assess whether a legitimate investigative nexus existed. The combination of the public declaration’s general attestations and the ex parte declaration’s specifics met the court’s need for a reasoned, evidentiary basis showing the records were law-enforcement materials.

Context supplied by the immigration proceedings. The opinion also treats the litigation background as corroborative context. In earlier immigration proceedings before other tribunals, the government had introduced evidence and testimony alleging that Farahi had connections to high-level terrorists and various organizations implicated in terrorism financing and support. The court cites specific allegations in the immigration record—ties to organizations designated as foreign terrorist organizations, connections to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other Al Qaeda affiliates, links to convicted or indicted terrorists such as Jose Padilla, and support for charities later tied to terrorism financing (e.g., the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation). Those prior allegations, which Farahi himself acknowledged in the district-court filings, reinforced the reasonableness of the FBI’s characterization of its file as law-enforcement material focused on national-security threats.

Why that combination sufficed. The opinion explains that the public Hardy declaration established the existence of an investigation tied to national-security law-enforcement duties, while the sealed Hardy declaration provided the necessary factual specificity about the investigation’s contours. The immigration-record allegations supplied independent context that made the agency’s assertions plausible. Under the Pratt/Campbell framework, and given the deference ordinarily afforded the FBI in national-security matters, this mix of public attestation, sealed elaboration, and corroborating context met the agency’s burden to show a rational nexus between the FBI’s law-enforcement responsibilities and the records compiled about Farahi.

Holding on the threshold. The court therefore held that the FBI satisfied Exemption 7’s threshold: the Farahi file was compiled for law-enforcement purposes. The decision illustrates a few practical lessons for reading FOIA opinions: (1) a public declaration that links records to an identifiable enforcement function is important; (2) sealed, ex parte declarations are often the vehicle for the granular factual detail courts need when national security or sensitive investigations are involved; and (3) corroborating factual context from parallel proceedings can strengthen the agency’s showing without substituting for the formal declaration requirement.

# Interference with Pending or Anticipated Proceedings under Exemption 7(A)

The court’s Exemption 7(A) analysis turns on two linked points: (1) whether disclosure of the requested records “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings,” and (2) whether those proceedings were “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The opinion applies a familiar, fact-specific test: the FBI must show a rational connection between its investigatory files and law‑enforcement duties and then demonstrate that disclosure would pose a concrete risk to ongoing or foreseeable proceedings. That showing can be made through agency declarations, including classified or ex parte submissions when necessary, but those submissions must be sufficient to establish that the proceedings are more than speculative.

Interference rationale: tipping off targets to scope and direction of investigation
A central, well-established justification for Exemption 7(A) invoked here is the risk that disclosure will “tip off” investigation targets about the scope, direction, or evidence-gathering techniques of an investigation. The Hardy declaration explicitly relied on that rationale: revealing information contained in Farahi’s FBI file would be likely to alert targets and thereby frustrate evidence collection, witness cooperation, or future prosecutorial strategy. The court treats this as a paradigmatic 7(A) harm—one that courts have repeatedly recognized in the context of criminal and national‑security investigations. In short, the asserted interference is not a nebulous claim of embarrassment or inconvenience but a concrete policing concern: premature disclosure would materially impair the government’s ability to pursue or perfect enforcement actions.

Evidence of pendency: Hardy’s public attestation and ex parte elaboration
To invoke 7(A) the government must show that enforcement proceedings are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The court relied first on the public Hardy declaration, which affirmatively stated that the withheld records were compiled in furtherance of investigations into national‑security and terrorism matters and that those investigations remained ongoing. That public attestation establishes the “existence” of an investigation—sufficient, under our precedents, to show a proceeding for 7(A) purposes when coupled with a showing that disclosure would risk interference. The government supplemented the public declaration with an ex parte (sealed) Hardy declaration that provided further factual detail about the nature, scope, and sensitivity of the investigation. Although the court did not disclose the sealed contents, it credited them as supplying the specificity necessary to explain why the FBI reasonably anticipated continued enforcement activity and how disclosure would interfere.

Bender’s declarations as updates confirming continued pendency as of March 2023
When the district court initially hesitated because of the elapsed time between the FBI’s first declaration and its decision, the government filed updated declarations from Joseph Bender. The public Bender declaration succinctly stated that the agent supervising Farahi’s file had confirmed that investigations remained ongoing; the Bender submission also included an ex parte elaboration. The court treated these materials not as freestanding proofs of pendency but as contemporaneous updates that confirmed nothing material had changed since the Hardy declarations. In that role, Bender’s statements performed the necessary evidentiary function: they bridged the temporal gap and supported the continued applicability of 7(A) as of March 2023.

Addressing CREW’s caution about vagueness: context matters
Farahi relied on CREW, which warns that conclusory or vague assertions that an investigation is “ongoing” may not suffice to sustain Exemption 7(A), particularly when a long time has elapsed. The court recognized that Bender’s public declaration, taken alone, was terse and could look conclusory. But CREW does not require that every update be a stand-alone mosaic of facts; rather, it requires that the overall record—public and ex parte declarations taken together—provide an adequate factual basis. Here the court emphasized context: the detailed ex parte Hardy declaration and the district court’s earlier finding that the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden together supply the specificity CREW demands. Thus, Bender’s brief confirmation functions as a permissible update to an already detailed factual record rather than an insufficient, isolated claim of continued investigation.

Role of national‑security deference and breadth of the counterterrorism investigation
The opinion also underscores institutional and contextual deference to the FBI’s judgment in national‑security and counterterrorism matters. The court repeatedly notes precedent that grants the FBI a “fair measure of deference” when it invokes law‑enforcement exemptions, and it treats such deference as particularly appropriate where disclosure would implicate counterterrorism work. That deference operates at two levels: first, in evaluating whether the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes; and second, in assessing whether disclosure would reasonably be expected to interfere with ongoing enforcement activity. The court also considered the breadth of the underlying investigation—public materials in related immigration proceedings and the sealed declarations describing ties to known terrorist actors—to conclude that ongoing investigatory work was plausible and that the government’s non‑speculative fear of interference was reasonable.

Conclusion: existence of a concrete prospective law‑enforcement proceeding established; Exemption 7(A) satisfied
Weighing the public and sealed evidence together, and giving appropriate deference in a national‑security context, the court concluded that the FBI met its burden under Exemption 7(A). The record showed a concrete, prospective law‑enforcement proceeding that was pending or reasonably anticipated; it showed a plausible, concrete risk that disclosure would tip off targets and impede evidence-gathering; and it supplied adequate explanation—including ex parte detail—for why nonexempt material could not be meaningfully segregated. For those reasons, the court upheld the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the FBI under Exemption 7(A).

# Segregability, Categorical Withholdings, and Use of Ex Parte Declarations

## Segregability standard and when full withholding is proper

Under FOIA an agency must disclose “any reasonably segregable portion of a record” after deleting exempt material. 5 U.S.C. § 552(b); see also § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii). That means courts will order disclosure of nonexempt information within a record unless the agency demonstrates that whatever nonexempt material exists is not “reasonably segregable.” The opinions the Farahi court relied upon frame how courts apply the segregability obligation in practice. Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force explained that when nonexempt material is “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material, or when nonexempt words are scattered line-by-line throughout a document, meaningful redaction may be impracticable and full withholding is permissible. Juarez v. DOJ reiterates that a court may rely on agency affidavits that explain with reasonable specificity why documents cannot be further segregated. And Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. VA emphasizes that the FOIA’s purpose is to disclose information, not merely isolated words: disclosure of a few stray words does not necessarily satisfy FOIA if those words convey no meaningful information once separated from the exempt material.

In short, the agency bears the burden of showing that any nonexempt portions are not reasonably producible in a way that would preserve the exempt material. Courts assess that showing contextually, asking whether segregation would produce useful, releasable information without revealing what the exemption protects.

## The district court’s reliance on Bender’s supplemental ex parte declaration

In Farahi the district court initially required updated factual support because significant time had passed since the FBI’s first declarations. After the FBI produced updated public and ex parte declarations from Bender (and previously from Hardy), the district court concluded on the basis of those submissions that the agency had adequately explained why protected material was not reasonably segregable. The D.C. Circuit affirmed that conclusion.

Two features of the court’s treatment are important to understand. First, the district court did not rely on the terse public Bender statement in isolation; it treated Bender’s submission as a supplement to the earlier, more detailed Hardy declarations and to the ex parte materials that gave greater factual particularity about the investigation and the records. Second, the court found — based on the detailed, although partially sealed, explanation in the ex parte submission — that the nonexempt material was “interspersed line-by-line” or otherwise so entwined with exempt content that disclosure of the nonexempt portions would either reveal protected information or produce no meaningful additional information. That is precisely the type of reasonable-specificity showing Mead and Juarez contemplate.

## Rejecting Farahi’s volume-based argument: information, not words

Farahi argued that the sheer volume of responsive records (over 10,700 pages and 80 CDs) meant there must be segregable, releasable information. The court rejected that contention. The governing principle is that FOIA’s goal is disclosure of meaningful information, not the disclosure of isolated words or phrases that lack context. If nonexempt fragments are scattered throughout documents in a way that extracting them would either reveal the exempt material or yield nothing informative, they are not “reasonably segregable.” The court relied on Mead’s teaching that randomly interspersed nonexempt material can render segregation impracticable, and on Perioperative Services’ reaffirmation that quantity alone does not establish meaningful segregability. Thus volume, without a showing that segregable excerpts would convey useful, nonexempt information, is insufficient to overcome the agency’s showing.

## Categorical withholdings and Farahi’s forfeiture

Agencies often justify withholdings on a category-by-category basis and must use categories that are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied.” Gallant v. NLRB (as applied in later cases such as CREW) requires that categorical descriptions be precise enough to permit judicial review of the exemption’s applicability to each category. In Farahi the FBI organized records into categories and explained why each category was exempt.

Farahi objected that some categories were overbroad, but the Court of Appeals treated that contention as forfeited because Farahi did not provide specific explanations demonstrating how or why the categories were overbroad. The court cited Khine v. DHS to make clear that a bare or generalized objection is insufficient; a challenger must identify with specificity the categories and the particular defects in the agency’s categorizations so the court can assess whether the classification masks improper withholding. Because Farahi failed to make that showing, the appellate court declined to entertain his categorical-withholding challenge.

## Propriety of ex parte submissions in FOIA national-security contexts

The Farahi opinion underscores that ex parte, in-camera submissions may be appropriate and sometimes necessary in FOIA cases that involve national-security or ongoing law-enforcement investigations. Ex parte declarations allow the government to provide factual detail that would itself be sensitive to place on the public record — for example, descriptions of investigative techniques, targets, or the scope of an active probe — without disclosing the very information that supports withholding. The court recognized two limiting features here. First, Farahi did not oppose the district court’s use of ex parte declarations below, so he cannot now complain about reliance on them. Second, the circuit recognized the unfortunate tension these procedures pose for FOIA plaintiffs but treated that tension as inherent where protecting the secrecy of the investigation is itself the object of the exemption. Precedent (including Perioperative Services and earlier FOIA cases dealing with national security) permits courts to consider sealed submissions when doing so is necessary to evaluate whether an exemption legitimately applies and whether segregability analysis is satisfied.

That does not mean ex parte submissions are unreviewable; courts still assess them for adequacy and reasonableness. But where plaintiffs have not objected to use of sealed materials and where disclosure of the justification would defeat the privacy or national-security interests at issue, courts may appropriately rely on those sealed explanations.

## Disposition — affirmance of summary judgment for the FBI

Applying these principles, the D.C. Circuit affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment for the FBI. The appellate court concluded the agency had carried its burden to show that the records were law‑enforcement materials subject to Exemption 7(A), that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings, and that no meaningful, reasonably segregable information could be disclosed without compromising exempt material. The court also declined to entertain Farahi’s categorical-withholding challenge because he failed to raise specific, preserved arguments to show overbreadth. The upshot for practitioners is clear: in national-security FOIA litigation, carefully detailed agency affidavits (including, when necessary, sealed ex parte submissions) that explain both why an exemption applies and why redaction would be ineffective are critical; generalized volume-based arguments or unsupported attacks on categorical descriptions are unlikely to prevail.
