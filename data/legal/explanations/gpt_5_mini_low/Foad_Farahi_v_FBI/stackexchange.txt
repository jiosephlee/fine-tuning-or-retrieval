Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What qualifies as an "enforcement proceeding" under FOIA Exemption 7(A), and do long-running counterterrorism investigations count?
Question:
The panel says ongoing criminal investigations count as "proceedings" and that the government can meet its burden by attesting to their existence (citing CREW and Juarez). Here, the FBI relied on both public and ex parte declarations to say the investigation into Farahi is ongoing. I'm confused about the line between a general counterterrorism investigation that could go on for years and a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" that CREW seems to require. What makes an investigation sufficiently concrete to qualify as a pending or reasonably anticipated proceeding for 7(A) purposes, especially when no indictment has issued after many years?
Answer:
Short answer
- Under the D.C. Circuit’s controlling framework (as applied in Farahi), an “enforcement proceeding” for FOIA Exemption 7(A) includes ongoing criminal investigations and also “concrete prospective law enforcement” proceedings that are reasonably anticipated. (CREW; Juarez.)
- What makes a long-running counterterrorism investigation sufficiently “concrete” is not merely passage of time, but (1) a showing that the investigation is still active and continuing to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and (2) a factual nexus tying the investigation to particular individuals/incidents or possible federal violations such that premature disclosure would jeopardize that future enforcement. (Juarez; Campbell; CREW; CNSS.)
- The government may meet its burden by attesting to the existence of such proceedings and by supplying more detailed (including ex parte) declarations when necessary to explain the nature, scope, and ongoing activity of the investigation. (CREW; Farahi opinion.)

What the opinion says about the legal standard
- Exemption 7(A) covers records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes if their disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096 (quoted in Farahi).
- The court treats ongoing criminal investigations as “proceedings” and relies on Juarez for the rule that Exemption 7(A) applies “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59 (quoted in Farahi).
- The government may satisfy its burden by attesting to the existence of the investigation; where more detail is required, the agency’s public declaration plus an ex parte sealed submission can supply the needed specifics without disclosing the very information FOIA would otherwise force it to reveal. (Farahi, discussing CREW and reliance on public and ex parte Hardy/Bender declarations.)

How that applies to long-running counterterrorism investigations
- Time alone does not defeat Exemption 7(A). The court in Farahi acknowledged the plaintiff’s point that a bare, vague assertion that an investigation “remains ongoing” might be insufficient (drawing on CREW), but it upheld withholding because:
  - the FBI’s public declaration attested the investigation’s existence and described its national‑security/terrorism focus;
  - the sealed/ex parte declaration supplied substantial detail about the investigation’s nature and scope; and
  - the updated (public) attestation confirmed nothing material had changed while the case proceeded. (Farahi pp. IV.B.)
- The panel emphasized deference to the FBI—especially for national‑security/counterterrorism matters—and relied on publicly known connections (in the record) between the subject and terrorist actors/organizations to support the conclusion that a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” continued to exist. (Farahi citing Campbell and CNSS; factual context from Farahi’s immigration record.)

Practical markers a court will look for
If you are evaluating whether a long-running counterterrorism investigation counts as a pending or reasonably anticipated “proceeding” under 7(A), the opinion shows courts will look for:
1. Active evidence‑gathering: statements or declarations showing the investigation remains active and is still collecting evidence that could support prosecution (Juarez language quoted in Farahi).
2. A nexus to specific persons/incidents/possible federal violations: a rational connection between the file and one of the agency’s law‑enforcement duties or a particular security risk (Campbell/Pratt cited in Farahi).
3. More than a conclusory assertion: a bare, unexplained “still ongoing” claim can be thin (CREW), but can be cured by contextual public facts and/or a sealed ex parte declaration that supplies particulars without revealing sensitive material (Farahi).
4. National‑security deference: courts give the FBI a fair measure of deference in counterterrorism contexts, which affects how much detail the government must publicly disclose (Farahi, CNSS, Campbell).
5. Segregability analysis: even if 7(A) applies, the agency must explain why nonexempt material is not reasonably segregable; ex parte submissions can justify that showing where disclosure on the public record would defeat the protective purpose. (Farahi pp. V.)

Bottom line for your hypothetical
A long‑running counterterrorism probe can qualify as a “pending or reasonably anticipated” enforcement proceeding so long as the government can credibly show the investigation remains active (i.e., still gathering evidence for a possible case) and is tied to concrete enforcement risks such that disclosure would jeopardize future proceedings. Mere longevity is not dispositive; courts will require enough factual specificity—sometimes supplied ex parte and with due deference to national‑security concerns—to conclude the investigation is still concrete rather than a vague, indefinite inquiry. Farahi is an illustration of that approach: the combination of public attestations, sealed elaboration, and demonstrable ties between the subject and terrorism‑related activity satisfied Exemption 7(A) despite the investigation’s duration.

### How much deference does the court give the FBI’s assertions in national security FOIA cases, and what evidentiary showing is still required?
Question:
The opinion emphasizes deference to the FBI, especially where national security is implicated (CNSS; Campbell). But it also says the agency must show a rational nexus to law-enforcement duties and a link to a possible security risk or violation (Pratt/Campbell), and must attest to the existence of ongoing proceedings (CREW). In practice, what is the threshold detail required in public filings before the court will accept ex parte material to fill in the gaps? Is a largely conclusory public assertion that "investigations remain ongoing" acceptable if supplemented ex parte, or does CREW require more public specificity?
Answer:
Short answer
- The court gives the FBI substantial deference—especially in national‑security matters—but that does not eliminate the agency’s burden to show Exemption 7(A) applies.
- Public filings must at least attest to the existence of pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings and show a rational nexus to law‑enforcement duties and a possible security risk. CREW warned that “vague” public assertions may be insufficient.
- A largely conclusory public statement that “investigations remain ongoing” can be acceptable only when it is supported by (1) earlier public declarations or sworn material that establish the basic predicate (nature/scope/connection to law enforcement) and (2) ex parte sealed submissions that supply the factual detail the public filing omits. The court in Farahi accepted a terse public update because it was grounded in earlier public and ex parte Hardy declarations and because the district court had already found the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden. Farahi, slip op. §§ III–IV (discussing CREW, Hardy, and Bender).

Explanation (doctrine + how Farahi applies it)
- Deference: The D.C. Circuit reiterates that because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency and the records implicate national security, the agency’s invocation of Exemption 7(A) is “entitled to deference.” The opinion cites Campbell and CNSS for that principle. Farahi, slip op. § III; see Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32; Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud., 331 F.3d at 927–28.

- Government’s burden: Deference does not replace the statutory/precedential showing. To carry its burden under Exemption 7(A) the agency must show (a) the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes (a “rational nexus” to the agency’s law‑enforcement duties and to a possible security risk — Pratt/Campbell), and (b) disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated” (CREW/Juarez). Farahi, slip op. § IV.A–B.

- CREW and public specificity: CREW holds (and Farahi reiterates) that the government may establish ongoing investigations by attesting to their “existence,” but CREW also warned that a “vague” assertion that investigations are ongoing may not suffice. Farahi acknowledges that CREW’s caveat is real: the court said the Bender (March 2023) declaration “appears surprisingly terse” when viewed alone and that CREW had criticized similarly vague assertions. Farahi, slip op. § IV.B (citing CREW).

- When ex parte supplements will be accepted: Farahi makes clear the practical rule the court applied: a conclusory public update can be acceptable where it is not the government’s sole showing. The court approved the FBI’s approach because the terse public update (Bender) was an update to, and corroboration of, earlier public and ex parte Hardy declarations that (publicly) attested to pendency and (under seal) provided “considerable detail” about the investigation. The district court had also earlier found the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden, so the update was treated as confirmation rather than an independent proof. Farahi, slip op. § IV.B.

- Segregability and ex parte material: For segregability the agency must show “with reasonable specificity” why non‑exempt parts cannot be segregated. Farahi permits the district court to rely on ex parte submissions for that analysis (and accepts that ex parte affidavits are sometimes the only way to decide without exposing the very material at issue). Farahi, slip op. § V (citing Juarez, Mead Data, Perioperative).

Practical takeaway for litigants / drafts
- Best practice for the public declaration: at minimum, publicly state (1) an attestation that an investigation or proceedings are pending or reasonably anticipated, (2) the basic nature or subject matter of the investigation (e.g., counterterrorism, alleged ties to listed organizations), and (3) the rational nexus to the FBI’s law‑enforcement duties and potential harm from disclosure. That public showing reduces the risk a court will treat later public statements as merely “vague.” Farahi, slip op. § IV (relying on public Hardy material and the publicly known allegations in Farahi’s immigration proceedings).
- Use ex parte filings to supply the factual detail courts cannot publish (specific investigative facts, why disclosure would jeopardize evidence or sources, and why allegedly nonexempt bits are not reasonably segregable). But do not rely on ex parte material alone: the public record should provide the basic predicate that an investigation exists and the general grounds for withholding. Farahi, slip op. §§ IV.B, V.

Bottom line
Deference is strong but not absolute. CREW’s command that the government at least attest to the existence of ongoing proceedings remains binding; the court will accept a terse public assertion only when it is reasonably anchored by other public statements and by sealed ex parte material that fills the factual gaps. Farahi affirms that combination as sufficient in the national‑security context, but also reiterates CREW’s caution that a naked, vague public assertion standing alone may fail.

### Did the court miscite FOIA by referring to § 552(b)(7)(B) instead of (b)(7)(A), and if so, does that matter?
Question:
In Section IV.B, the panel quotes the interference standard and cites 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B), but Exemption 7(B) is about depriving a person of a fair trial, while 7(A) is the interference-with-proceedings exemption at issue here. Is this just a typographical error without substantive effect, or could it create ambiguity about the standard the court applied?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — the opinion contains a miscitation in IV.B (it cites § 552(b)(7)(B) where it plainly means Exemption 7(A) ) — but it is a typographical error without substantive effect. The panel repeatedly identifies, quotes, and applies Exemption 7(A) and the correct “pending or reasonably anticipated” standard, so there is no real ambiguity about the legal standard the court used.

Why that is clear from the opinion
- The opinion’s holding and framework consistently invoke Exemption 7(A). The opening summary states the records were protected by “FOIA’s Exemption 7(A).” Section I frames the relevant exemption as § 552(b)(7)(A) and quotes the interference language from that subsection. Section III and IV.A likewise discuss § 552(b)(7)(A) and the requirement that records be “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” Those are the operative provisions.
- The disputed passage in IV.B uses the correct substantive standard — that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated” — and it cites CREW and Juarez, the court’s precedents applying Exemption 7(A). The opinion explains that ongoing investigations qualify and discusses how the FBI’s declarations establish pendency/anticipation. Those doctrinal points track Exemption 7(A), not 7(B).
- The apparent miscitation is isolated. Elsewhere the opinion (including the statutory quotations and the burdens the government must meet) points to § 552(b)(7)(A). The court’s analysis, factual findings, and ultimate disposition all rest on Exemption 7(A).

Legal effect
- A single incorrect parenthetical statutory citation that conflicts with the text and repeated holdings in the same opinion is a clerical error that does not change the legal standard applied. The opinion demonstrates what the court intended: to apply Exemption 7(A)’s interference-with-proceedings rule. Thus the miscitation does not create a plausible ambiguity about the governing law or the result of the case.

What a litigant who is concerned could do
- If a party wanted to eliminate even this minor risk of confusion in later citation, they could move for panel rehearing or rehearing en banc to request correction/clarification. But given the opinion’s consistent text and holdings applying 7(A), such a motion would be unlikely to change the outcome; it would be principally housekeeping.

Bottom line: the reference to § 552(b)(7)(B) in IV.B is a typographical/citation error, and the opinion itself makes clear the court applied § 552(b)(7)(A)’s interference standard.

### When can a district court require "refresh" declarations to address staleness, and what makes an initial 7(A) showing become stale?
Question:
The district court initially denied summary judgment without prejudice and required updated declarations due to the passage of time, then granted summary judgment after receiving the Bender updates. Is there a doctrinal standard for when a 7(A) declaration becomes too stale to support summary judgment? How much time or what kinds of changed circumstances trigger a duty to supplement, and is this within the district court’s discretion?
Answer:
Short answer
- A district court may require updated or “refresh” declarations when the passage of time or changed circumstances cast doubt on an agency’s earlier showing that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” under Exemption 7(A).  
- The doctrinal touchstone is whether the agency has shown that enforcement proceedings are “pending or reasonably anticipated”; because that fact is time‑sensitive, an otherwise adequate declaration can become stale if enough time has passed or if events could have altered whether proceedings remain pending or foreseeable.  
- Deciding when to demand an update is a matter for the district court’s discretion, informed by the court’s responsibility to ensure the government still meets its burden and by the particular context (nature of the investigation, prior evidentiary detail, and any intervening developments).

Why — the governing law and how it was applied in Farahi
- Exemption 7(A) requires the agency to show that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096 (quoted in the opinion). That showing is inherently temporal: it depends on whether an investigation or prospective proceeding still exists and is likely to be harmed by disclosure.  
- Courts in this circuit accept agency attestations of the “existence” or pendency of investigations, and give the FBI some deference (Campbell; CNSS), but the court still must be satisfied that the record supports the claim. Where the agency’s initial declarations are years old, a court may reasonably doubt that the asserted pendency still obtains without some confirmation. That is what happened here: the district court denied the FBI’s first summary‑judgment motion without prejudice because “given the significant passage of time” it needed an updated assessment of whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated, and more information on segregability (J.A. 121–22). The court acted to ensure the government still carried its burden before entering judgment.  
- The court of appeals approved that approach. It explained that Bender’s later declaration was not required to establish pendency from scratch; rather it functioned as an update confirming that “nothing had changed” since the earlier Hardy declarations. The panel treated the initial Hardy submissions (including an ex parte Hardy declaration) as providing the substantive basis for the 7(A) showing, and Bender’s update as a timely confirmation that the investigation remained ongoing (J.A. 131–39). The panel therefore sustained the district court’s demand for updated material and its reliance on the update.

What makes a 7(A) showing “stale”
- Passage of time alone can make a prior declaration stale if the claimed basis for withholding (an ongoing or anticipated enforcement proceeding) could plausibly have ended or materially changed in the interim. The Farahi court explicitly recognized that “the passage of time casts doubt on whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated,” so an update may be needed (opinion ¶ IV.B).  
- Changed circumstances that would undermine the factual predicate of the initial declaration (e.g., an investigation closed, charges filed and resolved, targets arrested or deceased, or public events that reveal or negate the agency’s prior factual assertions) likewise render an earlier attestation inadequate.  
- Conversely, if the initial declarations provided detailed factual support (including ex parte elaboration where appropriate) and the investigation’s scope reasonably explains ongoing activity (for example, a broad national‑security probe), a court may conclude that the showing is still adequate without a fresh affidavit. The Farahi court stressed context: Bender was a brief update because the detailed Hardy declarations (including sealed details) had already established the nature and pendency of the investigation.

Practical implications and district‑court discretion
- The district court has discretion to require supplementation. That discretion flows from its duty to ensure the government meets its statutory burden and to adjudicate segregability, balanced against recognized deference to agency affidavits—especially in sensitive law‑enforcement or national‑security contexts. The Farahi district court used that discretion to deny without prejudice, request updates, and consider ex parte material; the D.C. Circuit affirmed.  
- There is no bright‑line rule in the opinion (no fixed number of months/years). The analysis is fact‑specific: (1) how old are the declarations; (2) how detailed were the initial public and, if provided, sealed declarations; (3) how broad or enduring is the underlying investigation; and (4) whether intervening facts suggest the investigation may have changed. A court may demand an update when these factors together make the prior showing doubtful.  
- If the court requires an update, the update need only do what is necessary to dispel the doubt: confirm that investigations remain ongoing or explain why earlier factual predicates still obtain. That is what Bender’s declaration accomplished in Farahi: a short public confirmation supported by a sealed ex parte elaboration and grounded in the earlier Hardy material.

Bottom line
Farahi illustrates the governing principle: Exemption 7(A) predicates are time‑sensitive, so district courts may, in their discretion, require refreshed declarations when the passage of time or changed circumstances could undermine an earlier showing that proceedings are pending or reasonably anticipated. Whether a particular declaration is “stale” depends on the case‑specific context and the sufficiency of the original factual detail; there is no fixed temporal cutoff announced in the opinion.

### What are the rules for category-by-category withholdings and Vaughn submissions under 7(A), and how did Farahi forfeit that challenge?
Question:
The opinion briefly mentions that when an agency withholds on a category basis, the categories must be sufficiently distinct to assess the exemption (CREW; Gallant). The court says Farahi forfeited any overbreadth argument by not specifically explaining why the FBI’s categories were improper. What level of specificity must a requester provide to preserve a challenge to categorical withholdings or the adequacy of a Vaughn index in a 7(A) case, and what kinds of categorical descriptions have courts found too vague?
Answer:
Short answer
- Under this Circuit’s cases (CREW and Gallant, cited in the opinion), an agency that withholds records category-by-category must define those categories with enough particularity that a court can tell whether the claimed exemption actually fits each category. 
- To preserve a challenge, a requester must point to particular categories and explain, with specificity, how those category descriptions are improper or overbroad—i.e., why the labels are too vague to permit judicial review of the exemption claim. A general or global objection is insufficient and will be treated as forfeited (as the court held here under Khine).

What the opinion says about the legal standard
- The court repeats the CREW/Gallant rule: categories must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” (opinion §IV.C (citing CREW and Gallant). That is the governing test for category-by-category withholding in an Exemption 7(A) case.  
- If a requester wants to attack the categorical withholdings (or the adequacy of a Vaughn-style showing), the requester must “specifically explain” why particular categories are overbroad or otherwise improper. The court rejected Farahi’s challenge because he “provides no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad” and therefore “forfeited his arguments on this score” (opinion §IV.C (citing Khine)).

What level of specificity is required to preserve the challenge
- The requester must identify particular category labels and articulate concrete defects in them — for example, that a category is so broadly worded that a court cannot tell which kinds of information it covers, or that the label sweeps in material that has no nexus to the asserted exemption. In short: point to the category, explain why its wording prevents meaningful judicial review of the exemption, and (if possible) show how the agency’s description fails to tie the documents to the statutory criteria (here, law‑enforcement nexus and a reasonable expectation of interference).  
- Mere assertions that the categories are “overbroad,” “vague,” or that there must be some releasable material in a large set of records—without tying those complaints to specific category labels and showing how they preclude judicial assessment—won’t preserve the claim.

What kinds of categorical descriptions courts have treated as too vague (as reflected in the opinion)
- The opinion does not catalogue specific offending labels, but its citations and ruling make clear the point: labels that are conclusory or so broad that a court cannot determine what types of documents are in the category defeat meaningful review. The court also treated a bare, terse update (the Bender declaration saying things “remain ongoing”) as thin when considered alone, but it did not treat that abbreviated wording as fatal because the government had already provided fuller, non‑conclusory declarations elsewhere.  
- Practically, then, descriptions that are merely conclusory (“investigatory files,” “all documents relating to subject X,” or similarly blanket labels) and are not explained with facts that link the category to the exemption are the sort of vague, review‑preventing categories the case law condemns. The opinion’s holding is that challenge to such categories must be made specifically or is forfeited.

How Farahi forfeited
- Farahi made a generalized objection to the FBI’s category‑by‑category withholdings, but he did not “specifically explain” why any particular category was improper or overbroad. The court therefore applied Khine and held the argument forfeited: because Farahi failed to identify and explain defects in the FBI’s category descriptions, the court would not entertain his overbreadth challenge (opinion §IV.C).

Practical takeaway for a requester
- When litigating a categorical withholding or a Vaughn description, don’t rely on global objections. Point to each contested category, quote the category label or description, and explain concretely why that label prevents the court from evaluating the exemption (e.g., no nexus shown, temporally unlimited, sweeps in unrelated material, or is purely conclusory). If you do not, the court may treat your challenge as forfeited.

### How does the segregability requirement work when non-exempt bits are interspersed throughout highly sensitive records?
Question:
The court upholds withholding in full because any non-exempt material is interspersed line-by-line and not "reasonably segregable" (Mead; Perioperative Services). What is the governing test for when line-by-line redactions are deemed unreasonable, and how do courts evaluate an agency's ex parte explanation that further segregation would be meaningless? Are agencies required to release context-free names/dates or boilerplate headings if those could be extracted without harm, or is the "meaningless snippet" concept dispositive?
Answer:
Short answer
- When non‑exempt bits are scattered “line‑by‑line” through highly sensitive material, the governing test is whether any extractable material would produce meaningful, non‑exempt information—if not, further segregation is “not reasonably segregable.” See Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1069; Foad Farahi v. FBI, slip op. at § V.
- A court may accept an agency’s ex parte explanation for non‑segregability so long as the agency’s affidavits (including sealed/ex parte submissions) explain with reasonable specificity why further redaction or extraction would harm the exempt interest or produce only meaningless snippets. Juarez v. DOJ; Foad Farahi §§ III, V.
- Agencies are not mechanically required to release isolated names, dates, or boilerplate headings if those items would be “meaningless” or if their extraction would itself reveal sensitive context or is impossible without disclosing exempt material. The FOIA duty is to release meaningful information, not isolated words. Mead, quoted in Foad Farahi § V.

What the court in Farahi actually held (doctrine and application)
1) Statutory baseline: segregability
- FOIA requires agencies to take “reasonable steps” to segregate and release non‑exempt information; any “reasonably segregable portion” must be provided after deleting exempt portions. 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); § 552(b). (Foad Farahi § V.)

2) Governing test for line‑by‑line interspersion
- The controlling line is Mead Data: if non‑exempt material is “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material (for example, non‑exempt words scattered line‑by‑line), then the agency may withhold the document in full because the scattered pieces do not amount to “meaningful information.” Foad Farahi § V (citing Mead, 566 F.2d at 261).
- Perioperative reinforced that when tiny non‑exempt portions are randomly interspersed, segregability can be unreasonable: a court evaluates whether the isolated disclosures would produce meaningful information or merely “snippet[s]” that convey nothing useful. Foad Farahi § V (citing Perioperative Servs.).

3) What the agency must show
- The agency retains the burden to show the exemption applies and to justify non‑segregability, but courts may rely on agency declarations that explain with “reasonable specificity” why documents cannot be further segregated. Juarez v. DOJ; Foad Farahi §§ III, IV.A.
- Ex parte (sealed) declarations are permissible where revealing the basis for segregation analysis on the public record would itself disclose sensitive information. The district court may review those sealed submissions and rely on them in finding non‑segregability; Farahi affirms that practice and explains it does not violate FOIA procedure where the plaintiff did not object to ex parte filings. Foad Farahi § V.

4) How courts evaluate ex parte explanations
- Courts look to the level of detail: a conclusory assertion may be insufficient standing alone (see CREW), but when the public and sealed declarations together give adequate detail about the investigation and the reasons why extraction would harm enforcement, the court can credit the agency. Farahi § IV.B (discussing CREW and the interplay of public and ex parte declarations).
- The court asks whether the segregable bits would convey any substantive information independent of the exempt material. If the only possible disclosures are context‑free words, headings, or names that, by themselves, are meaningless or would reveal sensitive context when unmoored, a court can accept the agency’s position that further segregation is futile. Foad Farahi § V (quoting Mead: “the focus of FOIA is information, … not simply words”).

5) Names/dates/boilerplate — are they always disclosable?
- No. Farahi rejects a rule that agencies must release isolated words or boilerplate wherever they can be extracted. The opinion explains that even if “individual words or phrases” could be disclosed without apparent harm, that fact “is neither here nor there” if the words do not amount to meaningful information or their release would risk exposing the exempt material. Foad Farahi § V.
- Thus, the “meaningless snippet” concept can be dispositive: if what remains after redaction would be non‑informative or would reveal sensitive context when pieced together, the agency need not release it. But that conclusion must be supported by adequate (and, where appropriate, sealed) explanation. Foad Farahi §§ V, IV.B.

Practical takeaways for litigation and FOIA practice
- Agencies should provide detailed declarations explaining why line‑by‑line segregation would either (a) produce only meaningless snippets, or (b) itself disclose exempt material or reveal the scope/methods of an investigation. Those explanations can be made ex parte if public disclosure would defeat the protective purpose. Foad Farahi §§ IV–V.
- Requesters should press the agency to identify distinguishable categories of documents and to explain whether any categories contain genuinely extractable substantive information (CREW/Gallant point); otherwise, courts may treat broad category arguments as forfeited. Foad Farahi § IV.C.
- If you can point to specific examples where isolated items (e.g., a name or date) would be meaningful standing alone or could be released without revealing exempt context, that helps rebut a non‑segregability claim; absent that showing, Mead/Perioperative and Farahi will favor the agency when the interspersion is pervasive.

Conclusion
Farahi follows Mead and Perioperative: pervasive, line‑by‑line interspersion permits full withholding when the remaining words would be meaningless or would reveal exempt information, and courts may rely on reasonably specific agency affidavits — including sealed, ex parte submissions — to reach that conclusion. Agencies are not compelled to release context‑free names/dates or boilerplate when those extractions would produce no substantive, non‑exempt information or would itself reveal sensitive context; but the agency must justify that position with sufficient specificity.

### To what extent may courts rely on ex parte, in camera declarations in FOIA cases, and does the requester’s failure to object waive later challenges?
Question:
The district court accepted ex parte declarations; Farahi didn’t object, and on appeal the panel says he can’t challenge reliance on them. What is the doctrinal basis for using ex parte submissions to decide FOIA cases (especially segregability and 7(A) issues), and how does a requester preserve objections without forcing disclosure of the very material at issue? Are there best practices or limits (e.g., requiring maximum feasible public summaries) that courts enforce before turning to ex parte review?
Answer:
Short answer
- Courts may rely on ex parte, in camera declarations in FOIA suits when the agency’s sealed submissions are the only practicable way to explain why an exemption applies (including why disclosure would interfere with enforcement proceedings under Exemption 7(A) and why non‑exempt material is not reasonably segregable). The D.C. Circuit treats such declarations as a permissible tool so long as the government’s affidavits provide “reasonable specificity” and the court can assess the exemption claim (see Juarez, Mead, CREW, CNSS, Perioperative).  
- But a FOIA requester who affirmatively fails to object to the district court’s receipt of sealed ex parte submissions generally cannot later complain on appeal about the court’s reliance on those submissions — the Farahi panel held that Farahi “did not oppose the district court’s acceptance of ex parte declarations, so he cannot challenge the district court’s reliance on them.”  
- To preserve objections without forcing disclosure, a requester should timely oppose the agency’s motion to file ex parte submissions (or move to limit their use), press for updated public declarations, ask the court to state on the public record as much of its reasoning as possible, and insist on the narrowest feasible sealed submission (and segregability detail) — the opinion endorses these procedural safeguards as alternatives to wholesale public disclosure.

Explanation and doctrinal basis (grounded in the opinion)
1) Why ex parte, in camera declarations are permitted
- The opinion recognizes a settled FOIA practice: courts may rely on agency affidavits (including sealed ex parte declarations) that “show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated.” Juarez v. DOJ; Mead Data. The government bears the burden of proving the exemption, but where national‑security or law‑enforcement interests are implicated, courts give the FBI a “fair measure of deference” (Campbell; CNSS).  
- In some cases the only way to explain why disclosure would harm an ongoing investigation or why particular material is not segregable is to describe sensitive facts that cannot be made public without defeating the very protection the exemption is designed to provide. The court acknowledged that “ex parte declarations no doubt handicap FOIA plaintiffs,” but also accepted that the handicap “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” (Perioperative Servs. quotation in the opinion.)

2) Application to Exemption 7(A) and segregability
- Exemption 7(A) requires that records be “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated” (statute, CREW, Juarez). The government may prove the pendency or anticipation of proceedings by attesting to their existence; detailed sealed statements can supply context that public filings cannot. In Farahi the court relied on a combination of public declarations and sealed ex parte declarations to conclude the FBI met its burden.  
- On segregability, the statute requires release of “any reasonably segregable portion.” The court may credit sealed explanations demonstrating that non‑exempt material is “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material or that releasable bits are not “reasonably segregable” (Mead, Juarez). The Farahi court accepted the district court’s conclusion — based on ex parte material — that meaningful, reasonably segregable information did not exist in the voluminous, interspersed record.

3) Waiver / preservation
- The panel’s holding on waiver is straightforward: because Farahi “did not oppose the district court’s acceptance of ex parte declarations, so he cannot challenge the district court’s reliance on them.” In other words, a failure to object in the district court forfeited the right to challenge the court’s use of those sealed submissions on appeal. The opinion treats preservation as a matter of ordinary appellate forfeiture doctrines: contest the district court’s procedure at the time it is taking place, or you generally cannot raise that complaint later.

Practical steps a requester can take (consistent with the opinion)
- Object promptly in the district court to the agency’s motion to file ex parte, sealed declarations and preserve the objection on the record. That is the primary way to avoid forfeiture under Farahi.  
- If the court is inclined to permit sealed submissions, ask the court to: (a) require the agency to submit the narrowest possible sealed declaration, (b) require an updated public declaration that provides as much non‑sensitive information as possible, and (c) put on the public record as much of the court’s reasoning as can be disclosed without harming the government interest. The Farahi opinion itself credits the district court’s approach of requiring updated declarations and distinguishing between the public and sealed submissions.  
- Press the court to require specificity in the sealed submission analogous to what the public declaration must contain: explain the existence/ongoing nature of investigations, the specific risks of disclosure, and why particular categories of material cannot be segregated. The opinion cites the need for “reasonable specificity” and that category descriptions must be “sufficiently distinct” (CREW, Gallant) to allow judicial review.  
- If appropriate, move for in camera review by the court rather than full disclosure to the requester, and preserve objection to any ruling that relies exclusively on untimely or overly broad sealed submissions.

Limits and best practices the opinion recognizes
- Courts will not accept conclusory sealed statements in isolation; sealed material must be considered alongside public submissions and other context. The opinion rejected treating a terse update as sufficient in isolation but accepted it as an update when paired with earlier public and sealed declarations (Farahi discussion of Bender vs. Hardy).  
- The agency still carries the statutory duty to identify and release “any reasonably segregable portion.” Courts may credit sealed explanations for why segregation is not feasible, but they expect specificity (Juarez, Mead).  
- The opinion implicitly endorses a sliding, pragmatic approach: require updated public information where feasible; allow sealed detail where necessary to avoid defeating the exemption; but expect courts to review and weigh all of that material to ensure the exemption is properly applied.

Bottom line
- Ex parte, sealed declarations are a recognized and sometimes necessary method for an agency to meet its burden in sensitive FOIA cases (Exemption 7(A), segregability), and courts in this Circuit will rely on them when they provide the needed “reasonable specificity” and the public record is supplemented as feasible.  
- But a requester must object timely in district court if she wants to preserve arguments about the propriety or weight of sealed ex parte submissions; Farahi holds that failure to object forecloses an appellate challenge to the court’s reliance on those submissions.

### Can adverse information used in immigration proceedings affect the 7(A) analysis in a separate FOIA case?
Question:
The panel references allegations and evidence from Farahi’s immigration proceedings (e.g., ties to designated terrorist organizations and specific individuals) as contextual support for the FBI’s ongoing-investigation claim. Is it proper for a FOIA court to rely on allegations from related civil or immigration matters to buttress the existence and breadth of a law-enforcement investigation under 7(A), or should the analysis be confined to the agency’s FOIA declarations and Vaughn materials?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — a FOIA court may consider publicly available allegations from related civil or immigration proceedings as background corroboration for an agency’s claim that an investigation is ongoing and broad, but those allegations cannot replace the agency’s FOIA declarations (or a proper in‑camera/ex parte submission) as the primary evidentiary basis for Exemption 7(A).

Why: the controlling law

- Exemption 7(A) turns on two things the government must show: the records were “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” and disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096–99; 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A).
- The government satisfies its burden ordinarily through agency affidavits/declarations that describe the nature and status of the investigation with “reasonable specificity.” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008); CREW requires more than a conclusory assertion that an investigation is “ongoing.”
- Courts give a measure of deference to law‑enforcement agencies (especially the FBI and on national‑security matters), and may accept ex parte/in‑camera declarations when necessary to avoid disclosing the very secrets at issue. See Campbell v. DOJ, CNSS, and the panel’s discussion in Farahi.

How the Farahi opinion applies

- The D.C. Circuit in Farahi upheld Exemption 7(A) because the FBI supplied public and sealed declarations (Hardy and ex parte Hardy; then a later public update from Bender) that together showed the investigation’s nature and that it remained ongoing. The court emphasized the agency’s affidavits as the core evidentiary basis: e.g., it relied on Hardy’s public declaration that the records were “in furtherance of investigations of … international terrorism” and on the ex parte Hardy declaration that supplied “considerable detail” (Op. §§ IV.A–B).
- The court also noted contextual corroboration from Farahi’s immigration proceedings — publicly available allegations that he had ties to designated terrorist organizations and certain individuals — but it used those allegations as confirmatory context, not as a substitute for the FBI’s declarations. The opinion says: “Broader context confirms that assertion” and cites the immigration record immediately after describing the agency’s affidavits (Op. § IV.A). Similarly, when addressing pendency, the court treated Bender’s terse update as supplementing, not replacing, the earlier Hardy affidavits (Op. § IV.B).

What this means for practice

- Permissible: Courts may and do look to public materials (including pleadings, testimony, or published rulings in related proceedings) to contextualize or corroborate an agency’s sworn FOIA declarations. In Farahi the immigration record was cited to make the FBI’s representations more plausible.
- Impermissible: A court should not rest an Exemption 7(A) finding solely on allegations from unrelated proceedings or on material outside the FOIA record if the agency’s declarations are lacking. CREW warns against accepting merely “vague” or conclusory assertions that an investigation is “ongoing.” The decisive evidence must be the agency’s FOIA‑record submissions (public declarations, Vaughn index or category descriptions, and where necessary sealed/ex parte declarations).
- Practical takeaway: If you’re litigating a FOIA 7(A) claim, push the agency to supply specific, contemporaneous declarations about investigation status and scope. Public documents from parallel cases can be useful to the court as corroboration, but they won’t compensate for deficient FOIA declarations.

Bottom line: courts may rely on public allegations from related civil or immigration matters as corroborating background, but Exemption 7(A) requires the agency’s own FOIA declarations (and, when legitimately necessary, sealed/ex parte submissions) to establish the existence, scope, and continued pendency of law‑enforcement proceedings. The Farahi opinion demonstrates that balance.
