Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### When can a district court require "refresh" declarations to address staleness, and what makes an initial 7(A) showing become stale?
Question:
The district court initially denied summary judgment without prejudice and required updated declarations due to the passage of time, then granted summary judgment after receiving the Bender updates. Is there a doctrinal standard for when a 7(A) declaration becomes too stale to support summary judgment? How much time or what kinds of changed circumstances trigger a duty to supplement, and is this within the district court’s discretion?
Answer:
Short answer
- A district court may require updated or “refresh” declarations when the passage of time or changed circumstances cast doubt on an agency’s earlier showing that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” under Exemption 7(A).  
- The doctrinal touchstone is whether the agency has shown that enforcement proceedings are “pending or reasonably anticipated”; because that fact is time‑sensitive, an otherwise adequate declaration can become stale if enough time has passed or if events could have altered whether proceedings remain pending or foreseeable.  
- Deciding when to demand an update is a matter for the district court’s discretion, informed by the court’s responsibility to ensure the government still meets its burden and by the particular context (nature of the investigation, prior evidentiary detail, and any intervening developments).

Why — the governing law and how it was applied in Farahi
- Exemption 7(A) requires the agency to show that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096 (quoted in the opinion). That showing is inherently temporal: it depends on whether an investigation or prospective proceeding still exists and is likely to be harmed by disclosure.  
- Courts in this circuit accept agency attestations of the “existence” or pendency of investigations, and give the FBI some deference (Campbell; CNSS), but the court still must be satisfied that the record supports the claim. Where the agency’s initial declarations are years old, a court may reasonably doubt that the asserted pendency still obtains without some confirmation. That is what happened here: the district court denied the FBI’s first summary‑judgment motion without prejudice because “given the significant passage of time” it needed an updated assessment of whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated, and more information on segregability (J.A. 121–22). The court acted to ensure the government still carried its burden before entering judgment.  
- The court of appeals approved that approach. It explained that Bender’s later declaration was not required to establish pendency from scratch; rather it functioned as an update confirming that “nothing had changed” since the earlier Hardy declarations. The panel treated the initial Hardy submissions (including an ex parte Hardy declaration) as providing the substantive basis for the 7(A) showing, and Bender’s update as a timely confirmation that the investigation remained ongoing (J.A. 131–39). The panel therefore sustained the district court’s demand for updated material and its reliance on the update.

What makes a 7(A) showing “stale”
- Passage of time alone can make a prior declaration stale if the claimed basis for withholding (an ongoing or anticipated enforcement proceeding) could plausibly have ended or materially changed in the interim. The Farahi court explicitly recognized that “the passage of time casts doubt on whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated,” so an update may be needed (opinion ¶ IV.B).  
- Changed circumstances that would undermine the factual predicate of the initial declaration (e.g., an investigation closed, charges filed and resolved, targets arrested or deceased, or public events that reveal or negate the agency’s prior factual assertions) likewise render an earlier attestation inadequate.  
- Conversely, if the initial declarations provided detailed factual support (including ex parte elaboration where appropriate) and the investigation’s scope reasonably explains ongoing activity (for example, a broad national‑security probe), a court may conclude that the showing is still adequate without a fresh affidavit. The Farahi court stressed context: Bender was a brief update because the detailed Hardy declarations (including sealed details) had already established the nature and pendency of the investigation.

Practical implications and district‑court discretion
- The district court has discretion to require supplementation. That discretion flows from its duty to ensure the government meets its statutory burden and to adjudicate segregability, balanced against recognized deference to agency affidavits—especially in sensitive law‑enforcement or national‑security contexts. The Farahi district court used that discretion to deny without prejudice, request updates, and consider ex parte material; the D.C. Circuit affirmed.  
- There is no bright‑line rule in the opinion (no fixed number of months/years). The analysis is fact‑specific: (1) how old are the declarations; (2) how detailed were the initial public and, if provided, sealed declarations; (3) how broad or enduring is the underlying investigation; and (4) whether intervening facts suggest the investigation may have changed. A court may demand an update when these factors together make the prior showing doubtful.  
- If the court requires an update, the update need only do what is necessary to dispel the doubt: confirm that investigations remain ongoing or explain why earlier factual predicates still obtain. That is what Bender’s declaration accomplished in Farahi: a short public confirmation supported by a sealed ex parte elaboration and grounded in the earlier Hardy material.

Bottom line
Farahi illustrates the governing principle: Exemption 7(A) predicates are time‑sensitive, so district courts may, in their discretion, require refreshed declarations when the passage of time or changed circumstances could undermine an earlier showing that proceedings are pending or reasonably anticipated. Whether a particular declaration is “stale” depends on the case‑specific context and the sufficiency of the original factual detail; there is no fixed temporal cutoff announced in the opinion.
