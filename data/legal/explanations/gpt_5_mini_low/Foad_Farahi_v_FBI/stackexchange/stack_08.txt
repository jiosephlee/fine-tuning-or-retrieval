Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### Can adverse information used in immigration proceedings affect the 7(A) analysis in a separate FOIA case?
Question:
The panel references allegations and evidence from Farahi’s immigration proceedings (e.g., ties to designated terrorist organizations and specific individuals) as contextual support for the FBI’s ongoing-investigation claim. Is it proper for a FOIA court to rely on allegations from related civil or immigration matters to buttress the existence and breadth of a law-enforcement investigation under 7(A), or should the analysis be confined to the agency’s FOIA declarations and Vaughn materials?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — a FOIA court may consider publicly available allegations from related civil or immigration proceedings as background corroboration for an agency’s claim that an investigation is ongoing and broad, but those allegations cannot replace the agency’s FOIA declarations (or a proper in‑camera/ex parte submission) as the primary evidentiary basis for Exemption 7(A).

Why: the controlling law

- Exemption 7(A) turns on two things the government must show: the records were “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” and disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096–99; 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A).
- The government satisfies its burden ordinarily through agency affidavits/declarations that describe the nature and status of the investigation with “reasonable specificity.” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008); CREW requires more than a conclusory assertion that an investigation is “ongoing.”
- Courts give a measure of deference to law‑enforcement agencies (especially the FBI and on national‑security matters), and may accept ex parte/in‑camera declarations when necessary to avoid disclosing the very secrets at issue. See Campbell v. DOJ, CNSS, and the panel’s discussion in Farahi.

How the Farahi opinion applies

- The D.C. Circuit in Farahi upheld Exemption 7(A) because the FBI supplied public and sealed declarations (Hardy and ex parte Hardy; then a later public update from Bender) that together showed the investigation’s nature and that it remained ongoing. The court emphasized the agency’s affidavits as the core evidentiary basis: e.g., it relied on Hardy’s public declaration that the records were “in furtherance of investigations of … international terrorism” and on the ex parte Hardy declaration that supplied “considerable detail” (Op. §§ IV.A–B).
- The court also noted contextual corroboration from Farahi’s immigration proceedings — publicly available allegations that he had ties to designated terrorist organizations and certain individuals — but it used those allegations as confirmatory context, not as a substitute for the FBI’s declarations. The opinion says: “Broader context confirms that assertion” and cites the immigration record immediately after describing the agency’s affidavits (Op. § IV.A). Similarly, when addressing pendency, the court treated Bender’s terse update as supplementing, not replacing, the earlier Hardy affidavits (Op. § IV.B).

What this means for practice

- Permissible: Courts may and do look to public materials (including pleadings, testimony, or published rulings in related proceedings) to contextualize or corroborate an agency’s sworn FOIA declarations. In Farahi the immigration record was cited to make the FBI’s representations more plausible.
- Impermissible: A court should not rest an Exemption 7(A) finding solely on allegations from unrelated proceedings or on material outside the FOIA record if the agency’s declarations are lacking. CREW warns against accepting merely “vague” or conclusory assertions that an investigation is “ongoing.” The decisive evidence must be the agency’s FOIA‑record submissions (public declarations, Vaughn index or category descriptions, and where necessary sealed/ex parte declarations).
- Practical takeaway: If you’re litigating a FOIA 7(A) claim, push the agency to supply specific, contemporaneous declarations about investigation status and scope. Public documents from parallel cases can be useful to the court as corroboration, but they won’t compensate for deficient FOIA declarations.

Bottom line: courts may rely on public allegations from related civil or immigration matters as corroborating background, but Exemption 7(A) requires the agency’s own FOIA declarations (and, when legitimately necessary, sealed/ex parte submissions) to establish the existence, scope, and continued pendency of law‑enforcement proceedings. The Farahi opinion demonstrates that balance.
