Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What qualifies as an "enforcement proceeding" under FOIA Exemption 7(A), and do long-running counterterrorism investigations count?
Question:
The panel says ongoing criminal investigations count as "proceedings" and that the government can meet its burden by attesting to their existence (citing CREW and Juarez). Here, the FBI relied on both public and ex parte declarations to say the investigation into Farahi is ongoing. I'm confused about the line between a general counterterrorism investigation that could go on for years and a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" that CREW seems to require. What makes an investigation sufficiently concrete to qualify as a pending or reasonably anticipated proceeding for 7(A) purposes, especially when no indictment has issued after many years?
Answer:
Short answer
- Under the D.C. Circuit’s controlling framework (as applied in Farahi), an “enforcement proceeding” for FOIA Exemption 7(A) includes ongoing criminal investigations and also “concrete prospective law enforcement” proceedings that are reasonably anticipated. (CREW; Juarez.)
- What makes a long-running counterterrorism investigation sufficiently “concrete” is not merely passage of time, but (1) a showing that the investigation is still active and continuing to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and (2) a factual nexus tying the investigation to particular individuals/incidents or possible federal violations such that premature disclosure would jeopardize that future enforcement. (Juarez; Campbell; CREW; CNSS.)
- The government may meet its burden by attesting to the existence of such proceedings and by supplying more detailed (including ex parte) declarations when necessary to explain the nature, scope, and ongoing activity of the investigation. (CREW; Farahi opinion.)

What the opinion says about the legal standard
- Exemption 7(A) covers records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes if their disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096 (quoted in Farahi).
- The court treats ongoing criminal investigations as “proceedings” and relies on Juarez for the rule that Exemption 7(A) applies “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence.” Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59 (quoted in Farahi).
- The government may satisfy its burden by attesting to the existence of the investigation; where more detail is required, the agency’s public declaration plus an ex parte sealed submission can supply the needed specifics without disclosing the very information FOIA would otherwise force it to reveal. (Farahi, discussing CREW and reliance on public and ex parte Hardy/Bender declarations.)

How that applies to long-running counterterrorism investigations
- Time alone does not defeat Exemption 7(A). The court in Farahi acknowledged the plaintiff’s point that a bare, vague assertion that an investigation “remains ongoing” might be insufficient (drawing on CREW), but it upheld withholding because:
  - the FBI’s public declaration attested the investigation’s existence and described its national‑security/terrorism focus;
  - the sealed/ex parte declaration supplied substantial detail about the investigation’s nature and scope; and
  - the updated (public) attestation confirmed nothing material had changed while the case proceeded. (Farahi pp. IV.B.)
- The panel emphasized deference to the FBI—especially for national‑security/counterterrorism matters—and relied on publicly known connections (in the record) between the subject and terrorist actors/organizations to support the conclusion that a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” continued to exist. (Farahi citing Campbell and CNSS; factual context from Farahi’s immigration record.)

Practical markers a court will look for
If you are evaluating whether a long-running counterterrorism investigation counts as a pending or reasonably anticipated “proceeding” under 7(A), the opinion shows courts will look for:
1. Active evidence‑gathering: statements or declarations showing the investigation remains active and is still collecting evidence that could support prosecution (Juarez language quoted in Farahi).
2. A nexus to specific persons/incidents/possible federal violations: a rational connection between the file and one of the agency’s law‑enforcement duties or a particular security risk (Campbell/Pratt cited in Farahi).
3. More than a conclusory assertion: a bare, unexplained “still ongoing” claim can be thin (CREW), but can be cured by contextual public facts and/or a sealed ex parte declaration that supplies particulars without revealing sensitive material (Farahi).
4. National‑security deference: courts give the FBI a fair measure of deference in counterterrorism contexts, which affects how much detail the government must publicly disclose (Farahi, CNSS, Campbell).
5. Segregability analysis: even if 7(A) applies, the agency must explain why nonexempt material is not reasonably segregable; ex parte submissions can justify that showing where disclosure on the public record would defeat the protective purpose. (Farahi pp. V.)

Bottom line for your hypothetical
A long‑running counterterrorism probe can qualify as a “pending or reasonably anticipated” enforcement proceeding so long as the government can credibly show the investigation remains active (i.e., still gathering evidence for a possible case) and is tied to concrete enforcement risks such that disclosure would jeopardize future proceedings. Mere longevity is not dispositive; courts will require enough factual specificity—sometimes supplied ex parte and with due deference to national‑security concerns—to conclude the investigation is still concrete rather than a vague, indefinite inquiry. Farahi is an illustration of that approach: the combination of public attestations, sealed elaboration, and demonstrable ties between the subject and terrorism‑related activity satisfied Exemption 7(A) despite the investigation’s duration.
