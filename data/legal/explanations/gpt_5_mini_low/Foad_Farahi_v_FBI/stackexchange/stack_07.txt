Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### To what extent may courts rely on ex parte, in camera declarations in FOIA cases, and does the requester’s failure to object waive later challenges?
Question:
The district court accepted ex parte declarations; Farahi didn’t object, and on appeal the panel says he can’t challenge reliance on them. What is the doctrinal basis for using ex parte submissions to decide FOIA cases (especially segregability and 7(A) issues), and how does a requester preserve objections without forcing disclosure of the very material at issue? Are there best practices or limits (e.g., requiring maximum feasible public summaries) that courts enforce before turning to ex parte review?
Answer:
Short answer
- Courts may rely on ex parte, in camera declarations in FOIA suits when the agency’s sealed submissions are the only practicable way to explain why an exemption applies (including why disclosure would interfere with enforcement proceedings under Exemption 7(A) and why non‑exempt material is not reasonably segregable). The D.C. Circuit treats such declarations as a permissible tool so long as the government’s affidavits provide “reasonable specificity” and the court can assess the exemption claim (see Juarez, Mead, CREW, CNSS, Perioperative).  
- But a FOIA requester who affirmatively fails to object to the district court’s receipt of sealed ex parte submissions generally cannot later complain on appeal about the court’s reliance on those submissions — the Farahi panel held that Farahi “did not oppose the district court’s acceptance of ex parte declarations, so he cannot challenge the district court’s reliance on them.”  
- To preserve objections without forcing disclosure, a requester should timely oppose the agency’s motion to file ex parte submissions (or move to limit their use), press for updated public declarations, ask the court to state on the public record as much of its reasoning as possible, and insist on the narrowest feasible sealed submission (and segregability detail) — the opinion endorses these procedural safeguards as alternatives to wholesale public disclosure.

Explanation and doctrinal basis (grounded in the opinion)
1) Why ex parte, in camera declarations are permitted
- The opinion recognizes a settled FOIA practice: courts may rely on agency affidavits (including sealed ex parte declarations) that “show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated.” Juarez v. DOJ; Mead Data. The government bears the burden of proving the exemption, but where national‑security or law‑enforcement interests are implicated, courts give the FBI a “fair measure of deference” (Campbell; CNSS).  
- In some cases the only way to explain why disclosure would harm an ongoing investigation or why particular material is not segregable is to describe sensitive facts that cannot be made public without defeating the very protection the exemption is designed to provide. The court acknowledged that “ex parte declarations no doubt handicap FOIA plaintiffs,” but also accepted that the handicap “is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” (Perioperative Servs. quotation in the opinion.)

2) Application to Exemption 7(A) and segregability
- Exemption 7(A) requires that records be “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated” (statute, CREW, Juarez). The government may prove the pendency or anticipation of proceedings by attesting to their existence; detailed sealed statements can supply context that public filings cannot. In Farahi the court relied on a combination of public declarations and sealed ex parte declarations to conclude the FBI met its burden.  
- On segregability, the statute requires release of “any reasonably segregable portion.” The court may credit sealed explanations demonstrating that non‑exempt material is “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material or that releasable bits are not “reasonably segregable” (Mead, Juarez). The Farahi court accepted the district court’s conclusion — based on ex parte material — that meaningful, reasonably segregable information did not exist in the voluminous, interspersed record.

3) Waiver / preservation
- The panel’s holding on waiver is straightforward: because Farahi “did not oppose the district court’s acceptance of ex parte declarations, so he cannot challenge the district court’s reliance on them.” In other words, a failure to object in the district court forfeited the right to challenge the court’s use of those sealed submissions on appeal. The opinion treats preservation as a matter of ordinary appellate forfeiture doctrines: contest the district court’s procedure at the time it is taking place, or you generally cannot raise that complaint later.

Practical steps a requester can take (consistent with the opinion)
- Object promptly in the district court to the agency’s motion to file ex parte, sealed declarations and preserve the objection on the record. That is the primary way to avoid forfeiture under Farahi.  
- If the court is inclined to permit sealed submissions, ask the court to: (a) require the agency to submit the narrowest possible sealed declaration, (b) require an updated public declaration that provides as much non‑sensitive information as possible, and (c) put on the public record as much of the court’s reasoning as can be disclosed without harming the government interest. The Farahi opinion itself credits the district court’s approach of requiring updated declarations and distinguishing between the public and sealed submissions.  
- Press the court to require specificity in the sealed submission analogous to what the public declaration must contain: explain the existence/ongoing nature of investigations, the specific risks of disclosure, and why particular categories of material cannot be segregated. The opinion cites the need for “reasonable specificity” and that category descriptions must be “sufficiently distinct” (CREW, Gallant) to allow judicial review.  
- If appropriate, move for in camera review by the court rather than full disclosure to the requester, and preserve objection to any ruling that relies exclusively on untimely or overly broad sealed submissions.

Limits and best practices the opinion recognizes
- Courts will not accept conclusory sealed statements in isolation; sealed material must be considered alongside public submissions and other context. The opinion rejected treating a terse update as sufficient in isolation but accepted it as an update when paired with earlier public and sealed declarations (Farahi discussion of Bender vs. Hardy).  
- The agency still carries the statutory duty to identify and release “any reasonably segregable portion.” Courts may credit sealed explanations for why segregation is not feasible, but they expect specificity (Juarez, Mead).  
- The opinion implicitly endorses a sliding, pragmatic approach: require updated public information where feasible; allow sealed detail where necessary to avoid defeating the exemption; but expect courts to review and weigh all of that material to ensure the exemption is properly applied.

Bottom line
- Ex parte, sealed declarations are a recognized and sometimes necessary method for an agency to meet its burden in sensitive FOIA cases (Exemption 7(A), segregability), and courts in this Circuit will rely on them when they provide the needed “reasonable specificity” and the public record is supplemented as feasible.  
- But a requester must object timely in district court if she wants to preserve arguments about the propriety or weight of sealed ex parte submissions; Farahi holds that failure to object forecloses an appellate challenge to the court’s reliance on those submissions.
