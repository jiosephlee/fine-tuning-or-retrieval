Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### Did the court miscite FOIA by referring to § 552(b)(7)(B) instead of (b)(7)(A), and if so, does that matter?
Question:
In Section IV.B, the panel quotes the interference standard and cites 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(B), but Exemption 7(B) is about depriving a person of a fair trial, while 7(A) is the interference-with-proceedings exemption at issue here. Is this just a typographical error without substantive effect, or could it create ambiguity about the standard the court applied?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — the opinion contains a miscitation in IV.B (it cites § 552(b)(7)(B) where it plainly means Exemption 7(A) ) — but it is a typographical error without substantive effect. The panel repeatedly identifies, quotes, and applies Exemption 7(A) and the correct “pending or reasonably anticipated” standard, so there is no real ambiguity about the legal standard the court used.

Why that is clear from the opinion
- The opinion’s holding and framework consistently invoke Exemption 7(A). The opening summary states the records were protected by “FOIA’s Exemption 7(A).” Section I frames the relevant exemption as § 552(b)(7)(A) and quotes the interference language from that subsection. Section III and IV.A likewise discuss § 552(b)(7)(A) and the requirement that records be “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” Those are the operative provisions.
- The disputed passage in IV.B uses the correct substantive standard — that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated” — and it cites CREW and Juarez, the court’s precedents applying Exemption 7(A). The opinion explains that ongoing investigations qualify and discusses how the FBI’s declarations establish pendency/anticipation. Those doctrinal points track Exemption 7(A), not 7(B).
- The apparent miscitation is isolated. Elsewhere the opinion (including the statutory quotations and the burdens the government must meet) points to § 552(b)(7)(A). The court’s analysis, factual findings, and ultimate disposition all rest on Exemption 7(A).

Legal effect
- A single incorrect parenthetical statutory citation that conflicts with the text and repeated holdings in the same opinion is a clerical error that does not change the legal standard applied. The opinion demonstrates what the court intended: to apply Exemption 7(A)’s interference-with-proceedings rule. Thus the miscitation does not create a plausible ambiguity about the governing law or the result of the case.

What a litigant who is concerned could do
- If a party wanted to eliminate even this minor risk of confusion in later citation, they could move for panel rehearing or rehearing en banc to request correction/clarification. But given the opinion’s consistent text and holdings applying 7(A), such a motion would be unlikely to change the outcome; it would be principally housekeeping.

Bottom line: the reference to § 552(b)(7)(B) in IV.B is a typographical/citation error, and the opinion itself makes clear the court applied § 552(b)(7)(A)’s interference standard.
