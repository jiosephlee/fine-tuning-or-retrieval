Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### How does the segregability requirement work when non-exempt bits are interspersed throughout highly sensitive records?
Question:
The court upholds withholding in full because any non-exempt material is interspersed line-by-line and not "reasonably segregable" (Mead; Perioperative Services). What is the governing test for when line-by-line redactions are deemed unreasonable, and how do courts evaluate an agency's ex parte explanation that further segregation would be meaningless? Are agencies required to release context-free names/dates or boilerplate headings if those could be extracted without harm, or is the "meaningless snippet" concept dispositive?
Answer:
Short answer
- When non‑exempt bits are scattered “line‑by‑line” through highly sensitive material, the governing test is whether any extractable material would produce meaningful, non‑exempt information—if not, further segregation is “not reasonably segregable.” See Mead, 566 F.2d at 261; Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1069; Foad Farahi v. FBI, slip op. at § V.
- A court may accept an agency’s ex parte explanation for non‑segregability so long as the agency’s affidavits (including sealed/ex parte submissions) explain with reasonable specificity why further redaction or extraction would harm the exempt interest or produce only meaningless snippets. Juarez v. DOJ; Foad Farahi §§ III, V.
- Agencies are not mechanically required to release isolated names, dates, or boilerplate headings if those items would be “meaningless” or if their extraction would itself reveal sensitive context or is impossible without disclosing exempt material. The FOIA duty is to release meaningful information, not isolated words. Mead, quoted in Foad Farahi § V.

What the court in Farahi actually held (doctrine and application)
1) Statutory baseline: segregability
- FOIA requires agencies to take “reasonable steps” to segregate and release non‑exempt information; any “reasonably segregable portion” must be provided after deleting exempt portions. 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); § 552(b). (Foad Farahi § V.)

2) Governing test for line‑by‑line interspersion
- The controlling line is Mead Data: if non‑exempt material is “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material (for example, non‑exempt words scattered line‑by‑line), then the agency may withhold the document in full because the scattered pieces do not amount to “meaningful information.” Foad Farahi § V (citing Mead, 566 F.2d at 261).
- Perioperative reinforced that when tiny non‑exempt portions are randomly interspersed, segregability can be unreasonable: a court evaluates whether the isolated disclosures would produce meaningful information or merely “snippet[s]” that convey nothing useful. Foad Farahi § V (citing Perioperative Servs.).

3) What the agency must show
- The agency retains the burden to show the exemption applies and to justify non‑segregability, but courts may rely on agency declarations that explain with “reasonable specificity” why documents cannot be further segregated. Juarez v. DOJ; Foad Farahi §§ III, IV.A.
- Ex parte (sealed) declarations are permissible where revealing the basis for segregation analysis on the public record would itself disclose sensitive information. The district court may review those sealed submissions and rely on them in finding non‑segregability; Farahi affirms that practice and explains it does not violate FOIA procedure where the plaintiff did not object to ex parte filings. Foad Farahi § V.

4) How courts evaluate ex parte explanations
- Courts look to the level of detail: a conclusory assertion may be insufficient standing alone (see CREW), but when the public and sealed declarations together give adequate detail about the investigation and the reasons why extraction would harm enforcement, the court can credit the agency. Farahi § IV.B (discussing CREW and the interplay of public and ex parte declarations).
- The court asks whether the segregable bits would convey any substantive information independent of the exempt material. If the only possible disclosures are context‑free words, headings, or names that, by themselves, are meaningless or would reveal sensitive context when unmoored, a court can accept the agency’s position that further segregation is futile. Foad Farahi § V (quoting Mead: “the focus of FOIA is information, … not simply words”).

5) Names/dates/boilerplate — are they always disclosable?
- No. Farahi rejects a rule that agencies must release isolated words or boilerplate wherever they can be extracted. The opinion explains that even if “individual words or phrases” could be disclosed without apparent harm, that fact “is neither here nor there” if the words do not amount to meaningful information or their release would risk exposing the exempt material. Foad Farahi § V.
- Thus, the “meaningless snippet” concept can be dispositive: if what remains after redaction would be non‑informative or would reveal sensitive context when pieced together, the agency need not release it. But that conclusion must be supported by adequate (and, where appropriate, sealed) explanation. Foad Farahi §§ V, IV.B.

Practical takeaways for litigation and FOIA practice
- Agencies should provide detailed declarations explaining why line‑by‑line segregation would either (a) produce only meaningless snippets, or (b) itself disclose exempt material or reveal the scope/methods of an investigation. Those explanations can be made ex parte if public disclosure would defeat the protective purpose. Foad Farahi §§ IV–V.
- Requesters should press the agency to identify distinguishable categories of documents and to explain whether any categories contain genuinely extractable substantive information (CREW/Gallant point); otherwise, courts may treat broad category arguments as forfeited. Foad Farahi § IV.C.
- If you can point to specific examples where isolated items (e.g., a name or date) would be meaningful standing alone or could be released without revealing exempt context, that helps rebut a non‑segregability claim; absent that showing, Mead/Perioperative and Farahi will favor the agency when the interspersion is pervasive.

Conclusion
Farahi follows Mead and Perioperative: pervasive, line‑by‑line interspersion permits full withholding when the remaining words would be meaningless or would reveal exempt information, and courts may rely on reasonably specific agency affidavits — including sealed, ex parte submissions — to reach that conclusion. Agencies are not compelled to release context‑free names/dates or boilerplate when those extractions would produce no substantive, non‑exempt information or would itself reveal sensitive context; but the agency must justify that position with sufficient specificity.
