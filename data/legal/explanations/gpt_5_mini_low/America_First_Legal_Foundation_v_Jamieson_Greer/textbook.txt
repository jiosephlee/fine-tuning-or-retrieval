Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Statutory and Institutional Framework

Understanding America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer requires a clear picture of the federal institutional actors and the statutory provisions that define their investigative and enforcement roles. The dispute turns on how two statutory schemes—the Civil Service Reform Act (CSRA) provisions creating and empowering the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) and the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA)—overlap and interact. This chapter explains OSC’s role, the corrective and disciplinary pathways available under the CSRA, OSC’s special investigatory authority under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 (including the provision addressing alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings), FOIA’s separate disciplinary-trigger provision in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), and the particular tensions between these provisions that underlie the case.

## Role of the Office of Special Counsel (OSC)

OSC is an independent federal agency created to protect the integrity of the Civil Service. The agency is both investigative and prosecutorial: it receives allegations of misconduct or unlawful personnel practices by federal employees, investigates those allegations, and—when warranted—seeks corrective or disciplinary relief through administrative proceedings. In the opinion, the court frames OSC as the statutory actor charged with ensuring “a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service,” and its functions must be read through the CSRA’s statutory text.

OSC’s authority is not unlimited: it operates under statutory mandates and procedures that define which allegations it must investigate, which remedies it may seek, and the processes that govern notice and participation for complainants and employees. The case hinges on competing readings of those statutes—whether OSC must investigate certain FOIA-related misconduct and what procedural protections (if any) attach to such investigations.

## Corrective and Disciplinary Pathways: 5 U.S.C. § 1214 and § 1215

The CSRA provides two principal pathways by which OSC can seek relief against government employees found to have engaged in unlawful personnel practices or other covered misconduct:

- 5 U.S.C. § 1214 governs corrective actions. Under § 1214, when OSC investigates and finds merit to allegations (traditionally “prohibited personnel practices”), it can seek corrective relief before the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB). Section 1214 also contains procedural provisions governing how OSC conducts investigations that allege prohibited personnel practices, including notice and periodic updates to complainants about the investigation’s status (for example, provisions requiring OSC to notify the complaining party about the progress of an investigation). These procedures structure participation and informational rights during a § 1214-type investigation.

- 5 U.S.C. § 1215 sets out OSC’s authority to seek disciplinary sanctions. If OSC determines that disciplinary action is appropriate, it can pursue removal, suspension, or other disciplinary outcomes against responsible employees through the MSPB or other authorized mechanisms.

The CSRA therefore supplies both the substantive paths for redress (corrective and disciplinary) and procedural rules for investigations when OSC proceeds under the statutory rubric of “prohibited personnel practices.”

## OSC’s Authority under 5 U.S.C. § 1216, Including § 1216(a)(3)

Section 1216 expands OSC’s investigatory reach beyond classic “prohibited personnel practices.” It authorizes OSC to investigate other forms of employee misconduct that implicate the integrity of the civil service, and it explicitly cross-references OSC’s corrective and disciplinary powers. Two features of § 1216 are central to the case:

- Section 1216(a)(3) commands that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552”—that is, FOIA-based withholdings. The use of the mandatory “shall” signals in the statute the possibility of an affirmative duty to investigate certain FOIA allegations.

- Section 1216(c) provides that for such FOIA-related allegations OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” This clause links § 1216 investigations to the corrective and disciplinary mechanisms of §§ 1214–1215 and suggests that OSC can, in FOIA cases, initiate the same kinds of corrective or disciplinary proceedings available for prohibited personnel practices.

These provisions thus do two things: (1) give OSC an express investigatory role over alleged FOIA-withholdings, and (2) authorize OSC to employ the same corrective or disciplinary remedies available under § 1214 and § 1215. But the text leaves open substantial questions: does § 1216 create an absolute duty to investigate and, if so, does it automatically import the procedural rules of § 1214 (including notice obligations) into § 1216 investigations? The court’s opinion addresses these questions (and the parties’ dispute about them), but the statutory text is the starting point for that analysis.

## Relationship Between § 1216 and the Procedures in § 1214

Section 1216(c)’s reference to seeking corrective action “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved” creates a textual link to § 1214’s procedures. Practically, that raises two related issues:

- Whether, when OSC investigates a FOIA-related allegation under § 1216, the procedural protections and notice-and-information duties listed in § 1214 automatically apply to the investigation and to the complaining party (i.e., whether complainants are entitled to the same kinds of notices and updates that § 1214 provides for prohibited personnel practice investigations).

- Whether OSC’s decision whether to apply § 1214 procedures in § 1216 investigations is discretionary. The statutory language could be read to require that § 1214 procedures govern § 1216 investigations in the same mandatory fashion; alternatively, it could be read as authorizing OSC to use § 1214’s mechanisms where appropriate but leaving procedural decisions to agency discretion.

The opinion notes that lower-court authority has treated the application of § 1214’s procedural requirements to § 1216 investigations as discretionary—an important premise the D.C. Circuit cites later when weighing whether AFL had an entitlement to information and thus Article III standing.

Understanding this relationship is central to the case because AFL seeks both a declaration that OSC must investigate under § 1216 and relief that would produce the notices and investigative information that § 1214 provides. The legal sticking point is whether § 1216 both compels investigation and carries with it a statutory right to § 1214-style procedural protections.

## FOIA’s Distinct Disciplinary Trigger: 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)

FOIA itself contains an independent, narrowly tailored provision that triggers OSC involvement in FOIA disputes. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) sets out three specific judicial predicates that, when satisfied, require OSC to act:

1. A court has ordered production of agency records that were alleged to have been improperly withheld under FOIA;
2. The court has awarded attorneys’ fees against the agency; and
3. The court has specifically questioned whether the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding.”

If all three conditions are met, FOIA mandates that OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding.” That provision is remedial and narrowly focused: it ties OSC’s mandatory disciplinary role to the event of a court’s finding and attendant fee award in a FOIA litigation context.

FOIA’s three-part trigger therefore creates a distinct, judicially conditioned path that guarantees OSC’s involvement—subject to the occurrence of those judicial determinations. The presence of this mechanism complicates the interpretation of § 1216(a)(3): does the FOIA provision represent an exclusive, procedurally conditioned route for OSC discipline in FOIA matters, or does § 1216(a)(3) authorize OSC to act independently (and perhaps more broadly) without the three FOIA predicates being met?

## The Tension Framing the Case

The crux of the statutory dispute in the case is the tension between two lines of authority:

- On one side, § 1216(a)(3) appears to impose a mandatory duty on OSC to “shall … conduct an investigation” of alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings and to permit OSC to use § 1214–§ 1215 corrective and disciplinary tools “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” That language supports an interpretation that OSC must investigate FOIA withholding allegations and may pursue corrective or disciplinary remedies using the established § 1214 procedures, including notice obligations.

- On the other side, FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) creates a specific, court-triggered obligation to initiate disciplinary proceedings when three judicial conditions are present, which suggests a different, narrower route for OSC engagement in FOIA enforcement tied explicitly to judicial findings and fee awards.

AFL’s position in the litigation invoked § 1216(a)(3) to demand that OSC investigate DOJ’s asserted policy of not searching employee emails absent employee consent. OSC declined, reasoning that FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) governs OSC’s disciplinary role and that the mandatory conditions in that provision were not satisfied. The district court initially concluded that FOIA’s three-part trigger did not limit OSC’s power under § 1216 (i.e., it accepted that § 1216 independently required investigation), but it then remanded to OSC because it deemed the decision whether to apply § 1214 procedures discretionary. On appeal, the D.C. Circuit did not resolve every statutory question; instead, it disposed of the case on Article III standing. Nonetheless, the statutory tensions framed the core legal disagreement: whether § 1216 creates an independent, mandatory investigatory duty (and whether that duty imports § 1214’s procedures), or whether FOIA’s separate disciplinary trigger circumscribes OSC’s mandatory obligations in FOIA cases.

Readers studying the opinion should therefore keep three statutory components in view: (1) OSC’s basic investigatory and prosecutorial role under the CSRA; (2) § 1216(a)(3)’s explicit command to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings and its cross-reference to § 1214–§ 1215 remedies; and (3) FOIA’s own § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) which, by statute, mandates OSC action only when a court has both ordered production and raised questions of arbitrary withholding coupled with an award of fees. The interplay of these provisions—and the interpretive choices about mandatory duties versus discretionary procedural practices—frames both the parties’ arguments and the district court’s and appellate court’s analyses.

# Facts and Procedural History

America First Legal Foundation (AFL) filed a FOIA request with the Department of Justice (DOJ) seeking emails exchanged between an outside attorney and the DOJ Civil Division as part of AFL’s broader inquiry into judicial recusals. In response, the Civil Division asserted a policy under which it does not search employees’ personal or work emails for responsive records without the employees’ consent. That policy was the central complaint in AFL’s follow-up actions: AFL maintained that the Civil Division’s purported refusal to search employee emails amounted to an arbitrary or capricious withholding of records under FOIA.

AFL pursued parallel litigation against DOJ under FOIA challenging the Civil Division’s asserted search practice. DOJ has produced records in that FOIA case and continued to produce additional responsive documents thereafter. Importantly for the appeal before the D.C. Circuit, the district court proceedings and this Court’s review do not take up the sufficiency or adequacy of DOJ’s production in that FOIA suit; the production itself is not at issue in the appeal from the district court’s disposition of AFL’s claims against the Office of Special Counsel (OSC).

Concurrently, AFL asked the Office of Special Counsel to investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). That provision directs OSC to “conduct an investigation” of allegations concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited by FOIA, and it authorizes OSC to seek corrective or disciplinary action under the corrective-action and disciplinary provisions applicable to prohibited personnel practices. AFL’s petition to OSC therefore sought an OSC investigation into what AFL characterized as a systemic failure by DOJ to search employee emails in response to FOIA requests.

OSC declined to open an investigation. Its initial declination rested on a different statutory provision in FOIA, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), which directs OSC to initiate disciplinary proceedings when a court (1) orders the production of records improperly withheld, (2) awards attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) “specifically question[s] whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding.” OSC concluded that those three FOIA predicates were not satisfied and therefore that it lacked authority to proceed under the FOIA-specific enforcement mechanism—an approach on which OSC relied to disavow a mandatory duty to investigate under § 1216.

AFL sued OSC in district court, alleging that OSC’s refusal to investigate violated § 1216 and seeking injunctive relief compelling OSC to investigate. The district court issued a mixed ruling. It held that FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) provision does not limit OSC’s authority under § 1216: in the court’s view, § 1216(a)(3)’s command that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of arbitrary FOIA withholdings was not confined by the FOIA provision that addresses OSC’s obligations following a judicial FOIA judgment. The district court therefore concluded that OSC’s initial declination rested on a legal error about OSC’s statutory obligations. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023).

Despite ruling that OSC had misconstrued its authority, the district court did not order OSC to investigate the alleged DOJ policy. The court determined that even if § 1216 authorized an investigation, § 1216(c) gives OSC discretion as to whether to pursue corrective or disciplinary proceedings in the same manner as prohibited personnel practices, and the decision to initiate enforcement was therefore committed to agency discretion. Accordingly, the district court remanded the matter to OSC for further consideration rather than compelling an investigation.

Following remand, OSC again declined to open an investigation into DOJ’s alleged search policy. AFL then moved for entry of final judgment; the district court dismissed AFL’s remaining claims on the merits and entered judgment against AFL. AFL appealed that final judgment to the United States Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit. The D.C. Circuit stated that its review would be de novo, as is customary for statutory and jurisdictional questions of this character.

On initial consideration, neither party had addressed whether AFL possessed Article III standing to pursue its claims against OSC. Because standing implicates subject-matter jurisdiction, the D.C. Circuit ordered supplemental briefing specifically on that threshold issue. The Court then treated standing as the dispositive question; it evaluated whether AFL had alleged a concrete, particularized, and redressable injury traceable to OSC’s declination to investigate under § 1216, and whether a favorable judicial order compelling OSC to investigate would likely remedy any such injury. The Circuit ultimately concluded that AFL lacked Article III standing and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

# Article III Standing and the Procedural-Injury Framework

This chapter explains how Article III standing doctrine operates in the context of claimed violations of statutory procedural rights, using America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer as the focal opinion. The court’s analysis shows how the familiar tripartite standing test (injury in fact, causation, redressability) is applied when a plaintiff asserts only a procedural entitlement to agency action, and it clarifies which elements courts may relax and which they will insist upon.

## Core elements: injury in fact, causation, and redressability

Article III confines federal courts to “Cases” and “Controversies,” so a plaintiff must demonstrate standing before a court may adjudicate the merits. Standing requires three familiar elements: (1) an injury in fact that is concrete and particularized and actual or imminent; (2) causation—a fairly traceable connection between the defendant’s challenged conduct and the alleged injury; and (3) redressability—a substantial likelihood that a favorable decision will remedy the injury. The Supreme Court’s leading rulings that anchor these requirements are Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife (setting the three-element test and the concreteness/imminence requirements), Allen v. Wright (explaining the case-or-controversy foundation), and Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment (explaining standing as a jurisdictional threshold courts must resolve).

In practice, courts examine the factual and legal linkages that bind the alleged injury to the defendant and that make judicial relief likely to redress the harm. The D.C. Circuit applies these elements at the motion-to-dismiss stage by asking whether the plaintiff plausibly alleges facts supporting each element.

## Injury must be concrete, particularized, and actual or imminent; procedural rights in vacuo are insufficient

Not every statutory procedural entitlement supplies standing. The Supreme Court has warned that the mere deprivation of a procedural right, standing alone and unconnected to any concrete interest, does not create Article III standing. In Summers v. Earth Island Institute, the Court held that “a procedural right in vacuo” is not a sufficient injury: a plaintiff must show that the procedural violation affects some concrete, particularized interest.

The America First Legal (AFL) opinion follows Summers: when a plaintiff claims only that an agency unlawfully refused to provide a statutory procedure (here, an OSC investigation under 5 U.S.C. § 1216), the court asks whether that procedural breach caused any concrete, individualized harm. Absent such a concrete interest, the procedural claim cannot sustain Article III jurisdiction.

## Relaxed standing for procedural injuries: what is relaxed and what remains

Courts have developed a calibrated approach when plaintiffs allege statutory procedural violations that are designed to protect concrete interests. That approach “relaxes—but does not eliminate—issues of imminence and redressability.” The D.C. Circuit has described this framework in cases such as Center for Law & Education v. Department of Education and Florida Audubon Society v. Bentsen. Under that framework, a plaintiff may show standing even if the injury is not as imminent or the redress not as direct as in a garden-variety claim, but the plaintiff still must satisfy the bedrock requirements of injury in fact and causation. In short:

- Relaxed: The strictness with which courts demand imminence and direct redress may be eased when a statute gives a procedural protection intended to protect a concrete interest.
- Not relaxed: The requirement that the plaintiff show a concrete, particularized injury in fact, and a traceable causal link between the defendant’s conduct and that injury, remains firm.

The AFL opinion adopts this approach. The court acknowledged that procedural-rights plaintiffs may sometimes benefit from relaxed considerations of imminence and redressability, but it stressed that AFL still must demonstrate a concrete injury and that the injury is fairly traceable to OSC’s refusal to investigate.

## Informational standing: the entitlement-based model

A special, well-established category of concrete harms is informational injury: a plaintiff is injured when a statute entitles them to receive information and the defendant unlawfully withholds it. The Supreme Court and this circuit have enforced an entitlement-based rule for informational standing: if a statute grants a person a right to obtain specified information, then denial of that information can constitute a concrete injury. Key cases include FEC v. Akins, Public Citizen v. DOJ, Zivotofsky (as applied in the circuit), and American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals (ASPCA) v. Feld Entertainment. Those decisions hold that a statutory entitlement to information creates a concrete interest whose deprivation can satisfy injury in fact.

But informational standing depends on an actual statutory entitlement to the information at issue. If the statute does not create a right to receive the specific information claimed, then the plaintiff cannot bootstrap an informational injury from the statute’s procedural language. The AFL opinion enforces this principle: even if a statute requires an agency to “conduct an investigation,” that obligation does not automatically create a separate right to receive the investigation’s fruits (for example, notices or reports) unless some provision explicitly entitles the plaintiff to that information.

## Application to AFL’s framing: § 1216 investigation and the two asserted concrete interests

Applying these principles, the D.C. Circuit analyzed AFL’s two ways of converting its asserted procedural right to an OSC investigation under § 1216 into a concrete injury.

1. FOIA records withheld by DOJ

AFL’s primary asserted injury was informational: because OSC declined to investigate under § 1216, AFL says DOJ still refuses to produce the records AFL sought under FOIA. The court rejected this theory on causation and redressability grounds. The central problem is that any withholding by DOJ is attributable primarily to DOJ, not OSC; OSC’s decision not to investigate is too attenuated from DOJ’s withholding to be a fairly traceable cause of AFL’s lack of access to the records. To accept AFL’s theory requires chaining together a series of speculative events: OSC would have to (a) exercise enforcement discretion to pursue corrective or disciplinary action under §§ 1214 or 1215; (b) the MSPB would have to impose sanctions or corrective actions; and (c) DOJ would then respond by producing the records. The court emphasized that standing cannot rest on speculation about the unfettered choices of independent actors (here, the MSPB and DOJ) or on how an agency might exercise enforcement discretion that courts traditionally cannot compel (Heckler v. Chaney). Because the causal chain was speculative, a judicial order requiring OSC to investigate would not be likely to “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” AFL’s informational injury. The court therefore held that AFL lacked standing to sue based on the deprivation of the FOIA records.

2. Notices and information from an OSC investigation (§ 1214 procedural benefits)

AFL’s second asserted injury was denial of the informational benefits that would flow from an OSC investigation—specifically, notices and procedural information that OSC gives complainants when pursuing corrective actions under § 1214. Here the court focused on entitlement: the informational-injury line of cases requires that a statute actually entitle the plaintiff to the withheld information. Section 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to “conduct an investigation” of alleged FOIA misconduct, but it does not create a parallel entitlement to receive the notices and other procedural communications that arise under § 1214. AFL conceded that OSC’s decision whether to adopt § 1214’s procedures in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary (the court cited Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel on that point). Because § 1216 does not, by its terms, guarantee AFL the § 1214 notice regime, AFL cannot claim an entitlement-based informational injury. And because there is no statutory entitlement to the § 1214-style notices in a § 1216 matter, ordering OSC to investigate would not be likely to redress AFL’s asserted informational deprivation.

Taken together, these applications show the limits of the relaxed procedural-injury framework. Even where a statute arguably grants a procedural right to agency action, a plaintiff must (1) identify a concrete, statutory or other legally protected interest that was affected by the procedural denial, and (2) show that the defendant’s conduct is the fairly traceable cause of that concrete injury and that a favorable order would likely redress it. AFL’s theories failed both prongs: one because causation and redressability were too attenuated and speculative, and the other because there was no statutory entitlement to the requested information.

Conclusion

America First Legal illustrates the careful balancing courts perform when plaintiffs assert procedural statutory rights. The procedural-rights framework may relax the stringency of some standing elements, but it does not eliminate the twin bedrock requirements of a concrete injury in fact and a plausible causal link to the defendant’s conduct. Informational injuries remain cognizable when the plaintiff is entitled by statute to receive the withheld information; absent such an entitlement or a non-speculative causal path to redress, a procedural claim will not suffice to establish Article III standing.

# AFL’s First Theory: Denial of FOIA Records and the Causation/Redressability Gap

This chapter explains why the D.C. Circuit rejected America First Legal Foundation’s (AFL) argument that the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) refusal to investigate DOJ under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 produced a concrete informational injury—namely, AFL’s nonreceipt of records it sought through FOIA—and therefore could support Article III standing. The court’s analysis centers on traceability (who caused the injury), the speculative chain of causation that would be required to turn an OSC investigation into actual disclosure, the limits imposed by independent actors and agencies not before the court, and the special role of agency enforcement discretion. Together these considerations show that AFL’s FOIA-records theory fails both causation and redressability requirements.

Traceability: the informational injury is attributable to DOJ, not OSC
A necessary element of Article III standing is that the plaintiff’s injury be fairly traceable to the defendant’s conduct. Lujan, 504 U.S. at 560–61. AFL’s asserted injury is that DOJ has not produced the records it requested. The court emphasized the straightforward point that that information deficit is traceable to DOJ’s withholding decisions, not to OSC’s refusal to investigate. OSC’s declination, even if mistaken, is not the proximate source of the nondisclosure; DOJ’s independent choice to withhold or produce records is. Because the allegedly injured informational interest is directly attributable to DOJ, AFL cannot show that OSC’s action (or inaction) is the cause of its nonreceipt in the requisite Article III sense.

Speculative causal chain required to link OSC inaction to disclosure
Even assuming OSC had a duty to investigate under § 1216, AFL’s theory depends on a long series of contingent events: OSC conducts a § 1216 investigation; OSC decides to pursue corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215; the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) finds a violation and imposes sanctions; DOJ (or the responsible employees) respond by changing their FOIA practices and producing the requested records. The court viewed this chain as both attenuated and speculative. Standing requires more than a conceivable sequence of events; the plaintiff must show a plausible causal chain tying the defendant’s conduct to the injury that is not reliant on speculation about multiple future choices by others. The court concluded that AFL’s needed linkages were too uncertain to satisfy traceability.

The independent-actor barrier: courts do not count on choices by nonparties
The opinion draws on recent and longstanding precedents that disfavour standing theories that rest on independent actors’ free choices. FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine (cited in the opinion) underscores that causation cannot be established by speculation about “unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” Likewise, Haaland v. Brackeen reinforces the idea that redressability focuses on how a judicial remedy would bind the parties, not whether a court’s reasoning might persuade third parties to act. Here, two independent institutions—MSPB and DOJ—would have to make discretionary decisions favorable to AFL for the injury to be redressed. Because those actors are not defendants in the case and their future actions are not compelled by a ruling against OSC, the causal chain founders on the independent-actor problem.

Agency enforcement discretion and Heckler v. Chaney
The court also relied on the principle from Heckler v. Chaney that courts generally cannot direct how an agency exercises its enforcement or prosecutorial discretion. Even if OSC had investigated and found misconduct, whether and how OSC pursues corrective or disciplinary routes is an enforcement choice, subject to agency discretion. For standing, relying on an agency’s hypothetical discretionary enforcement choices to redress an informational injury is speculative: courts may not force OSC to pursue particular enforcement steps that would be needed to produce the records AFL seeks. Thus, the specter of compelled downstream enforcement cannot save AFL’s causation or redressability story.

Redressability shortfall and the “very likely” standard
Redressability asks whether it is likely—not merely possible—that a favorable court decision will remedy the plaintiff’s injury. The court applied this standard, citing Hecate Energy v. FERC: a successful judgment must make it “very likely” that the next steps required for relief will occur. The opinion found that ordering OSC to investigate would not meet that test, because even a mandatory OSC probe would not force MSPB or DOJ to act in a way that would result in disclosure. A judicial order against OSC therefore would not “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” AFL’s nonreceipt of FOIA records. The court reinforced that redressability looks to what the judgment will bind (the defendant OSC), not to whether it would merely influence independent agencies to change conduct.

Conclusion: speculative causation and lack of likely redressability defeat standing
Putting these considerations together, the court concluded that AFL’s FOIA-records theory fails. The alleged informational injury is attributable to DOJ, not OSC; proving causation would require an attenuated, speculative chain of discretionary choices by OSC, the MSPB, and DOJ; and a favorable decree against OSC would not be likely to produce the disclosures AFL seeks. Because AFL cannot show the requisite traceability and redressability, its claim predicated on nonreceipt of FOIA records does not establish Article III standing. The D.C. Circuit therefore did not reach the merits of AFL’s statutory-contention, instead vacating and remanding with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

# AFL’s Second Theory: Informational Injury from OSC Investigation Processes and the Absence of Entitlement

## Entitlement prerequisite for informational standing

The D.C. Circuit’s analysis begins from a foundational principle of informational standing: to show a concrete informational injury, a plaintiff must be denied information to which a statute gives it an entitlement. This principle appears in the court’s citation to cases like Akins and Public Citizen and is applied here through the test described in American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Entertainment: it is not enough that a plaintiff would like to receive information; the statute must “entitle” the plaintiff to it. That entitlement requirement is what distinguishes a mere interest in information from a legally cognizable injury in fact. The court therefore frames AFL’s claim by asking whether any statutory provision gives AFL a right to receive investigatory notices or other information from the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) when OSC investigates alleged FOIA violations under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3).

## Why § 1216(a)(3)’s investigatory mandate does not itself create an entitlement to information

AFL’s second theory rests in part on § 1216(a)(3), which commands that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” AFL contends that a mandatory investigatory duty therefore gives rise to an informational entitlement: if OSC must investigate, then AFL should be able to receive the notices and progress updates that accompany OSC investigations. The court rejects that leap. The mere existence of a statutory duty to investigate does not automatically confer a right for particular outsiders to obtain investigatory notices or updates. In short, a plaintiff cannot convert a procedural obligation owed to the public or the government into an individualized entitlement to information without a statutory text that does that work. Section 1216(a)(3) imposes a duty to investigate, but it does not, by its terms, give FOIA requesters a right to receive OSC investigatory notices.

## The role of § 1214 notice provisions and the Carson line: § 1214 procedures are not automatically ported into § 1216 investigations

AFL points to § 1214, which governs corrective actions for “prohibited personnel practices” and includes provisions about notifying complainants and informing them of the progress of an investigation. The argument is that because § 1216(c) authorizes OSC to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved,” an OSC investigation under § 1216 might be accompanied by the § 1214 notice procedures. But the court identifies two limiting points that defeat AFL’s argument.

First, § 1214 is directed to the procedures for prohibited-personnel-practice investigations; its notice and progress-reporting provisions create entitlements tied to that statutory investigative context. Second, the court points out—and AFL concedes—that existing precedents treat OSC’s choice whether to apply § 1214 procedures in a § 1216 investigation as discretionary. The opinion cites Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel for the proposition that OSC’s decision whether to follow § 1214’s procedural requirements when it is acting under § 1216 is not compelled. That concession undermines AFL’s attempt to establish a statutory entitlement to § 1214-style notices in the § 1216 context. Because AFL does not (and cannot) identify a statutory text that expressly requires OSC to provide § 1214 notices when it is investigating under § 1216, there is no entitlement on which to base informational standing.

## Absence of a statutory entitlement means no concrete informational injury

Applying the entitlement prerequisite, the court concludes that AFL has not alleged the kind of statutory entitlement that would transform its informational interest into a concrete, Article III injury. Even if § 1216(a)(3) creates a mandatory investigatory duty, that duty does not itself provide AFL a right to the particular information (notices, progress updates, outcomes) that it seeks. Because AFL lacks a statutory right to receive those investigatory materials, the denial of such materials does not constitute the kind of concrete deprivation necessary for injury in fact. Put another way, AFL’s complaint alleges a disappointment of expectations about notice and disclosure, not the violation of a legally protected information right.

## Redressability: ordering an investigation would not be likely to yield the § 1214-style notices AFL seeks

Beyond the entitlement/injury analysis, the court addresses redressability. Even if § 1216 did require OSC to investigate (and even if that gave rise to some procedural right), AFL must show that a favorable judicial order would likely redress its asserted injury. The court reasons that ordering OSC to investigate under § 1216 would not, as a practical matter, produce the § 1214-type notices that AFL wants. Because OSC has discretion whether to apply § 1214 procedures in a § 1216 investigation, a judicial mandate that OSC investigate would not bind OSC to provide the specific notices and updates AFL seeks. Thus any relief would be unlikely to produce the concrete information AFL claims it was denied, leaving redressability unsatisfied. The court ties this point to the entitlement analysis: absent a statutory command to provide § 1214 notices in the § 1216 context, a judgment ordering OSC to investigate would not be “reasonably likely” to yield those notices.

## Disposition and doctrinal takeaways

Because AFL cannot identify a statutory entitlement to investigatory notices or information, it fails to plead a concrete informational injury and cannot satisfy the relaxed standards that sometimes apply to procedural-right claims. Redressability likewise fails because a court-ordered § 1216 investigation would not compel OSC to provide § 1214-style notices. The D.C. Circuit therefore concludes AFL lacks Article III standing, vacates the district court’s merits judgment, and remands with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

The practical lesson for students is twofold. First, informational standing requires more than a statutory duty owed generally to the public; it requires a statutory entitlement to receive the specific information. Second, when a plaintiff’s asserted injury depends on discretionary choices by government officials (especially those not parties to the lawsuit, or discretionary elements within the defendant agency’s control), both causation and redressability raise difficult jurisdictional obstacles. This opinion illustrates how courts police the boundary between generalized procedural grievances and concrete, redressable injuries necessary to invoke federal judicial power.
