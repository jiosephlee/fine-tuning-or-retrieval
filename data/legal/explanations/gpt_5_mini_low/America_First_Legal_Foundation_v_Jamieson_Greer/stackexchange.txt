Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Does § 1216(a)(3)’s "shall … conduct an investigation" create a judicially enforceable duty, or is it still committed to OSC’s enforcement discretion under Chaney?
Question:
The statute says OSC "shall … conduct an investigation" of arbitrary or capricious FOIA withholdings. That sounds mandatory. Yet the opinion leans on Heckler v. Chaney and treats OSC’s choices as discretionary for causation/redressability, and the district court also said § 1216(c) committed the decision to OSC’s discretion. How do courts reconcile mandatory language in § 1216(a)(3) with traditional nonreviewability of enforcement discretion? Would a § 706(1) APA claim (to "compel agency action unlawfully withheld") or mandamus ever lie to force at least the initiation of an investigation, even if remedies (corrective/disciplinary action) remain discretionary?
Answer:
Short answer
Textually, §1216(a)(3) uses mandatory language—“shall … conduct an investigation”—but the D.C. Circuit treated the OSC’s enforcement choices as functionally discretionary for standing/cause-and-redress purposes. The court did not finally decide whether §1216 creates a judicially enforceable duty because AFL lacked Article III standing; instead it relied on Chaney-type principles to explain why any causal chain from an order compelling OSC to the plaintiff’s FOIA relief would be too speculative to support redressability. That means a §706(1) APA claim or mandamus could still succeed in a different case if (and only if) the plaintiff can show a clear, non‑discretionary statutory duty and concrete, redressable injury — but the opinion makes plain that courts will resist compelling ordinary enforcement choices absent such a showing.

Why the opinion comes out this way (doctrine tied to the opinion)
- The opinion acknowledges the plain text: §1216(a)(3) “shall … conduct an investigation.” But the court’s analysis rests on standing and redressability, not on a merits holding that the duty is non‑discretionary. The court expressly declined to resolve whether AFL’s asserted injuries were procedural or substantive because AFL failed even the relaxed standing rules for procedural claims (slip op. II).
- On causation and redressability the court invoked the Chaney principle: “[i]t requires speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel. See Heckler v. Chaney” (slip op. II, ¶ explaining causal chain). The court treated the requested relief—forcing OSC to investigate—as one link in a long, speculative chain involving independent actors (OSC, MSPB, DOJ), and concluded that a favorable judgment here would not “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” AFL’s informational injury.
- The court also relied on existing precedent that OSC has discretion about whether to follow §1214 procedures when acting under §1216(c) (citing Carson). That precedent supports the idea that aspects of OSC’s investigatory process are discretionary even where §1216(a)(3) uses “shall.”

What this means for §706(1)/mandamus claims
- Governing rule: Courts will enforce a statutory duty under the APA mandamus framework only where the statute imposes a specific, nondiscretionary obligation that is not committed to agency discretion by law. Chaney teaches that prosecutorial/enforcement decisions are presumptively committed to agency discretion and hence nonreviewable unless Congress has clearly commanded otherwise.
- Practical test, as applied in this opinion: a plaintiff seeking relief under §706(1) or mandamus must (1) identify a clear, ministerial statutory duty that leaves no room for agency judgment; and (2) establish Article III standing (concrete injury that would be redressed by the court’s order). If satisfying neither element (as AFL failed), relief will be denied.
- The D.C. Circuit left open the possibility that mandatory language could be enforced in some circumstances. But in this case the combination of (a) the enforcement/prosecutorial context, (b) precedent treating parts of §1216/§1214 interplay as discretionary (Carson), and (c) the speculative chain needed to get AFL its FOIA records led the court to treat OSC’s choices as functionally discretionary for standing/redress purposes.

Bottom line for future litigants / exam answer
- Don’t assume “shall” automatically yields a judicially enforceable duty in the enforcement/prosecutorial context. Courts will (i) look to whether the statute clearly removes agency discretion, and (ii) insist on a concrete, redressable injury before ordering an agency to act.
- A §706(1) or mandamus claim could succeed to compel at least initiation of an investigation if the plaintiff can show (a) the statute unambiguously imposes a nondiscretionary duty to act, and (b) a direct, non‑speculative causal link to a redressable injury. The America First Legal decision shows that in the absence of those showings (and given Chaney and Carson), courts will treat OSC’s investigatory choices as effectively discretionary and dismiss for lack of standing or nonreviewability.

### Why isn’t AFL’s lack of FOIA records an injury traceable to OSC for standing purposes?
Question:
AFL says OSC’s refusal to investigate DOJ’s alleged policy caused AFL not to receive FOIA records. The court says the injury is traceable to DOJ, not OSC, and the causal chain through OSC action, MSPB sanctions, and DOJ compliance is too speculative. I’m struggling to see why this fails even under the "relaxed" procedural-injury framework. If § 1216 exists to deter arbitrary FOIA withholdings, why doesn’t the absence of an OSC investigation count as a cause of the ongoing withholding, at least for redressability? Where exactly do cases like Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine and Hecate Energy draw the line on third-party causation in this setting?
Answer:
Short answer
Because AFL’s asserted injury (not getting DOJ FOIA records) is caused by DOJ’s withholding, not OSC’s declination, the causal link required for Article III is too speculative. Even under the “relaxed” procedural-injury framework, a procedural-right violation must have actually resulted in a concrete, traceable injury that a favorable ruling would likely redress. The court treats the relevant causal chain here (OSC investigates → MSPB disciplines → DOJ produces records) as contingent on independent actors and prosecutorial discretion, so it fails both causation and redressability.

Why the court rejects AFL’s traceability/redressability theory (point-by-point, grounded in the opinion)
1) The basic standing rule applied. Article III requires (among other things) that the plaintiff’s injury be caused by the defendant and likely to be redressed by a favorable decision. Lujan; Steel Co. The D.C. Circuit applied that baseline even while discussing the relaxed standard for procedural-right cases (citing Summers and the Circuit’s relaxed-procedural-injury precedents).

2) Procedural-right relaxation doesn’t eliminate causation/redressability. The court reiterated that the relaxed approach eases (but does not eliminate) imminence and redressability; it does not eliminate the need to show that the procedural-violation produced a concrete injury or that relief would likely redress it. Ctr. for Law & Educ.; Fla. Audubon.

3) The asserted informational injury is traceable to DOJ, not OSC. AFL’s FOIA injury (no production of records) is “plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC.” The complaint is ultimately that DOJ withheld records; OSC’s refusal to investigate is at best an upstream event, not the proximate cause.

4) The causal chain is too speculative. The court mapped AFL’s needed chain: OSC would investigate under §1216 → OSC would bring corrective or disciplinary proceedings under §§1214/1215 → the MSPB would sanction → DOJ would produce the records. Each link depends on the discretionary choices of multiple agencies not before the court (OSC’s enforcement discretion; MSPB’s decision to sanction; DOJ’s compliance). The court treated that as precisely the sort of contingent, attenuated causation barred by Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine (FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367 (2024))—you can’t rely on “speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.”

5) Prosecutorial/enforcement discretion matters. The court emphasized that compelling OSC to investigate would still require courts to second-guess enforcement discretion (Heckler v. Chaney), which generally is nonjusticiable. Even if §1216 created a mandatory investigatory duty, the later choice to pursue corrective or disciplinary action and the MSPB’s response remain discretionary and uncertain.

6) Redressability focuses on what a judgment would bind. The court noted that redressability “generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale ‘might persuade actors who are not before the court.’” It cited Haaland v. Brackeen for that principle and Hecate Energy for the requirement that relief be likely to spur the next steps necessary to redress the injury. Because a favorable decision against OSC wouldn’t bind DOJ or the MSPB to act in the required way, redressability fails.

7) The second (informational) theory fails too. Even if OSC had to investigate under §1216, AFL has no statutory entitlement to receive the investigatory notices that §1214 would provide; courts have treated whether §1214 procedures apply to §1216 investigations as discretionary (Carson). Without a statute entitling AFL to the information, there’s no concrete informational injury (Akins, Pub. Citizen, ASPCA cited by the court).

Where Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine and Hecate Energy draw the line
- Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine (All. for Hippocratic Med.) stands for the proposition that standing cannot rest on speculative chains that depend on independent third parties’ discretionary decisions; courts will not infer causation/redressability from such contingencies.
- Hecate Energy reinforces that a plaintiff must show a favorable judgment would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the injury; a speculative sequence of independent agency choices is insufficient.

Put another way: if redress requires action by agencies not before the court (or multiple discretionary acts by independent actors), then the plaintiff cannot show that the defendant’s conduct caused the injury in the sense Article III requires, nor that court-ordered relief against the defendant would likely remedy it.

Bottom line
AFL’s claim collapses because the injury it complains of (DOJ’s withholding of FOIA records) is not the sort of concrete, traceable harm that flows directly from OSC’s declination. The intervening, discretionary decisions by OSC, the MSPB, and DOJ render causation and redressability speculative, and that defeats standing even under the relaxed procedural-injury framework the court described.

### Can a requester claim informational standing to receive § 1214 notices when OSC investigates under § 1216?
Question:
The panel says § 1216 doesn’t entitle AFL to any information, and that § 1214’s notice provisions don’t automatically apply to § 1216 investigations. But § 1216(c) says OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." Doesn’t "in the same way" import § 1214’s procedures, including complainant notices? How do Carson v. OSC (6th Cir.) and any D.C. Circuit precedent treat whether § 1214 procedural entitlements travel along when § 1216 is the predicate? If they’re discretionary, what text or precedent makes them discretionary rather than mandatory?
Answer:
Short answer: No—under the panel’s reasoning AFL could not establish informational standing to compel OSC to give it the § 1214-style notices when OSC acts under § 1216. The court treated the § 1214 notice rules as not automatically or mandatorily “carried over” by § 1216(c), and it held that even if OSC must investigate under § 1216(a)(3), § 1216 does not itself create an entitlement to receive information that would support informational standing.

Why (doctrinally and as the opinion explains)

- Informational-standing rule. The D.C. Circuit applies the familiar line from Akins, Pub. Citizen, Zivotofsky, and Feld Ent.: a plaintiff suffers a cognizable “informational” injury only when a statute entitles it to receive specific information and the denial of that entitlement deprives it of the information. The opinion cites Feld Ent. and Zivotofsky for that rule (Op. § II).

- § 1216(c) language. Section 1216(c) says OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” The panel did not read that phrase to create an automatic private right to the § 1214 procedural notices. Instead, the court observed that AFL itself conceded that OSC’s decision whether to follow § 1214’s procedural requirements when acting under § 1216 is treated as discretionary in at least some precedent (citing Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487 (6th Cir. 2011)). The panel therefore concluded § 1216 does not on its face entitle AFL to receive § 1214 notices and information (Op. § II).

- Discretion v. entitlement. Because informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to information, the court held AFL had no cognizable informational injury from lack of § 1214 notices. AFL’s own concession that following § 1214’s procedures in § 1216 investigations is discretionary undermined its claim that § 1216 creates a mandatory right to notice. The opinion also tied this to redressability: even if the court ordered OSC to investigate, the chain by which that would produce the FOIA records or § 1214 notices depended on speculative, independent decisions by OSC, the MSPB, or DOJ—precisely the kind of speculation that defeats redressability (Op. §§ II–III; cites Heckler v. Chaney logic).

- Precedent relied on. The panel relied on: the informational-standing cases (Akins, Pub. Citizen, Zivotofsky, Feld Ent.), Summers for the limits on procedural-right standing, and Carson for the proposition (as AFL recognized) that OSC’s application of § 1214 procedures to § 1216 investigations is discretionary. The opinion also invokes Heckler v. Chaney and subsequent standing/redressability principles to explain why speculative chains of enforcement do not confer standing (Op. §§ II–III).

Putting it together for the specific question
- The clause “in the same way” in § 1216(c) does not, according to the panel, automatically import § 1214’s notice-and-information entitlements as mandatory rights enforceable by third parties. The court treated those § 1214 procedures as something OSC may follow in § 1216 cases, not something that gives private parties a statutory right to information that can ground Article III standing.
- Carson (6th Cir.) is the principal on-point decision the opinion cites for the proposition that whether OSC applies § 1214’s procedures in § 1216 investigations is discretionary. The D.C. Circuit opinion rests on the same practical legal effect: absent a clear statutory entitlement to information, there is no informational injury for standing purposes.
- Text and precedent making the entitlements discretionary rather than mandatory: the opinion relies on (1) the statutory formulation of § 1216(c) using “may … in the same way,” which the panel read as permissive rather than creating a new private right; (2) Carson’s holding that OSC’s application of § 1214 procedures is discretionary in § 1216 investigations; and (3) the Supreme Court and D.C. Circuit standing and prosecutorial-discretion precedents (Heckler, Summers, Lujan) showing courts cannot remedy speculative enforcement choices by other agencies or compel the exercise of prosecutorial discretion.

Net practical effect for a requester
- If you want to claim informational standing to force OSC to give you § 1214 notices when OSC is operating under § 1216, America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer says you’re on weak ground: § 1216 does not plainly create the statutory entitlement to information that the informational-standing line of cases requires, and the relevant precedents treat the application of § 1214 procedures in § 1216 cases as discretionary rather than mandatory.

### How do § 552(a)(4)(F) referrals under FOIA interact with OSC’s independent § 1216 authority?
Question:
The district court held that OSC misread its authority by insisting that § 552(a)(4)(F) predicates (a court order, fees, and a finding questioning arbitrariness) were necessary to investigate, because § 1216 independently authorizes investigations. The D.C. Circuit didn’t reach the merits due to standing. For future cases, what’s the correct statutory relationship: is § 552(a)(4)(F) an additional, mandatory trigger in some circumstances, while § 1216 provides a separate, discretionary or mandatory authority otherwise? Is there any case law or OSC practice guidance that reconciles these provisions?
Answer:
Short answer
- The most textually and practically coherent reading is that the FOIA referral provision, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), is a specific, mandatory referral mechanism that kicks in when three narrow judicial predicates are met (a court orders production, awards fees, and specifically questions arbitrariness).  
- 5 U.S.C. § 1216 is a separate, independent grant of OSC authority that “shall … conduct an investigation” of alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings; it therefore can require OSC to investigate even when the §552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates are not satisfied.  
- That does not mean §1216 automatically imports all §1214 procedural protections; courts (and OSC practice) treat application of §1214 procedures to §1216 investigations as discretionary. The D.C. district court reached this reconciliation (Kerner), the D.C. Circuit in America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer did not reach the merits because of standing, and the Sixth Circuit in Carson is consistent on the point about §1214 procedures.

Why this is the correct statutory relationship (doctrine and statutory text)
- Two statutes: FOIA’s enforcement-referral clause, §552(a)(4)(F)(i), and OSC’s investigatory authority for FOIA matters, §1216(a)(3). They are addressing related conduct (arbitrary FOIA withholdings) but in different ways.  
  - §552(a)(4)(F)(i) says: if a court (1) orders production, (2) awards attorneys’ fees, and (3) specifically questions whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously, then OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding” to determine disciplinary liability. That language is mandatory and conditioned on discrete judicial findings—it is a clear, statutory trigger for a mandatory OSC response in those circumstances.  
  - §1216(a)(3) instructs OSC that it “shall … conduct an investigation” of allegations of arbitrary withholdings under FOIA and authorizes OSC to use §1214 and §1215 mechanisms “in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved” (see §1216(c)). The “shall conduct an investigation” language is a general duty to investigate allegations of arbitrary FOIA withholdings.

- Canon of construction: a specific statute creating a mandatory referral when particular judicial findings are present (the §552 mechanism) coexists with a general statutory duty to investigate similar conduct (§1216). Reading §552(a)(4)(F)(i) to be the exclusive means by which OSC can investigate FOIA withholding allegations would render §1216’s separate “shall … conduct an investigation” language superfluous. The district court in Kerner adopted that nonexclusive reading (Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5), and that is the better textual reconciliation.

Practical implication and limits
- §552(a)(4)(F)(i) is an additional, mandatory trigger: when a court meets those three statutory predicates, OSC has a compelled obligation to initiate a proceeding to determine disciplinary action. That provision thus creates a specific, statutorily-created obligation that supplements OSC’s general duties.  
- §1216 supplies independent authority (and in language, a duty) to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings even where no court has made the §552 predicates. But §1216 does not necessarily compel OSC to adopt every procedural step that §1214 prescribes; circuits have held that whether OSC applies §1214’s procedural requirements in a §1216 investigation is in significant respects discretionary (see Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011), cited in the D.C. Circuit opinion). The district court in Kerner likewise treated the decision to apply §1214’s notice-and-procedure provisions in a §1216 matter as committed to OSC’s discretion.

Authority and practice guidance
- America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer (D.C. Cir. Oct. 3, 2025): the D.C. Circuit described both statutory provisions and agreed with the district court’s reading that §552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC’s power under §1216—but the court did not decide the merits because AFL lacked standing. Thus the D.C. Circuit left the Kerner statutory reconciliation intact without adopting or rejecting further doctrinal refinements. Key points in the opinion:
  - It quotes and contrasts §1216(a)(3) (“shall … conduct an investigation”) and §552(a)(4)(F)(i)’s three judicial predicates.  
  - It cites Carson for the proposition that application of §1214 procedural requirements to §1216 investigations is discretionary.
- Kerner (D.D.C. 2023): the district court held §552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC’s §1216 authority and remanded because it viewed §1216(c) as committing certain choices to OSC’s discretion. The D.C. Circuit did not disturb that statutory reading for reasons of standing.
- Carson (6th Cir. 2011): held that OSC’s decision whether to follow §1214’s procedural requirements in a §1216 investigation is discretionary—useful on the procedural-question point.

Bottom line for future cases
- If a litigant wants to force OSC to act, the clearest path is to obtain the specific judicial findings that trigger §552(a)(4)(F)(i): an order of production, an award of fees, and a specific judicial statement questioning arbitrariness. That will obligate OSC to “promptly initiate” a proceeding under the statute.  
- Absent those three judicial predicates, §1216 gives OSC independent authority—and arguably a statutory duty—to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings, but courts have treated aspects of how OSC conducts §1216 investigations (including whether to apply §1214 procedures) as matters of agency discretion. Because the D.C. Circuit in America First did not resolve the merits, there remains no binding D.C. Circuit holding that §1216 creates an enforceable, specific procedural entitlement to particular investigatory steps beyond OSC’s discretionary choices.

If you’re briefing or litigating this question, focus on:
- Textual split between the narrow, mandatory §552(a)(4)(F)(i) trigger and the broader §1216 duty;  
- Evidence of Congress’s intent (if any) to make §552’s referral exclusive (none apparent in these opinions); and  
- Precedent on agency discretion to shape procedure in §1216 investigations (Carson and Kerner are the most directly relevant decisions discussed in the D.C. Circuit opinion).

### Why didn’t the "relaxed" procedural standing doctrine save AFL’s claim here?
Question:
The opinion cites Center for Law & Education and Florida Audubon to say procedural injury relaxes imminence/redressability but not injury-in-fact or causation, and requires a threatened concrete interest. AFL claimed a procedural right to an investigation plus informational harms. The court said AFL lacked a statutory entitlement to information (for informational standing) and failed causation/redressability for the FOIA records theory. In practical terms, what would AFL have needed to allege to clear the injury and causation hurdles under the procedural-injury standard? For example, would a statutory text clearly guaranteeing requesters certain notices/information during § 1216 investigations have changed the analysis?
Answer:
Short answer
AFL lost because it alleged only a bare procedural duty and speculative downstream effects. To survive under the “relaxed” procedural‑injury line the court required (per Center for Law & Education and Florida Audubon), AFL had to plead (1) a threatened or actual concrete interest that the procedural statute was designed to protect (not a procedural right in vacuo) and (2) a non‑speculative causal and redressable link from OSC’s failure to investigate to the concrete harm. If section 1216 or some other statute actually (a) created a private entitlement to particular information or notices tied to OSC’s investigations, or (b) created a mandatory, non‑discretionary chain of consequences (so that an OSC investigation would predictably force DOJ or the MSPB to produce the records), then AFL’s standing story would look very different. The opinion itself makes these points: informational standing requires a statutory entitlement, and causation/redressability cannot rest on speculation about independent actors or discretionary enforcement. 

What the opinion requires (doctrine and the court’s application)
- The court applied the relaxed procedural‑injury framework but reiterated that relaxation affects only imminence/redressability, not injury‑in‑fact or causation. A plaintiff still must show a concrete, particularized interest harmed by the procedural violation (citing Summers and Center for Law & Education), and must show a causal chain that is not speculative (citing All. for Hippocratic Med., Haaland v. Brackeen).
- For informational standing, the court required that the statute actually entitle the plaintiff to receive the information (citing Feld Ent., Akins, Pub. Citizen). Because AFL could not point to a statutory entitlement to notices or investigation results under §1216 (and conceded §1214’s notice rules are discretionary as applied to §1216 investigations), its informational theories failed.
- For the FOIA‑records theory the court rejected AFL’s causation/redressability because the chain (OSC investigates → MSPB disciplines → DOJ produces records) depended on discretionary decisions by multiple independent actors and therefore was too speculative to support Article III standing.

What AFL would have needed to plead to survive standing
Below are concrete allegations (tied to the court’s reasoning) that would have strengthened AFL’s standing showing:

1) A statutory entitlement to information or notices
- Allegation: statutory text (in §1216 itself or a cross‑reference) expressly entitles FOIA requesters or complainants to specific notices, findings, or investigative reports when OSC investigates under §1216 (i.e., not merely permissive or discretionary).  
- Why this matters: the court said informational injury requires a right “to receive” the information. An express entitlement converts the withheld information from a discretionary benefit into a concrete, legally protected interest (cites Feld Ent./Akins logic in the opinion).

2) A non‑speculative causal link between OSC action and agency production
- Allegation: a statute, regulation, or binding MSPB rule makes certain remedial consequences automatic or mandatory upon an OSC finding (for example, an OSC finding of arbitrary withholding requires MSPB corrective orders that compel agency disclosure, or triggers a statutory duty on DOJ to reassess/produce responsive records). Alternatively, plead a consistent, non‑random historical pattern showing that OSC investigations in materially identical situations have reliably led to MSPB orders and then production.  
- Why this matters: the court rejected causal chains that required speculation about the discretionary choices of MSPB and DOJ. If the chain contains mandatory or reliably predictable steps, the court’s concern about speculation is neutralized.

3) A narrower, directly traceable injury
- Allegation: the denial of statutorily guaranteed investigatory notices or reports deprived AFL of a specific, concrete procedural benefit that the statute protects (e.g., opportunity to submit evidence, participate in remedies, or receive findings needed to challenge agency conduct in another forum).  
- Why this matters: Summers and the court require that procedural rights be tied to concrete interests. Showing how the lost notice concretely prevented AFL from protecting or vindicating an interest (not just “we want information”) addresses the injury‑in‑fact element.

4) Relief that would directly remedy the concrete injury
- Allegation: the relief sought (an order compelling OSC to investigate and provide specified notices or reports) would, as a legal matter, obligate OSC to provide the information and thereby redress AFL’s concrete injury—because the information is statutorily required and not subject to OSC discretion.  
- Why this matters: the opinion stresses redressability turns on whether a favorable judgment would likely spur the legally meaningful steps to remedy the injury; a court order directing compliance with a statutory entitlement accomplishes that.

Would a statutory text guaranteeing notices/information during §1216 investigations have changed the analysis?
Yes, in the way the opinion frames the problem. The court rejected AFL’s informational theory because §1216 as pleaded did not create a right to receive notices/information and because any attachment of §1214 procedures to §1216 was acknowledged to be discretionary. If Congress had written §1216 (or an implementing regulation) to create a clear, mandatory entitlement to specific notices, reports, or investigatory findings for complainants or FOIA requesters whenever OSC opens a §1216 investigation, then:

- AFL would have a concrete, particularized informational interest the statute was designed to protect (satisfying injury‑in‑fact under the court’s cited precedents); and
- A court order enforcing that statutory entitlement would directly redress the deprivation (avoiding the court’s reliance on speculative chains involving independent actors).

But the opinion also makes clear a second obstacle: causation/redressability targeting DOJ’s FOIA production. Even with an entitlement to OSC notices, AFL would still need to link OSC’s conduct to DOJ’s disclosure in a non‑speculative way. So a statutory entitlement that both (a) gives AFL a right to OSC investigatory findings/notice and (b) makes OSC findings trigger a non‑discretionary remedial pathway (e.g., mandatory MSPB review or an obligation on DOJ to act) would be the cleanest fix to the two deficiencies the court identified.

Bottom line
The court dismissed AFL because it alleged only a bare procedural duty plus speculative downstream effects. To survive, AFL needed to plead a statutory entitlement to the particular information or process it wanted and a non‑speculative, legally meaningful causal chain (or mandatory remedial mechanism) showing that an OSC investigation would very likely produce the records or notices that constitute AFL’s concrete injury. An express statutory guarantee of notices/information during §1216 investigations — especially coupled with a mandatory remedial mechanism tying OSC findings to agency or MSPB action — would have addressed the two defects the opinion identifies.

### How does Haaland v. Brackeen limit redressability based on a judgment’s persuasive effect on nonparties?
Question:
The panel says redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might persuade nonparties like DOJ or MSPB. Are there recognized exceptions where likely persuasive effects on nonparties can count toward redressability, or is Haaland establishing a categorical rule? How does that square with earlier cases that credited likelihoods of third-party reactions to government policies in assessing redressability?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel applies Haaland to hold that redressability cannot rest on the hope that a court’s reasoning will merely persuade nonparties (DOJ, MSPB) to act. That does not create a novel, categorical rule that persuasion can never count; it reiterates the settled principle that redressability requires a non‑speculative causal chain tying the plaintiff’s injury to relief the court can order. Where the next steps are independent, discretionary choices by nonparties, reliance on their likely persuasive reaction is too attenuated to establish standing.

Explanation (with doctrinal anchors from the opinion)
1) What the panel says Haaland means. The panel quotes Haaland for the proposition that redressability “turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale ‘might persuade actors who are not before the court.’” America First Legal Found. v. Jamieson Greer, slip op. § II (quoting Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023)). The panel uses Haaland to reject AFL’s claim that a judicial order requiring OSC to investigate would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” the FOIA injury—because those next steps (OSC bringing corrective/disciplinary proceedings, MSPB sanctioning, DOJ producing records) would require discretionary action by agencies not bound by the court’s judgment.

2) How this fits into the ordinary redressability test. The panel applies familiar Lujan principles: injury, causation, and redressability. It treats Haaland as reinforcing the point that redressability cannot rest on “speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” (citing FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024), and Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821 (1985)). The opinion also cites the relaxed standard for procedural-right claims but concludes AFL fails even that relaxed test because any chain from a court order to the production of records depends on multiple discretionary, independent choices.

3) Is Haaland a categorical bar on persuasive effects? No, the opinion does not treat Haaland as establishing an absolute, new rule that persuasive effects never count. Rather, it treats Haaland as a statement of the limiting principle: redressability requires that relief materially change the legal relations of parties or produce a non‑speculative, likely change in the challenged actor’s behavior. The panel contrasts two types of situations:
- Where a court’s judgment directly binds the defendant (or compels a change in legal status), redressability is straightforward.
- Where relief would merely create an argument that might influence third parties whose choices remain discretionary and independent, that chain is too speculative.

4) How this squares with earlier cases that credited third‑party reactions. The opinion implicitly distinguishes permissible reliance on third‑party reactions from impermissible speculation. Earlier precedents can treat likely third‑party responses as part of a non‑speculative causal chain when those responses are not truly independent or when the defendant’s change is itself binding and will force third‑party compliance. By contrast, in AFL’s case the chain required (OSC initiates → MSPB acts → DOJ produces records) involves multiple independent, discretionary actors and enforcement choices (including agency prosecutorial discretion under Heckler), so the court properly refuses to treat a mere persuasive effect as redress. The opinion also invokes the “very likely” formulation from Hecate Energy and All. for Hippocratic Med. to emphasize that the requisite likelihood must be concrete, not conjectural.

Practical rule of thumb for standing analysis (drawn from the opinion)
- Redressability is satisfied when a favorable judgment will directly change a party’s legal obligations or will predictably (not speculatively) force a change that cures the plaintiff’s injury.
- Redressability fails when it depends on discretionary acts by nonparties not bound by the court, where the causal chain involves multiple speculative steps and independent agencies exercising prosecutorial or enforcement discretion.

Conclusion
The panel uses Haaland as a limiting principle: courts should not base Article III redressability on hopes that their reasoning will persuade independent, discretionary actors to act. That is not an absolute prohibition on any consideration of third‑party reactions, but it does require plaintiffs to show a realistic, non‑speculative causal pathway by which the court’s order will, in fact, produce the relief sought. The America First opinion applies that rule to reject AFL’s standing because the needed sequence of discretionary steps by OSC, MSPB, and DOJ is too attenuated.

### What was the appropriate remedy once the district court found OSC’s legal error but also found discretion under § 1216(c)?
Question:
The district court said OSC erred by treating § 552(a)(4)(F) as a prerequisite but also concluded the decision to investigate was discretionary, so it remanded for reconsideration instead of ordering an investigation. Is remand the standard APA remedy under § 706(2) when an agency relies on a legal error in exercising discretion? Could the court have ordered limited relief, like requiring OSC to consider the complaint under the correct statutory framework but leaving ultimate investigatory action to OSC? How does this interact with § 706(1) claims to compel action unlawfully withheld?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — when a court finds an agency has relied on a legal error but the agency’s ultimate choice is committed to enforcement discretion, the usual remedy is to set aside the erroneous legal conclusion and remand the matter to the agency for reconsideration under the correct legal standard. A court can order the agency to apply the correct statutory framework (or vacate the legal ruling), but it generally cannot force the agency to exercise its prosecutorial/investigative discretion (i.e., to open an investigation) unless the statute creates a nondiscretionary duty to act.

Why (doctrine and how it appears in the opinion)
- The district court in this case did exactly that: it concluded OSC “erred by treating § 552(a)(4)(F) as a prerequisite,” but because § 1216(c) left the decision to OSC’s discretion the court “declined to order OSC to investigate” and instead remanded “for further consideration.” See Kerner, No. 22‑cv‑3576, 2023 WL 9546644, at *5–*6 (quoted and discussed in the opinion). That is the classic relief when legal error is found but the agency retains discretionary authority.
- The opinion also relies on Heckler v. Chaney’s teaching that courts generally may not compel an agency to exercise its enforcement discretion. The court explained that compelling OSC to investigate would require second‑guessing enforcement choices and would rest on “speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel.” (Slip op. III.A, citing Heckler.) That explains why remand (rather than an order to investigate) was the chosen remedy.

What a court can and cannot order
- What the court can order: courts routinely set aside an agency’s action or reasoning that is legally incorrect and remand with instructions to consider the matter under the correct legal standard. That can include directing the agency to reconsider the complaint “under the correct statutory framework” and to explain any discretionary decision reached thereafter. Such relief corrects the legal error and preserves the agency’s role in weighing enforcement priorities and facts.
- What the court cannot (ordinarily) order absent a nondiscretionary duty: a court may not, consistent with Heckler, force an agency to initiate investigations or prosecutions when the statute leaves that choice to agency judgment. If the statute imposed a mandatory, nondiscretionary duty to act (one that leaves no room for agency judgment), then a plaintiff could pursue relief under APA § 706(1) to “compel agency action unlawfully withheld.” But in this case the district court read § 1216(c) as preserving OSC’s discretion, and the panel emphasized that speculative chains of enforcement and remedies through other agencies (MSPB, DOJ) cannot supply redressability or a basis to compel OSC to act.

Interaction with APA § 706(1) v. § 706(2)
- § 706(2) remedies: when an agency has acted unlawfully (for example, legal error in its reasoning), courts typically set aside that action and remand for reconsideration under the correct law — that is a § 706(2) style remedy (set aside/hold unlawful and remand). The district court’s remand fits that mode.
- § 706(1) remedies: that provision can be used to compel agency action unlawfully withheld where the statute imposes a nondiscretionary duty to act. If a plaintiff could show OSC had a clear, mandatory statutory duty to investigate and had unlawfully withheld that action, § 706(1) relief (an order to perform the duty) could be appropriate. But the opinion rejects that posture here because § 1216(c) was treated as discretionary and because compelling OSC to investigate would rest on speculative downstream effects and choices by independent actors (MSPB, DOJ), undercutting standing and the basis for a § 706(1) order.

Bottom line
Remand for reconsideration under the correct legal standard is the standard and appropriate remedy where a court finds legal error but the statute leaves the enforcement decision to the agency. A court can order the agency to reconsider and to apply the correct statutory framework (and can vacate the erroneous reasoning), but it normally cannot compel the agency to open an investigation unless the statute imposes a nondiscretionary duty to do so. The Kerner/district‑court handling described in the opinion exemplifies that approach, and the panel’s discussion of standing and Heckler explains why an order forcing OSC to investigate would have been inappropriate here.

### Does parallel FOIA litigation and rolling production undermine standing to force OSC action?
Question:
The opinion notes DOJ is producing records in AFL’s FOIA suit and the sufficiency of that production isn’t at issue here. Does the fact that AFL has an independent avenue for relief against DOJ (with actual production underway) defeat or diminish injury/redressability for claims seeking to compel OSC’s investigation? Would the standing analysis differ if DOJ had closed the FOIA request with no production, or if a court had already found an improper withholding but denied fees so § 552(a)(4)(F) wasn’t triggered?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. As the D.C. Circuit explained, parallel FOIA litigation that is producing records (or even a mere refusal by DOJ to produce) means any informational injury is traceable to DOJ, not OSC, and the causal chain from a court order forcing OSC to investigate to DOJ’s production is too speculative to support Article III standing. Only when the three FOIA predicates in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) are satisfied — i.e., a court orders production, awards fees, and questions arbitrary withholding — does FOIA itself compel OSC to act, and that statutory hook would materially change the redressability analysis.

Why — the governing legal doctrine (as the opinion applies it)
- Article III requires an injury in fact that is caused by the defendant and likely to be redressed by a favorable judgment. Lujan; Steel Co.
- Procedural-right claims get a relaxed view of imminence/redressability only when (and to the extent) the procedural statute is designed to protect a threatened concrete interest; but even then plaintiffs must allege a concrete injury and a non-speculative causal link and redressability. Summers; Ctr. for Law & Educ.; Fla. Audubon.
- Informational standing requires an entitlement to the information under statute. Akins; ASPCA v. Feld Ent.; Zivotofsky.

How the court applied those doctrines in this opinion
- The court rejected AFL’s attempt to treat OSC’s refusal to investigate as the cause of its FOIA nonproduction. Any informational injury from DOJ’s withholding is traceable to DOJ, not OSC; the asserted causal chain (OSC investigates → MSPB sanctions → DOJ produces) depends on multiple independent actors and speculative exercises of enforcement discretion, so it fails causation and redressability. The opinion cites the principle that causation cannot rest on speculation about independent actors and Heckler v. Chaney’s rule against judicially compelling agency enforcement choices.
- The court also rejected “informational standing” insofar as AFL lacked a statutory entitlement to receive investigative information. Section 1216(a)(3) does not themselves create a right to the section 1214 notice/information AFL sought, and prior cases treat the application of section 1214’s procedural rules to section 1216 investigations as discretionary. Thus AFL had no concrete informational entitlement that a court order compelling OSC to investigate would necessarily vindicate.

Applying that reasoning to your hypotheticals
- Parallel FOIA suit with rolling production (the actual scenario in the opinion): The court held this undermines standing. The production (or the FOIA dispute) is traceable to DOJ; a victory compelling OSC would not be “very likely” to cause production because it would require discretionary enforcement steps by OSC and MSPB and then remedial choices by DOJ. So AFL lacked standing.
- If DOJ had closed the FOIA request with no production: The opinion’s reasoning focuses on traceability and speculative causation from OSC to DOJ; those same problems would persist. The injury still stems from DOJ’s nondisclosure, and any judicial compulsion of OSC would rely on the same attenuated chain of third-party decisions. So, under the opinion’s analysis, standing would likely still be lacking.
- If a court had already found improper withholding but denied fees (so § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) wasn’t triggered): The opinion draws a clear line about FOIA’s separate enforcement provision. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) creates an occasion where OSC “shall promptly initiate” proceedings when a court (1) orders production, (2) awards fees, and (3) specifically questions arbitrariness. When those predicates are met, FOIA itself compels OSC to act — a much more direct, non-speculative redress link than AFL had here. By contrast, where those predicates are not satisfied (as in your example), OSC’s action remains discretionary and the same standing defects identified in the opinion persist.

Bottom line
Under the court’s reasoning, parallel FOIA litigation (with production underway or with defendants refusing to produce) generally undermines a plaintiff’s Article III standing to sue solely to force OSC to investigate because the injury is traceable to DOJ and any redress via OSC would be speculative. The situation changes only if the statutory FOIA triggers in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) are satisfied, because then FOIA itself compels OSC to initiate proceedings and the redressability/cause-of-action hook is direct rather than attenuated.
