Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

## Procedural Rights Without Remedies: The D.C. Circuit Tightens Standing in OSC–FOIA Disputes

Why this matters: The D.C. Circuit’s opinion in America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer (Oct. 3, 2025) is not a long doctrinal treatise, but it is consequential. On its face the case concerns a narrow statutory dispute over the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) investigatory obligations under 5 U.S.C. § 1216. At bottom, however, the court issued a stand-alone Article III decision that narrows the avenues by which FOIA requesters, public-interest groups, and other watchdogs can convert asserted procedural wrongs into a cognizable federal lawsuit. The panel assumes, for argument’s sake, that § 1216 may require OSC to investigate allegedly arbitrary FOIA withholdings; still, it holds that America First Legal Foundation (AFL) lacks standing because it cannot show a concrete, traceable, and redressable injury. That holding tightens the limits on using downstream enforcement mechanisms to bootstrap informational injuries into Article III claims.

What the court decided, in brief: AFL sought an OSC investigation into what it described as DOJ’s policy of not searching employee emails without employee consent—as a way to police alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings. The district court thought OSC had misread the statutory text and remanded for OSC to reconsider, but ultimately dismissed when OSC again declined to open an investigation. On appeal the D.C. Circuit did not reach the merits. Instead, it ordered supplemental briefing on standing and concluded AFL failed to show Article III injury. The panel rejected both of AFL’s informational-injury theories: (1) that OSC’s failure to investigate deprived AFL of the underlying FOIA records; and (2) that OSC’s refusal denied AFL the informational benefits it would receive from an OSC probe itself (notably, notices and updates comparable to the § 1214 process). The court vacated the district-court judgment and directed dismissal for lack of jurisdiction.

How the court reasoned: The opinion is doctrinally methodical. It starts from the familiar Lujan framework—injury-in-fact, causation, and redressability—and the Summers principle that mere deprivation of a procedural right, standing alone, is insufficient. The panel applies the “relaxed” approach applicable where a procedural statute is designed to protect a threatened concrete interest, but emphasizes that the relaxation does not dissolve the need for a concrete injury or a credible causal link to redress.

On AFL’s first theory (the underlying FOIA production), the court found causation and redressability wanting. Any failure to produce records is traceable to DOJ’s decision-making, not OSC’s inaction. To get from OSC’s non-investigation to production would require a speculative chain: OSC might investigate; it might seek corrective or disciplinary action under §§1214–1215; the MSPB might impose sanctions; DOJ might then change its practices and produce records. The court relied on recent Supreme Court authority rejecting standing theories that hinge on the independent choices of non-party actors—chiefly FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine and Haaland v. Brackeen—and analogous D.C. Circuit decisions such as Hecate Energy. The panel also drew on Heckler v. Chaney to underscore that courts generally may not compel executive enforcement decisions. The upshot: the causal chain is too attenuated and uncertain to support Article III standing.

On AFL’s second theory (entitlement to information from an OSC probe), the court asked whether § 1216 creates a statutory entitlement to investigatory notices or other information. Following Amer. Soc’y for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Enterprises and Akins, the court observed that informational standing requires a statute that actually grants the plaintiff the right to receive the information. Here, even if § 1216 mandates investigations of FOIA-withholding allegations, it does not, in the panel’s view, confer upon a FOIA requester a right to the procedural notices that accompany § 1214 investigations. AFL conceded that OSC has discretion whether to adopt § 1214-like procedures in § 1216 probes and pointed to precedents allowing OSC flexibility (e.g., Carson), but could not identify a statutory entitlement to notices. Without that entitlement, AFL lacked the requisite concrete informational injury and, further, could not show that a court order compelling OSC to investigate would necessarily deliver the informational benefits AFL sought.

What to take away — doctrinal implications and downstream effects: The decision has three practical and doctrinal teeth.

First, it curtails a potential litigation strategy: using an OSC enforcement pathway as a backdoor to obtain FOIA records. Plaintiffs and watchdog groups who hope to vindicate FOIA interests by forcing a third-party enforcement process into motion will now confront a heightened standing gate. Where the requested relief depends on a chain of discretionary decisions across multiple agencies or tribunals, courts are likely to treat the causal link as too speculative.

Second, the opinion underscores the limits of the relaxed procedural-rights framework. The D.C. Circuit reiterates that the “relaxed” approach does not license plaintiffs to sue on procedural complaints divorced from a concrete interest or realistic redress. That message will matter in other contexts where plaintiffs rely on statutory investigatory processes (inspectors general, independent counsels, other enforcement offices) to obtain information or remedial outcomes.

Third, the case highlights the importance of statutory text that creates explicit private entitlements to information. Akins-style informational standing remains viable, but only where Congress has conferred a right to receive specific information. Absent such a textual hook, courts will treat informational claims skeptically.

A final, practical point: the opinion may push litigants toward different strategies. Where standing is precarious, requesters may prefer to litigate directly under FOIA against the agency that controls the records and whose actions directly affect disclosure. Or they may seek § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) relief—the explicit FOIA-triggered OSC enforcement provision that ties OSC action to a judicial finding of improper withholding and attorneys’ fees—rather than relying on the more attenuated § 1216 route.

Conclusion: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer is a crisp reminder that not all procedural rights create judicially cognizable injuries. The D.C. Circuit accepts, for argument’s sake, that OSC has an investigatory role under § 1216, but it insists that Article III requires a concrete, traceable, and likely redressable injury before federal courts may intervene. For watchdogs and FOIA practitioners, the practical lesson is straightforward: when your claim to information depends on persuading a chain of enforcement actors to act, Article III may very well be the first hurdle you cannot clear.



## The Merits That Weren’t: Unresolved Tensions Between § 1216, § 552(a)(4)(F), and § 1214

Why this case matters

America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer was litigated as a straightforward fight over whether the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) must investigate alleged FOIA-withholdings on a plaintiff’s demand. The district court concluded OSC had erred as a matter of law in treating FOIA’s post-litigation disciplinary trigger—§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—as the only path into OSC’s investigatory authority for FOIA matters. But the D.C. Circuit never reached that statutory question: it vacated on Article III standing grounds and sent the case back for dismissal. That procedural end leaves important doctrinal questions unresolved about how three statutory provisions—5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), and § 1214—fit together. Those questions will matter for practitioners and affected requesters because they determine when OSC must act, what process it must follow if it does, and how much courts can compel when they conclude OSC misconstrued its duties.

What the court actually decided (and what it didn’t)

At bottom, the court’s narrow holding is jurisdictional: AFL lacked Article III standing. The panel accepted the basic premise that OSC has some role in policing FOIA withholdings (a role rooted in the Civil Service Reform Act and § 1216’s explicit reference to FOIA-withholdings), but it concluded that AFL’s pleaded injuries were too attenuated from OSC’s refusal to investigate to establish causation and redressability. The panel gave two reasons the asserted informational injuries fell short. First, any FOIA nonproduction injury traces principally to DOJ, not OSC, and the speculative chain by which an OSC investigation might ultimately induce DOJ to produce records depended on intervening actors and discretionary choices (OSC enforcement choices, MSPB action, DOJ’s decisions). Second, AFL claimed only an informational entitlement to whatever notices OSC might give under § 1214 if OSC treated a § 1216 investigation like a § 1214 matter—but § 1216 does not itself create a right to those § 1214 notices, and controlling precedent suggests OSC’s choice to apply § 1214 procedures in a § 1216 probe is discretionary. Because AFL could not clear even the relaxed standing regime for procedural-right claims, the court vacated and ordered dismissal for lack of jurisdiction. The panel explicitly declined to resolve the core merits question left open by the district court.

The statutory puzzle the court left unresolved

Three statutory provisions sit at the heart of this dispute, and their relationship remains ambiguous after the decision:

- § 1216(a)(3) provides that the OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” The mandatory “shall” is the linchpin of AFL’s claim: it suggests an unqualified duty to investigate FOIA-withholding allegations.

- FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) establishes a different-looking enforcement route: if a court orders production, awards fees, and “specifically question[s]” whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously in withholding, OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding” to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the responsible officer or employee. That formulation is explicitly tied to outcomes in litigation and imposes a post-litigation disciplinary trigger.

- § 1214 prescribes procedures for OSC investigations and remedial processes when OSC treats misconduct as a prohibited personnel practice; it also contains notice and progress-reporting provisions that serve as the source for AFL’s claimed informational entitlements.

There are at least three plausible ways to read the statutory scheme:

1. § 1216(a)(3) independently requires OSC to investigate any allegation of arbitrary FOIA withholding on receipt of an allegation, without regard to FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F) post-judgment triggers. Under this view, the “shall” in § 1216(a)(3) is mandatory and broad; § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) provides an additional, litigation-contingent enforcement mechanism but does not cabin OSC’s non-litigation investigatory duties.

2. § 1216(a)(3) is best read in light of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i): FOIA’s detailed post-judgment language reflects Congress’s intent to channel OSC’s formal disciplinary proceedings to situations where a court has already found wrongful withholding. On this account, § 1216(a)(3) authorizes investigations but does not create an independent, enforceable obligation to initiate them absent the FOIA-specified triggers.

3. A hybrid approach treats § 1216(a)(3) as imposing a duty to consider or screen allegations but leaves substantial discretion to OSC about whether to open a full § 1214-style investigative proceeding and whether to apply § 1214’s procedures. Under this reading, § 1216(c)’s cross-reference to §§ 1214 and 1215 supplies the mechanism OSC may use—but it does not automatically import every § 1214 procedural entitlement into every § 1216 investigation.

How prior precedent and the district court framed the issue

The district court read the statutes in the first (independent duty) vein: it concluded § 1216(a)(3)’s “shall conduct” language did not collapse into FOIA’s narrower § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) trigger. That conclusion turned on the plain statutory cross-references and the different functions the provisions serve—§ 1216(a)(3) as a general investigatory mandate; § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) as an explicit enforcement requirement tied to litigation outcomes. The district court nevertheless declined to order OSC to apply § 1214’s procedures in any § 1216 investigation, viewing § 1216(c) as leaving some procedural choices to OSC. In other words, the district court believed OSC had misread the scope of its investigatory duty, but it also thought courts should stop short of dictating how OSC must run every investigation.

By contrast, the Sixth Circuit’s Carson decision (cited in the D.C. Circuit opinion) has been read to allow OSC discretion about whether to follow § 1214’s specific procedural requirements in § 1216 investigations. That line of authority suggests at least some procedural nonportability: an OSC decision not to apply § 1214’s notice scheme during a § 1216 probe is not necessarily reviewable as a categorical denial of statutory process.

Where courts might go next

Because the D.C. Circuit disposed of the case on standing grounds, lower courts and litigants are left without a controlling clarification of how these provisions interact. Future litigation will likely test three related questions.

First, what operational force does § 1216(a)(3)’s “shall conduct an investigation” actually have? A plaintiff seeking to compel OSC to investigate should craft pleading allegations that make causation and redressability plausible—focusing on an injury traceable to OSC’s inaction rather than an injury traceable to the underlying agency (DOJ in AFL’s case). For example, a plaintiff whose only need is to obtain the specific investigatory notice and progress reports that § 1214 supplies might have a stronger standing hook if that plaintiff can allege an entitlement to those communications (or a statutory scheme that more clearly confers them) rather than relying on the speculative chain that an OSC inquiry might ultimately change another agency’s FOIA disclosure practices.

Second, to what extent do § 1214 procedures automatically apply to § 1216 investigations? Courts sympathetic to administrative noninterference will emphasize Heckler v. Chaney and the judiciary’s reluctance to compel an agency to exercise enforcement discretion. Those courts may treat § 1216 as a delegation of investigative authority in which OSC may choose applicable procedures case-by-case. Other courts may hew to a textualist/structuralist approach and hold that § 1216(c)’s reference to §§ 1214 and 1215 imports the corrective and disciplinary machinery (including certain procedural protections) whenever OSC pursues corrective or disciplinary action under § 1214 or § 1215. The district court in AFL adopted something like that latter approach on the question of duty (but not on mandatory procedure), so a future court could split the difference: mandate investigation where the text supports it but leave specific procedural choices to OSC unless Congress’s text establishes a clear entitlement.

Third, how will courts reconcile separation-of-powers and administrative-prudence concerns with statutory commands? Compelling OSC to investigate and then micromanaging the details of that investigation risks pulling courts into enforcement decisionmaking. That risk will be a significant factor for courts reluctant to order affirmative investigatory steps against agencies with broad prosecutorial discretion. Litigation framing that emphasizes discrete informational entitlements (and direct injuries from being denied those discrete entitlements) rather than outcomes-driven chains of causation will be more palatable to such courts.

Practical takeaways for a second bite at the apple

For plaintiffs hoping to revive AFL’s statutory theory, the D.C. Circuit’s opinion is instructive about how to plead standing and structure relief. First, plead an injury more directly traceable to OSC’s inaction—something like a statutory right to periodic notices or decisions from OSC in the context of a § 1216 matter, if such rights can be plausibly grounded in the statutes or their implementing regulations. Second, avoid reliance on multiple independent actors to bridge the causation gap; tie the complained-of injury to OSC’s conduct (or nonconduct) itself rather than to a speculative downstream change in DOJ’s FOIA posture. Third, seek relief tailored to compel OSC to take discrete nonprosecutorial steps (e.g., to issue a decision or notice) rather than to order a particular enforcement outcome; such relief is both more consistent with individual informational harms and less likely to collide with Heckler’s bar on judicially compelling enforcement decisions.

Where this leaves the law

Because the D.C. Circuit did not address the statutory merits, the core interpretive tensions among § 1216, § 552(a)(4)(F), and § 1214 remain open. The district court and the Sixth Circuit point in different directions on the portability of § 1214’s procedures and the mandatory reach of § 1216(a)(3). The D.C. Circuit’s standing analysis, while dispositive here, signals the limits of Article III in policing agency investigatory discretion and the evidentiary barriers plaintiffs face when their injuries rest on chains of discretionary governmental acts. Absent congressional clarification or a plaintiff who can plead a more direct injury traceable to OSC, these statutory frictions are likely to fester in future litigation. When a future case does squarely reach the merits, expect a careful statutory reading coupled with strong deference to prosecutorial and investigatory discretion—unless Congress’s text is plain that OSC must do more than merely consider FOIA allegations.



# Heckler in the Background: Why Courts Won’t Compel OSC’s FOIA Policing

## Why this case matters

America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer is a compact but significant decision about two recurring themes in modern administrative litigation: the limits of Article III standing for claims that seek to force agencies to investigate, and the continuing vitality of Heckler v. Chaney’s enforcement‑discretion principle as a standing and redressability constraint. At issue was not whether DOJ’s FOIA responses were adequate in AFL’s underlying FOIA suit, but whether the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) could be compelled to investigate an alleged FOIA policy under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). The D.C. Circuit disposed of the case on standing grounds, holding that AFL’s asserted informational injuries were either traceable to other actors or too speculative to be redressed by a court order compelling OSC to act. That reasoning underscores the court’s reluctance to let plaintiffs bootstrap procedural entitlements into Article III jurisdiction by imagining a downstream chain of discretionary enforcement choices.

## The short version of the court’s reasoning

The opinion, authored by Judge Katsas, begins with statutory groundwork: OSC has investigatory authority under the Civil Service Reform Act to probe “prohibited personnel practices” and, via § 1216(a)(3), it “shall … conduct an investigation” into alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings. FOIA itself contains a separate enforcement-trigger provision—§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—that requires OSC to initiate a proceeding only if a court orders disclosure, awards attorneys’ fees, and explicitly questions arbitrariness. AFL argued that § 1216’s “shall” meant OSC had an enforceable duty to investigate DOJ’s claimed policy of not searching employee emails without consent; OSC demurred, pointing to the FOIA-specific three‑predicate trigger and to its own discretion in how to handle § 1216 inquiries.

The D.C. Circuit did not reach the statutory parsing question in the end. Instead, it held that AFL lacked Article III standing under the familiar Lujan trilogy. AFL advanced two informational injuries: (1) the alleged OSC refusal deprived it of the FOIA records it sought from DOJ; and (2) the refusal deprived it of the notices and procedural fruits that might attend an OSC investigation. The court rejected both. The first injury, the panel said, is traceable to DOJ’s withholding, not OSC’s inaction, and the causal chain from an OSC investigation to actual production would require speculation about multiple independent actors (OSC’s enforcement choices, potential MSPB sanctions, and DOJ’s response), which is precisely the kind of attenuated causation that precedents like All. for Hippocratic Med. and Heckler forbid courts to rely on. The second injury failed because AFL could not point to any statutory entitlement to the specific investigatory information it wanted; § 1216 may require an investigation, but it does not necessarily import § 1214’s notice regime, and OSC’s choice whether to apply § 1214 procedures to a § 1216 investigation has been treated as discretionary. Without a concrete statutory informational entitlement, the court concluded, AFL had only a procedural gripe in the abstract, insufficient for Article III.

## Heckler’s gravitational pull

Heckler v. Chaney doesn’t appear as the captioned holding here, but it looms large. Chaney taught that prosecutorial and enforcement decisions are presumptively unreviewable because of their inherently discretionary, predictive, and resource‑allocation aspects. The D.C. Circuit applies that logic not only at the merits stage to limit judicial review of non‑enforcement, but also at the threshold of Article III jurisdiction by treating enforcement discretion as a break in the causal and redressability chain. If the relief a plaintiff seeks depends on a favored exercise of prosecutorial discretion (by the agency being sued or by other agencies), that renders the plaintiff’s injury speculative and thus non‑justiciable.

This case neatly illustrates both sides of that coin. On causation, the court said you cannot shoehorn OSC’s refusal to investigate into a direct cause of DOJ’s alleged FOIA noncompliance because too many discretionary choices by independent actors intervene. On redressability, even if a court ordered OSC to investigate, there was no reason to think that such an order would “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” AFL’s grievance—OSC might still decline corrective enforcement, the MSPB might not sanction, and DOJ might remain unmoved. Chaney therefore functions as a jurisdictional backstop: when success requires prosecutorial choices by agencies not bound to act in any particular way by a court’s decree, Article III precludes litigation.

## Why courts bridle at transforming “shall” into a judicially enforceable command

Plaintiffs naturally point to mandatory statutory language—“shall”—as a jurisdictional hook for compelling agency investigation. But appellate courts have good institutional reasons for skepticism. First, converting every statutory “shall” in enforcement or investigatory contexts into a judicially enforceable command risks micromanaging discretionary prioritization and resource allocation, tasks for which courts lack competence and institutional legitimacy. Second, many investigatory statutes (including § 1216) sit within an enforcement architecture involving multiple independent decision‑makers (OSC, MSPB, other executive agencies). Turning a single statutory “shall” into a binding judicial instrument could produce hollow victories: a court order to investigate does not easily translate into the production of documents when the subsequent enforcement and remedial junctures remain discretionary. Third, imposing judicially enforceable investigatory duties could flood courts with suits seeking to direct prosecutorial priorities, thereby shifting line‑drawing from the political branches to the judiciary.

The D.C. Circuit’s standing-centric disposition here accomplishes the same practical restraint as a direct Chaney merits decision but does so at the jurisdictional gate. That approach is doctrinally efficient: it avoids answering close statutory questions about how § 1216 relates to FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) trigger, while reinforcing the boundary that courts should not be used to convert abstract procedural rights into umbrellas for speculative remedial chains.

## Downstream effects

America First Legal Foundation will be a useful precedent for agencies resisting suits that seek to weaponize investigatory “shall” provisions into compelled investigations. It reiterates that plaintiffs cannot rely on conjectural enforcement outcomes to establish Article III injury; they must show either a directly cognizable, statutory informational entitlement or a more immediate causal link between agency inaction and their injury. The case also signals that litigants unhappy with agency non‑investigation may be better off pursuing FOIA litigation against record holders (to obtain the records) or legislative remedies to tighten enforcement‑trigger mechanics, rather than expecting courts to police investigatory priorities indirectly.

Finally, the decision leaves unresolved the statutory question it ducked: the precise relationship between § 1216’s broad investigatory language and FOIA’s specific enforcement trigger. That will remain fertile ground for future litigation if a plaintiff can identify a clearer statutory entitlement to information or a narrower causal chain unbroken by discretionary actors. For now, however, Heckler’s shadow keeps courts from ordering OSC to play FOIA cop on behalf of disappointed requesters.



# FOIA Watchdogs After AFL v. Greer: A Litigation Playbook That Survives Standing

Why this matters: the D.C. Circuit’s decision in America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer (No. 24‑5168, Oct. 3, 2025) doesn’t change the basic tools available to FOIA requesters, but it does tighten the limits on one creative — and increasingly popular — approach: trying to conscript the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) into enforcing FOIA by suing OSC for failing to investigate. The court concluded AFL lacked Article III standing because its alleged injuries were either traceable to other agencies (DOJ) or premised on speculative chains of events involving multiple independent actors (OSC → MSPB → DOJ). That reasoning rests on familiar standing precedents (Lujan, Summers, Haaland, All. for Hippocratic Med., Heckler) and supplies a succinct road map for what will and will not fly going forward.

Below I offer a practical guide for litigants and watchdog organizations who want to maximize the odds of relief after AFL — four viable routes, plus evidentiary and pleading strategies to avoid the standing pitfalls the D.C. Circuit identified.

## 1) Stick with ordinary FOIA litigation for records (the baseline, and often the best bet)
The simplest takeaway is procedural: if you want documents, sue the record-holding agency under FOIA. As the court emphasized, any informational injury from withheld documents is causally traceable to the agency that has the records (here, DOJ), not to OSC. A direct FOIA suit gives you immediate standing, an established discovery and summary-judgment posture, and a clear path to judicial orders compelling production and fee awards.

Practical tips
- Craft a clear administrative-exhaustion timeline in the complaint so there’s no doubt the suit is ripe.
- Seek expedited processing only when you can show urgency; otherwise preserve resources.
- Use motion practice to press for tailored searches (e.g., custodial searches, date ranges, targeted search terms) and to obtain Vaughn indices or affidavits that can be tested with interrogatories or depositions where appropriate.
- When possible, develop evidence of a systemic agency practice (e.g., written policy not to search employee emails without consent) in the administrative record or through affidavits — that can convert a FOIA claim from a single‑custodian denial into a wider pattern case that’s harder to defend as ad hoc.

## 2) If you want OSC involvement, aim for the § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) trigger — get a judge to say the magic words
AFL sought to flip enforcement duties onto OSC under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). The D.C. Circuit distinguished that statutory route from the separate FOIA enforcement trigger in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). That FOIA provision creates a simpler, non‑speculative causal link: if a court (1) orders production, (2) awards fees, and (3) “specifically questions” whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding, OSC “shall promptly initiate” a proceeding. A plaintiff who wants to enlist OSC can therefore work within FOIA’s built‑in mechanism.

Practical tips
- When briefing or arguing in the FOIA case, request not only production and fees but also an express judicial statement that the agency “acted arbitrarily or capriciously” in withholding records if the facts support it. Don’t assume courts will use that phrasing on their own.
- Preserve record evidence to support an “arbitrary and capricious” finding (e.g., internal guidance showing inconsistent searches, records of categorical non‑search policies, internal emails contradicting public statements).
- If you obtain a favorable order and fee award, follow up with OSC to trigger § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). That statutory route bypasses the speculative, multi‑actor chain the D.C. Circuit rejected in AFL because the chain is short and mandatory under the statute.

## 3) If you must target OSC directly, plead an informational entitlement — not just a bare right to an investigation
AFL argued it had an informational injury from OSC’s refusal to investigate. The court rejected that theory because a statutory right to an investigation, standing alone, is a “procedural right in vacuo.” To get informational standing against OSC you need to show that a statute gives you an entitlement to particular information or benefits — not merely a discretionary investigatory process.

How to do that
- Identify statutory or regulatory provisions that create a concrete entitlement to notice or information. For example, 5 U.S.C. § 1214 contains procedural notice provisions that apply when OSC pursues corrective action for prohibited personnel practices. If you can plausibly show that those notice entitlements attach to the specific statutory route you rely on, you’re in stronger territory.
- Don’t concede that notice under § 1214 is always discretionary for § 1216 investigations. Courts have sometimes treated the application of § 1214’s procedures to § 1216 investigations as discretionary, but a careful record can demonstrate factual circumstances where § 1214 entitlements are triggered as a matter of statutory interpretation or agency practice.
- If you can show OSC’s practice has been to follow § 1214 procedures in analogous investigations, document that with agency guidance, prior OSC notices, or declarations. That documentary footing helps turn a hypothetical investigatory benefit into a concrete informational entitlement.

Limits to remember
- Even with these efforts, OSC’s enforcement choices implicate Heckler v. Chaney and the traditional non‑reviewability of certain prosecutorial or enforcement decisions. A plaintiff cannot compel OSC to bring disciplinary proceedings where Congress left the decision to OSC’s prosecutorial discretion. Your best bet is to press for information and notices that the statute compels, not to force particular enforcement outcomes.

## 4) Develop non‑speculative redress theories that don’t hinge on multiple independent actors
AFL failed in large part because the causal chain it proposed required OSC to act, MSPB to sanction, and DOJ to change production decisions — too many independent choices. To survive standing challenge, plead and prove redressability that is more direct and predictable.

Concrete strategies
- Seek relief that directly binds the defendant and meaningfully narrows the causal gap. Against an agency, request a declaration that a particular policy is unlawful and an injunction requiring specific remedial steps (e.g., search employee custodians, run specified search terms, produce certain categories). Courts can order agencies to comply with FOIA obligations; they are less likely to order or be able to order third parties or independent actors that are not before the court.
- When targeting OSC, ask for orders the court can actually grant: a declaration about OSC’s statutory duties or an order compelling the agency to take a record‑specific administrative step that will produce information to the plaintiff. Be careful not to seek an order that would require OSC to exercise enforcement discretion in a particular way (that runs into Heckler).
- Avoid hypothetical chains that rest on persuading intervening, independent agencies to act. If relief depends on persuading outside entities, the court will likely find redressability too speculative.

## Evidentiary and pleading tactics to survive Summers and Lujan
Organizational plaintiffs run into two frequent obstacles: Summers’s “procedural right in vacuo” rule and the requirement that informational injuries be “concrete and particularized.” To meet those tests:

- Plead concrete use or imminent use of the requested records. Show how the documents are tied to specific organizational missions (e.g., litigation strategy, investigations, advocacy campaigns) and how the absence of the records frustrates defined programs or activities.
- Produce or allege concrete harm beyond mere curiosity: for instance, state that without the records you are unable to press a reform petition, file an administrative complaint, or inform a pending proceeding where timing matters. Courts accept programmatic harm when it’s tied to a specific activity the organization otherwise would undertake.
- If you assert an informational entitlement, cite statutory text and show how the statute’s procedures map onto your request. Include examples of prior enforcement notice or investigatory practice that creates expectation.
- When alleging a policy (like a blanket no‑search‑without‑consent rule), amass contemporaneous agency materials (policies, internal emails, declarations) or witness testimony that shows the policy is real and operational. That defeats characterization of the policy as speculative or merely aspirational and makes causation to withheld records more plausible.
- Use narrow, concrete factual allegations rather than sweeping hypotheticals. The D.C. Circuit is particularly skeptical of speculative injury theories; tighten the pleading to the projected chain of events’ most immediate step.

## Other paths to consider (administrative and strategic)
If OSC is not a promising defendant, other mechanisms can be effective. Complain to agencies’ own FOIA liaisons and oversight offices; build an administrative record that supports later litigation; consider complaints to the agency Office of Inspector General or Congressional oversight; and coordinate FOIA litigation with discovery or parallel proceedings where permissible.

Also think about fee strategy: a successful FOIA suit that yields both documents and a fee award not only gets you the records but, as noted above, can trigger OSC under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) without the standing chokepoints the D.C. Circuit described.

## Bottom line
AFL v. Greer sharply underscores standing constraints for plaintiffs trying to convert OSC’s investigatory nonfeasance into a judicially redressable injury. For requesters and watchdogs, the durable playbook is (1) bring direct FOIA suits against the record-holder, (2) when seeking OSC involvement use FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) trigger by trying to secure an “arbitrary and capricious” finding plus fees, (3) if you must go after OSC, frame a statutory entitlement to information (not merely an investigatory hope), and (4) plead redress with minimal dependence on speculative, independent actors. Careful fact‑development and narrowly framed relief requests will keep cases in court and out of the jurisdictional graveyard the D.C. Circuit described.
