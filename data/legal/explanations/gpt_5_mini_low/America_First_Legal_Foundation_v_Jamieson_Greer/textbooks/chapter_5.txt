Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 5: # AFL’s Second Theory: Informational Injury from OSC Investigation Processes and the Absence of Entitlement

## Entitlement prerequisite for informational standing

The D.C. Circuit’s analysis begins from a foundational principle of informational standing: to show a concrete informational injury, a plaintiff must be denied information to which a statute gives it an entitlement. This principle appears in the court’s citation to cases like Akins and Public Citizen and is applied here through the test described in American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Entertainment: it is not enough that a plaintiff would like to receive information; the statute must “entitle” the plaintiff to it. That entitlement requirement is what distinguishes a mere interest in information from a legally cognizable injury in fact. The court therefore frames AFL’s claim by asking whether any statutory provision gives AFL a right to receive investigatory notices or other information from the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) when OSC investigates alleged FOIA violations under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3).

## Why § 1216(a)(3)’s investigatory mandate does not itself create an entitlement to information

AFL’s second theory rests in part on § 1216(a)(3), which commands that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” AFL contends that a mandatory investigatory duty therefore gives rise to an informational entitlement: if OSC must investigate, then AFL should be able to receive the notices and progress updates that accompany OSC investigations. The court rejects that leap. The mere existence of a statutory duty to investigate does not automatically confer a right for particular outsiders to obtain investigatory notices or updates. In short, a plaintiff cannot convert a procedural obligation owed to the public or the government into an individualized entitlement to information without a statutory text that does that work. Section 1216(a)(3) imposes a duty to investigate, but it does not, by its terms, give FOIA requesters a right to receive OSC investigatory notices.

## The role of § 1214 notice provisions and the Carson line: § 1214 procedures are not automatically ported into § 1216 investigations

AFL points to § 1214, which governs corrective actions for “prohibited personnel practices” and includes provisions about notifying complainants and informing them of the progress of an investigation. The argument is that because § 1216(c) authorizes OSC to “investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved,” an OSC investigation under § 1216 might be accompanied by the § 1214 notice procedures. But the court identifies two limiting points that defeat AFL’s argument.

First, § 1214 is directed to the procedures for prohibited-personnel-practice investigations; its notice and progress-reporting provisions create entitlements tied to that statutory investigative context. Second, the court points out—and AFL concedes—that existing precedents treat OSC’s choice whether to apply § 1214 procedures in a § 1216 investigation as discretionary. The opinion cites Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel for the proposition that OSC’s decision whether to follow § 1214’s procedural requirements when it is acting under § 1216 is not compelled. That concession undermines AFL’s attempt to establish a statutory entitlement to § 1214-style notices in the § 1216 context. Because AFL does not (and cannot) identify a statutory text that expressly requires OSC to provide § 1214 notices when it is investigating under § 1216, there is no entitlement on which to base informational standing.

## Absence of a statutory entitlement means no concrete informational injury

Applying the entitlement prerequisite, the court concludes that AFL has not alleged the kind of statutory entitlement that would transform its informational interest into a concrete, Article III injury. Even if § 1216(a)(3) creates a mandatory investigatory duty, that duty does not itself provide AFL a right to the particular information (notices, progress updates, outcomes) that it seeks. Because AFL lacks a statutory right to receive those investigatory materials, the denial of such materials does not constitute the kind of concrete deprivation necessary for injury in fact. Put another way, AFL’s complaint alleges a disappointment of expectations about notice and disclosure, not the violation of a legally protected information right.

## Redressability: ordering an investigation would not be likely to yield the § 1214-style notices AFL seeks

Beyond the entitlement/injury analysis, the court addresses redressability. Even if § 1216 did require OSC to investigate (and even if that gave rise to some procedural right), AFL must show that a favorable judicial order would likely redress its asserted injury. The court reasons that ordering OSC to investigate under § 1216 would not, as a practical matter, produce the § 1214-type notices that AFL wants. Because OSC has discretion whether to apply § 1214 procedures in a § 1216 investigation, a judicial mandate that OSC investigate would not bind OSC to provide the specific notices and updates AFL seeks. Thus any relief would be unlikely to produce the concrete information AFL claims it was denied, leaving redressability unsatisfied. The court ties this point to the entitlement analysis: absent a statutory command to provide § 1214 notices in the § 1216 context, a judgment ordering OSC to investigate would not be “reasonably likely” to yield those notices.

## Disposition and doctrinal takeaways

Because AFL cannot identify a statutory entitlement to investigatory notices or information, it fails to plead a concrete informational injury and cannot satisfy the relaxed standards that sometimes apply to procedural-right claims. Redressability likewise fails because a court-ordered § 1216 investigation would not compel OSC to provide § 1214-style notices. The D.C. Circuit therefore concludes AFL lacks Article III standing, vacates the district court’s merits judgment, and remands with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

The practical lesson for students is twofold. First, informational standing requires more than a statutory duty owed generally to the public; it requires a statutory entitlement to receive the specific information. Second, when a plaintiff’s asserted injury depends on discretionary choices by government officials (especially those not parties to the lawsuit, or discretionary elements within the defendant agency’s control), both causation and redressability raise difficult jurisdictional obstacles. This opinion illustrates how courts police the boundary between generalized procedural grievances and concrete, redressable injuries necessary to invoke federal judicial power.
