Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 3: # Article III Standing and the Procedural-Injury Framework

This chapter explains how Article III standing doctrine operates in the context of claimed violations of statutory procedural rights, using America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer as the focal opinion. The court’s analysis shows how the familiar tripartite standing test (injury in fact, causation, redressability) is applied when a plaintiff asserts only a procedural entitlement to agency action, and it clarifies which elements courts may relax and which they will insist upon.

## Core elements: injury in fact, causation, and redressability

Article III confines federal courts to “Cases” and “Controversies,” so a plaintiff must demonstrate standing before a court may adjudicate the merits. Standing requires three familiar elements: (1) an injury in fact that is concrete and particularized and actual or imminent; (2) causation—a fairly traceable connection between the defendant’s challenged conduct and the alleged injury; and (3) redressability—a substantial likelihood that a favorable decision will remedy the injury. The Supreme Court’s leading rulings that anchor these requirements are Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife (setting the three-element test and the concreteness/imminence requirements), Allen v. Wright (explaining the case-or-controversy foundation), and Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment (explaining standing as a jurisdictional threshold courts must resolve).

In practice, courts examine the factual and legal linkages that bind the alleged injury to the defendant and that make judicial relief likely to redress the harm. The D.C. Circuit applies these elements at the motion-to-dismiss stage by asking whether the plaintiff plausibly alleges facts supporting each element.

## Injury must be concrete, particularized, and actual or imminent; procedural rights in vacuo are insufficient

Not every statutory procedural entitlement supplies standing. The Supreme Court has warned that the mere deprivation of a procedural right, standing alone and unconnected to any concrete interest, does not create Article III standing. In Summers v. Earth Island Institute, the Court held that “a procedural right in vacuo” is not a sufficient injury: a plaintiff must show that the procedural violation affects some concrete, particularized interest.

The America First Legal (AFL) opinion follows Summers: when a plaintiff claims only that an agency unlawfully refused to provide a statutory procedure (here, an OSC investigation under 5 U.S.C. § 1216), the court asks whether that procedural breach caused any concrete, individualized harm. Absent such a concrete interest, the procedural claim cannot sustain Article III jurisdiction.

## Relaxed standing for procedural injuries: what is relaxed and what remains

Courts have developed a calibrated approach when plaintiffs allege statutory procedural violations that are designed to protect concrete interests. That approach “relaxes—but does not eliminate—issues of imminence and redressability.” The D.C. Circuit has described this framework in cases such as Center for Law & Education v. Department of Education and Florida Audubon Society v. Bentsen. Under that framework, a plaintiff may show standing even if the injury is not as imminent or the redress not as direct as in a garden-variety claim, but the plaintiff still must satisfy the bedrock requirements of injury in fact and causation. In short:

- Relaxed: The strictness with which courts demand imminence and direct redress may be eased when a statute gives a procedural protection intended to protect a concrete interest.
- Not relaxed: The requirement that the plaintiff show a concrete, particularized injury in fact, and a traceable causal link between the defendant’s conduct and that injury, remains firm.

The AFL opinion adopts this approach. The court acknowledged that procedural-rights plaintiffs may sometimes benefit from relaxed considerations of imminence and redressability, but it stressed that AFL still must demonstrate a concrete injury and that the injury is fairly traceable to OSC’s refusal to investigate.

## Informational standing: the entitlement-based model

A special, well-established category of concrete harms is informational injury: a plaintiff is injured when a statute entitles them to receive information and the defendant unlawfully withholds it. The Supreme Court and this circuit have enforced an entitlement-based rule for informational standing: if a statute grants a person a right to obtain specified information, then denial of that information can constitute a concrete injury. Key cases include FEC v. Akins, Public Citizen v. DOJ, Zivotofsky (as applied in the circuit), and American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals (ASPCA) v. Feld Entertainment. Those decisions hold that a statutory entitlement to information creates a concrete interest whose deprivation can satisfy injury in fact.

But informational standing depends on an actual statutory entitlement to the information at issue. If the statute does not create a right to receive the specific information claimed, then the plaintiff cannot bootstrap an informational injury from the statute’s procedural language. The AFL opinion enforces this principle: even if a statute requires an agency to “conduct an investigation,” that obligation does not automatically create a separate right to receive the investigation’s fruits (for example, notices or reports) unless some provision explicitly entitles the plaintiff to that information.

## Application to AFL’s framing: § 1216 investigation and the two asserted concrete interests

Applying these principles, the D.C. Circuit analyzed AFL’s two ways of converting its asserted procedural right to an OSC investigation under § 1216 into a concrete injury.

1. FOIA records withheld by DOJ

AFL’s primary asserted injury was informational: because OSC declined to investigate under § 1216, AFL says DOJ still refuses to produce the records AFL sought under FOIA. The court rejected this theory on causation and redressability grounds. The central problem is that any withholding by DOJ is attributable primarily to DOJ, not OSC; OSC’s decision not to investigate is too attenuated from DOJ’s withholding to be a fairly traceable cause of AFL’s lack of access to the records. To accept AFL’s theory requires chaining together a series of speculative events: OSC would have to (a) exercise enforcement discretion to pursue corrective or disciplinary action under §§ 1214 or 1215; (b) the MSPB would have to impose sanctions or corrective actions; and (c) DOJ would then respond by producing the records. The court emphasized that standing cannot rest on speculation about the unfettered choices of independent actors (here, the MSPB and DOJ) or on how an agency might exercise enforcement discretion that courts traditionally cannot compel (Heckler v. Chaney). Because the causal chain was speculative, a judicial order requiring OSC to investigate would not be likely to “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” AFL’s informational injury. The court therefore held that AFL lacked standing to sue based on the deprivation of the FOIA records.

2. Notices and information from an OSC investigation (§ 1214 procedural benefits)

AFL’s second asserted injury was denial of the informational benefits that would flow from an OSC investigation—specifically, notices and procedural information that OSC gives complainants when pursuing corrective actions under § 1214. Here the court focused on entitlement: the informational-injury line of cases requires that a statute actually entitle the plaintiff to the withheld information. Section 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to “conduct an investigation” of alleged FOIA misconduct, but it does not create a parallel entitlement to receive the notices and other procedural communications that arise under § 1214. AFL conceded that OSC’s decision whether to adopt § 1214’s procedures in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary (the court cited Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel on that point). Because § 1216 does not, by its terms, guarantee AFL the § 1214 notice regime, AFL cannot claim an entitlement-based informational injury. And because there is no statutory entitlement to the § 1214-style notices in a § 1216 matter, ordering OSC to investigate would not be likely to redress AFL’s asserted informational deprivation.

Taken together, these applications show the limits of the relaxed procedural-injury framework. Even where a statute arguably grants a procedural right to agency action, a plaintiff must (1) identify a concrete, statutory or other legally protected interest that was affected by the procedural denial, and (2) show that the defendant’s conduct is the fairly traceable cause of that concrete injury and that a favorable order would likely redress it. AFL’s theories failed both prongs: one because causation and redressability were too attenuated and speculative, and the other because there was no statutory entitlement to the requested information.

Conclusion

America First Legal illustrates the careful balancing courts perform when plaintiffs assert procedural statutory rights. The procedural-rights framework may relax the stringency of some standing elements, but it does not eliminate the twin bedrock requirements of a concrete injury in fact and a plausible causal link to the defendant’s conduct. Informational injuries remain cognizable when the plaintiff is entitled by statute to receive the withheld information; absent such an entitlement or a non-speculative causal path to redress, a procedural claim will not suffice to establish Article III standing.
