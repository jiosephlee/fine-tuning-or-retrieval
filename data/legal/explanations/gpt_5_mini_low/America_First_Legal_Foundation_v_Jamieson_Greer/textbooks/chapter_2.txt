Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 2: # Facts and Procedural History

America First Legal Foundation (AFL) filed a FOIA request with the Department of Justice (DOJ) seeking emails exchanged between an outside attorney and the DOJ Civil Division as part of AFL’s broader inquiry into judicial recusals. In response, the Civil Division asserted a policy under which it does not search employees’ personal or work emails for responsive records without the employees’ consent. That policy was the central complaint in AFL’s follow-up actions: AFL maintained that the Civil Division’s purported refusal to search employee emails amounted to an arbitrary or capricious withholding of records under FOIA.

AFL pursued parallel litigation against DOJ under FOIA challenging the Civil Division’s asserted search practice. DOJ has produced records in that FOIA case and continued to produce additional responsive documents thereafter. Importantly for the appeal before the D.C. Circuit, the district court proceedings and this Court’s review do not take up the sufficiency or adequacy of DOJ’s production in that FOIA suit; the production itself is not at issue in the appeal from the district court’s disposition of AFL’s claims against the Office of Special Counsel (OSC).

Concurrently, AFL asked the Office of Special Counsel to investigate DOJ’s alleged FOIA policy under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3). That provision directs OSC to “conduct an investigation” of allegations concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited by FOIA, and it authorizes OSC to seek corrective or disciplinary action under the corrective-action and disciplinary provisions applicable to prohibited personnel practices. AFL’s petition to OSC therefore sought an OSC investigation into what AFL characterized as a systemic failure by DOJ to search employee emails in response to FOIA requests.

OSC declined to open an investigation. Its initial declination rested on a different statutory provision in FOIA, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), which directs OSC to initiate disciplinary proceedings when a court (1) orders the production of records improperly withheld, (2) awards attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) “specifically question[s] whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding.” OSC concluded that those three FOIA predicates were not satisfied and therefore that it lacked authority to proceed under the FOIA-specific enforcement mechanism—an approach on which OSC relied to disavow a mandatory duty to investigate under § 1216.

AFL sued OSC in district court, alleging that OSC’s refusal to investigate violated § 1216 and seeking injunctive relief compelling OSC to investigate. The district court issued a mixed ruling. It held that FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) provision does not limit OSC’s authority under § 1216: in the court’s view, § 1216(a)(3)’s command that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of arbitrary FOIA withholdings was not confined by the FOIA provision that addresses OSC’s obligations following a judicial FOIA judgment. The district court therefore concluded that OSC’s initial declination rested on a legal error about OSC’s statutory obligations. See Am. First Legal Found. v. Kerner, No. 22-cv-3576, 2023 WL 9546644 (D.D.C. Sept. 29, 2023).

Despite ruling that OSC had misconstrued its authority, the district court did not order OSC to investigate the alleged DOJ policy. The court determined that even if § 1216 authorized an investigation, § 1216(c) gives OSC discretion as to whether to pursue corrective or disciplinary proceedings in the same manner as prohibited personnel practices, and the decision to initiate enforcement was therefore committed to agency discretion. Accordingly, the district court remanded the matter to OSC for further consideration rather than compelling an investigation.

Following remand, OSC again declined to open an investigation into DOJ’s alleged search policy. AFL then moved for entry of final judgment; the district court dismissed AFL’s remaining claims on the merits and entered judgment against AFL. AFL appealed that final judgment to the United States Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit. The D.C. Circuit stated that its review would be de novo, as is customary for statutory and jurisdictional questions of this character.

On initial consideration, neither party had addressed whether AFL possessed Article III standing to pursue its claims against OSC. Because standing implicates subject-matter jurisdiction, the D.C. Circuit ordered supplemental briefing specifically on that threshold issue. The Court then treated standing as the dispositive question; it evaluated whether AFL had alleged a concrete, particularized, and redressable injury traceable to OSC’s declination to investigate under § 1216, and whether a favorable judicial order compelling OSC to investigate would likely remedy any such injury. The Circuit ultimately concluded that AFL lacked Article III standing and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.
