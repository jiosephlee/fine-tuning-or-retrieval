Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

## Procedural Rights Without Remedies: The D.C. Circuit Tightens Standing in OSC–FOIA Disputes

Why this matters: The D.C. Circuit’s opinion in America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer (Oct. 3, 2025) is not a long doctrinal treatise, but it is consequential. On its face the case concerns a narrow statutory dispute over the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) investigatory obligations under 5 U.S.C. § 1216. At bottom, however, the court issued a stand-alone Article III decision that narrows the avenues by which FOIA requesters, public-interest groups, and other watchdogs can convert asserted procedural wrongs into a cognizable federal lawsuit. The panel assumes, for argument’s sake, that § 1216 may require OSC to investigate allegedly arbitrary FOIA withholdings; still, it holds that America First Legal Foundation (AFL) lacks standing because it cannot show a concrete, traceable, and redressable injury. That holding tightens the limits on using downstream enforcement mechanisms to bootstrap informational injuries into Article III claims.

What the court decided, in brief: AFL sought an OSC investigation into what it described as DOJ’s policy of not searching employee emails without employee consent—as a way to police alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings. The district court thought OSC had misread the statutory text and remanded for OSC to reconsider, but ultimately dismissed when OSC again declined to open an investigation. On appeal the D.C. Circuit did not reach the merits. Instead, it ordered supplemental briefing on standing and concluded AFL failed to show Article III injury. The panel rejected both of AFL’s informational-injury theories: (1) that OSC’s failure to investigate deprived AFL of the underlying FOIA records; and (2) that OSC’s refusal denied AFL the informational benefits it would receive from an OSC probe itself (notably, notices and updates comparable to the § 1214 process). The court vacated the district-court judgment and directed dismissal for lack of jurisdiction.

How the court reasoned: The opinion is doctrinally methodical. It starts from the familiar Lujan framework—injury-in-fact, causation, and redressability—and the Summers principle that mere deprivation of a procedural right, standing alone, is insufficient. The panel applies the “relaxed” approach applicable where a procedural statute is designed to protect a threatened concrete interest, but emphasizes that the relaxation does not dissolve the need for a concrete injury or a credible causal link to redress.

On AFL’s first theory (the underlying FOIA production), the court found causation and redressability wanting. Any failure to produce records is traceable to DOJ’s decision-making, not OSC’s inaction. To get from OSC’s non-investigation to production would require a speculative chain: OSC might investigate; it might seek corrective or disciplinary action under §§1214–1215; the MSPB might impose sanctions; DOJ might then change its practices and produce records. The court relied on recent Supreme Court authority rejecting standing theories that hinge on the independent choices of non-party actors—chiefly FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine and Haaland v. Brackeen—and analogous D.C. Circuit decisions such as Hecate Energy. The panel also drew on Heckler v. Chaney to underscore that courts generally may not compel executive enforcement decisions. The upshot: the causal chain is too attenuated and uncertain to support Article III standing.

On AFL’s second theory (entitlement to information from an OSC probe), the court asked whether § 1216 creates a statutory entitlement to investigatory notices or other information. Following Amer. Soc’y for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Enterprises and Akins, the court observed that informational standing requires a statute that actually grants the plaintiff the right to receive the information. Here, even if § 1216 mandates investigations of FOIA-withholding allegations, it does not, in the panel’s view, confer upon a FOIA requester a right to the procedural notices that accompany § 1214 investigations. AFL conceded that OSC has discretion whether to adopt § 1214-like procedures in § 1216 probes and pointed to precedents allowing OSC flexibility (e.g., Carson), but could not identify a statutory entitlement to notices. Without that entitlement, AFL lacked the requisite concrete informational injury and, further, could not show that a court order compelling OSC to investigate would necessarily deliver the informational benefits AFL sought.

What to take away — doctrinal implications and downstream effects: The decision has three practical and doctrinal teeth.

First, it curtails a potential litigation strategy: using an OSC enforcement pathway as a backdoor to obtain FOIA records. Plaintiffs and watchdog groups who hope to vindicate FOIA interests by forcing a third-party enforcement process into motion will now confront a heightened standing gate. Where the requested relief depends on a chain of discretionary decisions across multiple agencies or tribunals, courts are likely to treat the causal link as too speculative.

Second, the opinion underscores the limits of the relaxed procedural-rights framework. The D.C. Circuit reiterates that the “relaxed” approach does not license plaintiffs to sue on procedural complaints divorced from a concrete interest or realistic redress. That message will matter in other contexts where plaintiffs rely on statutory investigatory processes (inspectors general, independent counsels, other enforcement offices) to obtain information or remedial outcomes.

Third, the case highlights the importance of statutory text that creates explicit private entitlements to information. Akins-style informational standing remains viable, but only where Congress has conferred a right to receive specific information. Absent such a textual hook, courts will treat informational claims skeptically.

A final, practical point: the opinion may push litigants toward different strategies. Where standing is precarious, requesters may prefer to litigate directly under FOIA against the agency that controls the records and whose actions directly affect disclosure. Or they may seek § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) relief—the explicit FOIA-triggered OSC enforcement provision that ties OSC action to a judicial finding of improper withholding and attorneys’ fees—rather than relying on the more attenuated § 1216 route.

Conclusion: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer is a crisp reminder that not all procedural rights create judicially cognizable injuries. The D.C. Circuit accepts, for argument’s sake, that OSC has an investigatory role under § 1216, but it insists that Article III requires a concrete, traceable, and likely redressable injury before federal courts may intervene. For watchdogs and FOIA practitioners, the practical lesson is straightforward: when your claim to information depends on persuading a chain of enforcement actors to act, Article III may very well be the first hurdle you cannot clear.
