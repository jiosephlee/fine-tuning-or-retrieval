Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

## The Merits That Weren’t: Unresolved Tensions Between § 1216, § 552(a)(4)(F), and § 1214

Why this case matters

America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer was litigated as a straightforward fight over whether the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) must investigate alleged FOIA-withholdings on a plaintiff’s demand. The district court concluded OSC had erred as a matter of law in treating FOIA’s post-litigation disciplinary trigger—§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—as the only path into OSC’s investigatory authority for FOIA matters. But the D.C. Circuit never reached that statutory question: it vacated on Article III standing grounds and sent the case back for dismissal. That procedural end leaves important doctrinal questions unresolved about how three statutory provisions—5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), and § 1214—fit together. Those questions will matter for practitioners and affected requesters because they determine when OSC must act, what process it must follow if it does, and how much courts can compel when they conclude OSC misconstrued its duties.

What the court actually decided (and what it didn’t)

At bottom, the court’s narrow holding is jurisdictional: AFL lacked Article III standing. The panel accepted the basic premise that OSC has some role in policing FOIA withholdings (a role rooted in the Civil Service Reform Act and § 1216’s explicit reference to FOIA-withholdings), but it concluded that AFL’s pleaded injuries were too attenuated from OSC’s refusal to investigate to establish causation and redressability. The panel gave two reasons the asserted informational injuries fell short. First, any FOIA nonproduction injury traces principally to DOJ, not OSC, and the speculative chain by which an OSC investigation might ultimately induce DOJ to produce records depended on intervening actors and discretionary choices (OSC enforcement choices, MSPB action, DOJ’s decisions). Second, AFL claimed only an informational entitlement to whatever notices OSC might give under § 1214 if OSC treated a § 1216 investigation like a § 1214 matter—but § 1216 does not itself create a right to those § 1214 notices, and controlling precedent suggests OSC’s choice to apply § 1214 procedures in a § 1216 probe is discretionary. Because AFL could not clear even the relaxed standing regime for procedural-right claims, the court vacated and ordered dismissal for lack of jurisdiction. The panel explicitly declined to resolve the core merits question left open by the district court.

The statutory puzzle the court left unresolved

Three statutory provisions sit at the heart of this dispute, and their relationship remains ambiguous after the decision:

- § 1216(a)(3) provides that the OSC “shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” The mandatory “shall” is the linchpin of AFL’s claim: it suggests an unqualified duty to investigate FOIA-withholding allegations.

- FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) establishes a different-looking enforcement route: if a court orders production, awards fees, and “specifically question[s]” whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously in withholding, OSC “shall promptly initiate a proceeding” to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the responsible officer or employee. That formulation is explicitly tied to outcomes in litigation and imposes a post-litigation disciplinary trigger.

- § 1214 prescribes procedures for OSC investigations and remedial processes when OSC treats misconduct as a prohibited personnel practice; it also contains notice and progress-reporting provisions that serve as the source for AFL’s claimed informational entitlements.

There are at least three plausible ways to read the statutory scheme:

1. § 1216(a)(3) independently requires OSC to investigate any allegation of arbitrary FOIA withholding on receipt of an allegation, without regard to FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F) post-judgment triggers. Under this view, the “shall” in § 1216(a)(3) is mandatory and broad; § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) provides an additional, litigation-contingent enforcement mechanism but does not cabin OSC’s non-litigation investigatory duties.

2. § 1216(a)(3) is best read in light of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i): FOIA’s detailed post-judgment language reflects Congress’s intent to channel OSC’s formal disciplinary proceedings to situations where a court has already found wrongful withholding. On this account, § 1216(a)(3) authorizes investigations but does not create an independent, enforceable obligation to initiate them absent the FOIA-specified triggers.

3. A hybrid approach treats § 1216(a)(3) as imposing a duty to consider or screen allegations but leaves substantial discretion to OSC about whether to open a full § 1214-style investigative proceeding and whether to apply § 1214’s procedures. Under this reading, § 1216(c)’s cross-reference to §§ 1214 and 1215 supplies the mechanism OSC may use—but it does not automatically import every § 1214 procedural entitlement into every § 1216 investigation.

How prior precedent and the district court framed the issue

The district court read the statutes in the first (independent duty) vein: it concluded § 1216(a)(3)’s “shall conduct” language did not collapse into FOIA’s narrower § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) trigger. That conclusion turned on the plain statutory cross-references and the different functions the provisions serve—§ 1216(a)(3) as a general investigatory mandate; § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) as an explicit enforcement requirement tied to litigation outcomes. The district court nevertheless declined to order OSC to apply § 1214’s procedures in any § 1216 investigation, viewing § 1216(c) as leaving some procedural choices to OSC. In other words, the district court believed OSC had misread the scope of its investigatory duty, but it also thought courts should stop short of dictating how OSC must run every investigation.

By contrast, the Sixth Circuit’s Carson decision (cited in the D.C. Circuit opinion) has been read to allow OSC discretion about whether to follow § 1214’s specific procedural requirements in § 1216 investigations. That line of authority suggests at least some procedural nonportability: an OSC decision not to apply § 1214’s notice scheme during a § 1216 probe is not necessarily reviewable as a categorical denial of statutory process.

Where courts might go next

Because the D.C. Circuit disposed of the case on standing grounds, lower courts and litigants are left without a controlling clarification of how these provisions interact. Future litigation will likely test three related questions.

First, what operational force does § 1216(a)(3)’s “shall conduct an investigation” actually have? A plaintiff seeking to compel OSC to investigate should craft pleading allegations that make causation and redressability plausible—focusing on an injury traceable to OSC’s inaction rather than an injury traceable to the underlying agency (DOJ in AFL’s case). For example, a plaintiff whose only need is to obtain the specific investigatory notice and progress reports that § 1214 supplies might have a stronger standing hook if that plaintiff can allege an entitlement to those communications (or a statutory scheme that more clearly confers them) rather than relying on the speculative chain that an OSC inquiry might ultimately change another agency’s FOIA disclosure practices.

Second, to what extent do § 1214 procedures automatically apply to § 1216 investigations? Courts sympathetic to administrative noninterference will emphasize Heckler v. Chaney and the judiciary’s reluctance to compel an agency to exercise enforcement discretion. Those courts may treat § 1216 as a delegation of investigative authority in which OSC may choose applicable procedures case-by-case. Other courts may hew to a textualist/structuralist approach and hold that § 1216(c)’s reference to §§ 1214 and 1215 imports the corrective and disciplinary machinery (including certain procedural protections) whenever OSC pursues corrective or disciplinary action under § 1214 or § 1215. The district court in AFL adopted something like that latter approach on the question of duty (but not on mandatory procedure), so a future court could split the difference: mandate investigation where the text supports it but leave specific procedural choices to OSC unless Congress’s text establishes a clear entitlement.

Third, how will courts reconcile separation-of-powers and administrative-prudence concerns with statutory commands? Compelling OSC to investigate and then micromanaging the details of that investigation risks pulling courts into enforcement decisionmaking. That risk will be a significant factor for courts reluctant to order affirmative investigatory steps against agencies with broad prosecutorial discretion. Litigation framing that emphasizes discrete informational entitlements (and direct injuries from being denied those discrete entitlements) rather than outcomes-driven chains of causation will be more palatable to such courts.

Practical takeaways for a second bite at the apple

For plaintiffs hoping to revive AFL’s statutory theory, the D.C. Circuit’s opinion is instructive about how to plead standing and structure relief. First, plead an injury more directly traceable to OSC’s inaction—something like a statutory right to periodic notices or decisions from OSC in the context of a § 1216 matter, if such rights can be plausibly grounded in the statutes or their implementing regulations. Second, avoid reliance on multiple independent actors to bridge the causation gap; tie the complained-of injury to OSC’s conduct (or nonconduct) itself rather than to a speculative downstream change in DOJ’s FOIA posture. Third, seek relief tailored to compel OSC to take discrete nonprosecutorial steps (e.g., to issue a decision or notice) rather than to order a particular enforcement outcome; such relief is both more consistent with individual informational harms and less likely to collide with Heckler’s bar on judicially compelling enforcement decisions.

Where this leaves the law

Because the D.C. Circuit did not address the statutory merits, the core interpretive tensions among § 1216, § 552(a)(4)(F), and § 1214 remain open. The district court and the Sixth Circuit point in different directions on the portability of § 1214’s procedures and the mandatory reach of § 1216(a)(3). The D.C. Circuit’s standing analysis, while dispositive here, signals the limits of Article III in policing agency investigatory discretion and the evidentiary barriers plaintiffs face when their injuries rest on chains of discretionary governmental acts. Absent congressional clarification or a plaintiff who can plead a more direct injury traceable to OSC, these statutory frictions are likely to fester in future litigation. When a future case does squarely reach the merits, expect a careful statutory reading coupled with strong deference to prosecutorial and investigatory discretion—unless Congress’s text is plain that OSC must do more than merely consider FOIA allegations.
