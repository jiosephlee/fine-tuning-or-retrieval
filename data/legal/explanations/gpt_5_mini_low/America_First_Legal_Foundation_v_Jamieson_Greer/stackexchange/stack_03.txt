Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Can a requester claim informational standing to receive § 1214 notices when OSC investigates under § 1216?
Question:
The panel says § 1216 doesn’t entitle AFL to any information, and that § 1214’s notice provisions don’t automatically apply to § 1216 investigations. But § 1216(c) says OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." Doesn’t "in the same way" import § 1214’s procedures, including complainant notices? How do Carson v. OSC (6th Cir.) and any D.C. Circuit precedent treat whether § 1214 procedural entitlements travel along when § 1216 is the predicate? If they’re discretionary, what text or precedent makes them discretionary rather than mandatory?
Answer:
Short answer: No—under the panel’s reasoning AFL could not establish informational standing to compel OSC to give it the § 1214-style notices when OSC acts under § 1216. The court treated the § 1214 notice rules as not automatically or mandatorily “carried over” by § 1216(c), and it held that even if OSC must investigate under § 1216(a)(3), § 1216 does not itself create an entitlement to receive information that would support informational standing.

Why (doctrinally and as the opinion explains)

- Informational-standing rule. The D.C. Circuit applies the familiar line from Akins, Pub. Citizen, Zivotofsky, and Feld Ent.: a plaintiff suffers a cognizable “informational” injury only when a statute entitles it to receive specific information and the denial of that entitlement deprives it of the information. The opinion cites Feld Ent. and Zivotofsky for that rule (Op. § II).

- § 1216(c) language. Section 1216(c) says OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” The panel did not read that phrase to create an automatic private right to the § 1214 procedural notices. Instead, the court observed that AFL itself conceded that OSC’s decision whether to follow § 1214’s procedural requirements when acting under § 1216 is treated as discretionary in at least some precedent (citing Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns., 633 F.3d 487 (6th Cir. 2011)). The panel therefore concluded § 1216 does not on its face entitle AFL to receive § 1214 notices and information (Op. § II).

- Discretion v. entitlement. Because informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to information, the court held AFL had no cognizable informational injury from lack of § 1214 notices. AFL’s own concession that following § 1214’s procedures in § 1216 investigations is discretionary undermined its claim that § 1216 creates a mandatory right to notice. The opinion also tied this to redressability: even if the court ordered OSC to investigate, the chain by which that would produce the FOIA records or § 1214 notices depended on speculative, independent decisions by OSC, the MSPB, or DOJ—precisely the kind of speculation that defeats redressability (Op. §§ II–III; cites Heckler v. Chaney logic).

- Precedent relied on. The panel relied on: the informational-standing cases (Akins, Pub. Citizen, Zivotofsky, Feld Ent.), Summers for the limits on procedural-right standing, and Carson for the proposition (as AFL recognized) that OSC’s application of § 1214 procedures to § 1216 investigations is discretionary. The opinion also invokes Heckler v. Chaney and subsequent standing/redressability principles to explain why speculative chains of enforcement do not confer standing (Op. §§ II–III).

Putting it together for the specific question
- The clause “in the same way” in § 1216(c) does not, according to the panel, automatically import § 1214’s notice-and-information entitlements as mandatory rights enforceable by third parties. The court treated those § 1214 procedures as something OSC may follow in § 1216 cases, not something that gives private parties a statutory right to information that can ground Article III standing.
- Carson (6th Cir.) is the principal on-point decision the opinion cites for the proposition that whether OSC applies § 1214’s procedures in § 1216 investigations is discretionary. The D.C. Circuit opinion rests on the same practical legal effect: absent a clear statutory entitlement to information, there is no informational injury for standing purposes.
- Text and precedent making the entitlements discretionary rather than mandatory: the opinion relies on (1) the statutory formulation of § 1216(c) using “may … in the same way,” which the panel read as permissive rather than creating a new private right; (2) Carson’s holding that OSC’s application of § 1214 procedures is discretionary in § 1216 investigations; and (3) the Supreme Court and D.C. Circuit standing and prosecutorial-discretion precedents (Heckler, Summers, Lujan) showing courts cannot remedy speculative enforcement choices by other agencies or compel the exercise of prosecutorial discretion.

Net practical effect for a requester
- If you want to claim informational standing to force OSC to give you § 1214 notices when OSC is operating under § 1216, America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer says you’re on weak ground: § 1216 does not plainly create the statutory entitlement to information that the informational-standing line of cases requires, and the relevant precedents treat the application of § 1214 procedures in § 1216 cases as discretionary rather than mandatory.
