Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

## When Threats Aren’t Persecution: The Tenth Circuit’s High Threshold in Jimenez v. Bondi

Why this case matters

Jimenez v. Bondi is the latest in a line of Tenth Circuit decisions that underscore how high the court sets the bar for establishing “persecution” in asylum and withholding claims based primarily on threats and psychological harm. At first blush, the facts in Jimenez are alarming: repeated death threats, armed men confronting and searching for the family at their workplace, an unsettling schoolyard incident in which a stranger grabbed a child’s mouth and told him to “run to your dad,” and ongoing panic and sleeplessness for family members. Yet the Tenth Circuit, affirming the Board of Immigration Appeals, concluded that these harms, taken together, still fell short of past persecution. The opinion is consequential because it clarifies — and arguably hardens — how the court applies its precedent on cumulative threats, nexus, government protection, and internal relocation. Advocates whose cases rest on repeated intimidation and psychological harms need to account for how this court distinguishes between “serious” harms and “persecution” as a legal threshold.

The court’s reasoning in brief

The panel framed its analysis with the familiar tripwire for asylum claims: petitioners must show (1) persecution, (2) on account of a protected ground (here, political opinion), and (3) that the government is unable or unwilling to protect them. The Tenth Circuit applied the substantial-evidence standard to uphold the BIA’s factual findings.

On persecution the court invoked its controlling precedents — Wiransane, Ritonga, and Karki — emphasizing that persecution “must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty.” The panel treated threats as relevant but not dispositive, and leaned heavily on petitioners’ concession that they suffered no physical harm or lasting physical injury. It also discounted psychological harms, noting that Tenth Circuit case law has “rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone.” Comparing the aggregate facts to prior losses the court found insufficient (e.g., protracted threats, brief detention, or episodic violence in past cases), the panel concluded that the cumulative incidents here still did not reach the statutory meaning of persecution.

On nexus the court refused to infer that politics was a central motive. Although the family were active in the Liberal Party and campaigned for a presidential candidate, the Tenth Circuit found persuasive the timing that connected the escalation to Jimenez’s involvement in a property dispute with “Don Rafa,” a narco-trafficking figure whose interests (and alleged revenue loss) provided a more plausible, non-political motive. The panel reiterated the familiar rule that mixed motives are possible but that a protected ground must be at least one “central reason” for harm.

On government protection and internal relocation the court again credited the BIA’s factual conclusions: petitioners did not exhaust avenues for broader police assistance, their contact with two unhelpful officers was not evidence of systemic government unwillingness, and the record showed law enforcement had at times tried to address the criminal activity at the compound linked to Don Rafa. The Tenth Circuit also pointed to the petitioners’ temporary safe stay in the Bogota countryside and the continued safety of other relatives in Colombia to support the availability of internal relocation.

Analytical perspective: the Tenth Circuit’s skeptical posture

Jimenez reflects a consistent Tenth Circuit posture: a relatively stringent, context-sensitive reading of “persecution” that resists expanding asylum protection based on threats and emotional distress alone. The panel’s repeated references to earlier decisions (Karki, Ritonga, Wiransane) show an effort to maintain doctrinal continuity, but the practical effect is to preserve a narrow category of cases that qualify as past persecution absent physical injury or extraordinary psychological harm documented with medical evidence.

Two features of the opinion stand out. First, the court gives considerable weight to the absence of physical injury. Although statute and precedent do not require physical harm, Jimenez illustrates that, in the Tenth Circuit, the absence of bodily harm makes it harder to clear the persecution bar. Second, the court treats the causal question (nexus) and motive assessment with a fact-intensive skepticism that can be difficult to rebut on appeal: when adversaries’ non-political interests plausibly explain targeting, claims that politics was “central” are easy to discount.

Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

The decision tightens several litigation levers. Practitioners should expect the Tenth Circuit and the BIA to:

- Require more robust proof of psychological injury when physical harm is absent. Vague testimony about panic attacks or insomnia will often be insufficient; corroboration through medical or mental-health evaluations may be pivotal.

- Treat cumulative threats skeptically unless they amount to a pattern of violence, detention, or severe economic or social deprivation that past Tenth Circuit precedents have characterized as persecution.

- Scrutinize motive evidence, especially where non-protected motives (criminal extortion, property disputes, personal retaliation) can plausibly explain the persecution. Political-activity claims that occur alongside, or after, a personal dispute will face uphill battles on nexus.

- Demand stronger proof that government protection is unavailable beyond one or two unhelpful encounters with local officers; systemic failure or corruption should be shown through objective country conditions evidence or specific evidence that police refusal was not idiosyncratic.

Comparative context: not uniform across circuits

Other circuits have occasionally been more receptive to claims based on severe threats and psychological harm, particularly where threats are coupled with targeting of family members or credible patterns of intimidation suggesting future harm. But Jimenez reveals the reality of circuit variation: in the Tenth Circuit, counsel should not rely on persuasive-but-non-physical harms alone. Where a petition hinges on fear stemming from threats, success may depend on producing medical documentation, contemporaneous reports to authorities, or clear evidence that the persecutor’s motive was political rather than personal or criminal.

Practical takeaways for advocates

Jimenez suggests several practical steps when building cases grounded in threats and psychological harm:

- Develop corroboration for mental-health claims. Obtain contemporaneous medical or psychological evaluations, school records documenting children’s distress, or expert declarations tying symptoms to the claimed trauma.

- Preserve and emphasize contemporaneous reporting to authorities, including written police reports, complaints, or evidence that such reporting was attempted and rebuffed. If reporting was not attempted, develop a record showing futility or danger in doing so.

- Intensify nexus proof. Show public statements linking the persecutor to political motives, or demonstrate how the target’s political activities were known and salient to the persecutor. Distinguish personal disputes from political targeting with documentary or testimonial corroboration.

- Build country-condition evidence showing systemic problems with protection if government unwillingness is central to the claim: local press, NGO reports, or expert declarations can help transform isolated police apathy into evidence of a broader failure.

Conclusion

Jimenez v. Bondi is a reminder that in the Tenth Circuit, the legal line between “threats” and “persecution” remains a demanding one. The panel’s careful application of circuit precedent highlights the evidentiary pitfalls of claims that rest primarily on intimidation and psychological distress without physical injury or robust documentary support. For advocates, the decision underscores the importance of granular, corroborated evidence—medical, documentary, and country-specific—to bridge the gap between lived fear and legally cognizable persecution.



## Politics or Payback? Mixed-Motive Nexus and the ‘One Central Reason’ Test

Why this case matters: Jimenez v. Bondi is a useful recent Tenth Circuit application of the “one central reason” test in asylum law, and it shows how courts parse mixed-motive persecution claims where political activity overlaps with private criminal retaliation. At first blush the Jimenez family’s membership in the Colombian Liberal Party and active campaigning might suggest political targeting. The court, however, credited evidence tying the escalation of threats to a separate episode: Wilson Jimenez’s meddling in a property dispute that interfered with a narco-trafficker’s business. By treating political opinion as “tangential” and criminal retaliation as the dominant motive, the panel denied the asylum claim. The opinion therefore illustrates (1) the evidentiary line courts draw between political motive and other motives, (2) how prosecutor- or law-related conduct complicates nexus analysis, and (3) what petitioners need in the record to push political opinion from subsidiary to central.

How the court reasoned

The Tenth Circuit framed the dispositive issue narrowly: did petitioners show the persecutor’s motive included a political opinion that was at least one central reason for the harm? The panel applied the familiar mixed-motive framework—citing Rivera-Barrientos and O.C.V.—which allows for multiple motives but requires that a protected ground be one central reason for harm, not merely incidental or subordinate.

The court accepted the BIA and IJ’s factual findings under the substantial-evidence standard. It emphasized several record features supporting a nonpolitical motive: the initial politically flavored threats predated any confrontation with the narco-trafficker, but the threats escalated only after Jimenez intervened in the tenant/property dispute tied to “Don Rafa.” Threats and attacks explicitly labeled Jimenez a “snitch” and blamed him for searches and seizures at the property; the assailants reportedly said they acted at Don Rafa’s behest. The IJ also found (and the court found supported by the record) that petitioners’ political activity continued despite earlier threats, suggesting political affiliation alone did not provoke the increased hostility. Those facts led the panel to conclude the predominant motive was retribution for interfering with criminal enterprises rather than political animus.

Analytical perspective: why the court’s approach is defensible — and where it bites

The panel’s analysis is doctrinally orthodox. Mixed-motive cases are inherently fact-driven, and reviewing courts owe deference to the BIA on factual credibility and motive findings. Jimenez demonstrates the gatekeeping role of evidence that links the persecutor’s actions to a protected ground. Where the record contains contemporaneous statements by assailants attributing conduct to nonpolitical reasons—here, protecting revenue and avenging a perceived “snitch”—courts are likely to treat political opinion as peripheral.

That said, the decision also highlights a tension in asylum doctrine: political activity and law-related conduct often overlap in contexts of weak state control and pervasive criminal politics. When a political campaign or a prosecutor-affiliated role exposes someone to criminal retaliation, the driver of harm can be genuinely mixed. Deference to the BIA’s reading of these facts can result in hardship being classified as ordinary crime or retaliation, not persecution on account of political opinion, unless petitioners marshal strong record evidence that political opinion was central.

What would have pushed the political motive to “central”?

Jimenez is instructive about the types of evidence that can tip the scales. The court identified (implicitly, through the facts it emphasized) several evidentiary anchors that weigh heavily in nexus analysis:

- Persecutor statements explicitly referencing political views or electoral opposition at the time of harm. In mixed-motive contexts, contemporaneous proclamations by attackers that name politics—or identify the victim’s party or candidate—as a reason for violence are powerful. Absent that, attributions to being a “snitch” or interfering with a criminal enterprise strengthen a nonpolitical inference.

- Temporal linkage between political activity and escalation. If escalation closely follows a political act (speech, rally, vote mobilization) rather than a separate private dispute, that sequencing supports a political nexus. Here, the court stressed escalation occurred after the property dispute.

- Evidence tying the persecutor to political actors or organized repression of the victim’s party. If attackers are shown to act on behalf of political actors, or if actions are coordinated with government or party-linked forces targeting the applicant for political reasons, that tends to show political motive. By contrast, the record here showed law enforcement had tried to address the property and did not indicate a state-orchestrated attack.

- Patterns of targeting that align with political identity. Demonstrating that members of the same party, in the same area, were targeted for their political activity (and not for personal disputes) helps establish that political opinion was central. Although petitioners here had colleagues who received threats, the court found no evidence these threats were linked to the narco-actor or to systematic political targeting.

- Evidence that state actors were complicit or indifferent toward politically motivated attacks. Showing governmental alignment with persecutors—or systemic failure to protect those targeted for political reasons—can reinforce a political nexus. The Tenth Circuit found opposite evidence: the government had attempted searches at the implicated property, and the police interaction described by petitioners was too limited to show systemic unwillingness to protect political actors.

Downstream implications

Jimenez v. Bondi underscores a practical point for practitioners and adjudicators: motive is often in the details. Where a petitioner’s political identity coexists with law-related activities (e.g., prosecutorial work, property disputes involving criminal actors), successful nexus proof will usually require more than party membership and generalized threats. Claimants should develop evidence that links threats or attacks directly to political opinion—through assailant statements, contemporaneous documentation, patterns of political targeting, or proof of persecutor-state alignment. Conversely, adjudicators will likely view threats tied to interference with criminal enterprises as nonpolitical retaliation unless the record demonstrates otherwise.

The decision also highlights how the “one central reason” test can be outcome-determinative in borderland cases between political persecution and ordinary criminal violence. In countries where criminal groups have local political influence, that line will be litigated repeatedly. Jimenez suggests courts will scrutinize sequencing, actor statements, and affirmative police response when parsing motive—placing a premium on contemporaneous evidence and on showing how political activism specifically triggered harm.

Conclusion

Jimenez is a reminder that the mixed-motive framework is not an invitation to infer protected-ground motive from proximity alone. Political affiliation plus threats is not always enough; petitioners need to anchor the persecutor’s intent in the record. For claimants who operate at the intersection of politics and law enforcement or who tangle with criminal enterprises, the path to asylum runs through concrete, contemporaneous indicators that political opinion was at least one central reason for the harm—not merely a background characteristic.



## Unwilling or Unable, or Just Unreported? The Reporting Requirement After Singh v. Bondi

Why this matters: Jimenez v. Bondi is the latest Tenth Circuit decision to sharpen the asylum calculus around the “unable or unwilling” prong of refugee law, and it underscores the court’s insistence that claimants engage with domestic authorities—or at least explain convincingly why they could not. In a case involving credible threats, a kidnapping and murder of a political colleague, and repeated warnings from men tied to a narco-trafficking leader, the court nevertheless upheld the Board of Immigration Appeals’ denial of asylum primarily because Petitioners did not show that Colombian authorities were given adequate notice or had a meaningful opportunity to intervene. Jimenez builds on Singh v. Bondi and several recent precedents to refine the evidentiary contours of the futility/danger exception to the reporting requirement. The practical stakes are significant: for applicants fleeing nonstate actors with alleged government connections, the decision signals that one-off conversations with officers or vague allegations of corruption are unlikely by themselves to carry the day.

What the court did: The Tenth Circuit applied the well-worn three-part test for asylum based on past persecution and an inability or unwillingness of the government to protect. Rather than contesting credibility, the court accepted the IJ’s and BIA’s factual findings: multiple threats, an assault on an employee, and an alarming encounter with an armed man and the petitioners’ son. But it concluded that these harms—though scary—did not rise to “persecution” as the circuit’s precedent defines the term when considered in the aggregate, and more critically, that Petitioners failed to show government inability or unwillingness to protect.

The court’s unwilling-or-unable analysis leaned heavily on Singh. Two features of that reasoning are worth flagging. First, the court insists a key empirical input is whether the applicant reported past persecution to the authorities because without such notice “there is no way to know how the police would have reacted.” Second, while acknowledging a futility/danger exception exists, the panel treated minimal contact with government or isolated instances of police apathy as insufficient to demonstrate a systemic failure or protective impotence. The record here contained limited police contact—an officer who told the family to leave, and another who said nothing could be done—but the court found these episodes did not establish that the Colombian government as a whole was unwilling or unable to act, particularly where there was independent evidence that authorities had investigated the property connected to the narco-traffickers.

The doctrinal takeaways

1) Reporting as a central evidentiary focal point. Jimenez reiterates a trend in Tenth Circuit case law: reporting to authorities (or documenting why reporting was futile/dangerous) is often the hinge on which an inability/unwillingness claim turns. Courts will weigh whether the government had meaningful notice and opportunity to act; absent notice, a petitioner bears the burden of showing that reporting would have been futile, dangerous, or otherwise unreasonable.

2) Narrow scope for “one-off” evidence of police inaction. The opinion signals skepticism about elevating isolated instances of police apathy into proof of systemic complicity. A single unhelpful officer’s comment falls short of demonstrating a widespread inability/unwillingness, especially when other record evidence suggests authorities at least attempted to address related criminality (as with the searches and seizures at the implicated property).

3) Futility/danger remains viable but fact-sensitive. The court did not eliminate the futility/danger exception; rather, it demanded a fuller record to invoke it. To persuade the BIA and federal appeals courts, applicants will need to develop evidence showing (a) plausible reasons why reporting would have been useless or put them at greater risk; (b) corroboration of those reasons (e.g., prior retaliation against reporters, systemic corruption reports, documented collusion between state actors and persecutors); and (c) specific efforts—to the extent feasible—to seek protection beyond a single conversation in their immediate neighborhood.

Practical implications for practitioners and adjudicators

For advocates litigating asylum claims that involve powerful nonstate actors or alleged government collusion, Jimenez provides a checklist of what the record must contain to make the futility/danger exception credible. It’s not enough to assert general corruption or to note country reports; counsel should gather contemporaneous police reports (even if unsuccessful), written communications, affidavits from witnesses who tried and were rebuffed, documentation of prior reprisals against complainants in the locality, and any efforts to seek higher-level or alternative protective channels (e.g., judicial complaints, prosecutor contacts, human-rights NGOs). When reporting is genuinely too dangerous to attempt, detailed testimony explaining why—and corroboration if possible—will be crucial.

For adjudicators, Jimenez is a reminder to scrutinize whether an applicant has provided sufficient evidence that the government had notice and an opportunity to act, and whether exceptions to reporting are adequately substantiated. It also cautions against overreliance on broad country-condition materials to substitute for specific facts showing a petitioner’s inability to access protection.

Broader doctrinal ripple effects

Jimenez may encourage a modest evidentiary arms race in asylum litigation: petitioners will need to do more to document failed avenues of domestic protection, and respondents will emphasize gaps in contemporaneous reporting. The decision also tightens the stream through which politically motivated violence by nonstate actors becomes asylum-qualifying—particularly when those actors have alleged ties to the state. That can be a double-edged sword: it protects against speculative claims but risks excluding genuine refugees who, for fear of retaliation or because of community-level collusion, reasonably did not—or could not—engage authorities in any documented way.

Conclusion

Jimenez v. Bondi reaffirms the centrality of notice-and-opportunity to the “unable or unwilling” inquiry and clarifies that the futility/danger exception is no panacea. Asylum applicants fleeing nonstate actors with apparent governmental ties must build a record that goes beyond unhelpful police comments or general assertions of corruption. The Tenth Circuit’s approach balances deference to the BIA’s factfinding with a demand for concrete proof of systemic protection failures. Practitioners should take the ruling as a roadmap: if reporting is impossible, explain why in detail and back it up; if reporting happened and failed, document the failure thoroughly.



## Relocate and Deny: Internal Relocation as a Backstop in Colombian Narco Cases

Why this case matters
The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi is a compact but consequential application of familiar asylum rules to a factual pattern increasingly common in Colombian claims: campaign activity and professional contact with narco-linked actors, repeated threats, a violent colleague, and at least one frightening encounter with a child. The court denied relief largely by treating internal relocation as a dispositive backstop. Even accepting many of the Petitioners’ harms and the presence of violent criminal actors, the panel concluded that (1) the mistreatment did not amount to past persecution, (2) any political motive was tangential to a private dispute with a narco-affiliate, and (3) the Colombian state was neither unable nor unwilling to protect the family — and, crucially, that reasonable internal relocation remained available.

Practitioners and judges should take notice: when government protection and nexus are in doubt or marginal, courts will often resolve asylum claims on the relocation prong, especially where the record includes family ties elsewhere in the country or where the applicants have already spent time away from the locus of danger without harm. Jimenez underscores how courts apply the substantial-evidence standard to hold petitioners to a concrete showing of national reach, systemic government complicity, or relocation impracticability.

How the court reasoned
The opinion follows the standard three-part asylum inquiry (persecution, nexus, government protection), but devotes considerable attention to whether internal relocation is “reasonable and feasible” as an alternative to asylum. Key factual predicates for the court’s relocation conclusion were that the family had relatives living safely in Colombia, that they had previously lived in the Bogota countryside for three weeks without incident, and that the assailants’ threats appeared geographically localized — “move out of the neighborhood,” not “leave the country.” The court also stressed gaps in Petitioners’ efforts to seek state protection: they spoke to two police officers who were unhelpful, but did not make broader use of available reporting channels or Jimenez’s government contacts, and they offered no evidence that police inaction was systemic rather than isolated.

Doctrinal takeaways
Jimenez reaffirms several settled propositions while illuminating how they interact in cases involving powerful criminal actors:

- Internal relocation remains central, and often dispositive, once an asylum applicant’s evidence of past persecution or state inability is less than compelling. The government may rebut a well-founded fear by showing that geographic relocation within the home country is reasonable under the totality of the circumstances.

- Courts expect more than generalized claims that criminals are “closely aligned” with officials; claimants must develop the record showing systemic corruption, pervasive collusion, or specific, credible evidence that reporting would be futile or dangerous. Anecdotal evidence — a single unhelpful officer or hearsay of government ties — typically falls short.

- Evidence of actual, successful relocation within the country is powerful. Jimenez’s temporary, unharmful stay in the countryside and the continued safety of relatives greatly diminished the Petitioners’ claim that relocation was not an option.

- The panel applied the familiar mixed-motive and “central reason” principles for political-opinion nexus: even if campaign activity preceded threats, an intervening private dispute that threatened a narco-actor’s revenue or security can explain escalation and render political opinion tangential.

What the opinion means for national-reach and narco-trafficker claims
A party arguing that narco-traffickers have national reach or enjoy protection from top political actors must ensure the record contains more than assertions. Courts look for evidence showing either (a) the criminal group has demonstrated capability and intent to pursue victims nationwide (e.g., repeated contacts or attacks in multiple locales, threats indicating national scope), or (b) government complicity beyond isolated police apathy (e.g., documentation of investigations stymied by officials, credible reports of collusion, official statements, or corroborating country-condition evidence showing systemic failure to control the group).

Jimenez illustrates that where the record shows targeted wrongdoing tied to a discrete property dispute and threats limited to a home neighborhood, courts will be reluctant to infer a nationwide reach sufficient to render relocation unreasonable. Nor will courts credit speculative claims that administrative burdens or economic hardships make relocation impracticable unless those claims are developed and coupled with evidence that relocation would not mitigate the risk.

What claimants must show to defeat the relocation prong
To avoid defeat on internal relocation grounds in comparable Colombian narco cases, practitioners should focus on building a robust, multi-faceted record:

- Documented attempts to report: show that claimants (or people in similar circumstances) actually reported threats and received systemic non-response, intimidation by officials, or active collusion. Specifics matter: dates, officers’ names (if available), written complaints, and follow-up (or lack thereof).

- Evidence of national reach: demonstrate that persecutors have followed victims across regions or that their organization operates with impunity beyond a single municipality. Police reports, news coverage, NGO or UN reports, and expert declarations about the particular group’s modus operandi help here.

- Corroborated country conditions: rely on up-to-date, detailed reports (State Department, Human Rights Watch, local human-rights groups) describing the group’s ties to state actors or systemic failures in law enforcement.

- Economic and administrative barriers: if relocation is assertedly impracticable for economic or bureaucratic reasons, develop concrete proof — inability to obtain housing, work authorization, identity documents, or children’s enrollment in school in the alternative locale — and show those barriers are not merely hypothetical or common-sense hurdles.

- Demonstrate futility or danger in reporting: if filing a police report would have placed the family at increased risk, establish that with specific facts or patterns indicating reporting triggers retaliation.

Practical implications and litigation strategy
Given Jimenez, practitioners should litigate relocation early and explicitly. Don’t rely on general assertions about narco reach or political collusion that are only cursorily briefed. When nexus and persecution are borderline, make relocation the battleground: identify and undermine the government’s proposed alternative locales, show why those places are unsafe in fact (not just in theory), and support claims with contemporaneous, corroborative evidence.

At the same time, counsel should not abandon efforts to prove past persecution, nexus, and failure of state protection. A strong showing on those fronts remains the most direct path to relief. But when those elements are weaker, careful development of relocation evidence — especially evidence showing either (a) lack of viable safe havens or (b) demonstrable nationwide reach by persecutors — can keep the asylum claim alive.

Conclusion
Jimenez v. Bondi is a reminder that internal relocation is not an afterthought; it is an integrated component of asylum adjudication that frequently decides cases involving organized criminal actors. To defeat relocation, claimants must produce a concrete, well-documented record showing that moving elsewhere in the home country would not meaningfully reduce risk, would be impossible, or would be futile because of systemic government complicity. In the surge of immigration claims tied to narco-trafficking and local political engagement, the evidentiary line the Tenth Circuit draws in Jimenez will be one litigants cannot afford to ignore.
