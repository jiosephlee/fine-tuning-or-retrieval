Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural Posture

This case involves Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons—Colombian nationals who left Colombia for the United States in December 2022 and later applied for asylum, withholding of removal, and protection under the Convention Against Torture. The family’s narrative centers on political activity and a subsequent conflict with a local criminal actor known as Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, “Don Rafa.” Their claims before the immigration authorities rest on threats and harassment that they say flowed from both political involvement and Jimenez’s professional interaction with Don Rafa.

Petitioners trace the outset of their problems to long-standing political activity. Since 2012 the family had members in the Colombian Liberal Party, and in 2022 Jimenez and others went door-to-door campaigning for Federico Gutiérrez in the presidential contest. Beginning around late May 2022, Jimenez began receiving threatening phone calls concerning his party involvement; other local Liberal Party activists also received threats, and one colleague who had reported similar threats was later kidnapped and murdered. Although these earlier threats and the colleague’s death alarmed the family, Petitioners continued campaign work at that time.

The conflict with Don Rafa arose out of what, on its face, was a routine property matter. In June 2022 a woman from San Bernardo asked Jimenez—then a provisional lawyer and a law student who also interned with Colombia’s National Prosecutor’s office—to intervene in a dispute over unpaid rent and possession of a property. Jimenez learned that the tenant occupying the property was the client’s ex-husband and that the occupant was Rafael (“Don Rafa”), whom Jimenez identified as a leader of a narco-trafficking guerrilla group. Jimenez observed indicia of illicit use at the premises: the health department had sealed the third floor, reports tied the location to migrant day-rentals, trafficking, prostitution, and episodes of kidnapping and murder, and police had made several—but apparently unsuccessful—attempts at searches and seizures. A local officer told Jimenez to steer clear of the property.

Once Don Rafa’s connection to the property was known, Petitioners’ encounters with intimidation escalated. Late in June 2022 Jimenez received a brochure at his furniture factory reading, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” In July two men accosted Jimenez at the factory at night, held him at gunpoint, and instructed him to leave the neighborhood, accusing him of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office. About two months later, while the family was out of town, the same men returned to the factory, assaulted an employee, again brandished a firearm, demanded the family’s whereabouts, and threatened to kill them—telling the employee that the order came from Don Rafa. Days after that incident, Jimenez confronted a man who put his arm over his younger son’s mouth outside the child’s school and told the boy to “run to your dad.” After that incident the family temporarily moved to Jimenez’s mother’s house in the Bogota countryside for three weeks, and then decided to depart for the United States.

Petitioners made limited overtures to Colombian authorities. Jimenez told a police patrol officer about the incident with his son, and the officer told him little could be done and advised the family to leave rather than seek further trouble; Petitioners read this to mean that seeking police assistance would be futile. There is also evidence in the record that law enforcement knew of the problematic property and had attempted searches and seizures. Petitioners, and members of their family who work in government institutions, did not otherwise file formal complaints or seek broader assistance beyond the neighborhood-level contacts described at the hearing.

Procedurally, after arriving in the United States without admission or parole, DHS served the family with individual Notices to Appear alleging removability under the Immigration and Nationality Act. At a removal hearing in August 2023 the Petitioners admitted the truth of DHS’s allegations and conceded removability. They applied for asylum, withholding of removal, and CAT protection; the two minor sons proceeded as derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s asylum claim.

At the merits hearing the immigration judge (IJ) found Petitioners credible but denied relief. The IJ concluded that, even taking their testimony and supporting evidence as true, Petitioners had not shown that the harm they endured amounted to past persecution, had not established a nexus between the mistreatment and a statutorily protected ground (political opinion or membership in a particular social group), had not shown that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them, and had not demonstrated that internal relocation in Colombia was unreasonable or infeasible. The IJ also denied Petitioners’ CAT claim and rejected their due-process argument concerning counsel. Petitioners appealed to the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA), which—through a single member—affirmed and dismissed the appeal.

On appeal to the Tenth Circuit, Petitioners limited their challenge to the BIA’s denial of asylum and withholding of removal; they did not pursue the CAT denial or the due-process claim. The Tenth Circuit therefore reviewed the BIA’s factual findings under the substantial-evidence standard, with the ability to consult the IJ’s fuller explanation, and analyzed whether the record compelled a contrary conclusion on past persecution, nexus to a protected ground, government protection, and internal relocation. The court ultimately concluded that substantial evidence supported the BIA’s and IJ’s conclusions and denied the petition for review.
