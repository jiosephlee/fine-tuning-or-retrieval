Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 3: # Past Persecution Analysis

The court’s past-persecution analysis asks whether the harm the Jimenez family endured—viewed as a whole—meets the legal threshold of “persecution” under Tenth Circuit precedent and the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). This section explains the governing legal standard, walks through how the IJ and the BIA (and ultimately the Tenth Circuit) applied that standard to the petitioners’ specific incidents, and summarizes why the court concluded the record contains substantial evidence supporting the agency finding that the family did not suffer past persecution.

Definition and contours: persecution requires more than restrictions or threats to life and liberty

The INA does not define “persecution,” so courts apply the precedent-developed meaning. The Tenth Circuit has emphasized that persecution requires the infliction of suffering or harm that is “regarded as offensive” because of a protected characteristic and that it must be more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats to life and liberty. In other words, the harm must be qualitatively and quantitatively serious enough to go beyond ordinary harassment, intimidation, or isolated criminal acts. This is a fact-intensive inquiry, and multiple lesser incidents can be aggregated—if their cumulative effect reaches the requisite level. But courts will not treat every threat, disturbance, or nonsevere harm as persecution; context and severity matter.

Cumulative assessment of incidents

The petitioners presented a sequence of hostile acts and threats spanning several months: periodic threatening phone calls; a threatening brochure stating “death to snitches from Liberal Party”; an armed, in-person confrontation at the furniture factory in which two men held Jimenez at gunpoint and ordered him to leave the neighborhood; a later assault on a factory employee combined with another gun threat seeking the petitioners’ whereabouts; and a frightening near-abduction scare in which a man put his arm around the petitioners’ younger son, covered the boy’s mouth, and told him to “run to your dad.” The family also moved temporarily to a relative’s home in the countryside and eventually left Colombia after a police officer told them to do so.

When the IJ and BIA evaluated these incidents, they considered the totality of events rather than isolating each act. That cumulative approach is consistent with precedent allowing a court to assess whether the combined effect of multiple hostile acts amounts to persecution. The key questions were whether the incidents, taken together, inflicted sufficient harm—physical, economic, psychological, or social—to qualify as persecution and whether those harms were enduring or severe.

Concession of no physical injury and lack of lasting physical harm

A critical factual concession influenced the court’s analysis: at the merits hearing petitioners admitted they were not physically harmed, and their opening brief acknowledged they experienced no lasting physical injury. The Tenth Circuit recognizes that physical injury is not a necessary component of persecution, but the absence of physical harm reduces the likelihood that threats and harassment alone will meet the persecution threshold. Because the petitioners conceded no bodily injury, the court focused on whether the nonphysical harms—threats, forced relocation, and psychological distress—could collectively amount to persecution. That concession severely limited their path to prevailing, since many successful persecution claims rest on either actual physical attacks or serious, prolonged abuse.

Emotional and psychological harm claims and the court’s treatment

Petitioners relied in part on emotional and psychological harms: Duarte’s insomnia and the children’s panic attacks and stress. The court acknowledged these harms as unfortunate and weighty in the family’s lived experience, but it applied Tenth Circuit precedent holding that psychological harms unaccompanied by sufficiently severe physical attacks or other objectively egregious incidents rarely suffice to prove persecution. The court pointed to prior decisions in which comparable emotional distress arising from threats, short-term detention, or other nonsevere mistreatment was deemed legally insufficient. The central reasoning is twofold: (1) the persecution inquiry is objective and requires harms that courts recognize as substantially injurious in the relevant legal sense, and (2) without physical injury or other indicia of severe, prolonged targeting, psychological harm—however real—does not typically cross the persecution line in this circuit.

Comparative case law: threats alone and the circuit’s approach

The opinion repeatedly cites Tenth Circuit authorities that illustrate a consistent theme: threats, even when repeated and menacing, have often been held legally inadequate on their own. Karki is cited for the proposition that threats alone ordinarily do not constitute persecution. The court also referenced unpublished precedents in which threat patterns and other adverse experiences—such as a ten-hour detention combined with threats or multiple thefts and machete threats—were still not enough to establish persecution. These comparative cases show the court’s reluctance to expand the persecution category to include many common forms of political or criminal intimidation unless the facts show a severe level of harm, an extended campaign of abuse, or physical injury and lasting consequences.

Application to the Jimenez facts and conclusion

Applying these principles, the Tenth Circuit concluded that substantial evidence supports the agency’s finding that the petitioners’ experiences, though troubling and disruptive, did not rise to the level of persecution. The court emphasized several interrelated reasons: the absence of any physical injury, the petitioners’ own concession of no lasting bodily harm, the nature of the threats (which, although menacing, were not shown to have produced coercive, prolonged physical abuse), and the comparative case law holding similar or more extreme threat-patterns insufficient. The court also noted that petitioners’ continuation of political activity after earlier threats indicated a lack of directly linked, escalating state-actor persecution prior to the property dispute—factual nuances relevant to whether the mistreatment was so severe and pervasive as to be legally “persecution.”

Taken together, those factual findings and legal benchmarks led the court to affirm the BIA and IJ: the cumulative incidents—threatening calls and brochure, the factory gunpoint episode, the assault on an employee, and the near-abduction scare involving the child—while serious, did not objectively amount to persecution under Tenth Circuit law. Because the denial of past-persecution was supported by substantial evidence, the petitioners could not invoke the rebuttable presumption of future persecution that a finding of past persecution would have produced, and the court accordingly denied review of their asylum claim on that ground.
