Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### How does the Tenth Circuit review BIA decisions when a single Board member affirms—what parts of the IJ’s reasoning can the court rely on?
Question:
The opinion says it reviews the BIA’s decision but can consult the IJ’s 'more complete explanation' (citing Uanreroro). I’m confused about the practical scope: if the BIA issues a short affirmance focusing on a couple of grounds, can the court look to additional rationales discussed only by the IJ to uphold the result, or is it limited to the specific grounds the BIA expressly adopted? How does this interact with the substantial-evidence standard and the 'any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled' language?
Answer:
Short answer
When a single Board member issues a brief “affirm” of an IJ’s decision, the Tenth Circuit reviews the BIA’s decision. But it may consult the IJ’s “more complete explanation” only to the extent the IJ’s reasoning explains the same grounds the BIA actually affirmed. The court may not sustain the BIA’s order on new, independent rationales that appear only in the IJ’s opinion and were not adopted (even implicitly) by the BIA. All factual findings remain subject to the substantial‑evidence test—they are conclusive unless “any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.”

Why and how this works (doctrine and practical scope)
- Governing framework in the opinion: Jimenez v. Bondi follows the familiar rule: “we review the BIA’s decision, but we are not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds.” (quoting Uanreroro v. Gonzales). So the BIA is the primary decision reviewed; the IJ’s opinion is a subsidiary interpretive aid for the same issues the BIA relied on.
- What “those same grounds” means: the court may reach into the IJ’s fuller explanation to understand or flesh out the legal or factual bases that the BIA affirmed. But the court’s reliance must be confined to explanations that map onto the grounds the BIA actually adopted. If the BIA explicitly affirmed on ground X (or its order necessarily rests on X), the Tenth Circuit can consult the IJ’s discussion of X to determine whether substantial evidence supports the BIA’s finding on X.
- What the court cannot do: the court should not sustain the BIA’s order by adopting a new, independent rationale that appears only in the IJ’s decision if that rationale was not part of the BIA’s affirmation. In short, the IJ’s separate or alternative legal theories cannot bootstrap a different basis for affirmance that the BIA did not itself endorse.
- Interaction with the substantial‑evidence standard: factual findings reviewed under substantial evidence are “conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.” The practical effect: when consulting the IJ’s fuller explanation for the BIA‑affirmed ground, the court asks whether substantial evidence in the administrative record supports that ground such that no reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to reach a contrary factual finding. The IJ’s explanation is useful for parsing the record and identifying the factfinding the BIA affirmed, but it does not change the standard of review or permit the court to substitute its own view of the evidence.
Practical examples based on Jimenez
- If the BIA issued a short affirmance that mentions only “past persecution” and “nexus,” the Tenth Circuit can consult the IJ’s extended analysis of past persecution and nexus to determine whether substantial evidence supports the BIA’s determinations on those topics.
- If the IJ also discussed, say, an alternative ground (e.g., that government protection failed for a particular systemic reason) but the BIA’s short order did not adopt that rationale or indicate reliance on it, the court should not sustain the BIA by relying on that IJ‑only rationale.
Key takeaways for briefing and decision‑writing
- For petitioners: when the BIA issues a short affirmance, preserve arguments on every plausible ground because the court will treat the BIA as the decisionmaker and will only borrow the IJ’s reasoning to the extent it matches the BIA’s stated or necessarily adopted grounds.
- For adjudicators: if the BIA intends to adopt an IJ rationale, a brief notation or explanation tying the BIA’s affirmance to that particular IJ ground avoids ambiguity and prevents appellate courts from declining to consider the IJ’s independent rationale.
Conclusion
In Jimenez the Tenth Circuit follows Uanreroro: it reviews the BIA’s decision but may consult the IJ’s “more complete explanation” only to illuminate the exact grounds the BIA affirmed. Factual findings remain subject to the substantial‑evidence test—they stand unless the record would compel any reasonable adjudicator to conclude otherwise—and the court will not uphold the BIA on new rationales that appear only in the IJ’s opinion.

### What threshold for 'past persecution' did the court apply to repeated death threats and an apparent attempted child abduction?
Question:
The court emphasizes that 'threats alone' usually aren’t persecution and notes no lasting physical harm. But the record includes: (1) repeated threatening calls, (2) a death-threat brochure targeting Liberal Party 'snitches,' (3) two armed men twice confronting and threatening the family/employee, and (4) a man grabbing the child at school before the father intervened. How does the Tenth Circuit weigh the cumulative effect of armed confrontations and an apparent attempted abduction when there’s no actual injury? Are there Tenth Circuit cases where similar non-injury but highly menacing conduct did cross the persecution line?
Answer:
Short answer
The Tenth Circuit applied the settled (and relatively stringent) rule that threats, even violent‑sounding ones, are not enough by themselves to show past persecution — you must show “suffering or harm” that is “more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty.” The court weighed the incidents cumulatively, but found the aggregate here (repeated calls and brochures, two armed confrontations, and the man who grabbed the child but caused no lasting physical injury) still fell short because there was no physical harm or persistent, offensive injury and the threats were tied more to a property dispute than a protected ground. The opinion emphasizes that the court “has rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone.” (Jimenez v. Bondi, No. 24‑9573, 10th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025).

What legal standard the court applied
- Persecution requires “the infliction of suffering or harm … and must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty.” (citing Wiransane v. Ashcroft and Jimenez, § II.C).
- The court must consider incidents collectively — cumulative effects can constitute persecution — but even cumulative threats must produce offensive harm, not merely fear or stress. (Ritonga cited in Jimenez).
- Threats alone are usually insufficient. The opinion cites Karki: “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution,” and notes that physical harm is not strictly required, but that courts have “rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone.” (Jimenez § II.C (collecting cases including Karki, Brandy, Chhetri, Carias‑Mejia)).

How the court weighed these facts in Jimenez
- The court expressly evaluated the brochure, repeated phone threats, two armed in‑person confrontations, and the school incident together (it recounts Ritonga’s cumulative‑effects rule).
- Despite that aggregation, it found (1) Petitioners conceded no physical injury and no lasting physical harm; (2) the emotional/psychological harm (sleep loss, panic attacks) was insufficient by itself; and (3) prior Tenth Circuit precedent rejects asylum claims based primarily on threats or stress even when those incidents were severe or repeated. Thus the BIA’s denial was supported by substantial evidence. (Jimenez § II.C).
- The nexus and government‑protection findings buttressed the result: the threats appeared tied to Jimenez’s property dispute with a criminal leader (not primarily to political opinion), and the record did not show the government was unable or unwilling to help. Those points are relevant because even menacing conduct that is motivated by a protected ground and accompanied by state inability/unwillingness is more likely to be deemed persecution.

Are there Tenth Circuit precedents where non‑injury but menacing conduct crossed the line?
- The Jimenez opinion collects and relies on Tenth Circuit decisions that cut the other way. It cites Chhetri (four phone threats and one vague in‑person threat), Karki (telephone threats), Brandy, and Carias‑Mejia as examples where threatening or menacing conduct without lasting physical injury did not establish persecution. The court says courts have “rarely, if ever,” granted asylum on threats and psychological harm alone. (Jimenez § II.C).
- Jimenez does not identify a Tenth Circuit case where similar non‑injury but highly menacing conduct nevertheless met the persecution threshold. Its discussion implies that, within this circuit, mere threats plus episodic menacing conduct (absent real injury, persistent coercive control, or other offensive physical harm) are typically insufficient.

Practical takeaways for analogous claims
- Emphasize concrete, offensive harm (physical injury, sustained detention, severe bodily or mental suffering tied to a protected ground) when arguing past persecution.
- If the mistreatment is mostly threats or brief confrontations, develop strong evidence that (a) the conduct was persistent and coercive enough to amount to severe harm, (b) it was motivated centrally by a protected ground, and (c) the state was unable or unwilling to protect you — because the Tenth Circuit treats each element as crucial in close cases (as Jimenez demonstrates).

### Why did the court find the nexus to political opinion only 'tangential' despite explicit references to the Liberal Party?
Question:
Petitioners received a brochure stating 'death to snitches from Liberal Party,' and the men referenced Petitioners being 'in politics.' Yet the court concludes the 'predominant motive' was Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s criminal enterprise and his supposed cooperation with the prosecutor’s office. Under the 'at least one central reason' mixed-motive standard, how did the court determine that the political-opinion motive was subordinate rather than co-equal? What evidentiary markers pushed the court toward a personal/anti-informant motive over a political one?
Answer:
Short answer: the court treated the political references as incidental because the record shows the aggressors’ immediate and focal concern was Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s criminal enterprise (i.e., being a “snitch”/prosecutor’s collaborator), not the Petitioners’ partisan campaigning. Under the “at least one central reason” mixed‑motive test, the Tenth Circuit relied on timing, the substance of the perpetrators’ statements, and other contextual facts to conclude political opinion was subordinate.

Why that mattered under the law
- Legal standard: to establish persecution “on account of” political opinion, the applicant must show that political opinion was “at least one central reason” for the harm; it cannot be “incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate” to another motive. (Opinion quoting Rivera‑Barrientos and Dallakoti.)
- In mixed‑motive cases, multiple motives are possible, but the protected ground must be a central—not minor—reason for the targeting. (Opinion citing O.C.V. v. Bondi.)

Evidentiary markers the court relied on (all drawn from the opinion)
1. Temporal connection / escalation after the property dispute
   - The opinion emphasizes that the harassment “escalated” only after Jimenez intervened in the property dispute and after Don Rafa became aware Jimenez was investigating the rented property. The earlier politically framed threats did not intensify until Jimenez’s professional involvement with Don Rafa’s premises.

2. Content of the threats focused on “snitching” and prosecutor work
   - When confronted, the men accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for searches and seizures at Don Rafa’s property. The men’s explicit accusation tied the motive to his alleged cooperation with law enforcement rather than to his campaigning for the Liberal Party.

3. Perpetrators’ attribution to Don Rafa and criminal-revenge explanation
   - The same men later said the threats/orders “came from Don Rafa.” The BIA/IJ reasonably read these communications as signaling retaliation for interference with Don Rafa’s criminal operations (loss of revenue/security), not as politically motivated persecution.

4. Lack of evidence linking Don Rafa to prior political threats
   - The court noted Petitioners produced no record evidence that Don Rafa was behind the earlier threats tied to their party activity. That gap undercuts an inference that Don Rafa’s actions were politically driven.

5. Petitioners’ own conduct undermined a political‑opinion motive
   - Despite earlier politically flavored threats, Petitioners continued campaigning. The court saw this as inconsistent with being targeted primarily for partisan activity before the property dispute, suggesting the property dispute/prosecutor nexus explains the later escalation.

6. Context showing a law‑enforcement/anti‑informant dynamic
   - The record shows law enforcement had attempted searches/seizures of the property and a local officer warned Jimenez away; the men directly implicated searches/seizures as the grievance. That context points toward a reaction to perceived cooperation with prosecutors rather than to partisan disagreement.

How those markers map to the statutory test
- Because the contemporaneous statements and the timing squarely tie the perpetrators’ motive to Jimenez’s alleged role as a “snitch” and his interference with Don Rafa’s criminal enterprise, the BIA/IJ could reasonably conclude the political references were subordinate. Under the “at least one central reason” standard, such evidence supports treating political opinion as tangential rather than a central motive. The Tenth Circuit deferred to that factual determination under substantial‑evidence review.

Bottom line: explicit political language in some documents or earlier threats did not overcome the stronger, contemporaneous record evidence that the persecutors were acting primarily out of retaliation for alleged cooperation with prosecutors and interference with criminal activity. The court therefore found the political‑opinion motive subordinate, not co‑equal.

### What counts as showing the government is 'unable or unwilling' to protect if the applicants only spoke to individual officers and didn’t file reports?
Question:
The court faults Petitioners for minimal reporting and cites attempts by authorities to search the property as evidence against government-complicity. How much reporting is required to avoid the inference that the state might have helped if asked—does informing two patrol officers suffice? What kind of proof is needed to establish that filing a formal report would have been futile or dangerous (to fit the exception), especially when there’s some evidence of police action (attempted raids) but also localized warnings by officers to 'not seek trouble'?
Answer:
Short answer
Telling one or two patrol officers what happened—without more—generally will not prove the government was “unable or unwilling” to protect you. Jimenez makes clear that (1) the key inquiry is whether the state had notice and an opportunity to respond, and (2) a one‑off conversation with an unhelpful officer is not enough to show systemic inability or unwillingness. To fit the “futility/danger” exception you need concrete evidence showing that reporting would have been pointless or would have put you at greater risk (e.g., prior formal complaints ignored, documented official collusion, threats tied to police, or other objective proof that higher authorities would not or could not help).

Legal doctrine and how the court applied it in Jimenez
- Notice/opportunity to respond is central. The court cites Singh: whether a government is unable or unwilling hinges on its response to past persecution, and whether it had notice and an opportunity to act. Without notice, there is no basis to infer how police would have reacted. (Jimenez, §E (citing Singh, 130 F.4th at 861–62).)
- Reporting is therefore a key fact. The opinion repeats the familiar rule that failure to report can undercut an asylum claim because the record does not reveal how authorities would have responded. (Jimenez §E.)
- But reporting is not mechanically dispositive. The court acknowledges the futility/danger exception—an applicant may avoid reporting if they can show it would have been futile or dangerous—but stresses that the applicant must prove that exception with record evidence. (Jimenez §E (discussing Singh and Aviles‑Gonzalez).)
- Is speaking to two patrol officers enough? No, not here. Jimenez expressly held that the petitioners “spoke to two individual police officers who said that Petitioners should not seek trouble—but there is nothing to show that this reflects the Colombian government’s position.” The court relied on Osorio‑Morales and similar authority for the proposition that “a one‑off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect.” (Jimenez §E (citing Osorio‑Morales, 72 F.4th 738 (7th Cir. 2023)).)
- Evidence that undercuts the futility claim. The court contrasted the petitioners’ minimal reporting with independent evidence that Colombian authorities had attempted several searches and seizures of the property tied to the persecutors, which supported the conclusion that the government had in fact tried to address the criminal conduct. That objective evidence of law‑enforcement action weighed against finding government incapacity or unwillingness. (Jimenez §A & §E.)

What you need to show futility or danger in practice
- Objective evidence that reporting had no effect: prior formal complaints or police reports showing no action, records of investigations that were closed without result, police logs, or correspondence demonstrating inaction.
- Evidence of official complicity or collusion: documented links between perpetrators and government actors (e.g., records, media or NGO reports, witness statements showing officials protected or coordinated with persecutors).
- Evidence that reporting would increase risk: prior instances where victims who reported were targeted, or explicit threats tied to the act of reporting.
- Evidence that the unhelpful officers reflected systemic failure—not just one apathetic patrolman: multiple complaints to different levels ignored, complaints to supervisory/internal affairs and no response, or contemporaneous public/statewide evidence of police corruption in the relevant locality.
- Where available, seek corroboration from independent sources (NGO reports, media, official documents, affidavits from others who reported and were ignored).

Practical implications for fact‑development and advocacy
- Filing a formal police report or complaint (and preserving documentation) is strong proof of notice and gives the government an opportunity to act. If clients did not do that, develop proof showing why they reasonably refrained (threats, prior ineffective reporting, documented corruption).
- If only informal reports occurred (e.g., telling patrol officers), try to obtain: names/badges, contemporaneous notes, any written acknowledgment, witness statements, or proof that higher authorities were or were not informed.
- Collect objective evidence of government action (or lack of it) in the particular locale: records of raids, prosecutions, or, conversely, reports of police collusion.
- Where possible, show that unhelpful comments came from officials whose statements reflect official policy (e.g., supervisors, internal affairs, prosecutors) rather than a single patrolman—because Jimenez distinguishes between one‑off patrol apathy and systemic inability/unwillingness.

Bottom line
Jimenez treats a couple of conversations with patrol officers—and no formal reporting—as weak evidence that the state had notice and failed to protect. To invoke the futility/danger exception you must do more: produce record evidence that reporting would have been futile or dangerous, or otherwise show systemic failure or collusion. Absent that, courts will generally expect applicants to have given the government a real chance to respond.

### Who bears the burden on internal relocation in the absence of past persecution, and how did the court weigh the short-term countryside move?
Question:
Since the court found no past persecution, I understand the burden stays with Petitioners to show relocation isn’t reasonable. The panel highlights that (1) relatives remain unharmed, (2) Petitioners briefly stayed safely with Jimenez’s mother, and (3) the threats said 'leave the neighborhood' rather than the country. How do courts evaluate whether a brief three-week period in a different area is probative of long-term safety? What factors (reach of the persecutor, economic/administrative barriers, family ties, size of country) are most persuasive in the Tenth Circuit relocation analysis?
Answer:
Short answer
- Burden: When an applicant has not established past persecution, the applicant retains the burden to show that internal relocation within the home country is not reasonable. The government can rebut a well‑founded fear by proving by a preponderance that the applicant could avoid persecution by relocating (Rivera‑Barrientos / 8 C.F.R. §1208.13(b)(1)(i)); here the Tenth Circuit treated relocation as the petitioners’ burden to disprove once past persecution was not found.
- The three‑week countryside stay: The court treated the brief, safe stay with Jimenez’s mother as probative (not dispositive) evidence that internal relocation was feasible. It relied on that episode together with other facts (family in country unharmed; the threats were to “leave the neighborhood,” not the country; and evidence the government had taken some action against the criminal enterprise) to conclude relocation was reasonable.

Legal doctrine and how the court applied it
- Legal standard: If an applicant lacks past persecution (and thus lacks the rebuttable presumption of future persecution), the government may defeat an asylum claim by showing either (1) a fundamental change in circumstances or (2) that the applicant could safely relocate within the country. The relocation inquiry asks whether, under the totality of the evidence, relocation is reasonable (Rivera‑Barrientos; 8 C.F.R. §1208.13(b)(1)(i)). The BIA’s factual findings on this point are reviewed under the substantial‑evidence standard.
- Burden allocation in this case: The panel framed the issue consistent with that framework — because Petitioners failed to show past persecution, the reasonableness of internal relocation remained for them to show was impossible or unreasonable. The court upheld the BIA/IJ’s finding that the government had met its burden to show relocation was feasible.

Factors the Tenth Circuit found persuasive in Jimenez v. Bondi
The opinion shows which facts the court found most probative in deciding relocation was reasonable. Key factors (and how the court weighed them):

1) Evidence of safe short‑term relocation with relatives
- The family’s three‑week move to Jimenez’s mother’s home in the Bogota countryside was treated as meaningful evidence that the family could live safely elsewhere in Colombia. The court cited that episode directly in concluding relocation was feasible.

2) Continued safety of other family/relatives in country
- The fact that relatives remained in Colombia unharmed (and Jimenez’s father still worked in the criminal‑justice system) reinforced the conclusion that relocation could be safe.

3) Scope and content of the threats
- The court emphasized the content of the threats: the assailants told them to “leave the neighborhood,” not the country. That limited geographic scope supported a finding that moving within Colombia could mitigate the risk.

4) Government response and capacity
- Evidence that authorities had investigated the property used by Don Rafa (attempted searches and seizures) undercut an argument that the government was uniformly unable or unwilling to protect the family. The court relied on this to support the reasonableness of relocation and to reject a claim that perpetrators were effectively state actors preventing internal flight.

5) Applicant’s efforts to seek help and notice to authorities
- The court stressed that petitioners did not meaningfully seek broader governmental protection beyond a few local officers and that there was no showing that seeking help would have been futile. Lack of stronger attempts to use police or other protective measures weakened their relocation claim.

6) Economic/administrative burdens and other hardships
- Petitioners’ assertions about economic or administrative barriers were addressed but described as haphazardly briefed and insufficiently developed in the record; the court therefore did not treat them as persuasive.

7) Reach of the persecutor
- The court evaluated whether the persecutor’s reach made relocation unreasonable: the record showed localized threats tied to a property dispute with Don Rafa rather than evidence the persecutor effectively controlled the entire country or would pursue them nationwide.

How courts treat brief relocations generally (as illustrated here)
- A short, safe stay is circumstantial evidence that internal relocation is feasible, but it is not automatically dispositive. Courts weigh that episode against other evidence of the persecutor’s reach, government protection, whether the applicant sought help, and any special economic or administrative obstacles to relocation.
- In Jimenez, the short countryside stay, combined with family ties and the localized nature of the threats and some government action against the criminal enterprise, persuaded the court that relocation was reasonable.

Practical takeaways for advocates / students
- If past persecution is not established, be prepared to contest relocation by developing record evidence on:
  - The persecutor’s national reach and intent to follow the applicant;
  - Objective proof of systemic government unwillingness/ inability to protect (not just one unhelpful officer);
  - Economic or administrative barriers that would make relocation unreasonable (with concrete evidence);
  - Whether short stays elsewhere actually demonstrate sustainable safety (duration, safety incidents, ability to work/live there, and whether relocation would sever indispensable family/support ties).
- In the Tenth Circuit, a demonstrably safe short stay with relatives plus evidence that threats were geographically limited can be highly persuasive that internal relocation is reasonable, unless the record shows the persecutor or the state’s failures make such relocation untenable.

### Did Petitioners waive their 'particular social group' theory by not briefing it, and what is required to preserve a PSG claim on appeal?
Question:
The opinion says Petitioners 'did not brief' arguments on a PSG and limits nexus analysis to political opinion. Is that treated as waiver/forfeiture at the circuit level due to inadequate briefing, or as failure to exhaust before the BIA? What level of specificity is required in the opening brief to preserve a PSG-based claim and its nexus theory in the Tenth Circuit?
Answer:
Short answer: The Tenth Circuit treated the Petitioners’ PSG theory as forfeited on appeal for inadequate briefing, not as an exhaustion defect before the BIA. To preserve a PSG claim (and the particular nexus theory tied to it) for review in this circuit, an appellant must raise and meaningfully brief the theory in the opening brief — i.e., define the group, explain why it is cognizable, identify the claimant’s membership, show how the record supports that membership and nexus, and cite legal authority and record evidence.

Why that is the Court’s treatment
- The opinion expressly says Petitioners “did not brief any arguments regarding the existence of their particular social group nor did they establish their membership in such a group. Rather, in arguing the nexus requirement, Petitioners allege that they were persecuted … because of their political opinion. Accordingly, we limit our review to whether Petitioners establish a nexus between their alleged persecution and their political opinion.” That is a classic appellate-forfeiture result: the court confines review to the issues the appellant actually briefed.
- The Court’s limitation of review to political opinion therefore flows from inadequate briefing on PSG, not from an independent BIA-exhaustion ruling. The opinion nowhere says the PSG theory was abandoned before the BIA or that exhaustion was the problem; it points to the absence of briefing and the absence of any record showing PSG membership.

What the Tenth Circuit requires to preserve a PSG claim on appeal
- Raise the issue concretely before the tribunal whose decision is being reviewed (here, the IJ/BIA) if exhaustion could be an issue. But as the opinion shows, even if the BIA addressed the claim, the circuit will treat a failure to brief on appeal as forfeiture.
- On appeal, include in the opening brief:
  - A clear definition of the proposed particular social group (sufficiently specific to meet cognizability requirements);
  - A statement and citation to where in the record the claimant’s membership in that group is shown (testimony, documents, BIA/IJ findings);
  - A developed legal argument why the group is legally cognizable and why the persecution was “on account of” membership in that group (i.e., nexus analysis);
  - Citations to controlling authorities and to the administrative record supporting the factual predicates (so the court can apply its substantial-evidence review).
- In short: you must do more than mention PSG in passing. The Court in Jimenez faulted the Petitioners for not briefing the existence of a PSG or establishing membership — those are the discrete points an opening brief must address to preserve the claim for review.

Practical guidance for an opening brief in the Tenth Circuit
- Define the PSG precisely (avoid purely descriptive or amorphous labels).
- Quote/point to the record showing membership and persecutory acts tied to membership.
- Explain (with authority) why the persecutor’s motives satisfy the “on account of” standard as applied to PSG membership.
- If you rely on mixed motives, brief why membership was at least one central reason.
- If the BIA rejected PSG on the merits, point to that BIA holding and explain why it’s deficient; if the BIA did not consider PSG, explain why the court should still review it.

Bottom line: Jimenez treats the PSG claim as forfeited because Petitioners failed to brief or establish it on appeal. To preserve a PSG-based argument in the Tenth Circuit, you must present a developed legal and factual argument in your opening brief showing the group’s definition, membership, and nexus with the persecution, supported by record citations and legal authority.

### Does denial of asylum always foreclose withholding of removal in mixed-motive or non-state-actor cases?
Question:
The court says Petitioners fail to meet the 'higher standard' for withholding because they didn’t establish a well-founded fear for asylum. I get that withholding requires a 'clear probability,' but are there scenarios where an applicant loses asylum on a discretionary or nexus ground yet still wins withholding (e.g., if one protected ground fails as 'not a central reason' but another theory could meet the withholding standard)? Or is the failure here categorical because the same factual deficiencies (no persecution, no nexus, state able to protect) defeat both forms of relief?
Answer:
Short answer: No — denial of asylum does not always categorically foreclose withholding of removal. But in Jimenez the court held that it did here because the same factual failures (no persecution, no nexus, government protection and safe internal relocation available) meant petitioners could not meet asylum’s well‑founded‑fear standard and therefore could not meet withholding’s higher “clear probability” standard.

Why: the opinion sets out the two different legal standards and why they matter. Asylum is discretionary and requires the applicant to prove refugee status by a preponderance of the evidence (i.e., past persecution or a well‑founded fear of future persecution on account of a protected ground). Withholding of removal requires a higher, mandatory showing that persecution is “more likely than not” (a clear probability). Because withholding demands a higher quantum of proof, a failure to establish a well‑founded fear for asylum will often (but not inevitably) doom a withholding claim. The court put it succinctly: petitioners “fail to demonstrate” persecution, nexus, or lack of government protection, and therefore “because Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well‑founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal.” (Jimenez v. Bondi, slip op. § II, concluding paragraph; see also § II.B–E on standards, persecution, and nexus).

When denial of asylum might not preclude withholding: the opinion recognizes (and cites precedent) that mixed‑motive persecution is possible — a persecutor can have multiple motives and a protected ground need only be “at least one central reason.” (Jimenez § II.D (quoting Rivera‑Barrientos)). By implication (and consistent with governing law the court cites), there are conceivable fact patterns where an applicant could lose asylum on a discretionary ground or fail to meet the asylum preponderance/well‑founded‑fear test as applied to one theory, but still satisfy withholding’s higher but distinct “clear probability” showing under a different protected‑ground theory or on a different evidentiary record. Jimenez does not announce a categorical bar; it applies the general rule (higher withholding standard) to the specific facts and evidentiary failures here (no past persecution, nexus tied to property dispute rather than political opinion, no showing government unwilling/unable).

Bottom line for practice: treat asylum and withholding as distinct claims with different burdens. A denial of asylum is strong evidence against withholding when the denial rests on core factual failures (no persecution, no nexus, or available state protection) — as in Jimenez — but it is not, as a matter of law, an absolute or automatic bar in every conceivable mixed‑motive or non‑state‑actor case.

### What happens to derivative beneficiaries’ claims if the principal’s asylum application fails—can the spouse or children prevail independently?
Question:
The children were derivative on Jimenez’s asylum application. If the principal applicant fails on past persecution/nexus/government-protection, can a derivative spouse or child still obtain asylum or withholding based on their own individualized risk (e.g., the child who was grabbed)? What procedural steps would have been needed to preserve independent claims for the family members in this posture?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — but only if the spouse/child actually pursued an independent asylum (or withholding) claim and developed separate evidence on the IJ record. Derivative beneficiaries do not automatically get asylum if the principal fails; a derivative can prevail only by showing, on the record before the IJ/BIA, that he or she independently meets the statutory elements (past persecution or well‑founded fear, nexus to a protected ground, and inability/unwillingness of the government to protect). If no independent claim is preserved and adjudicated, the derivative’s fate is tied to the principal.

Why: legal standard and what the Jimenez opinion shows
- Jimenez treats the family’s applications as a single adjudication in which the minor children “proceeded as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s application for asylum.” (Background, B. Procedural Background.) That posture means the children’s claims were adjudicated as derivatives of the principal’s application rather than as separate principal claims.
- The governing law requires an applicant (including a would‑be derivative who asserts an independent claim) to prove refugee status by a preponderance of the evidence: past persecution or a well‑founded fear of future persecution, nexus to a protected ground, and government inability/unwillingness to protect. (B. Legal Framework; Niang and Rivera‑Barrientos citations in the opinion.)
- The Tenth Circuit denied relief here because, on the existing record, Petitioners (collectively) failed to prove past persecution, nexus, government protection problems, and safe relocation. The court deferred to the IJ/BIA factual findings under substantial‑evidence review. Because the family failed under asylum standards, they necessarily failed withholding (the higher standard). (Conclusion; last paragraph of III.)

What a derivative needed to do to prevail independently (procedural steps to preserve independent claims)
1. File or request adjudication as a principal applicant (or at least make clear on the record that the derivative is asserting an independent asylum/withholding claim). In Jimenez the children were treated as derivatives/riders; to preserve an independent claim the family member must ensure the IJ treats them as an applicant whose merits are separately considered.
2. Develop separate, individualized evidence and testimony before the IJ about the derivative’s own harm and risk. For example, the younger son’s “grabbed” incident is an individualized event that could be pressed as past persecution or as evidence supporting a well‑founded fear — but it had to be developed as an independent claim in the record. The IJ in Jimenez relied on the record as presented (including credibility findings) and concluded the aggregate facts did not amount to persecution.
3. Argue nexus and government protection specific to that family member. The court emphasized the need to show that a protected ground was “at least one central reason” for the harm and looked to government notice/response. A derivative must therefore present facts showing the persecutor’s motive as to that person and any attempts (or futility) of seeking state help.
4. Exhaust and preserve issues for appeal: raise the independent‑claim theory and any legal arguments to the IJ and then on appeal to the BIA so the Tenth Circuit could review them. Jimenez shows the court reviews only the administrative record and will defer when factual findings are supported; unpreserved or undeveloped arguments are likely to fail.
5. If relying on police reports or government inaction, introduce those records or explain why reporting would have been futile/dangerous. The opinion stresses that without reporting there is limited evidence about how police would have responded.

Bottom line: Derivatives can prevail on their own, but only if they (a) are treated or proceed as separate applicants, (b) present and fully develop individualized evidence on the statutory elements before the IJ/BIA, and (c) preserve those claims on appeal. In Jimenez the family did not carry those burdens on the record, so the denial of asylum (and withholding) as to the family stood.
