Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

This case involves Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons—Colombian nationals who left Colombia for the United States in December 2022 and later applied for asylum, withholding of removal, and protection under the Convention Against Torture. The family’s narrative centers on political activity and a subsequent conflict with a local criminal actor known as Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, “Don Rafa.” Their claims before the immigration authorities rest on threats and harassment that they say flowed from both political involvement and Jimenez’s professional interaction with Don Rafa.

Petitioners trace the outset of their problems to long-standing political activity. Since 2012 the family had members in the Colombian Liberal Party, and in 2022 Jimenez and others went door-to-door campaigning for Federico Gutiérrez in the presidential contest. Beginning around late May 2022, Jimenez began receiving threatening phone calls concerning his party involvement; other local Liberal Party activists also received threats, and one colleague who had reported similar threats was later kidnapped and murdered. Although these earlier threats and the colleague’s death alarmed the family, Petitioners continued campaign work at that time.

The conflict with Don Rafa arose out of what, on its face, was a routine property matter. In June 2022 a woman from San Bernardo asked Jimenez—then a provisional lawyer and a law student who also interned with Colombia’s National Prosecutor’s office—to intervene in a dispute over unpaid rent and possession of a property. Jimenez learned that the tenant occupying the property was the client’s ex-husband and that the occupant was Rafael (“Don Rafa”), whom Jimenez identified as a leader of a narco-trafficking guerrilla group. Jimenez observed indicia of illicit use at the premises: the health department had sealed the third floor, reports tied the location to migrant day-rentals, trafficking, prostitution, and episodes of kidnapping and murder, and police had made several—but apparently unsuccessful—attempts at searches and seizures. A local officer told Jimenez to steer clear of the property.

Once Don Rafa’s connection to the property was known, Petitioners’ encounters with intimidation escalated. Late in June 2022 Jimenez received a brochure at his furniture factory reading, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” In July two men accosted Jimenez at the factory at night, held him at gunpoint, and instructed him to leave the neighborhood, accusing him of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office. About two months later, while the family was out of town, the same men returned to the factory, assaulted an employee, again brandished a firearm, demanded the family’s whereabouts, and threatened to kill them—telling the employee that the order came from Don Rafa. Days after that incident, Jimenez confronted a man who put his arm over his younger son’s mouth outside the child’s school and told the boy to “run to your dad.” After that incident the family temporarily moved to Jimenez’s mother’s house in the Bogota countryside for three weeks, and then decided to depart for the United States.

Petitioners made limited overtures to Colombian authorities. Jimenez told a police patrol officer about the incident with his son, and the officer told him little could be done and advised the family to leave rather than seek further trouble; Petitioners read this to mean that seeking police assistance would be futile. There is also evidence in the record that law enforcement knew of the problematic property and had attempted searches and seizures. Petitioners, and members of their family who work in government institutions, did not otherwise file formal complaints or seek broader assistance beyond the neighborhood-level contacts described at the hearing.

Procedurally, after arriving in the United States without admission or parole, DHS served the family with individual Notices to Appear alleging removability under the Immigration and Nationality Act. At a removal hearing in August 2023 the Petitioners admitted the truth of DHS’s allegations and conceded removability. They applied for asylum, withholding of removal, and CAT protection; the two minor sons proceeded as derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s asylum claim.

At the merits hearing the immigration judge (IJ) found Petitioners credible but denied relief. The IJ concluded that, even taking their testimony and supporting evidence as true, Petitioners had not shown that the harm they endured amounted to past persecution, had not established a nexus between the mistreatment and a statutorily protected ground (political opinion or membership in a particular social group), had not shown that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them, and had not demonstrated that internal relocation in Colombia was unreasonable or infeasible. The IJ also denied Petitioners’ CAT claim and rejected their due-process argument concerning counsel. Petitioners appealed to the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA), which—through a single member—affirmed and dismissed the appeal.

On appeal to the Tenth Circuit, Petitioners limited their challenge to the BIA’s denial of asylum and withholding of removal; they did not pursue the CAT denial or the due-process claim. The Tenth Circuit therefore reviewed the BIA’s factual findings under the substantial-evidence standard, with the ability to consult the IJ’s fuller explanation, and analyzed whether the record compelled a contrary conclusion on past persecution, nexus to a protected ground, government protection, and internal relocation. The court ultimately concluded that substantial evidence supported the BIA’s and IJ’s conclusions and denied the petition for review.

# Standards of Review and Governing Legal Framework

This section explains the procedural and legal scaffolding the Tenth Circuit used in reviewing Jimenez v. Bondi. Understanding these standards is essential to follow the court’s later application of law to the facts — why certain factual findings were treated as conclusive, what the petitioners had to prove to obtain asylum or withholding, and how mixed-motive allegations are evaluated.

## Jurisdiction and the Administrative Record

The court invoked statutory jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252 to review the BIA’s final order of removal. That statute authorizes consolidated review of removal orders and channels review through the courts of appeals. Crucially, the opinion emphasizes the constraints of that review: the court’s examination is “limited to ‘the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.’” In practice, that means the appellate court does not receive or entertain new evidence; it decides whether the BIA’s (and, where relevant, the IJ’s) decision is supported by the record that was before the agency.

This limitation matters because the court will not reassess credibility or reweigh evidence de novo; its role is to ensure the agency’s decision conforms to law and is supported by the existing administrative record.

## Review Posture: Single-Member BIA Decision and the IJ’s Explanation

In Jimenez the BIA affirmed the IJ’s denial by a single member. When this occurs, the court reviews the BIA’s decision but may consult the IJ’s opinion “because the IJ often provides a more complete explanation” of the factual findings and legal reasoning. The court treats the BIA’s ruling as the operative agency determination while using the IJ’s opinions to illuminate the basis for the adverse decision. This layered review is practical: the BIA’s one-line or short opinions often rest on the IJ’s longer factual findings, and the court can look to both when applying the governing standards.

## Substantial-Evidence Standard

The court reviewed the BIA’s factual findings under the substantial-evidence standard. Under that standard, the BIA’s findings are “conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.” Substantial-evidence review is deferential: the court will not overturn findings merely because it might have reached a different conclusion. Instead, reversal requires that the evidence so overwhelmingly contradict the agency’s findings that no reasonable factfinder could have made them.

Because this is a fact-intensive inquiry (e.g., whether a set of incidents amounts to persecution or whether government protection was available), the substantial-evidence rule often preserves the agency’s determinations when the record is close or ambiguous.

## Asylum Eligibility and the Refugee Definition

The opinion anchors asylum eligibility in the statutory definition of “refugee” found at 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A). A refugee is someone outside his country of nationality who is “unable or unwilling to return” because of persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution “on account of” one of five protected grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

Establishing refugee status is the applicant’s burden and must be proved by a preponderance of the evidence. Asylum is discretionary relief that depends on satisfying this refugee definition.

## Past Persecution and the Rebuttable Presumption

One route to asylum status is a showing of past persecution. The significance of past persecution is procedural: an adjudicator who finds past persecution creates a rebuttable presumption that the applicant has a well-founded fear of future persecution. That presumption can be displaced if the government shows either a fundamental change in circumstances or that internal relocation within the applicant’s country would avoid future persecution.

The court emphasizes that proving past persecution requires more than episodic threats or administrative burdens; the mistreatment must be sufficiently severe and offensive to qualify as persecution under the INA.

## The Three Core Elements for Asylum Through Past Persecution

When a petitioner seeks refugee status based on past persecution (or a fear of future persecution), the agency — and the court on review — evaluate three interrelated elements:

1. Persecution: The applicant must show an incident or incidents rising to persecution. Persecution is not defined in the INA, so courts look to precedent. The harm must include suffering or serious harm that is more than mere restrictions, harassment, or sporadic threats. The court considers the cumulative effect of multiple incidents, not only each isolated event.

2. Nexus to a Protected Ground: The applicant must show the persecution was “on account of” one of the five protected grounds (e.g., political opinion). The protected-ground motivation must be a cause of the mistreatment; it is not sufficient that the applicant merely held the opinion or was incidentally associated with it.

3. Government Inability or Unwillingness: The persecution must be committed either by the government or by forces outside the government that the government is unable or unwilling to control. Evidence of the government’s response (or lack of response) to the persecution — whether it tried to stop persecutors or offered protection to the applicant — is central to this element.

The opinion treats these elements as conjunctive; failing to establish any one of them forecloses asylum relief.

## Withholding of Removal: A Higher Burden That Falls with Asylum Failure

Withholding of removal is distinct from asylum and imposes a higher standard: the applicant must show it is “more likely than not” that his life or freedom would be threatened on account of a protected ground if returned. Because this standard is more demanding, a failure to meet the asylum standard (which relies on a preponderance of the evidence and the well-founded-fear concept) necessarily dooms a withholding claim: if the applicant cannot show a well-founded fear under asylum standards, he cannot meet the higher “more likely than not” standard for withholding. The court therefore resolves withholding claims only after and dependent upon the asylum analysis.

## Mixed-Motive Cases and the “Central Reason” Requirement

The court explains how to handle cases where persecutors may have multiple motives. The governing rule is that a protected ground need only be “one central reason” for the persecution; the persecutor may also have other non-protected motives. But the protected-ground motivation cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate. In other words, even in mixed-motive scenarios, the protected reason must have been a central causal factor in the persecutor’s decision to target the applicant.

This standard matters in Jimenez because the agency concluded the petitioners’ political activity was tangential to the primary motive (the property dispute and interference with a criminal enterprise). Under the “central reason” test, that finding sufficed to defeat the nexus element where the record supported the conclusion that the persecutors targeted the petitioner principally for non-political reasons.

***

Taken together, these procedural and substantive standards frame the court’s inquiry in Jimenez: the appellate court operated within the bounds of the administrative record, gave deference to the BIA and IJ under the substantial-evidence rule, and applied settled legal tests for persecution, nexus, government protection, and withholding. Where the agency’s factual findings were supported by the record and the protected-ground motivation was not shown to be central, the court denied the petition for review.

# Past Persecution Analysis

The court’s past-persecution analysis asks whether the harm the Jimenez family endured—viewed as a whole—meets the legal threshold of “persecution” under Tenth Circuit precedent and the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). This section explains the governing legal standard, walks through how the IJ and the BIA (and ultimately the Tenth Circuit) applied that standard to the petitioners’ specific incidents, and summarizes why the court concluded the record contains substantial evidence supporting the agency finding that the family did not suffer past persecution.

Definition and contours: persecution requires more than restrictions or threats to life and liberty

The INA does not define “persecution,” so courts apply the precedent-developed meaning. The Tenth Circuit has emphasized that persecution requires the infliction of suffering or harm that is “regarded as offensive” because of a protected characteristic and that it must be more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats to life and liberty. In other words, the harm must be qualitatively and quantitatively serious enough to go beyond ordinary harassment, intimidation, or isolated criminal acts. This is a fact-intensive inquiry, and multiple lesser incidents can be aggregated—if their cumulative effect reaches the requisite level. But courts will not treat every threat, disturbance, or nonsevere harm as persecution; context and severity matter.

Cumulative assessment of incidents

The petitioners presented a sequence of hostile acts and threats spanning several months: periodic threatening phone calls; a threatening brochure stating “death to snitches from Liberal Party”; an armed, in-person confrontation at the furniture factory in which two men held Jimenez at gunpoint and ordered him to leave the neighborhood; a later assault on a factory employee combined with another gun threat seeking the petitioners’ whereabouts; and a frightening near-abduction scare in which a man put his arm around the petitioners’ younger son, covered the boy’s mouth, and told him to “run to your dad.” The family also moved temporarily to a relative’s home in the countryside and eventually left Colombia after a police officer told them to do so.

When the IJ and BIA evaluated these incidents, they considered the totality of events rather than isolating each act. That cumulative approach is consistent with precedent allowing a court to assess whether the combined effect of multiple hostile acts amounts to persecution. The key questions were whether the incidents, taken together, inflicted sufficient harm—physical, economic, psychological, or social—to qualify as persecution and whether those harms were enduring or severe.

Concession of no physical injury and lack of lasting physical harm

A critical factual concession influenced the court’s analysis: at the merits hearing petitioners admitted they were not physically harmed, and their opening brief acknowledged they experienced no lasting physical injury. The Tenth Circuit recognizes that physical injury is not a necessary component of persecution, but the absence of physical harm reduces the likelihood that threats and harassment alone will meet the persecution threshold. Because the petitioners conceded no bodily injury, the court focused on whether the nonphysical harms—threats, forced relocation, and psychological distress—could collectively amount to persecution. That concession severely limited their path to prevailing, since many successful persecution claims rest on either actual physical attacks or serious, prolonged abuse.

Emotional and psychological harm claims and the court’s treatment

Petitioners relied in part on emotional and psychological harms: Duarte’s insomnia and the children’s panic attacks and stress. The court acknowledged these harms as unfortunate and weighty in the family’s lived experience, but it applied Tenth Circuit precedent holding that psychological harms unaccompanied by sufficiently severe physical attacks or other objectively egregious incidents rarely suffice to prove persecution. The court pointed to prior decisions in which comparable emotional distress arising from threats, short-term detention, or other nonsevere mistreatment was deemed legally insufficient. The central reasoning is twofold: (1) the persecution inquiry is objective and requires harms that courts recognize as substantially injurious in the relevant legal sense, and (2) without physical injury or other indicia of severe, prolonged targeting, psychological harm—however real—does not typically cross the persecution line in this circuit.

Comparative case law: threats alone and the circuit’s approach

The opinion repeatedly cites Tenth Circuit authorities that illustrate a consistent theme: threats, even when repeated and menacing, have often been held legally inadequate on their own. Karki is cited for the proposition that threats alone ordinarily do not constitute persecution. The court also referenced unpublished precedents in which threat patterns and other adverse experiences—such as a ten-hour detention combined with threats or multiple thefts and machete threats—were still not enough to establish persecution. These comparative cases show the court’s reluctance to expand the persecution category to include many common forms of political or criminal intimidation unless the facts show a severe level of harm, an extended campaign of abuse, or physical injury and lasting consequences.

Application to the Jimenez facts and conclusion

Applying these principles, the Tenth Circuit concluded that substantial evidence supports the agency’s finding that the petitioners’ experiences, though troubling and disruptive, did not rise to the level of persecution. The court emphasized several interrelated reasons: the absence of any physical injury, the petitioners’ own concession of no lasting bodily harm, the nature of the threats (which, although menacing, were not shown to have produced coercive, prolonged physical abuse), and the comparative case law holding similar or more extreme threat-patterns insufficient. The court also noted that petitioners’ continuation of political activity after earlier threats indicated a lack of directly linked, escalating state-actor persecution prior to the property dispute—factual nuances relevant to whether the mistreatment was so severe and pervasive as to be legally “persecution.”

Taken together, those factual findings and legal benchmarks led the court to affirm the BIA and IJ: the cumulative incidents—threatening calls and brochure, the factory gunpoint episode, the assault on an employee, and the near-abduction scare involving the child—while serious, did not objectively amount to persecution under Tenth Circuit law. Because the denial of past-persecution was supported by substantial evidence, the petitioners could not invoke the rebuttable presumption of future persecution that a finding of past persecution would have produced, and the court accordingly denied review of their asylum claim on that ground.

# Nexus to Protected Ground: Political Opinion

The central question addressed in this part of the opinion is whether the harm that the Jimenez family experienced was inflicted "on account of" a protected ground—here, political opinion. The panel limits its analysis to political opinion because Petitioners did not meaningfully develop any argument that they belonged to a cognizable particular social group. That scope limitation is important: when an appellant fails to brief a theory, the court may treat it as abandoned and confine review to the grounds actually argued. The opinion therefore asks only whether Petitioners showed that their political activity or beliefs were at least one central reason the persecutors targeted them.

The legal test governing this inquiry is statutory and well settled in our circuit. Under the INA, an asylum applicant must show persecution "on account of" one of the enumerated grounds. In practice, that requires proof that the protected characteristic—here, political opinion—was "at least one central reason" for the harm. The protected motive cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason, nor can it play only a minor role in the persecutor’s decision to harm the applicant. These principles permit the recognition of mixed motives, but they also demand that at least one protected ground be a central motivating cause when motives are mixed.

Applying that standard, the court focuses on the temporal and causal sequence of events in the record. The Petitioners were politically active: they were members of the Colombian Liberal Party and canvassed for a presidential candidate in 2022, and they received threatening phone calls beginning around late May 2022. However, the panel highlights that Petitioners continued their political campaigning despite those early, politically flavored threats and despite the murder of a colleague who had reported threats. The escalation of targeted conduct—more pointed threats, an armed intimidation at the factory, an assault on an employee demanding the family’s whereabouts, and an incident involving the younger son at school—occurred only after Jimenez became personally involved in a property dispute with Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros ("Don Rafa") and began investigating and associating with enforcement efforts at a property tied to narco-trafficking. The timing matters: because the more serious, personalized harassment followed Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s property and apparent revenue stream, the court treats that sequence as probative on motive.

The opinion examines the direct evidence of the persecutors’ motives. The men who accosted and later threatened the family accused Jimenez of being a "snitch" for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for searches and seizures at the property. They also told Petitioners that the threats or orders came from Don Rafa. Those statements link the harassment to Jimenez’s professional and personal activities that impeded or threatened the criminal enterprise—not to his campaign work. The BIA and the IJ relied on those contemporaneous attributions of motive in concluding the predominant reason for the harm was to protect Don Rafa’s revenue and security, not to punish the family for political beliefs.

The court then applies the mixed-motive principle to these factual findings. It recognizes that a persecutor may have multiple motives and that an applicant need not prove exclusivity of political animus. But where the record shows a predominating non-protected motive—here, retaliation for threatening a criminal enterprise—the political motive, if present at all, is only tangential or subordinate. Under the substantial-evidence standard, the court will not reweigh the evidence or substitute its own credibility judgments for those of the BIA and IJ. Because substantial evidence supports the agency finding that the persecutors’ central motive was to protect their criminal and financial interests, the court affirms that Petitioners did not demonstrate persecution "on account of" political opinion.

In short, the opinion concludes that—even accepting the underlying facts—the record shows the persecutors were mainly motivated by Jimenez’s interference with a criminal operation. Political opinion, at most, played a minor or incidental role. That factual determination forecloses asylum relief under the political-opinion theory because the protected motive was not a central reason for the mistreatment.

# Government Protection and Internal Relocation

This chapter explains how the Tenth Circuit evaluated whether the Colombian government was “unable or unwilling” to protect the Jimenez family from their persecutors and whether internal relocation within Colombia would have been a reasonable and safe alternative to asylum. These two inquiries are distinct but related: an inability or unwillingness to protect can show a well‑founded fear of future persecution, while a feasible internal relocation can rebut a presumption of future persecution or defeat an asylum claim even when some risk exists. The court applied settled administrative and circuit law to the record facts and concluded substantial evidence supported the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) and the Immigration Judge (IJ) that petitioners failed to meet either burden.

## Unable-or-unwilling framework: reporting, governmental efforts, and protective measures

The governing framework asks whether the government, having notice and an opportunity to respond, was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors. The court emphasized two central evidentiary points that weigh heavily in that analysis: (1) whether the government tried to stop the persecutors and (2) whether the government offered protective measures to the applicant (for example, by placing the applicant in protective custody). The ability to evaluate both points depends on whether the applicant reported the persecution and thus gave authorities notice. Absent notice, courts generally cannot determine how the police or other government actors would have reacted.

This framework is applied as a fact question under the substantial-evidence standard. The court will uphold the BIA’s factual finding unless a reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to reach a contrary conclusion. Where the applicant did not meaningfully engage the state’s institutions, or where the record shows governmental activity against the persecutors, substantial evidence often supports a finding that the government was neither unable nor unwilling to protect.

## Notice to authorities: minimal reporting and individual officer responses

On this record, the petitioners’ contacts with Colombian authorities were minimal. Jimenez testified that he spoke to two police officers about at least one threatening incident. The IJ credited these statements but found they were insufficient to show systemic government unwillingness or incapacity. The court relied on precedent requiring more than an isolated, unhelpful remark by a single officer to establish that the government as a whole was unwilling or unable to act. An individual officer’s comment—that petitioners should “leave” or “not seek problems”—does not, by itself, demonstrate a systemic failure of protection.

The importance of reporting is practical: it gives the government the opportunity to act and provides evidence of whether it did so. Because petitioners did not make wider reports or use other available channels (and did not show they tried to use Jimenez’s law‑enforcement contacts), the record left unanswered how Colombian authorities would have responded had they been properly notified. The court therefore found that minimal reporting undermined petitioners’ claim that the government was unwilling or unable to protect them.

## Countervailing record: prior police actions at the contested property

A key factual point that weighed against petitioners was evidence that Colombian authorities had acted against the very criminal enterprise tied to their persecutors. The record contained information that the government had attempted multiple searches and seizures at the property associated with Don Rafa and that the health department had sealed parts of the premises because of illicit activity. These facts supplied affirmative evidence of governmental efforts to address the criminal conduct that petitioners claim motivated the threats and violence against them.

Where the record shows such law‑enforcement activity directed at the persecutors’ operations, an inference that the government is willing and able to intervene is reasonable. The court therefore treated the prior searches and seizures as counterevidence to petitioners’ assertion that the persecutors were so closely aligned with the government that reporting would have been futile or dangerous.

## Futility/danger exception: why petitioners did not meet it here

Applicants can avoid the reporting requirement if they show reporting would have been futile or placed them in danger—examples include credible evidence that the police are complicit with the persecutors or that reporting would trigger more harm. But the court requires record support for such a claim. In Jimenez, petitioners offered a bare theory of collusion between persecutors and the state, and pointed to two discouraging conversations with patrol officers. The IJ found, and the court agreed, that those limited interactions did not demonstrate systemic collusion or a pattern of official protection for the persecutors.

Importantly, Jimenez’s own family connections to law enforcement and the absence of any effort to leverage those contacts to obtain protection further weakened the futility claim. The record showed neither a pattern of arrests being blocked nor reliable evidence that filing reports would have produced retaliation. Given the documented governmental actions against the property and the paucity of evidence of official complicity, the court concluded that petitioners had not established that reporting would have been futile or dangerously counterproductive.

## Internal relocation factors: family, employment ties, and prior safe stay

Even if an applicant establishes some risk of future persecution, the government may rebut any presumption of a well‑founded fear by showing the applicant could reasonably avoid persecution by relocating elsewhere in the country. The court considered factors the BIA and IJ relied on: petitioners’ family and relatives remained in Colombia without harm; Jimenez’s father continued to work within the Colombian criminal justice system; and the family had already relocated safely to Jimenez’s mother’s home in the Bogota countryside for three weeks without incident. Those facts supported the IJ’s assessment that relocation within Colombia was both feasible and reasonable.

The court also noted practical indicators of the threat’s scope that bear on the relocation question. The men who threatened petitioners told them to leave their neighborhood; they did not threaten the family with harm if they left the country or moved elsewhere. That limited geographic scope made internal relocation more plausible. Where threats are localized, an applicant’s ability to relocate safely within the country is more likely to be credible.

## Threat scope and the reasonableness of relocation

Taken together, the record supported the BIA’s conclusion that internal relocation would be a reasonable alternative. The threats did not manifest as a nationwide or persistent campaign specifically targeting petitioners across Colombia; instead, the men’s statements and the sequence of events indicated a local dispute tied to the property and to Don Rafa’s operations. Because the family had relatives and a demonstrated safe temporary residence elsewhere, and because there was no convincing evidence that persecutors would find and harm them outside the neighborhood, reasonable relocation was available.

The court emphasized that administrative and economic burdens or speculative claims about collusion with high‑level political figures are insufficient on their own to defeat a relocation finding, particularly where petitioners only cursorily briefed those arguments and the record contained affirmative evidence undermining the collusion theory.

## Practical lesson and holding

Practically, the decision illustrates two lessons for asylum applicants and advocates. First, to show a government is unwilling or unable to protect, the record needs more than isolated discouraging comments from police officers; it needs evidence of systemic failure, demonstrable official collusion, or credible attempts to report and obtain protection that were rebuffed. Second, evidence that family members live safely in-country, that the applicant previously stayed safely elsewhere in the country, and that perpetrators’ threats are localized all support a conclusion that internal relocation is reasonable.

Applying those principles to the Jimenez record, the Tenth Circuit held that substantial evidence supported the BIA’s determination that the Colombian government was not shown to be unwilling or unable to protect the petitioners and that safe internal relocation was available. Accordingly, petitioners failed to establish a well‑founded fear of future persecution necessary for asylum, and the court denied relief.

# Disposition and Doctrinal Takeaways

This opinion resolves Jimenez v. Bondi by denying the petition for review. The Tenth Circuit affirms the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) and Immigration Judge (IJ) determinations that Petitioners—Colombian nationals who fled after receiving threats tied to both political activity and a dispute with a criminal figure—failed to meet the elements for asylum and, consequently, withholding of removal. The court held that substantial evidence supports the BIA’s findings that Petitioners did not prove past persecution, did not demonstrate that the primary motive for the harm was their political opinion, did not show the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them, and could reasonably relocate safely within Colombia.

Holding: no past persecution, no political nexus, no inability/unwillingness of government, safe internal relocation
The court first concluded that the harms Petitioners suffered—repeated threats, stressful incidents, and attendant emotional harm—did not, taken together, rise to “persecution” under Tenth Circuit precedent. Threats and psychological injury, without more, were insufficient here because Petitioners conceded no lasting physical injury and the factual pattern did not involve the kind or degree of harm the court has recognized as persecution. On nexus, the court determined the record supports the BIA’s conclusion that the persecutors’ primary motive was retaliation for Jimenez’s interference with the revenue and security interests of a local criminal leader (“Don Rafa”), not his political campaigning. Thus the political opinion was, at best, tangential. On government protection, the court found Petitioners did not show the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors—Petitioners had little or no record of meaningful attempts to seek broader police or government protection, law enforcement had at least attempted searches at the disputed property, and nothing in the record established systemic government complicity. Finally, the court affirmed that safe internal relocation was reasonable: Petitioners had successfully moved temporarily within Colombia before fleeing to the United States, and family ties and the absence of countrywide targeting supported relocation as a viable alternative.

Withholding of removal necessarily fails when asylum standard is not met
Because the standard for withholding of removal is more demanding than asylum (it requires that persecution is “more likely than not”), the court noted that failure to meet the asylum threshold forecloses withholding relief as a practical matter. The panel therefore denied withholding relief for the same reasons that defeated asylum—there was no established past persecution, no adequate nexus, and no showing that future persecution was both likely and beyond the government’s ability or willingness to prevent.

Key practice points for future asylum litigation
This opinion supplies several concrete lessons for counsel preparing asylum and withholding claims in the Tenth Circuit:

- Develop and document reporting to authorities, or show why reporting would have been futile or dangerous. The court emphasized that evidence of whether victims reported threats and what governmental responses followed is central to the inability-or-unwillingness inquiry. Minimal or informal contacts with individual officers, without broader attempts to secure protection or evidence that reporting would plainly have been futile, weaken claims that the government could not protect an applicant.

- If asserting a particular social group (PSG), brief and develop it carefully. Petitioners did not meaningfully present a PSG claim, and the court limited review accordingly. Practitioners must both define the group clearly and tie membership to the persecution at issue; failure to develop PSG arguments risks waiver.

- In mixed-motive situations involving criminals and political activity, build an evidentiary record that distinguishes criminal retaliation from protected-ground targeting. The court credited evidence showing escalation linked to the property dispute and the persecutors’ statements about economic threats to “Don Rafa.” If an applicant’s political activity preceded threats, counsel should gather evidence that persecutors knew of and targeted the applicant because of that activity (communications, contemporaneous threats referencing politics, patterns of targeting political actors, country reports showing persecution of the applicant’s political faction, etc.). Absent proof that protected-ground motives were at least one central reason, nexus will fail.

- Corroborate psychological and medical harms where those harms are central to the persecution claim. Because the court stressed that threats plus psychological stress rarely suffice, effective claims grounded on non-physical harm should include clinical documentation and expert testimony demonstrating severity and permanence sufficient to constitute persecution in context.

Role of substantial-evidence review and deference to IJ/BIA findings
A recurring procedural point in the opinion is the court’s application of the substantial-evidence standard. The Tenth Circuit reviews the BIA’s factual findings deferentially: findings stand unless the record would compel a reasonable adjudicator to reach a contrary conclusion. Here, the court repeatedly framed its conclusions as affirming the BIA and IJ where the record offered support for findings about motive, government response, and relocation. Practically, this means that a well-developed administrative record is essential—where the IJ/BIA have fact-intensive credibility and nexus determinations supported by documentary or testimonial evidence, appellate reversal is unlikely.

Significance: limits of threat-based claims and implications of mixed criminal–political motives
Jimenez v. Bondi exemplifies two doctrinal constraints in asylum practice. First, threat-based and primarily psychological harm claims face a high bar in the Tenth Circuit; threats must be part of a broader pattern of serious mistreatment or accompanied by corroborated, severe non-physical injury to reach persecution. Second, when persecutors have both criminal and political reasons to target an applicant, courts scrutinize whether protected grounds were a central reason. Successful claims in such contexts require evidence tying persecutor motivation to the protected ground, or demonstrating that criminal and political motives converged in a way that makes the protected ground central.

In short, the disposition reinforces that (1) clear, corroborated facts showing severity of harm, (2) focused evidence of the persecutor’s motives linked to a protected ground, and (3) documentation of attempts to secure government protection (or why such attempts were futile) are pivotal to prevailing on asylum and withholding claims in the Tenth Circuit.
