Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Does denial of asylum always foreclose withholding of removal in mixed-motive or non-state-actor cases?
Question:
The court says Petitioners fail to meet the 'higher standard' for withholding because they didn’t establish a well-founded fear for asylum. I get that withholding requires a 'clear probability,' but are there scenarios where an applicant loses asylum on a discretionary or nexus ground yet still wins withholding (e.g., if one protected ground fails as 'not a central reason' but another theory could meet the withholding standard)? Or is the failure here categorical because the same factual deficiencies (no persecution, no nexus, state able to protect) defeat both forms of relief?
Answer:
Short answer: No — denial of asylum does not always categorically foreclose withholding of removal. But in Jimenez the court held that it did here because the same factual failures (no persecution, no nexus, government protection and safe internal relocation available) meant petitioners could not meet asylum’s well‑founded‑fear standard and therefore could not meet withholding’s higher “clear probability” standard.

Why: the opinion sets out the two different legal standards and why they matter. Asylum is discretionary and requires the applicant to prove refugee status by a preponderance of the evidence (i.e., past persecution or a well‑founded fear of future persecution on account of a protected ground). Withholding of removal requires a higher, mandatory showing that persecution is “more likely than not” (a clear probability). Because withholding demands a higher quantum of proof, a failure to establish a well‑founded fear for asylum will often (but not inevitably) doom a withholding claim. The court put it succinctly: petitioners “fail to demonstrate” persecution, nexus, or lack of government protection, and therefore “because Petitioners fail to demonstrate a well‑founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal.” (Jimenez v. Bondi, slip op. § II, concluding paragraph; see also § II.B–E on standards, persecution, and nexus).

When denial of asylum might not preclude withholding: the opinion recognizes (and cites precedent) that mixed‑motive persecution is possible — a persecutor can have multiple motives and a protected ground need only be “at least one central reason.” (Jimenez § II.D (quoting Rivera‑Barrientos)). By implication (and consistent with governing law the court cites), there are conceivable fact patterns where an applicant could lose asylum on a discretionary ground or fail to meet the asylum preponderance/well‑founded‑fear test as applied to one theory, but still satisfy withholding’s higher but distinct “clear probability” showing under a different protected‑ground theory or on a different evidentiary record. Jimenez does not announce a categorical bar; it applies the general rule (higher withholding standard) to the specific facts and evidentiary failures here (no past persecution, nexus tied to property dispute rather than political opinion, no showing government unwilling/unable).

Bottom line for practice: treat asylum and withholding as distinct claims with different burdens. A denial of asylum is strong evidence against withholding when the denial rests on core factual failures (no persecution, no nexus, or available state protection) — as in Jimenez — but it is not, as a matter of law, an absolute or automatic bar in every conceivable mixed‑motive or non‑state‑actor case.
