Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### What threshold for 'past persecution' did the court apply to repeated death threats and an apparent attempted child abduction?
Question:
The court emphasizes that 'threats alone' usually aren’t persecution and notes no lasting physical harm. But the record includes: (1) repeated threatening calls, (2) a death-threat brochure targeting Liberal Party 'snitches,' (3) two armed men twice confronting and threatening the family/employee, and (4) a man grabbing the child at school before the father intervened. How does the Tenth Circuit weigh the cumulative effect of armed confrontations and an apparent attempted abduction when there’s no actual injury? Are there Tenth Circuit cases where similar non-injury but highly menacing conduct did cross the persecution line?
Answer:
Short answer
The Tenth Circuit applied the settled (and relatively stringent) rule that threats, even violent‑sounding ones, are not enough by themselves to show past persecution — you must show “suffering or harm” that is “more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty.” The court weighed the incidents cumulatively, but found the aggregate here (repeated calls and brochures, two armed confrontations, and the man who grabbed the child but caused no lasting physical injury) still fell short because there was no physical harm or persistent, offensive injury and the threats were tied more to a property dispute than a protected ground. The opinion emphasizes that the court “has rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone.” (Jimenez v. Bondi, No. 24‑9573, 10th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025).

What legal standard the court applied
- Persecution requires “the infliction of suffering or harm … and must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty.” (citing Wiransane v. Ashcroft and Jimenez, § II.C).
- The court must consider incidents collectively — cumulative effects can constitute persecution — but even cumulative threats must produce offensive harm, not merely fear or stress. (Ritonga cited in Jimenez).
- Threats alone are usually insufficient. The opinion cites Karki: “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution,” and notes that physical harm is not strictly required, but that courts have “rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone.” (Jimenez § II.C (collecting cases including Karki, Brandy, Chhetri, Carias‑Mejia)).

How the court weighed these facts in Jimenez
- The court expressly evaluated the brochure, repeated phone threats, two armed in‑person confrontations, and the school incident together (it recounts Ritonga’s cumulative‑effects rule).
- Despite that aggregation, it found (1) Petitioners conceded no physical injury and no lasting physical harm; (2) the emotional/psychological harm (sleep loss, panic attacks) was insufficient by itself; and (3) prior Tenth Circuit precedent rejects asylum claims based primarily on threats or stress even when those incidents were severe or repeated. Thus the BIA’s denial was supported by substantial evidence. (Jimenez § II.C).
- The nexus and government‑protection findings buttressed the result: the threats appeared tied to Jimenez’s property dispute with a criminal leader (not primarily to political opinion), and the record did not show the government was unable or unwilling to help. Those points are relevant because even menacing conduct that is motivated by a protected ground and accompanied by state inability/unwillingness is more likely to be deemed persecution.

Are there Tenth Circuit precedents where non‑injury but menacing conduct crossed the line?
- The Jimenez opinion collects and relies on Tenth Circuit decisions that cut the other way. It cites Chhetri (four phone threats and one vague in‑person threat), Karki (telephone threats), Brandy, and Carias‑Mejia as examples where threatening or menacing conduct without lasting physical injury did not establish persecution. The court says courts have “rarely, if ever,” granted asylum on threats and psychological harm alone. (Jimenez § II.C).
- Jimenez does not identify a Tenth Circuit case where similar non‑injury but highly menacing conduct nevertheless met the persecution threshold. Its discussion implies that, within this circuit, mere threats plus episodic menacing conduct (absent real injury, persistent coercive control, or other offensive physical harm) are typically insufficient.

Practical takeaways for analogous claims
- Emphasize concrete, offensive harm (physical injury, sustained detention, severe bodily or mental suffering tied to a protected ground) when arguing past persecution.
- If the mistreatment is mostly threats or brief confrontations, develop strong evidence that (a) the conduct was persistent and coercive enough to amount to severe harm, (b) it was motivated centrally by a protected ground, and (c) the state was unable or unwilling to protect you — because the Tenth Circuit treats each element as crucial in close cases (as Jimenez demonstrates).
