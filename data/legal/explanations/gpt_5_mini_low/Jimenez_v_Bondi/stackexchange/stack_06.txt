Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Did Petitioners waive their 'particular social group' theory by not briefing it, and what is required to preserve a PSG claim on appeal?
Question:
The opinion says Petitioners 'did not brief' arguments on a PSG and limits nexus analysis to political opinion. Is that treated as waiver/forfeiture at the circuit level due to inadequate briefing, or as failure to exhaust before the BIA? What level of specificity is required in the opening brief to preserve a PSG-based claim and its nexus theory in the Tenth Circuit?
Answer:
Short answer: The Tenth Circuit treated the Petitioners’ PSG theory as forfeited on appeal for inadequate briefing, not as an exhaustion defect before the BIA. To preserve a PSG claim (and the particular nexus theory tied to it) for review in this circuit, an appellant must raise and meaningfully brief the theory in the opening brief — i.e., define the group, explain why it is cognizable, identify the claimant’s membership, show how the record supports that membership and nexus, and cite legal authority and record evidence.

Why that is the Court’s treatment
- The opinion expressly says Petitioners “did not brief any arguments regarding the existence of their particular social group nor did they establish their membership in such a group. Rather, in arguing the nexus requirement, Petitioners allege that they were persecuted … because of their political opinion. Accordingly, we limit our review to whether Petitioners establish a nexus between their alleged persecution and their political opinion.” That is a classic appellate-forfeiture result: the court confines review to the issues the appellant actually briefed.
- The Court’s limitation of review to political opinion therefore flows from inadequate briefing on PSG, not from an independent BIA-exhaustion ruling. The opinion nowhere says the PSG theory was abandoned before the BIA or that exhaustion was the problem; it points to the absence of briefing and the absence of any record showing PSG membership.

What the Tenth Circuit requires to preserve a PSG claim on appeal
- Raise the issue concretely before the tribunal whose decision is being reviewed (here, the IJ/BIA) if exhaustion could be an issue. But as the opinion shows, even if the BIA addressed the claim, the circuit will treat a failure to brief on appeal as forfeiture.
- On appeal, include in the opening brief:
  - A clear definition of the proposed particular social group (sufficiently specific to meet cognizability requirements);
  - A statement and citation to where in the record the claimant’s membership in that group is shown (testimony, documents, BIA/IJ findings);
  - A developed legal argument why the group is legally cognizable and why the persecution was “on account of” membership in that group (i.e., nexus analysis);
  - Citations to controlling authorities and to the administrative record supporting the factual predicates (so the court can apply its substantial-evidence review).
- In short: you must do more than mention PSG in passing. The Court in Jimenez faulted the Petitioners for not briefing the existence of a PSG or establishing membership — those are the discrete points an opening brief must address to preserve the claim for review.

Practical guidance for an opening brief in the Tenth Circuit
- Define the PSG precisely (avoid purely descriptive or amorphous labels).
- Quote/point to the record showing membership and persecutory acts tied to membership.
- Explain (with authority) why the persecutor’s motives satisfy the “on account of” standard as applied to PSG membership.
- If you rely on mixed motives, brief why membership was at least one central reason.
- If the BIA rejected PSG on the merits, point to that BIA holding and explain why it’s deficient; if the BIA did not consider PSG, explain why the court should still review it.

Bottom line: Jimenez treats the PSG claim as forfeited because Petitioners failed to brief or establish it on appeal. To preserve a PSG-based argument in the Tenth Circuit, you must present a developed legal and factual argument in your opening brief showing the group’s definition, membership, and nexus with the persecution, supported by record citations and legal authority.
