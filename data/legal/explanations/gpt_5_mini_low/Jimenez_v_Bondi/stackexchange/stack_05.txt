Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Who bears the burden on internal relocation in the absence of past persecution, and how did the court weigh the short-term countryside move?
Question:
Since the court found no past persecution, I understand the burden stays with Petitioners to show relocation isn’t reasonable. The panel highlights that (1) relatives remain unharmed, (2) Petitioners briefly stayed safely with Jimenez’s mother, and (3) the threats said 'leave the neighborhood' rather than the country. How do courts evaluate whether a brief three-week period in a different area is probative of long-term safety? What factors (reach of the persecutor, economic/administrative barriers, family ties, size of country) are most persuasive in the Tenth Circuit relocation analysis?
Answer:
Short answer
- Burden: When an applicant has not established past persecution, the applicant retains the burden to show that internal relocation within the home country is not reasonable. The government can rebut a well‑founded fear by proving by a preponderance that the applicant could avoid persecution by relocating (Rivera‑Barrientos / 8 C.F.R. §1208.13(b)(1)(i)); here the Tenth Circuit treated relocation as the petitioners’ burden to disprove once past persecution was not found.
- The three‑week countryside stay: The court treated the brief, safe stay with Jimenez’s mother as probative (not dispositive) evidence that internal relocation was feasible. It relied on that episode together with other facts (family in country unharmed; the threats were to “leave the neighborhood,” not the country; and evidence the government had taken some action against the criminal enterprise) to conclude relocation was reasonable.

Legal doctrine and how the court applied it
- Legal standard: If an applicant lacks past persecution (and thus lacks the rebuttable presumption of future persecution), the government may defeat an asylum claim by showing either (1) a fundamental change in circumstances or (2) that the applicant could safely relocate within the country. The relocation inquiry asks whether, under the totality of the evidence, relocation is reasonable (Rivera‑Barrientos; 8 C.F.R. §1208.13(b)(1)(i)). The BIA’s factual findings on this point are reviewed under the substantial‑evidence standard.
- Burden allocation in this case: The panel framed the issue consistent with that framework — because Petitioners failed to show past persecution, the reasonableness of internal relocation remained for them to show was impossible or unreasonable. The court upheld the BIA/IJ’s finding that the government had met its burden to show relocation was feasible.

Factors the Tenth Circuit found persuasive in Jimenez v. Bondi
The opinion shows which facts the court found most probative in deciding relocation was reasonable. Key factors (and how the court weighed them):

1) Evidence of safe short‑term relocation with relatives
- The family’s three‑week move to Jimenez’s mother’s home in the Bogota countryside was treated as meaningful evidence that the family could live safely elsewhere in Colombia. The court cited that episode directly in concluding relocation was feasible.

2) Continued safety of other family/relatives in country
- The fact that relatives remained in Colombia unharmed (and Jimenez’s father still worked in the criminal‑justice system) reinforced the conclusion that relocation could be safe.

3) Scope and content of the threats
- The court emphasized the content of the threats: the assailants told them to “leave the neighborhood,” not the country. That limited geographic scope supported a finding that moving within Colombia could mitigate the risk.

4) Government response and capacity
- Evidence that authorities had investigated the property used by Don Rafa (attempted searches and seizures) undercut an argument that the government was uniformly unable or unwilling to protect the family. The court relied on this to support the reasonableness of relocation and to reject a claim that perpetrators were effectively state actors preventing internal flight.

5) Applicant’s efforts to seek help and notice to authorities
- The court stressed that petitioners did not meaningfully seek broader governmental protection beyond a few local officers and that there was no showing that seeking help would have been futile. Lack of stronger attempts to use police or other protective measures weakened their relocation claim.

6) Economic/administrative burdens and other hardships
- Petitioners’ assertions about economic or administrative barriers were addressed but described as haphazardly briefed and insufficiently developed in the record; the court therefore did not treat them as persuasive.

7) Reach of the persecutor
- The court evaluated whether the persecutor’s reach made relocation unreasonable: the record showed localized threats tied to a property dispute with Don Rafa rather than evidence the persecutor effectively controlled the entire country or would pursue them nationwide.

How courts treat brief relocations generally (as illustrated here)
- A short, safe stay is circumstantial evidence that internal relocation is feasible, but it is not automatically dispositive. Courts weigh that episode against other evidence of the persecutor’s reach, government protection, whether the applicant sought help, and any special economic or administrative obstacles to relocation.
- In Jimenez, the short countryside stay, combined with family ties and the localized nature of the threats and some government action against the criminal enterprise, persuaded the court that relocation was reasonable.

Practical takeaways for advocates / students
- If past persecution is not established, be prepared to contest relocation by developing record evidence on:
  - The persecutor’s national reach and intent to follow the applicant;
  - Objective proof of systemic government unwillingness/ inability to protect (not just one unhelpful officer);
  - Economic or administrative barriers that would make relocation unreasonable (with concrete evidence);
  - Whether short stays elsewhere actually demonstrate sustainable safety (duration, safety incidents, ability to work/live there, and whether relocation would sever indispensable family/support ties).
- In the Tenth Circuit, a demonstrably safe short stay with relatives plus evidence that threats were geographically limited can be highly persuasive that internal relocation is reasonable, unless the record shows the persecutor or the state’s failures make such relocation untenable.
