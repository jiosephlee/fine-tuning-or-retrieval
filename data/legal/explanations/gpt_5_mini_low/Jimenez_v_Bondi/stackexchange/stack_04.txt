Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### What counts as showing the government is 'unable or unwilling' to protect if the applicants only spoke to individual officers and didn’t file reports?
Question:
The court faults Petitioners for minimal reporting and cites attempts by authorities to search the property as evidence against government-complicity. How much reporting is required to avoid the inference that the state might have helped if asked—does informing two patrol officers suffice? What kind of proof is needed to establish that filing a formal report would have been futile or dangerous (to fit the exception), especially when there’s some evidence of police action (attempted raids) but also localized warnings by officers to 'not seek trouble'?
Answer:
Short answer
Telling one or two patrol officers what happened—without more—generally will not prove the government was “unable or unwilling” to protect you. Jimenez makes clear that (1) the key inquiry is whether the state had notice and an opportunity to respond, and (2) a one‑off conversation with an unhelpful officer is not enough to show systemic inability or unwillingness. To fit the “futility/danger” exception you need concrete evidence showing that reporting would have been pointless or would have put you at greater risk (e.g., prior formal complaints ignored, documented official collusion, threats tied to police, or other objective proof that higher authorities would not or could not help).

Legal doctrine and how the court applied it in Jimenez
- Notice/opportunity to respond is central. The court cites Singh: whether a government is unable or unwilling hinges on its response to past persecution, and whether it had notice and an opportunity to act. Without notice, there is no basis to infer how police would have reacted. (Jimenez, §E (citing Singh, 130 F.4th at 861–62).)
- Reporting is therefore a key fact. The opinion repeats the familiar rule that failure to report can undercut an asylum claim because the record does not reveal how authorities would have responded. (Jimenez §E.)
- But reporting is not mechanically dispositive. The court acknowledges the futility/danger exception—an applicant may avoid reporting if they can show it would have been futile or dangerous—but stresses that the applicant must prove that exception with record evidence. (Jimenez §E (discussing Singh and Aviles‑Gonzalez).)
- Is speaking to two patrol officers enough? No, not here. Jimenez expressly held that the petitioners “spoke to two individual police officers who said that Petitioners should not seek trouble—but there is nothing to show that this reflects the Colombian government’s position.” The court relied on Osorio‑Morales and similar authority for the proposition that “a one‑off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect.” (Jimenez §E (citing Osorio‑Morales, 72 F.4th 738 (7th Cir. 2023)).)
- Evidence that undercuts the futility claim. The court contrasted the petitioners’ minimal reporting with independent evidence that Colombian authorities had attempted several searches and seizures of the property tied to the persecutors, which supported the conclusion that the government had in fact tried to address the criminal conduct. That objective evidence of law‑enforcement action weighed against finding government incapacity or unwillingness. (Jimenez §A & §E.)

What you need to show futility or danger in practice
- Objective evidence that reporting had no effect: prior formal complaints or police reports showing no action, records of investigations that were closed without result, police logs, or correspondence demonstrating inaction.
- Evidence of official complicity or collusion: documented links between perpetrators and government actors (e.g., records, media or NGO reports, witness statements showing officials protected or coordinated with persecutors).
- Evidence that reporting would increase risk: prior instances where victims who reported were targeted, or explicit threats tied to the act of reporting.
- Evidence that the unhelpful officers reflected systemic failure—not just one apathetic patrolman: multiple complaints to different levels ignored, complaints to supervisory/internal affairs and no response, or contemporaneous public/statewide evidence of police corruption in the relevant locality.
- Where available, seek corroboration from independent sources (NGO reports, media, official documents, affidavits from others who reported and were ignored).

Practical implications for fact‑development and advocacy
- Filing a formal police report or complaint (and preserving documentation) is strong proof of notice and gives the government an opportunity to act. If clients did not do that, develop proof showing why they reasonably refrained (threats, prior ineffective reporting, documented corruption).
- If only informal reports occurred (e.g., telling patrol officers), try to obtain: names/badges, contemporaneous notes, any written acknowledgment, witness statements, or proof that higher authorities were or were not informed.
- Collect objective evidence of government action (or lack of it) in the particular locale: records of raids, prosecutions, or, conversely, reports of police collusion.
- Where possible, show that unhelpful comments came from officials whose statements reflect official policy (e.g., supervisors, internal affairs, prosecutors) rather than a single patrolman—because Jimenez distinguishes between one‑off patrol apathy and systemic inability/unwillingness.

Bottom line
Jimenez treats a couple of conversations with patrol officers—and no formal reporting—as weak evidence that the state had notice and failed to protect. To invoke the futility/danger exception you must do more: produce record evidence that reporting would have been futile or dangerous, or otherwise show systemic failure or collusion. Absent that, courts will generally expect applicants to have given the government a real chance to respond.
