Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### What happens to derivative beneficiaries’ claims if the principal’s asylum application fails—can the spouse or children prevail independently?
Question:
The children were derivative on Jimenez’s asylum application. If the principal applicant fails on past persecution/nexus/government-protection, can a derivative spouse or child still obtain asylum or withholding based on their own individualized risk (e.g., the child who was grabbed)? What procedural steps would have been needed to preserve independent claims for the family members in this posture?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — but only if the spouse/child actually pursued an independent asylum (or withholding) claim and developed separate evidence on the IJ record. Derivative beneficiaries do not automatically get asylum if the principal fails; a derivative can prevail only by showing, on the record before the IJ/BIA, that he or she independently meets the statutory elements (past persecution or well‑founded fear, nexus to a protected ground, and inability/unwillingness of the government to protect). If no independent claim is preserved and adjudicated, the derivative’s fate is tied to the principal.

Why: legal standard and what the Jimenez opinion shows
- Jimenez treats the family’s applications as a single adjudication in which the minor children “proceeded as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s application for asylum.” (Background, B. Procedural Background.) That posture means the children’s claims were adjudicated as derivatives of the principal’s application rather than as separate principal claims.
- The governing law requires an applicant (including a would‑be derivative who asserts an independent claim) to prove refugee status by a preponderance of the evidence: past persecution or a well‑founded fear of future persecution, nexus to a protected ground, and government inability/unwillingness to protect. (B. Legal Framework; Niang and Rivera‑Barrientos citations in the opinion.)
- The Tenth Circuit denied relief here because, on the existing record, Petitioners (collectively) failed to prove past persecution, nexus, government protection problems, and safe relocation. The court deferred to the IJ/BIA factual findings under substantial‑evidence review. Because the family failed under asylum standards, they necessarily failed withholding (the higher standard). (Conclusion; last paragraph of III.)

What a derivative needed to do to prevail independently (procedural steps to preserve independent claims)
1. File or request adjudication as a principal applicant (or at least make clear on the record that the derivative is asserting an independent asylum/withholding claim). In Jimenez the children were treated as derivatives/riders; to preserve an independent claim the family member must ensure the IJ treats them as an applicant whose merits are separately considered.
2. Develop separate, individualized evidence and testimony before the IJ about the derivative’s own harm and risk. For example, the younger son’s “grabbed” incident is an individualized event that could be pressed as past persecution or as evidence supporting a well‑founded fear — but it had to be developed as an independent claim in the record. The IJ in Jimenez relied on the record as presented (including credibility findings) and concluded the aggregate facts did not amount to persecution.
3. Argue nexus and government protection specific to that family member. The court emphasized the need to show that a protected ground was “at least one central reason” for the harm and looked to government notice/response. A derivative must therefore present facts showing the persecutor’s motive as to that person and any attempts (or futility) of seeking state help.
4. Exhaust and preserve issues for appeal: raise the independent‑claim theory and any legal arguments to the IJ and then on appeal to the BIA so the Tenth Circuit could review them. Jimenez shows the court reviews only the administrative record and will defer when factual findings are supported; unpreserved or undeveloped arguments are likely to fail.
5. If relying on police reports or government inaction, introduce those records or explain why reporting would have been futile/dangerous. The opinion stresses that without reporting there is limited evidence about how police would have responded.

Bottom line: Derivatives can prevail on their own, but only if they (a) are treated or proceed as separate applicants, (b) present and fully develop individualized evidence on the statutory elements before the IJ/BIA, and (c) preserve those claims on appeal. In Jimenez the family did not carry those burdens on the record, so the denial of asylum (and withholding) as to the family stood.
