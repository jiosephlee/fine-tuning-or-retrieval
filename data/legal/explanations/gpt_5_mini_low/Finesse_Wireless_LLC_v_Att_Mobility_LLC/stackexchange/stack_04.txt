Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Did the ’775 patent’s prosecution history clearly disclaim two-signal implementations when the claims were amended to ‘three signals S1, S2 and S3’?
Question:
Appellants argued that changing ‘two or three signals’ to ‘three signals’ surrendered two-signal embodiments (like Filipovic). The Federal Circuit rejected clear and unambiguous disavowal, emphasizing the patentee’s focus on passive (not active) IMPs and a construction requiring only ‘three separately identifiable signals,’ not necessarily three unique inputs. How do we reconcile the text ‘three signals S1, S2 and S3’ with the court’s acceptance that they need not be unique? What features of the amendment/arguments fell short of Cont’l Circuits’ clear-disavowal standard, and what are examples of language that would have constituted a clear surrender in this context?
Answer:
Short answer
No. Under Continental Circuits’ “clear and unmistakable” standard the prosecution record here did not clearly disclaim two‑signal implementations. The examiner rejection was overcome by arguing Filipovic addressed active (not passive) IMPs, and the patentee amended to “three signals S1, S2 and S3” without saying those must be three distinct/unique inputs or that two‑signal embodiments were being surrendered. The Federal Circuit therefore treated the amendment as limiting to “three separately identifiable signals,” not to three unique signal sources, and held that fell short of the clear‑disavowal required to strip two‑signal products from the claims.

Relevant doctrine (short)
When patent prosecution narrows claim scope to avoid prior art, that narrowing can be a prosecution disclaimer only if the patentee’s statements or amendments clearly and unmistakably surrender the broader scope. See Cont’l Circuits, 915 F.3d at 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019) (disavowal requires clear, unambiguous statement). Ambiguous or tangential remarks—e.g., focusing on a different aspect of the prior art—do not meet that standard.

How the court applied it here (grounded in the opinion)
- The examiner rejected claims reciting “two or three signals” over Filipovic. Finesse amended the claims to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3” and argued Filipovic was limited to mitigating active IMPs while the claimed invention addressed passive IMPs. The Federal Circuit emphasized that Finesse’s distinguishing argument targeted the nature of the IMPs (active vs. passive), not an unequivocal surrender of two‑signal implementations. J.A. 985; J.A. 110–11.
- The court relied on the district court’s construction that “three signals S1, S2 and S3” requires three “separately identifiable” signals, but does not require three unique inputs—consistent with inventor testimony and intrinsic evidence. J.A. 109–14; J.A. 115. Because Finesse never expressly said “we give up any claim to two‑signal systems” or that “S1, S2 and S3 must be three distinct transmitter inputs,” the amendment alone did not constitute the clear and unambiguous disavowal required by Continental Circuits. J.A. 110–11.
- The court concluded that—at most—the prosecution history required three separately identifiable signals, but not three unique sources, and so did not “rise to the level of ‘clear and unambiguous’ disavowal” of two‑signal products. J.A. 110–11.

What features of the amendment/arguments made them insufficient
- The narrowing amendment used the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3” but did not add language tying “three” to uniqueness (e.g., “three distinct/different transmitter signals” or “three different inputs”). Mere recitation of S1,S2,S3 without a statement that they must be different leaves room for embodiments where some signals are copies or duplicates (separately identifiable but not unique). J.A. 115; J.A. 968–69.
- The prosecution arguments focused on a different ground (Filipovic addressed active IMPs; claimed invention addresses passive IMPs). That is a technical distinction of the prior art, not an express surrender of two‑signal embodiments. The court treated this as a tangential distinction, not a disclaimer of two‑signal scope. J.A. 985; J.A. 110–11.
- There was no unambiguous admission that “two signals + a copy” or any two‑input architecture is excluded; therefore the record was ambiguous as to whether two‑signal implementations were intended to be given up.

Examples of language that would likely have constituted a clear surrender
- Explicit prosecution statement conceding two‑signal scope: e.g., “We amend to require three signals and therefore expressly disclaim any embodiments or prior art that use only two input signals (such as Filipovic). The claims no longer cover systems that use only two separate inputs.”
- Claim amendment plus explanatory argument making uniqueness explicit: e.g., “We add ‘three distinct signals from three distinct transmitter outputs’ (or ‘three unique transmitter inputs’), and we distinguish Filipovic because it uses only two distinct transmitter inputs, which the applicant expressly abandons.”
- Argument that carefully links the amendment to a lost scope: e.g., “To overcome Filipovic we have narrowed the claims to require three different signals (S1 ≠ S2 ≠ S3). We therefore surrender claim coverage of any device that operates with only two different inputs.”
Any such unambiguous, contemporaneous statement that the patentee gave up two‑signal embodiments would meet the Continental Circuits standard; the prosecution history here did not contain that kind of language. J.A. 110–11.

Bottom line
An amendment to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3” can be interpreted merely as identifying three separately identifiable operands; without a contemporaneous, explicit statement that “three” means three distinct/unique inputs (or that two‑signal architectures are surrendered), the Federal Circuit will not find a clear and unmistakable prosecution disclaimer under Continental Circuits. The record in Finesse falls on the ambiguous side of that line. J.A. 109–15, 110–11.
