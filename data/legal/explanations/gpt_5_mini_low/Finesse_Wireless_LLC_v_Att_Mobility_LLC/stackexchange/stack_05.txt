Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### For the ’775 patent, must the patentee prove each of the seven specific third-order multiplications, or can three distinct products ‘stand in’ for seven?
Question:
The claim recites generating an n-th order ICS by ‘digitally multiplying and filtering’ seven specified third-order combinations of S1, S2, and S3. Finesse’s expert identified only three distinct products in Nokia’s documents (e.g., x1·|x1|·|x1|; x1·x1·x2′; x1·|x2|·|x2|), and the court held that no reasonable jury could find all seven from this. Is there any doctrinal basis (e.g., equivalence, repeated operations, or signal identity) that would allow three distinct multiplications to satisfy a claim that literally enumerates seven? Also, do operations involving complex conjugates and moduli qualify as the ‘digitally multiplying’ of S1, S2, S3 as-claimed, or does the presence of conjugate/modulus transform them into different ‘signals’ that break one-to-one mapping to the seven listed terms?
Answer:
Short answer
The patentee must prove the seven specific third‑order products recited in the claim (or at least present evidence that the accused device actually produces those seven products or an admissible one‑for‑one mapping to them). Three distinct products cannot “stand in” for the seven unless the patentee adduces evidence at trial that (a) the three products are in fact repeated or otherwise produce the seven claimed terms, or (b) there is a legitimate, jury‑presented mapping showing how particular produced products correspond to the seven claimed products. And, per the Federal Circuit in this case, operations that produce complex conjugates or moduli are not interchangeable with literally multiplying the claimed S1, S2, S3 unless the patentee proved the required mapping — the court treated conjugate/modulus forms as different for purposes of the literal claim limitation.

Why (doctrinal and opinion‑based explanation)
- Claim scope and burden: A patentee bears the burden to prove infringement by substantial evidence. The ’775 claim expressly recites generating an n‑th order ICS “by, given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering” the seven enumerated third‑order products. The court reviewed whether the evidence at trial could support a jury finding that the accused radios performed those seven specific multiplications. The question is therefore one of literal infringement and whether the evidence presented at trial proved the presence of the claimed seven multiplications (or an acceptable mapping to them).

- What the court held about the three vs. seven products: The Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL because the only technical document relied on by Finesse listed only three distinct multiplications (x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|), and there was no evidence that any of those multiplications were repeated so as to produce all seven claimed multiplications. The court emphasized that Dr. Wells “did not testify how those three distinct multiplications map onto the seven claimed multiplications,” and Finesse’s post‑trial attempt to supply a mapping was not evidence the jury considered. Under those circumstances, the court concluded no reasonable jury could find the seven claimed multiplications were performed. (Op. pp. 19–22, 38–39.)

- When fewer distinct products can suffice: The opinion makes clear the short path for allowing fewer observed/distinct operations to satisfy an enumerated list: the patentee must produce evidence at trial that either (1) the fewer observed multiplications are in fact performed multiple times so that the enumerated list is literally produced, or (2) there is a clear mapping (presented to the jury) from the operations the accused device performs to each claimed term. Absent that evidence, three distinct products do not automatically “stand in” for seven claimed products. (Op. pp. 38–39.)

- Complex conjugates and moduli: The court specifically noted the Nokia document “involves modulus and complex conjugate operations and does not show the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications” because the document does not disclose multiplying x1 and x2 directly but rather their conjugates and moduli. The court observed that Finesse’s mapping would, for example, require S3 to be simultaneously mapped to x2, x2', and |x2| — a mapping that was not presented to the jury. Thus, in this case the presence of conjugate/modulus forms was treated as producing different signal forms that break a literal one‑to‑one mapping to the claimed S1×S2×S3 terms absent evidence linking them. (Op. pp. 38–39.)

Practical takeaway for proof
- If the claim literally lists seven specific products, the patentee should prove at trial that the accused system produces those seven products (or present admissible evidence that the system repeats or otherwise generates equivalents such that the seven are present) and must present any mapping from differently expressed signal forms (e.g., conjugates, moduli) to the claimed S1, S2, S3 products to the jury. Simply pointing to three distinct algebraic terms in a document without showing repetition or a demonstrable correspondence to each claimed term is insufficient under the Federal Circuit’s ruling in this case.
