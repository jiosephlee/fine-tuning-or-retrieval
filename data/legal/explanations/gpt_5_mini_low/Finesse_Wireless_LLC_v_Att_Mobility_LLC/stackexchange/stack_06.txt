Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Was the ‘uniqueness’ argument about S1, S2, S3 preserved for JMOL, and how does Rule 50(a) waiver apply here?
Question:
The district court said Appellants waived the argument that S1, S2, and S3 must be ‘unique’ by not raising it at Rule 50(a). On appeal, the Federal Circuit didn’t rely on uniqueness to reverse; it instead held the evidence didn’t show seven multiplications. For preserving issues in patent cases tried to a jury, how specific must a Rule 50(a) motion be to avoid waiver? If a party challenges sufficiency on ‘not three unique signals’ but also argues ‘not seven multiplications,’ is it enough to preserve the latter even if the former is waived? Any guidance on how granular Rule 50(a) contentions must be in complex claim-element disputes?
Answer:
Short answer
- The district court concluded AT&T/Nokia waived the “S1, S2, S3 must be unique” argument because they did not raise that particular theory at the Rule 50(a) stage. The Federal Circuit did not rely on uniqueness in its reversal; instead it reversed on a preserved sufficiency ground — that no reasonable jury could have found the accused radios performed the seven multiplications required by the claim.  
- Practically, Rule 50(a) must identify the specific legal/factual ground on which JMOL is sought with enough particularity to put the district court and opposing party on notice you are challenging that claim element. If you want multiple, distinct theories preserved you must articulate them in the Rule 50(a) motion; a more general sufficiency challenge may preserve some aspects but will not preserve a distinct element-by-element contention you never raised.

Explanation and how the opinion applies
1) What the district court and Federal Circuit did here
- The district court held the uniqueness point (that S1, S2, S3 must be “unique” signals) was waived because Appellants did not raise that precise contention at the Rule 50(a) JMOL stage. (Opinion pp. 38, 40.)  
- On appeal the Federal Circuit did not affirm on that waived uniqueness theory. Instead, the court reversed because Finesse’s evidence did not support the jury’s finding that the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications (limitation 1[d]). The court found the Nokia document and Dr. Wells’ testimony showed only three distinct multiplications and that Dr. Wells never mapped those to the seven claimed multiplications — a sufficiency failure the panel treated as preserved and dispositive. (Opinion pp. 38–39, 14966–70 discussion.)

2) Rule 50(a) waiver principle as applied
- Rule 50(a) requires a party to state the grounds for JMOL with sufficient specificity at trial so the court can resolve the issue before the case goes to the jury. If a party fails to present a distinct legal or factual theory in its Rule 50(a) motion, that theory is typically waived on appeal. The district court here applied that principle to uniqueness and found waiver. (Opinion p. 38.)  
- By contrast, Appellants did present and preserve a sufficiency challenge to the evidence showing the seven multiplications (they contested that the accused radios only used two signals and that the evidence only identified three multiplications). That contention was considered on appeal and was the basis for reversal. (Opinion pp. 38–39, 14966–70.)

3) Practical guidance for patent trials (how granular must Rule 50(a) be?)
- Be element-specific. If your noninfringement theory depends on a particular meaning or feature of a claim term (e.g., “unique” signals versus merely “separately identifiable” signals), raise that theory explicitly in Rule 50(a). The court may treat a generalized sufficiency challenge as preserving only limited aspects of the argument, not every possible theory you might assert on appeal.  
- Preserve alternative theories you want considered. If you have multiple independent reasons why the patentee cannot carry its burden (e.g., claim construction/claim scope argument, and an independent evidentiary mapping/multiplication-count argument), articulate each in the Rule 50(a) motion. The Federal Circuit here reversed on the independent evidentiary insufficiency because that was argued; it did not救 rely on the uniqueness theory because it was not preserved.  
- Tie the motion to record evidence. The court will look at what you pointed to at the JMOL stage (documents, testimony, mappings). The Federal Circuit faulted Finesse for attempting to make mapping arguments on appeal that were not presented to the jury. (Opinion pp. 17–18, 14966–70.)

Bottom line
- Uniqueness: the district court treated that argument as waived under Rule 50(a); the Federal Circuit did not rely on it.  
- Seven multiplications / evidentiary sufficiency: that was preserved and was the ground on which the Federal Circuit reversed for lack of substantial evidence.  
- Lesson: when trying a patent case, raise each discrete noninfringement theory (element-by-element) you want preserved in your Rule 50(a) motion and tie it to the record.
