Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### How do the Fifth Circuit JMOL standard and the Federal Circuit’s substantial evidence review interact here?
Question:
The opinion says it reviews denial of JMOL under Fifth Circuit law (de novo, reversing only if the evidence overwhelmingly favors one side) and also says infringement findings are reviewed for substantial evidence. I'm confused about which standard really drives the outcome: is the Federal Circuit applying the Fifth Circuit’s JMOL standard to assess whether substantial evidence supports the jury verdict, or are these two independent layers of review? How should I conceptualize the court’s task when it says it reverses because the verdict isn't supported by substantial evidence?
Answer:
Short answer
They’re two sides of the same coin in this appeal. The Federal Circuit follows Fifth Circuit law for reviewing a denial of JMOL (de novo, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict and reversing only if the evidence “points so strongly and overwhelmingly” for one side that no reasonable juror could disagree). At the same time it frames the jury’s infringement findings as factual findings reviewed for whether they are supported by substantial evidence (i.e., whether a reasonable jury could have found for the prevailing party). So the court’s task is a single inquiry couched in both doctrines: ask whether, viewing the record most favorably to Finesse, there is substantial evidence such that reasonable jurors could have found infringement. If there is not, the denial of JMOL must be reversed.

Why the opinion uses both formulations
- The Federal Circuit is required to apply regional-circuit law to review denials of JMOL (here, Fifth Circuit standard). The opinion states that duty and quotes the Fifth Circuit formulation (de novo review; reverse only if the evidence “points so strongly and overwhelmingly…”). (Opinion ¶ I, citing Wi‑LAN v. Apple.)
- The Federal Circuit also describes its review of the jury’s factual findings (infringement) as the familiar substantial‑evidence standard: whether a reasonable jury could have found for the prevailing party. (Opinion ¶ I, citing Apple v. Wi‑LAN.)

These are not independent, conflicting standards. Practically and functionally they require the same result: if there is not substantial evidence such that reasonable jurors could reach the verdict, then under the Fifth Circuit JMOL test the verdict cannot stand.

How the Court applied that approach here
- For the ’134 patent the court concluded the plaintiff’s case rested on confusing, self‑contradictory expert testimony (Dr. Wells). Because Finesse bore the burden, and the expert never clearly mapped the accused radio’s signals to the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” the court held “this sort of confusing change of course is not sufficient to support the jury verdict.” It therefore concluded the infringement finding lacked substantial evidence and reversed JMOL. (Opinion § I.A, citing Johns Hopkins precedent.)
- For the ’775 patent the court found the Nokia document and the expert’s testimony did not show the seven specific multiplications claimed (Dr. Wells identified three distinct multiplications but never mapped them to the seven required). The court therefore held no reasonable jury could have found the seven multiplications were performed — again framing the defect as an absence of substantial evidence and reversing JMOL. (Opinion § I.B.)

Bottom line for how to conceptualize the court’s task
- Think of it as one inquiry expressed two ways: (1) apply the Fifth Circuit JMOL test (de novo, viewing the evidence favorably to the verdict); and (2) ask whether the jury’s factual finding (infringement) is supported by substantial evidence (i.e., whether reasonable jurors could have found for the plaintiff on the facts shown). If the answer to (2) is “no,” then under (1) JMOL must be granted. The opinion uses both formulations to satisfy its obligation to apply regional JMOL law while describing the underlying factual review standard.
