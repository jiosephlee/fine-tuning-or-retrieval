Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Seven Means Seven: Algebraic Specificity and the Perils of Post‑Hoc Mapping in DSP Patent Cases

## Why this case matters

Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025) is a useful reminder that in digital signal processing (DSP) and software‑heavy patent cases, algebraic specificity is not an optional luxury: it is the sine qua non of proof. The Federal Circuit reversed the Eastern District of Texas’s denial of JMOL and vacated a nine‑figure damages award because Finesse’s technical proof simply did not show the accused Nokia radios performed the seven third‑order multiplications recited in claim 1 of the ’775 patent. What sank Finesse was not a debatable claim construction but a failure of evidentiary alignment: the only multiplications the expert pointed to were three distinct operations that involved moduli and complex conjugates, and the expert never explained—at trial—how those operations constituted the seven specific algebraic products the claim required. The court also rejected Finesse’s prosecution‑surrender argument and stressed that appellate counsel may not cure missing trial proof with post‑hoc mappings.

That combination of doctrinal points—substantial evidence review of jury findings, the role of claim construction and prosecution history in resolving scope questions, and the strict limits on supplying new technical mappings only on appeal—makes this opinion a short, sharp lesson for patentees, litigators, and experts in DSP and related fields.

## What the court did and why

The panel reversed JMOL denials as to both the ’134 and ’775 patents, but the ’775 analysis is the cleaner doctrinal example of why precision matters.

The asserted ’775 claim requires generating IMP cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2, and S3 in a particular set of seven third‑order products (S1×S1×S2; S1×S2×S2; S1×S2×S3; S1×S1×S3; S2×S2×S3; S1×S3×S3; S2×S3×S3). At trial Finesse’s expert relied on Nokia technical documents and testimony to argue the accused radios performed the claimed multiplications. But the documents, and the expert’s testimony about them, only identified three distinct multiplications—expressed in the notation used in the documents as x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2′ (complex conjugate), and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and involved modulus and complex conjugate operations rather than literal algebraic products of three separate signals as the claim recites.

The Federal Circuit held that: (1) the prosecution history did not amount to a clear and unmistakable surrender of claim scope to exclude devices with only two unique inputs, so the court did not import a “uniqueness” requirement beyond three “separately identifiable” signals; but (2) even accepting the district court’s construction, no reasonable jury could find the accused radios performed the seven specifically claimed multiplications because Finesse presented only three distinct operations and the expert never tied those operations, at trial, to the seven claimed algebraic products. Critically, the panel emphasized that Finesse tried to supply the missing mapping for the first time on appeal—pointing to ten multiplications mentioned in Nokia docs and proposing how a subset might correspond to the seven claimed products—but appellate counsel cannot bootstrap trial deficiencies into proof.

Because the jury’s infringement verdict lacked substantial evidentiary support, the court granted JMOL of noninfringement and vacated the damages award.

## Why mathematical exactitude matters in DSP patents

DSP claims commonly specify mathematical operations (multiplications, convolutions, transforms, etc.) or particular relationships among signals. Those claims impose objective, often algebraic, criteria that an accused device must meet. Unlike broad functional language, algebraic operations have structure: the identity of operands, the order of operations, and whether a transform or modulus has been applied all matter.

Two problems often recur in practice. First, technical documents (and sometimes experts) use notational conventions (modulus, conjugate, magnitude–phase decompositions, etc.) that are not always isomorphic to the algebraic forms recited in claims. Second, demonstratives that show operations at a high level (block diagrams, annotated screenshots) can create the misleading impression of equivalence without specifying the precise mapping from the accused system’s signals to the claim’s variables.

The Federal Circuit’s decision enforces the commonsense point: if a claim says generate S1×S1×S2, a patentee must show, with trial evidence, how the accused system produces that algebraic product (or an accepted functional equivalent under the asserted claim language and prosecution history). Pointing to operations that look similar or saying “these three things correspond” is not enough when the claim’s language admits of a precise numerical mapping.

## Trial practice implications: experts, demonstratives, and the record

This opinion should sharpen trial teams’ checklist for DSP and software cases.

First, experts must do the granular mapping at trial. An expert should testify, plainly and repeatedly, which signal in the accused system is S1, which is S2, and which is S3 (or explain why aliasing/unicity means two signals can function as three “separately identifiable” inputs under the claim construction). If the accused implementation uses conjugates, moduli, or transformed versions of signals, the expert needs to explain—on the record—how those forms correspond to the claimed variables and algebraic products. Vague testimony, or testimony that pivots mid‑stream without clear reconciliation, will not sustain a jury verdict under substantial‑evidence review.

Second, demonstratives must do the heavy lifting. Block diagrams and annotated excerpts from vendor documents are indispensable, but they must show the precise operations and, ideally, contain step‑by‑step algebraic expansion or a table mapping each claimed product to the accused implementation’s operation. Printed labels like “x1” or “modeled path” are inadequate unless the expert ties them to claim terms in a noncontradictory way.

Third, litigators must avoid saving critical technical explanations for appellate briefs. The Federal Circuit was explicit that the mapping that mattered had to be presented to the jury. You cannot first invent a mapping on appeal and expect to survive JMOL.

Finally, in cases involving mathematical claims, consider offering stipulated or special jury instructions that clarify the algebraic forms the jury must find, and require the patentee to present a witness walkthrough that explicitly fills each cell of that instruction.

## Doctrinal takeaways and downstream effects

Several doctrinal points follow from this opinion.

- Substantial evidence remains a meaningful constraint: the panel applied it to reject a jury verdict where the plaintiff’s only technical mapping was internally inconsistent or incomplete. That’s a reminder that technical complexity does not dilute the appellate court’s willingness to police evidentiary sufficiency.

- Claim construction and prosecution history will often be decisive, but here they played different roles. The court rejected Appellants’ contention that Finesse clearly surrendered two‑input implementations during prosecution; the prosecution record did not “clearly and unambiguously” disclaim two‑signal practice. So patentees should know that narrowing amendments do not automatically foreclose every intermediate interpretation, but they should not rely on that to paper over insufficient trial proof.

- The decision reinforces the rule against appellate supplementation of the trial record: maps, mappings, or technical rationales developed for the first time on appeal cannot save a defective trial showing.

For patentees, the opinion incentivizes drafting and prosecution practices that make the claimed algebraic forms explicit and defensible. If a claim recites seven specific products, consider including example expansions, alternative equivalent forms, and explicit disclosure that explains whether modulus/conjugate representations satisfy the claim. That way, when a dispute arises, there is less gap between claim language and the embodiments or prior art.

For defendants, the opinion signals that targeting the plaintiff’s expert mapping and demonstratives can be an effective path to JMOL: show inconsistencies, timing problems, or missing one‑to‑one correspondences and ask the court to grant JMOL if the patentee cannot cleanly thread the algebraic needle for the jury.

## Conclusion

Finesse’s loss is straightforward in substance and instructive in form. A claim that says “seven specific third‑order multiplications” requires proof that the accused device does those seven multiplications, not an invitation to reconstruct that proof after the verdict. In DSP and other mathematically intensive domains, trial success depends on granular, noncontradictory expert testimony and demonstratives that map each claimed algebraic operation to an accused implementation. The Federal Circuit’s decision enforces that practical norm—and it means that in future cases, parties will need to make sure that “seven” really does mean seven, on the record, before asking a jury (and later reviewing courts) to treat it as such.
