Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# When ‘Substantial Evidence’ Isn’t: The Federal Circuit’s Hard Look at Self-Contradictory Expert Testimony

## Why this case matters

In Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC, the Federal Circuit reversed denials of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement for every asserted claim of two patents, and vacated a $166 million damages award. What drove the reversal was not a novel rule about claim scope or damages methodology, but a blunt application of an old evidentiary principle: a plaintiff who bears the burden of proof cannot rest on an expert whose testimony is internally inconsistent in ways that defeat a necessary element-by-element mapping to the accused product. The panel treated the expert’s shifting testimony about what signals the accused Nokia radios sample—initially identifying a “modeled PIM path” as the interference-generating signal and then, when confronted, vaguely invoking x1 and x2—as the sort of incoherence that, under the substantial-evidence standard, cannot sustain a jury verdict. The decision reinforces that the Federal Circuit will police the sufficiency of infringement evidence when the plaintiff’s case rises or falls on a confused expert narrative.

## The court’s reasoning in brief

Both patents at issue involve fairly technical RF signal-processing methods for canceling passive intermodulation (PIM) interference. For the ’134 patent, a threshold factual showing was that the accused radios’ receiver “sample[d] a passband of received signals” that included both (1) “signals of interest” and (2) “interference generating signals.” Finesse’s infringement case depended primarily on expert testimony by Dr. Jonathan Wells and certain Nokia documents. At trial Dr. Wells identified two items on a Nokia schematic—the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and a “modeled PIM path”—as corresponding to the claimed categories. On cross, defense counsel exposed a critical timing problem: the modeled PIM path is generated after sampling (in the FPGA), so it could not have been sampled by the RF ADC. Rather than provide a precise mapping of x1 and x2 to the claimed categories, Dr. Wells shifted between descriptions, sometimes seeming to equate the red path’s two signals with x1 and x2 but repeatedly framing the mapping in terms of the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path.

The Federal Circuit concluded this testimony was internally inconsistent and legally insufficient. Relying on the Fifth Circuit’s de novo review of JMOL denials (the regional standard here) and the Federal Circuit’s own precedent in Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp., the panel held that when a patentee’s proof depends on an expert whose testimony contradicts itself on a necessary claim element, that proof can fail as a matter of law. The court found no substantial evidence supported the jury’s infringement finding for the ’134 patent because the expert never gave a clear, consistent mapping showing the receiver actually sampled both claimed signal types. The court reached a related result for the ’775 patent: even accepting Finesse’s construction that “S1, S2 and S3” need not be unique, the expert had not shown the accused radios performed the seven specific multiplicative operations the claim requires; the Nokia material the expert relied on disclosed only three distinct multiplications and did not tie them to the seven claimed products. Because the court reversed JMOL on noninfringement, it vacated the damages award.

## Johns Hopkins redux: why contradictory testimony is fatal

Johns Hopkins v. Datascope is the salient precedent the panel invoked. In Johns Hopkins, the Federal Circuit reversed where the patentee’s expert gave contradictory testimony about a physical-contact limitation—one statement suggested three-dimensional contact, another said the device contacted only in two spots—and the court treated that contradiction as fatal to the plaintiff’s burden. Finesse applies that logic to an inherently less tangible domain—signal identity and timing—but the doctrinal point is the same: an expert’s conclusory labels do not substitute for concrete, consistent testimony that ties each claim limitation to the accused product in a way a reasonable jury could rely on.

This is not an invitation to litigate exclusively on cross-examination theatrics. The panel’s insistence on internal coherence preserves familiar evidentiary guardrails. Substantial evidence means a reasonable jury could find for the patentee; where the evidentiary edifice consists largely of an expert repeatedly saying A is B, then later acknowledging B can’t be A because of sequencing, and never resolving the inconsistency, that edifice collapses. The Federal Circuit’s approach makes clear that plaintiffs must produce experts who can withstand the temporal, physical, and conceptual scrutiny necessary to show each claim element is met.

## The JMOL lens, regional law, and appellate review

Two procedural features shape the decision’s teeth. First, JMOL review is governed by the regional circuit’s standard; here the Fifth Circuit’s de novo review of JMOL denials aligns with the Federal Circuit’s practice of stepping in when evidence “points so strongly and overwhelmingly” in the defendant’s favor that no reasonable juror could find otherwise. Second, the court’s application of “substantial evidence” review to jury findings of infringement dovetails with that regional rule: where the patentee’s evidence rests on a shaky expert mapping, an appellate court can conclude that no reasonable juror could have reached the verdict.

The practical upshot: parties defending patent suits should not assume appellate reversal is only for clear legal error in claim construction or for egregious legal mistakes. When a defendant can show that the plaintiff’s essential linkage between claims and accused features is premised on contradictory or incoherent testimony, JMOL can be a powerful vehicle. Conversely, patentees cannot treat the jury verdict as a bulwark against appellate scrutiny when the verdict’s factual foundation is an ambiguous, shifting expert narrative.

## Doctrinal and downstream implications

Finesse does a few doctrinal jobs at once. It reaffirms that:

- Expert testimony must provide clear, non-contradictory element-by-element mappings for infringement. Labels alone—“this is the modeled PIM path” or “this is the signal of interest”—are insufficient if timing or functional facts undermine the mapping.
- Claim amendments during prosecution will not automatically doom a patentee’s case on a narrow point (the court rejected a forfeiture/disclaimer argument about whether “three signals” required three unique signals). But claim scope disputes still matter; proving that accused products meet every claimed limitation remains essential.
- Appellate courts will enforce the substantial-evidence standard by interrogating the internal coherence of the plaintiff’s proof, not merely whether some testimony could be construed favorably.

Downstream, expect defense teams to press for targeted JMOL motions whenever the patentee’s proof hangs on an expert with inconsistent statements—particularly in technical fields where timing, signal paths, or internal generation of signals can be dispositive. Plaintiffs will in turn double down on corroborating documentary evidence and on preparing experts to give crisp, testable mappings that can survive cross-examination showing sequencing, signal provenance, and whether a purported “path” is upstream or downstream of sampling.

## Practical lessons for trial teams

Plaintiffs should heed the following takeaways from Finesse:

- Build redundancies. Don’t rely on a single expert’s labels. Corroborate expert testimony with contemporaneous technical documents, precise diagrams, and demonstrative evidence showing timing and signal flow, and make sure the expert’s narrative aligns plainly with those documents.
- Anticipate sequencing attacks. If a claim requires the receiver to sample signals, be ready to show when and where each relevant signal exists relative to sampling and to explain why a modeled or reconstructed signal nonetheless qualifies under the claim language (or concede and craft alternative mappings).
- Prepare clean mappings. Experts must be prepared to map each claimed limitation to specific components or operations (e.g., “x1 is the DL(TX) reference and is sampled by RF ADC; x2 is the path modeled later but corresponds to a sampled upstream input because…”), and to explain any apparent inconsistencies succinctly and consistently on the record.
- Avoid careless nomenclature. A legend that reads “Red – DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path” is a trap if you don’t precisely explain whether the legend lists alternatives, concurrent signals, or simply two annotations that correspond to distinct conceptual elements. Ambiguous legend language should be unpacked, not assumed to be self-explanatory.

For defendants, Finesse is an encouragement to test expert mappings vigorously on cross, to develop documentary timelines showing signal generation/sampling order, and to move for JMOL when the plaintiff’s critical proof is internally contradictory and no other evidence cures the deficiency.

## Conclusion

Finesse is not a doctrinal revolution. It is, however, a clear reminder that the substantial-evidence standard is real and enforceable: a jury verdict cannot stand if it rests on expert testimony that contradicts itself on a claim-essential point and provides no coherent explanation tying claim elements to the accused product. In complex technical cases, where the identity and timing of signals or operations often determine infringement, litigants must present clean, consistent, and document-backed expert narratives. Absent that, appellate courts are willing to hold that there simply isn’t substantial evidence to sustain a verdict.



## “Three signals” means separately identifiable, not unique: claim construction, prosecution history, and avoiding disavowal

Why this case matters

Finesse Wireless v. AT&T/Nokia (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025) is a useful, practical reminder about the limits of prosecution-history-based claim narrowing — particularly in the digital-signal-processing world where claim language like “S1, S2, and S3” can be read in multiple technical ways. The panel rejected AT&T/Nokia’s contention that Finesse surrendered coverage of devices that use only two distinct physical signals by amending claims during prosecution to recite “three signals.” Instead, the court adopted (and affirmed the district court’s) view that the claims require three separately identifiable signals, not necessarily three unique inputs, because the intrinsic record lacked a clear and unambiguous disavowal. That distinction is consequential for many patentees and defendants litigating over “how many” signals or inputs a claimed DSP operation requires, and it underlines the careful line courts draw before reading a prosecution amendment as narrowing claim scope.

How the court got there — the path through intrinsic and extrinsic evidence

The patent at issue, the ’775, claims an interference-cancellation method that generates cancellation signals using “three signals S1, S2 and S3” and then performs seven third‑order multiplications of those signals (e.g., S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, etc.). AT&T/Nokia argued that during prosecution Finesse effectively surrendered scope over systems that use only two distinct input signals — a dispositive point because the accused Nokia radios used only two unique signals (x1 and x2). The district court had construed “three signals” as “separately identifiable,” not “unique,” relying on intrinsic text and inventor testimony; the Federal Circuit agreed.

The Federal Circuit’s key moves were familiar doctrinal ones. First, it asked whether the intrinsic record — the claims, specification, and prosecution history — contained a clear and unambiguous disavowal of coverage of devices that use fewer than three unique signals. The court stressed that an amendment to recite “three signals” does not automatically amount to a surrender of “two-signal” embodiments. Here, the examiner had rejected earlier claims and Finesse amended from broader language to recite three signals while arguing that the prior art addressed active IMPs (but not the passive-IMP problems the claims solved). The court concluded that, at most, the amendment required three separately identifiable signals; it did not amount to the “clear and unambiguous” disclaimer that would limit the claims to three unique inputs. The court cited Continental Circuits and similar precedents holding that prosecution statements must be unmistakable to disavow claim scope.

Second, the court accepted that extrinsic evidence (inventor testimony) and the specification supported reading the three signals as “signals generally” that could be the same or different. That extrinsic material, together with the prosecution record, reinforced the absence of a narrowing disclaimer.

But the court did not bless every piece of Finesse’s infringement proof. Separately, the panel reversed the jury verdict because Finesse failed to present substantial evidence that the accused radios actually performed the seven distinct multiplications required by the claim language — a different, evidentiary failing. So the take on “three signals” does not ensure victory where the factual mapping to accused systems is weak.

Analytical perspective — balancing prosecution amendments against public notice

The Federal Circuit’s approach is doctrinally conservative and predictable: prosecution amendments do not automatically rewrite claim meaning unless the patentee clearly and unmistakably disclaims alternatives. That rule preserves reliable public notice — competitors reading a granted patent can’t be expected to divine subtle prosecution gambits unless those gambits are explicit. At the same time, the court’s reading recognizes the realities of DSP claims: terms like “S1, S2, S3” are often placeholders for roles in an algorithm rather than rigid indications that three physically separate signals must exist in the accused device.

This opinion reiterates two cross-cutting principles. First, silence or equivocal statements in prosecution do not amount to disavowal. Patent applicants commonly add language to overcome art while also arguing differences in application context (here, active vs. passive intermodulation). Courts will not, without more, convert those tactical choices into restrictive claim readings. Second, when claim terms serve functional or structural roles (e.g., “three signals” as operands in multiplication operations), courts will look to the specification and inventor statements to understand whether the terms denote distinct physical entities or merely separately identifiable data streams that might be derived from fewer underlying signals.

Implications and downstream effects

For patentees: this decision is a modest win. It confirms that amending to add “three signals” doesn’t necessarily doom coverage of systems that, in practice, provide three distinguishable streams derived from fewer unique physical inputs. Inventors and prosecutors should still be cautious: a clear concession can and will narrow claims. But ambiguous or technical amendments that are tied to a substantive distinction (e.g., active vs. passive sources of IMPs) are less likely to be treated as admissions of total exclusion.

For defendants: you cannot rely solely on the face of a prosecution amendment to prove disavowal. Successful prosecution-history estoppel or disclaimer requires showing that the patentee made a clear, unmistakable statement that it intended to give up certain scope. Defendants should therefore press other routes: attack claim construction based on specification language; probe inventor testimony for explicit disavowals; and of course, press the factual mapping (as AT&T/Nokia did elsewhere in this case) to show the accused product does not perform all required claim steps (here, the seven multiplications).

For courts: the opinion follows established Federal Circuit precedent by demanding clarity in prosecution-based narrowing. The decision reinforces that claim meaning is rooted first in the intrinsic record, and prosecution history must contain unmistakable disclaimers to override otherwise broad readings.

Practical drafting and litigation takeaways

1. Be explicit during prosecution. If you intend to preserve embodiments with fewer physical signals but multiple logical streams, say so. Conversely, if you intend to narrow, make explicit, unmistakable statements to that effect — because ambiguity favors broader claim readings on later litigation.

2. Distinguish role from identity in claims. In DSP claims, consider drafting language that differentiates “separately identifiable” streams from “distinct physical inputs.” If you want to ensure uniqueness, use specific language (e.g., “three distinct input signals” or “three signals derived from three different transmitters”) rather than relying on numeric labels alone.

3. Preserve technical explanations in the specification. The court looked to specification and inventor statements to understand the meaning of “three signals.” Well-crafted descriptions of examples and permissible mappings between signals, conjugates, and moduli can be decisive evidence later.

4. Infringement proofs must map the claim literally. Even where claim construction favors the patentee, you still need evidence showing the accused device actually performs each claimed element. Finesse’s broader construction did not save it because its expert failed to demonstrate the seven multiplications in the accused radios.

5. Use prosecution for technical record-building, but avoid unintended disclaimers. Explanations distinguishing prior art are necessary; just be mindful that specific concessions can be read as limiting if phrased in unambiguous, categorical terms.

Conclusion

Finesse v. AT&T/Nokia is a pragmatic re-affirmation of the Federal Circuit’s restraint on using prosecution amendments as a sword to cut down claim scope unless the patentee’s statements are unambiguous. The court’s “separately identifiable, not unique” reading of “three signals” fits comfortably within existing doctrine and underscores that the devil in DSP disputes is often in the evidentiary details: claim construction matters, but so does the expert’s ability to map claims to accused systems.



# Seven Means Seven: Algebraic Specificity and the Perils of Post‑Hoc Mapping in DSP Patent Cases

## Why this case matters

Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025) is a useful reminder that in digital signal processing (DSP) and software‑heavy patent cases, algebraic specificity is not an optional luxury: it is the sine qua non of proof. The Federal Circuit reversed the Eastern District of Texas’s denial of JMOL and vacated a nine‑figure damages award because Finesse’s technical proof simply did not show the accused Nokia radios performed the seven third‑order multiplications recited in claim 1 of the ’775 patent. What sank Finesse was not a debatable claim construction but a failure of evidentiary alignment: the only multiplications the expert pointed to were three distinct operations that involved moduli and complex conjugates, and the expert never explained—at trial—how those operations constituted the seven specific algebraic products the claim required. The court also rejected Finesse’s prosecution‑surrender argument and stressed that appellate counsel may not cure missing trial proof with post‑hoc mappings.

That combination of doctrinal points—substantial evidence review of jury findings, the role of claim construction and prosecution history in resolving scope questions, and the strict limits on supplying new technical mappings only on appeal—makes this opinion a short, sharp lesson for patentees, litigators, and experts in DSP and related fields.

## What the court did and why

The panel reversed JMOL denials as to both the ’134 and ’775 patents, but the ’775 analysis is the cleaner doctrinal example of why precision matters.

The asserted ’775 claim requires generating IMP cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2, and S3 in a particular set of seven third‑order products (S1×S1×S2; S1×S2×S2; S1×S2×S3; S1×S1×S3; S2×S2×S3; S1×S3×S3; S2×S3×S3). At trial Finesse’s expert relied on Nokia technical documents and testimony to argue the accused radios performed the claimed multiplications. But the documents, and the expert’s testimony about them, only identified three distinct multiplications—expressed in the notation used in the documents as x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2′ (complex conjugate), and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and involved modulus and complex conjugate operations rather than literal algebraic products of three separate signals as the claim recites.

The Federal Circuit held that: (1) the prosecution history did not amount to a clear and unmistakable surrender of claim scope to exclude devices with only two unique inputs, so the court did not import a “uniqueness” requirement beyond three “separately identifiable” signals; but (2) even accepting the district court’s construction, no reasonable jury could find the accused radios performed the seven specifically claimed multiplications because Finesse presented only three distinct operations and the expert never tied those operations, at trial, to the seven claimed algebraic products. Critically, the panel emphasized that Finesse tried to supply the missing mapping for the first time on appeal—pointing to ten multiplications mentioned in Nokia docs and proposing how a subset might correspond to the seven claimed products—but appellate counsel cannot bootstrap trial deficiencies into proof.

Because the jury’s infringement verdict lacked substantial evidentiary support, the court granted JMOL of noninfringement and vacated the damages award.

## Why mathematical exactitude matters in DSP patents

DSP claims commonly specify mathematical operations (multiplications, convolutions, transforms, etc.) or particular relationships among signals. Those claims impose objective, often algebraic, criteria that an accused device must meet. Unlike broad functional language, algebraic operations have structure: the identity of operands, the order of operations, and whether a transform or modulus has been applied all matter.

Two problems often recur in practice. First, technical documents (and sometimes experts) use notational conventions (modulus, conjugate, magnitude–phase decompositions, etc.) that are not always isomorphic to the algebraic forms recited in claims. Second, demonstratives that show operations at a high level (block diagrams, annotated screenshots) can create the misleading impression of equivalence without specifying the precise mapping from the accused system’s signals to the claim’s variables.

The Federal Circuit’s decision enforces the commonsense point: if a claim says generate S1×S1×S2, a patentee must show, with trial evidence, how the accused system produces that algebraic product (or an accepted functional equivalent under the asserted claim language and prosecution history). Pointing to operations that look similar or saying “these three things correspond” is not enough when the claim’s language admits of a precise numerical mapping.

## Trial practice implications: experts, demonstratives, and the record

This opinion should sharpen trial teams’ checklist for DSP and software cases.

First, experts must do the granular mapping at trial. An expert should testify, plainly and repeatedly, which signal in the accused system is S1, which is S2, and which is S3 (or explain why aliasing/unicity means two signals can function as three “separately identifiable” inputs under the claim construction). If the accused implementation uses conjugates, moduli, or transformed versions of signals, the expert needs to explain—on the record—how those forms correspond to the claimed variables and algebraic products. Vague testimony, or testimony that pivots mid‑stream without clear reconciliation, will not sustain a jury verdict under substantial‑evidence review.

Second, demonstratives must do the heavy lifting. Block diagrams and annotated excerpts from vendor documents are indispensable, but they must show the precise operations and, ideally, contain step‑by‑step algebraic expansion or a table mapping each claimed product to the accused implementation’s operation. Printed labels like “x1” or “modeled path” are inadequate unless the expert ties them to claim terms in a noncontradictory way.

Third, litigators must avoid saving critical technical explanations for appellate briefs. The Federal Circuit was explicit that the mapping that mattered had to be presented to the jury. You cannot first invent a mapping on appeal and expect to survive JMOL.

Finally, in cases involving mathematical claims, consider offering stipulated or special jury instructions that clarify the algebraic forms the jury must find, and require the patentee to present a witness walkthrough that explicitly fills each cell of that instruction.

## Doctrinal takeaways and downstream effects

Several doctrinal points follow from this opinion.

- Substantial evidence remains a meaningful constraint: the panel applied it to reject a jury verdict where the plaintiff’s only technical mapping was internally inconsistent or incomplete. That’s a reminder that technical complexity does not dilute the appellate court’s willingness to police evidentiary sufficiency.

- Claim construction and prosecution history will often be decisive, but here they played different roles. The court rejected Appellants’ contention that Finesse clearly surrendered two‑input implementations during prosecution; the prosecution record did not “clearly and unambiguously” disclaim two‑signal practice. So patentees should know that narrowing amendments do not automatically foreclose every intermediate interpretation, but they should not rely on that to paper over insufficient trial proof.

- The decision reinforces the rule against appellate supplementation of the trial record: maps, mappings, or technical rationales developed for the first time on appeal cannot save a defective trial showing.

For patentees, the opinion incentivizes drafting and prosecution practices that make the claimed algebraic forms explicit and defensible. If a claim recites seven specific products, consider including example expansions, alternative equivalent forms, and explicit disclosure that explains whether modulus/conjugate representations satisfy the claim. That way, when a dispute arises, there is less gap between claim language and the embodiments or prior art.

For defendants, the opinion signals that targeting the plaintiff’s expert mapping and demonstratives can be an effective path to JMOL: show inconsistencies, timing problems, or missing one‑to‑one correspondences and ask the court to grant JMOL if the patentee cannot cleanly thread the algebraic needle for the jury.

## Conclusion

Finesse’s loss is straightforward in substance and instructive in form. A claim that says “seven specific third‑order multiplications” requires proof that the accused device does those seven multiplications, not an invitation to reconstruct that proof after the verdict. In DSP and other mathematically intensive domains, trial success depends on granular, noncontradictory expert testimony and demonstratives that map each claimed algebraic operation to an accused implementation. The Federal Circuit’s decision enforces that practical norm—and it means that in future cases, parties will need to make sure that “seven” really does mean seven, on the record, before asking a jury (and later reviewing courts) to treat it as such.



# From Liability to a Vacated $166 Million Award: Damages as a Passenger on JMOL Reversals—and Trial Preservation Lessons

Why this case matters
Finesse Wireless v. AT&T Mobility is a compact reminder of two things that trial lawyers and patent litigators often learn the hard way: appellate reversals on liability can erase even large damages awards, and shaky infringement proofs—especially when tied to technical expert testimony and ambiguous mappings to accused systems—are fragile on JMOL. The Federal Circuit reversed the Eastern District of Texas’ denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of two patents and, as a straightforward consequence, vacated a $166.3 million lump-sum damages verdict. The opinion underscores how liability proofs can make or break damages, and it offers concrete lessons about trial presentation, preservation of Rule 50 issues, and drafting damages models that rest on well-supported infringement theories.

What the court decided and why
The appeals turned on whether Finesse met its burden to show the Nokia radios embody the claim limitations of the ’134 and ’775 patents. For the ’134 patent, the dispositive limitation was claim 1[a], which requires sampling “a passband of received signals” that includes both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Finesse’s expert, Dr. Wells, pointed to a Nokia document and repeatedly equated the document’s “red path” annotations to the claimed signals. But at trial the only plausible mapping of the alleged “interference generating signals” was a modeled PIM path that, crucially, is generated downstream of the RF ADC—i.e., after sampling. The court found the expert’s testimony internally inconsistent and ambiguous: he variously identified the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path as the two signals on the red path, but never clearly tied the claim terms to the inputs (x1, x2) actually sampled by the RF ADC. Because Finesse bore the burden of proof, the Federal Circuit held no reasonable jury could have found the sampling limitation met given that contradiction and the timing problem (signals generated after sampling cannot have been sampled). The jury verdict on the ’134 claims therefore lacked substantial evidentiary support.

For the ’775 patent, the pivotal issues were (1) whether “three signals S1, S2 and S3” required three unique inputs or merely three separately identifiable signals, and (2) whether the accused system performed the seven third‑order multiplications recited by claim 1. The Federal Circuit declined to treat the prosecution amendment as a clear, unambiguous disavowal that would exclude systems with only two unique input signals; it accepted the district court’s construction limiting “three” to three separately identifiable signals. But it reversed on the second point: the Nokia documentation (and Dr. Wells’ testimony) showed at most three distinct multiplication-type operations involving complex conjugates and moduli (and possibly more multiplicative terms), not the seven distinct third‑order multiplications recited by the claim. The expert never mapped the accused device’s listed operations to the seven claimed multiplications in a way supported in the record, and the court found that gap fatal to the infringement verdict.

Because the Federal Circuit concluded that both patents’ asserted claims were not supported by substantial evidence of infringement, it reversed JMOL denial and vacated the damages award that depended on liability.

Analytical perspective: what the opinion is really teaching
Two themes run through the opinion. First, the ruling reiterates that the patentee carries the burden to establish each claim limitation with competent, consistent evidence, and that self‑contradictory expert testimony is a weak foundation for a verdict. The Johns Hopkins precedent the Federal Circuit cites is instructive: an expert cannot bootstrap a verdict by giving conflicting descriptions of what the accused system does. On highly technical fact patterns—sampling chains, signal generation ordering, and algebraic mappings—clarity matters. The court was unforgiving where the expert left key mappings opaque or internally inconsistent.

Second, the opinion demonstrates the practical limits of damage awards that are untethered to a robust linkage between infringement theory and the accused product. The damages here were a lump sum for the life of the patents; reversal on liability rendered those damages moot. Put differently, damages are downstream of liability: if the infringement theory collapses, so does the monetary remedy.

Doctrinal implications and appellate posture
Doctrinally, Finesse does not create novel law; it applies settled principles about substantial evidence review, claim construction, prosecution disclaimer, and the burden of proof. But it is a useful exemplar in three respects.

- Expert testimony and mapping: Courts will scrutinize expert mappings from claim terms to accused systems, particularly when the mapping requires reading construction‑level detail into complex technical documents. Vagueness or contradiction is dangerous in the face of JMOL.

- Prosecution history and claim scope: The court declined to infer a broad prosecution disclaimer from Finesse’s narrowing amendment to recite “three signals,” finding no “clear and unambiguous” surrender of systems with two unique inputs. That tracks prior Federal Circuit nuance: not every narrowing amendment equates to total disavowal of related embodiments. But the opinion also illustrates that an amendment can leave the patentee vulnerable if the trial evidence fails to demonstrate the required structural or functional consequences of the amendment (here, the actual seven multiplications).

- Waiver and Rule 50(a) preservation: The district court rejected appellants’ argument that Finesse had surrendered claim scope over two signals as waived for failure to raise it at Rule 50(a) (JMOL) stage. The Federal Circuit did not fully rely on waiver; it addressed the merits of the prosecution‑surrender argument (finding no clear disclaimer) but reversed on evidentiary sufficiency for other reasons. Practitioners should note that appellate courts may refrain from applying waiver mechanically, but preservation remains crucial: a preserved Rule 50(a) motion sharpens the record and preserves discrete arguments that can simplify appellate review.

Practical lessons for litigators
1) Align damages models to concrete, well-supported infringement theories. If damages depend on ongoing, life-of-patent royalties or lump sums, make sure the underlying technical mapping is robust and granular enough to survive scrutiny. Build damages models that can be scaled or reallocated if parts of the infringement theory are rejected.

2) Make expert mappings explicit, repeatable, and internally consistent. Experts should be prepared to identify—clearly and repeatedly—how each claimed element maps to specific, identifiable components, signals, or operations in the accused product. When the device under test has multiple signal paths or transformations (e.g., modeled PIM paths, complex conjugates, moduli), the testimony should explain timing (what occurs before/after sampling), the identity of inputs (x1, x2), and why those inputs correspond to the claim language.

3) Preserve Rule 50(a) issues and force the district court to rule on discrete claim‑scope questions early. If you believe an opponent has effectively narrowed their claim scope at the PTO, raise that precise argument in a Rule 50(a) motion at the close of the opponent’s evidence. Even if the district court denies the motion, a preserved record reduces the risk that the appeal will be saddled with waiver arguments and gives the appellate court a clean posture for review.

4) Avoid all‑or‑nothing verdict forms when liability is fragile and relies on technical minutiae. If infringement depends on multiple independent mechanisms or alternatives, consider special verdicts or interrogatories that require the jury to find specific mappings (e.g., which signals were sampled, whether seven multiplications were performed, which multiplications correspond to the claimed terms). That granularity improves the chance that at least part of a damages award survives if some infringement theories collapse.

5) Make the record on prosecution history explicit and linked to trial theory. If you rely on claim language that was amended during prosecution, be ready to explain at trial why the amendment does or does not exclude certain accused products. Cite the examiner’s reasoning and your responses; ensure the record shows how the claims as allowed cover the accused products.

A final thought
Finesse is a reminder that money follows proof. No amount of careful damages calculus can save an infringement award that rests on ambiguous or self‑contradictory technical testimony. For plaintiffs, the imperative is simple: close the loop between claim language, accused product operation, and damages. For defendants, the case offers a playbook: attack the linkage at trial—on the record and with focused Rule 50 motions—and you may convert a large verdict into a reversible error on appeal.
