Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### How do the Fifth Circuit JMOL standard and the Federal Circuit’s substantial evidence review interact here?
Question:
The opinion says it reviews denial of JMOL under Fifth Circuit law (de novo, reversing only if the evidence overwhelmingly favors one side) and also says infringement findings are reviewed for substantial evidence. I'm confused about which standard really drives the outcome: is the Federal Circuit applying the Fifth Circuit’s JMOL standard to assess whether substantial evidence supports the jury verdict, or are these two independent layers of review? How should I conceptualize the court’s task when it says it reverses because the verdict isn't supported by substantial evidence?
Answer:
Short answer
They’re two sides of the same coin in this appeal. The Federal Circuit follows Fifth Circuit law for reviewing a denial of JMOL (de novo, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict and reversing only if the evidence “points so strongly and overwhelmingly” for one side that no reasonable juror could disagree). At the same time it frames the jury’s infringement findings as factual findings reviewed for whether they are supported by substantial evidence (i.e., whether a reasonable jury could have found for the prevailing party). So the court’s task is a single inquiry couched in both doctrines: ask whether, viewing the record most favorably to Finesse, there is substantial evidence such that reasonable jurors could have found infringement. If there is not, the denial of JMOL must be reversed.

Why the opinion uses both formulations
- The Federal Circuit is required to apply regional-circuit law to review denials of JMOL (here, Fifth Circuit standard). The opinion states that duty and quotes the Fifth Circuit formulation (de novo review; reverse only if the evidence “points so strongly and overwhelmingly…”). (Opinion ¶ I, citing Wi‑LAN v. Apple.)
- The Federal Circuit also describes its review of the jury’s factual findings (infringement) as the familiar substantial‑evidence standard: whether a reasonable jury could have found for the prevailing party. (Opinion ¶ I, citing Apple v. Wi‑LAN.)

These are not independent, conflicting standards. Practically and functionally they require the same result: if there is not substantial evidence such that reasonable jurors could reach the verdict, then under the Fifth Circuit JMOL test the verdict cannot stand.

How the Court applied that approach here
- For the ’134 patent the court concluded the plaintiff’s case rested on confusing, self‑contradictory expert testimony (Dr. Wells). Because Finesse bore the burden, and the expert never clearly mapped the accused radio’s signals to the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” the court held “this sort of confusing change of course is not sufficient to support the jury verdict.” It therefore concluded the infringement finding lacked substantial evidence and reversed JMOL. (Opinion § I.A, citing Johns Hopkins precedent.)
- For the ’775 patent the court found the Nokia document and the expert’s testimony did not show the seven specific multiplications claimed (Dr. Wells identified three distinct multiplications but never mapped them to the seven required). The court therefore held no reasonable jury could have found the seven multiplications were performed — again framing the defect as an absence of substantial evidence and reversing JMOL. (Opinion § I.B.)

Bottom line for how to conceptualize the court’s task
- Think of it as one inquiry expressed two ways: (1) apply the Fifth Circuit JMOL test (de novo, viewing the evidence favorably to the verdict); and (2) ask whether the jury’s factual finding (infringement) is supported by substantial evidence (i.e., whether reasonable jurors could have found for the plaintiff on the facts shown). If the answer to (2) is “no,” then under (1) JMOL must be granted. The opinion uses both formulations to satisfy its obligation to apply regional JMOL law while describing the underlying factual review standard.

### Why can’t a post-ADC modeled PIM path satisfy the ’134 patent’s requirement that the receiver samples the interference-generating signals?
Question:
For the ’134 patent, the court holds that limitation 1[a] requires the RF ADC to sample a passband that includes both the signals of interest and the interference-generating signals. Finesse’s expert mapped the interference-generating signals to a modeled PIM path that, per the record, is generated downstream (after the RF ADC). Is the timing/location of signal generation dispositive as a matter of claim scope (i.e., interference-generating signals must pre-exist at the sampling stage), or could a model or estimate be considered part of the ‘received signals’? What claim language or construction forecloses using a modeled signal created later in the pipeline to meet the ‘interference generating signals’ limitation at the sampling step?
Answer:
Short answer: Because claim 1[a] expressly requires sampling a passband of “received signals” that “include … interference generating signals,” a modeled PIM path that is generated downstream of the RF ADC (after sampling) cannot satisfy that limitation. The court so held: the modeled PIM is produced in the GROOT FPGA after the RF ADC samples, and “signals cannot be sampled before they are generated,” so a post‑ADC model is not the claimed “interference generating signals.” The plaintiff therefore had to prove the receiver actually sampled interference‑generating signals, and the expert’s contradictory mapping to a post‑ADC model was legally insufficient.

Why (legal and claim‑construction reasons):

- Plain claim language controls: limitation 1[a] requires “over‑sampling … a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals.” That language ties the interference‑generating signals to the passband that is being sampled — i.e., they must be part of the received signals at the sampling stage. The court reads and enforces that textual requirement. (See opinion quoting claim 1[a] and treating the sampling requirement as the plaintiff’s burden.)

- Timing/location is dispositive under that claim language: the opinion rejects the expert’s attempt to treat a modeled signal produced after sampling as the claimed interference‑generating signal. The court noted the modeled PIM path “is generated in the GROOT FPGA” downstream of the RF ADC and emphasized that “the RF ADC cannot sample the modeled PIM path because the modeled PIM path is generated downstream of the RF ADC after sampling occurs, and signals cannot be sampled before they are generated.” In short, a post‑ADC model is not a “received signal” that was sampled.

- Burden of proof and evidentiary consequence: Finesse bore the burden to show the accused radios sampled interference‑generating signals. The court reversed JMOL because the expert’s testimony was internally inconsistent and relied on mapping the interference‑generating signal to something created after sampling; that testimony could not support the jury’s verdict. The opinion treats the distinction as fatal to infringement when the only evidence mapped the claimed element to a post‑sampling model.

What this forecloses: The opinion forecloses treating a modeled or estimated signal created later in the signal chain as the same thing as a “received” interference‑generating signal sampled by the RF ADC for purposes of limitation 1[a]. The claim’s sampling of “received signals” (and the court’s factual finding about where the modeled PIM path is produced) prevents a downstream model from satisfying the limitation.

Bottom line: Under the ’134 claim language and the court’s reading of the record, timing/location matters — interference‑generating signals must be present in the sampled received passband. A modeled PIM signal produced after sampling cannot be shoehorned into the claim as an “interference generating signal.”

### When does contradictory expert testimony become legally insufficient to support a verdict?
Question:
The court relies on Johns Hopkins v. Datascope to hold that Finesse’s infringement case (for the ’134 patent) failed because the expert gave inconsistent mappings (sometimes tying ‘interference generating signals’ to the modeled PIM path generated after ADC, other times referencing x1/x2 without clearly mapping them to the claim terms). What is the doctrinal threshold for deeming expert testimony too contradictory or confusing to constitute substantial evidence? Is it enough that there’s internal inconsistency, or must there be an affirmative concession that defeats a required element? How much latitude does a jury have to reconcile inconsistencies versus the appellate court declaring the testimony inadequate as a matter of law?
Answer:
Short answer
An expert’s testimony becomes legally insufficient when its contradictions go to the heart of the plaintiff’s burden on an essential claim element and the expert never gives a coherent, non‑contradictory mapping or explanation that a reasonable jury could rely on. The court need not wait for an express “I concede” answer; internal inconsistency that renders the expert’s proof of a required element irreconcilable is enough. The Federal Circuit will defer to the jury when reasonable jurors could reconcile a witness’s inconsistencies, but will take the evidence away from the jury where the expert’s testimony is so self‑contradictory or unexplained that “no reasonable jury could have” relied on it (Johns Hopkins v. Datascope), as the Finesse opinion applied.

Legal doctrine and standard (as used in Finesse)
- Standard of review and substantial‑evidence rule: Infringement findings are reviewed for substantial evidence (i.e., could a reasonable jury have found for the patentee on the record presented). JMOL is appropriate where, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence nevertheless “points so strongly and overwhelmingly” that reasonable jurors could not reach a contrary conclusion. Finesse (citing Fifth Circuit and Wi‑LAN).
- Johns Hopkins v. Datascope: The Federal Circuit has held that when a patentee’s case rests on an expert’s self‑contradictory testimony about whether an accused product meets a claim limitation, the testimony may be legally insufficient. In Datascope the expert said both that the device met a dimensional contact limitation and elsewhere that it only contacted in two places; the court concluded no reasonable jury could find literal infringement given the contradiction. Finesse explicitly cites and applies that principle (543 F.3d 1342, 1349).
- How the doctrine operates in practice (from Finesse): The court asked whether the expert ever clearly mapped accused‑device signals to the claim terms. Dr. Wells repeatedly (and expressly) mapped “interference generating signals” to a “modeled PIM path” that is generated after sampling — a factual impossibility for satisfying the claim limitation that requires sampling those signals. When cross‑examined, he pivoted to saying there are two signals (x1 and x2) but never clearly mapped x1/x2 to the claimed terms; he continued to use the original mapping language (DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path) without clarifying the relationship. The panel found this confusion substantive: the patentee rested its case on the expert’s mapping, and the mapping was internally inconsistent and unexplained. Applying Johns Hopkins, the court held the expert’s testimony was legally insufficient to support a finding of infringement.

When inconsistency is enough (and when it is not)
- Sufficient: Contradictions that (1) concern an essential claim limitation that the patentee must prove, and (2) are not resolved by testimony or other evidence presented at trial so that no reasonable juror could accept the expert’s account, will render the expert’s testimony inadequate as a matter of law. The Finesse court reversed where the expert’s only affirmative mapping (modeled PIM path → interference generating signals) conflicted with an uncontested technical fact (that the modeled path is produced after sampling), and the expert never provided a clear alternative mapping to satisfy the limitation.
- Not necessary to have an explicit concession: You do not need an express concession like “I concede that element X is not met.” The courts will treat an expert’s repeated, unexplained self‑contradiction as effectively defeating the proof of the element (Finesse relying on Johns Hopkins).
- Insufficient by itself: Mere minor inconsistencies, imprecision, or gaps that a reasonable juror could plausibly reconcile do not permit JMOL. The jury has wide latitude to weigh credibility and to harmonize testimony. Only when the inconsistencies make the expert’s proof of an essential element logically incoherent or impossible will the appellate court intervene.

Practical allocation of authority between jury and appellate court
- Jury latitude: Juries may and often do reconcile inconsistent testimony; credibility determinations and reasonable inferences are normally for the jury.
- Appellate supervision: Appellate courts step in when the testimony is so contradictory or internally impossible with respect to a required element that no reasonable jury could have resolved it in the patentee’s favor. Finesse demonstrates that appellate courts will apply Johns Hopkins to withdraw the case from the jury where the expert’s testimony both (a) supplies the only evidentiary foundation for an element and (b) contains uncorrected, fundamental contradictions that defeat that element.

Takeaway for litigators and students
- If your case depends on expert mapping of accused‑device features to claim terms, the expert must give a clear, consistent mapping on the record and be able to explain or reconcile any ambiguities under cross‑examination.
- Internal inconsistency that goes to an essential limitation can be fatal without an express concession; the key question is whether a reasonable juror could have relied on the expert despite the inconsistencies. Finesse shows the court will reverse where the answer is “no.”

### Did the ’775 patent’s prosecution history clearly disclaim two-signal implementations when the claims were amended to ‘three signals S1, S2 and S3’?
Question:
Appellants argued that changing ‘two or three signals’ to ‘three signals’ surrendered two-signal embodiments (like Filipovic). The Federal Circuit rejected clear and unambiguous disavowal, emphasizing the patentee’s focus on passive (not active) IMPs and a construction requiring only ‘three separately identifiable signals,’ not necessarily three unique inputs. How do we reconcile the text ‘three signals S1, S2 and S3’ with the court’s acceptance that they need not be unique? What features of the amendment/arguments fell short of Cont’l Circuits’ clear-disavowal standard, and what are examples of language that would have constituted a clear surrender in this context?
Answer:
Short answer
No. Under Continental Circuits’ “clear and unmistakable” standard the prosecution record here did not clearly disclaim two‑signal implementations. The examiner rejection was overcome by arguing Filipovic addressed active (not passive) IMPs, and the patentee amended to “three signals S1, S2 and S3” without saying those must be three distinct/unique inputs or that two‑signal embodiments were being surrendered. The Federal Circuit therefore treated the amendment as limiting to “three separately identifiable signals,” not to three unique signal sources, and held that fell short of the clear‑disavowal required to strip two‑signal products from the claims.

Relevant doctrine (short)
When patent prosecution narrows claim scope to avoid prior art, that narrowing can be a prosecution disclaimer only if the patentee’s statements or amendments clearly and unmistakably surrender the broader scope. See Cont’l Circuits, 915 F.3d at 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019) (disavowal requires clear, unambiguous statement). Ambiguous or tangential remarks—e.g., focusing on a different aspect of the prior art—do not meet that standard.

How the court applied it here (grounded in the opinion)
- The examiner rejected claims reciting “two or three signals” over Filipovic. Finesse amended the claims to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3” and argued Filipovic was limited to mitigating active IMPs while the claimed invention addressed passive IMPs. The Federal Circuit emphasized that Finesse’s distinguishing argument targeted the nature of the IMPs (active vs. passive), not an unequivocal surrender of two‑signal implementations. J.A. 985; J.A. 110–11.
- The court relied on the district court’s construction that “three signals S1, S2 and S3” requires three “separately identifiable” signals, but does not require three unique inputs—consistent with inventor testimony and intrinsic evidence. J.A. 109–14; J.A. 115. Because Finesse never expressly said “we give up any claim to two‑signal systems” or that “S1, S2 and S3 must be three distinct transmitter inputs,” the amendment alone did not constitute the clear and unambiguous disavowal required by Continental Circuits. J.A. 110–11.
- The court concluded that—at most—the prosecution history required three separately identifiable signals, but not three unique sources, and so did not “rise to the level of ‘clear and unambiguous’ disavowal” of two‑signal products. J.A. 110–11.

What features of the amendment/arguments made them insufficient
- The narrowing amendment used the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3” but did not add language tying “three” to uniqueness (e.g., “three distinct/different transmitter signals” or “three different inputs”). Mere recitation of S1,S2,S3 without a statement that they must be different leaves room for embodiments where some signals are copies or duplicates (separately identifiable but not unique). J.A. 115; J.A. 968–69.
- The prosecution arguments focused on a different ground (Filipovic addressed active IMPs; claimed invention addresses passive IMPs). That is a technical distinction of the prior art, not an express surrender of two‑signal embodiments. The court treated this as a tangential distinction, not a disclaimer of two‑signal scope. J.A. 985; J.A. 110–11.
- There was no unambiguous admission that “two signals + a copy” or any two‑input architecture is excluded; therefore the record was ambiguous as to whether two‑signal implementations were intended to be given up.

Examples of language that would likely have constituted a clear surrender
- Explicit prosecution statement conceding two‑signal scope: e.g., “We amend to require three signals and therefore expressly disclaim any embodiments or prior art that use only two input signals (such as Filipovic). The claims no longer cover systems that use only two separate inputs.”
- Claim amendment plus explanatory argument making uniqueness explicit: e.g., “We add ‘three distinct signals from three distinct transmitter outputs’ (or ‘three unique transmitter inputs’), and we distinguish Filipovic because it uses only two distinct transmitter inputs, which the applicant expressly abandons.”
- Argument that carefully links the amendment to a lost scope: e.g., “To overcome Filipovic we have narrowed the claims to require three different signals (S1 ≠ S2 ≠ S3). We therefore surrender claim coverage of any device that operates with only two different inputs.”
Any such unambiguous, contemporaneous statement that the patentee gave up two‑signal embodiments would meet the Continental Circuits standard; the prosecution history here did not contain that kind of language. J.A. 110–11.

Bottom line
An amendment to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3” can be interpreted merely as identifying three separately identifiable operands; without a contemporaneous, explicit statement that “three” means three distinct/unique inputs (or that two‑signal architectures are surrendered), the Federal Circuit will not find a clear and unmistakable prosecution disclaimer under Continental Circuits. The record in Finesse falls on the ambiguous side of that line. J.A. 109–15, 110–11.

### For the ’775 patent, must the patentee prove each of the seven specific third-order multiplications, or can three distinct products ‘stand in’ for seven?
Question:
The claim recites generating an n-th order ICS by ‘digitally multiplying and filtering’ seven specified third-order combinations of S1, S2, and S3. Finesse’s expert identified only three distinct products in Nokia’s documents (e.g., x1·|x1|·|x1|; x1·x1·x2′; x1·|x2|·|x2|), and the court held that no reasonable jury could find all seven from this. Is there any doctrinal basis (e.g., equivalence, repeated operations, or signal identity) that would allow three distinct multiplications to satisfy a claim that literally enumerates seven? Also, do operations involving complex conjugates and moduli qualify as the ‘digitally multiplying’ of S1, S2, S3 as-claimed, or does the presence of conjugate/modulus transform them into different ‘signals’ that break one-to-one mapping to the seven listed terms?
Answer:
Short answer
The patentee must prove the seven specific third‑order products recited in the claim (or at least present evidence that the accused device actually produces those seven products or an admissible one‑for‑one mapping to them). Three distinct products cannot “stand in” for the seven unless the patentee adduces evidence at trial that (a) the three products are in fact repeated or otherwise produce the seven claimed terms, or (b) there is a legitimate, jury‑presented mapping showing how particular produced products correspond to the seven claimed products. And, per the Federal Circuit in this case, operations that produce complex conjugates or moduli are not interchangeable with literally multiplying the claimed S1, S2, S3 unless the patentee proved the required mapping — the court treated conjugate/modulus forms as different for purposes of the literal claim limitation.

Why (doctrinal and opinion‑based explanation)
- Claim scope and burden: A patentee bears the burden to prove infringement by substantial evidence. The ’775 claim expressly recites generating an n‑th order ICS “by, given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering” the seven enumerated third‑order products. The court reviewed whether the evidence at trial could support a jury finding that the accused radios performed those seven specific multiplications. The question is therefore one of literal infringement and whether the evidence presented at trial proved the presence of the claimed seven multiplications (or an acceptable mapping to them).

- What the court held about the three vs. seven products: The Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL because the only technical document relied on by Finesse listed only three distinct multiplications (x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|), and there was no evidence that any of those multiplications were repeated so as to produce all seven claimed multiplications. The court emphasized that Dr. Wells “did not testify how those three distinct multiplications map onto the seven claimed multiplications,” and Finesse’s post‑trial attempt to supply a mapping was not evidence the jury considered. Under those circumstances, the court concluded no reasonable jury could find the seven claimed multiplications were performed. (Op. pp. 19–22, 38–39.)

- When fewer distinct products can suffice: The opinion makes clear the short path for allowing fewer observed/distinct operations to satisfy an enumerated list: the patentee must produce evidence at trial that either (1) the fewer observed multiplications are in fact performed multiple times so that the enumerated list is literally produced, or (2) there is a clear mapping (presented to the jury) from the operations the accused device performs to each claimed term. Absent that evidence, three distinct products do not automatically “stand in” for seven claimed products. (Op. pp. 38–39.)

- Complex conjugates and moduli: The court specifically noted the Nokia document “involves modulus and complex conjugate operations and does not show the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications” because the document does not disclose multiplying x1 and x2 directly but rather their conjugates and moduli. The court observed that Finesse’s mapping would, for example, require S3 to be simultaneously mapped to x2, x2', and |x2| — a mapping that was not presented to the jury. Thus, in this case the presence of conjugate/modulus forms was treated as producing different signal forms that break a literal one‑to‑one mapping to the claimed S1×S2×S3 terms absent evidence linking them. (Op. pp. 38–39.)

Practical takeaway for proof
- If the claim literally lists seven specific products, the patentee should prove at trial that the accused system produces those seven products (or present admissible evidence that the system repeats or otherwise generates equivalents such that the seven are present) and must present any mapping from differently expressed signal forms (e.g., conjugates, moduli) to the claimed S1, S2, S3 products to the jury. Simply pointing to three distinct algebraic terms in a document without showing repetition or a demonstrable correspondence to each claimed term is insufficient under the Federal Circuit’s ruling in this case.

### Was the ‘uniqueness’ argument about S1, S2, S3 preserved for JMOL, and how does Rule 50(a) waiver apply here?
Question:
The district court said Appellants waived the argument that S1, S2, and S3 must be ‘unique’ by not raising it at Rule 50(a). On appeal, the Federal Circuit didn’t rely on uniqueness to reverse; it instead held the evidence didn’t show seven multiplications. For preserving issues in patent cases tried to a jury, how specific must a Rule 50(a) motion be to avoid waiver? If a party challenges sufficiency on ‘not three unique signals’ but also argues ‘not seven multiplications,’ is it enough to preserve the latter even if the former is waived? Any guidance on how granular Rule 50(a) contentions must be in complex claim-element disputes?
Answer:
Short answer
- The district court concluded AT&T/Nokia waived the “S1, S2, S3 must be unique” argument because they did not raise that particular theory at the Rule 50(a) stage. The Federal Circuit did not rely on uniqueness in its reversal; instead it reversed on a preserved sufficiency ground — that no reasonable jury could have found the accused radios performed the seven multiplications required by the claim.  
- Practically, Rule 50(a) must identify the specific legal/factual ground on which JMOL is sought with enough particularity to put the district court and opposing party on notice you are challenging that claim element. If you want multiple, distinct theories preserved you must articulate them in the Rule 50(a) motion; a more general sufficiency challenge may preserve some aspects but will not preserve a distinct element-by-element contention you never raised.

Explanation and how the opinion applies
1) What the district court and Federal Circuit did here
- The district court held the uniqueness point (that S1, S2, S3 must be “unique” signals) was waived because Appellants did not raise that precise contention at the Rule 50(a) JMOL stage. (Opinion pp. 38, 40.)  
- On appeal the Federal Circuit did not affirm on that waived uniqueness theory. Instead, the court reversed because Finesse’s evidence did not support the jury’s finding that the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications (limitation 1[d]). The court found the Nokia document and Dr. Wells’ testimony showed only three distinct multiplications and that Dr. Wells never mapped those to the seven claimed multiplications — a sufficiency failure the panel treated as preserved and dispositive. (Opinion pp. 38–39, 14966–70 discussion.)

2) Rule 50(a) waiver principle as applied
- Rule 50(a) requires a party to state the grounds for JMOL with sufficient specificity at trial so the court can resolve the issue before the case goes to the jury. If a party fails to present a distinct legal or factual theory in its Rule 50(a) motion, that theory is typically waived on appeal. The district court here applied that principle to uniqueness and found waiver. (Opinion p. 38.)  
- By contrast, Appellants did present and preserve a sufficiency challenge to the evidence showing the seven multiplications (they contested that the accused radios only used two signals and that the evidence only identified three multiplications). That contention was considered on appeal and was the basis for reversal. (Opinion pp. 38–39, 14966–70.)

3) Practical guidance for patent trials (how granular must Rule 50(a) be?)
- Be element-specific. If your noninfringement theory depends on a particular meaning or feature of a claim term (e.g., “unique” signals versus merely “separately identifiable” signals), raise that theory explicitly in Rule 50(a). The court may treat a generalized sufficiency challenge as preserving only limited aspects of the argument, not every possible theory you might assert on appeal.  
- Preserve alternative theories you want considered. If you have multiple independent reasons why the patentee cannot carry its burden (e.g., claim construction/claim scope argument, and an independent evidentiary mapping/multiplication-count argument), articulate each in the Rule 50(a) motion. The Federal Circuit here reversed on the independent evidentiary insufficiency because that was argued; it did not救 rely on the uniqueness theory because it was not preserved.  
- Tie the motion to record evidence. The court will look at what you pointed to at the JMOL stage (documents, testimony, mappings). The Federal Circuit faulted Finesse for attempting to make mapping arguments on appeal that were not presented to the jury. (Opinion pp. 17–18, 14966–70.)

Bottom line
- Uniqueness: the district court treated that argument as waived under Rule 50(a); the Federal Circuit did not rely on it.  
- Seven multiplications / evidentiary sufficiency: that was preserved and was the ground on which the Federal Circuit reversed for lack of substantial evidence.  
- Lesson: when trying a patent case, raise each discrete noninfringement theory (element-by-element) you want preserved in your Rule 50(a) motion and tie it to the record.

### Why did the Federal Circuit vacate the damages award rather than remand for a new damages trial?
Question:
The court reversed JMOL denials on noninfringement for all asserted claims of both patents, then vacated the damages award. Is vacatur automatic whenever all liability findings are reversed, or are there scenarios where the Federal Circuit would still remand for a new damages trial (e.g., if some claims/products remained infringed)? If liability is entirely extinguished, is vacatur of damages the only proper disposition, and what happens to any ongoing royalty or future damages components in a lump-sum verdict covering the remaining patent term?
Answer:
Short answer
The Federal Circuit vacated the damages award because it reversed the jury’s infringement findings for all asserted claims of both patents. The panel treated the damages judgment as dependent on those liability findings, so once liability was extinguished the damages award could not stand. That is what the opinion says in plain terms: “Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we vacate the damages award.”

Why the court did that (doctrine and reasoning)
- Damages flow from liability. The court here reversed JMOL (noninfringement) for every asserted claim. With no surviving infringement finding, there is no legal basis to support the jury’s award; the damages verdict was therefore deprived of its predicate.
- The opinion does not undertake an independent evaluation of the damages proof because the dispositive liability reversal removes the need to do so — the damages award was vacated as the natural (and necessary) consequence of eliminating the underlying liability.

Is vacatur automatic whenever liability findings are reversed?
- Not necessarily in every conceivable scenario. The court’s action in this case follows the familiar proposition that a damages award cannot survive if the underlying liability it compensates for is fully negated. Here all asserted claims were reversed, so vacatur followed.
- The opinion implicitly recognizes the contrast: if some liability findings survived (for example, if some asserted claims or accused products remained found to infringe), the appellate court could instead (i) affirm liability as to the surviving claims and remand for recalculation of damages limited to those surviving bases, or (ii) remand for a new damages trial confined to the remaining infringing conduct. The opinion’s single-sentence damages disposition flows from the fact that no asserted claim survived.

What about ongoing royalties or future damages in a lump-sum verdict?
- The jury here awarded a lump sum “for the remaining life of the ’134 and ’775 patents.” That lump-sum award was explicitly tied to the (now-reversed) finding of infringement of those patents. Because the panel reversed infringement for all asserted claims, the award’s future damages/ongoing-royalty component loses its legal foundation and therefore must be vacated along with the rest of the award.
- The opinion does not address any hypothetical procedure for preserving or recalculating future damages if some form of liability had survived; it simply vacates the entire award because there is no surviving infringement to which past or future damages could attach.

Bottom line
In this case vacatur was the proper (and necessary) disposition because the Federal Circuit eliminated all liability on the asserted claims. If an appeal reversed only part of the liability (or left some claims/products infringing), the court could instead leave surviving liability intact and remand for damages limited to that surviving infringement. The panel’s terse damages disposition reflects the simple rule applied here: no infringement — no damages.
