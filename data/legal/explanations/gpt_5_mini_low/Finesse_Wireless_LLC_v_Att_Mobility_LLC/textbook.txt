Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

This chapter summarizes the parties, the accused technology, the asserted patent claims, the district court trial outcome, the post-trial motions, and the scope and standards of appellate review the Federal Circuit applied in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC. The goal is to give you the factual and procedural scaffold needed to follow the court’s legal analysis in the rest of the opinion.

## Parties and accused technology

The dispute involved Finesse Wireless LLC (the patentee) on one side and AT&T Mobility LLC and Nokia of America Corporation (collectively “Appellants”) on the other. Finesse sued AT&T, asserting that AT&T’s use of Nokia radios—specifically those that include Nokia’s PIM cancellation (PIM‑C) feature—infringed Finesse’s patents. Nokia intervened as a defendant-intervenor. The patented technology addresses passive intermodulation products (PIMs), a kind of radio-frequency interference that arises when multiple transmit signals interact with passive imperfections (for example, corroded connectors or metallic objects) to produce new frequencies (intermodulation products, or IMPs). The asserted patents claim methods for mitigating such IMP interference in radio receivers.

## Asserted patents and claims

Finesse asserted claims from two patents:

- U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 (“the ’134 patent”): Finesse asserted claims 1–3. The claims describe a method that samples a passband of received signals (including both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals”), isolates those signals (via decimating filters), computes an estimate of intermodulation products produced by the interference-generating signals, cancels the in-band IMPs using the estimates, and performs closed-loop phase and amplitude adjustments including sub-sample phase shifts.

- U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 (“the ’775 patent”): Finesse asserted claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36. Representative claim 1 recites a method for generating intermodulation product cancellation signals (ICSs) in a receiver by digitally multiplying three signals—labelled S1, S2, and S3—in seven specific third‑order multiplicative combinations (e.g., S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, etc.), with the specification focusing on passive IMPs produced in passive transmitter and receiver components downstream of the high-powered amplifier and transmitter filter.

The patents and accused Nokia documents involve technical distinctions between internal PIM (originating inside the radio) and external or passive PIM (originating outside or in passive components). These distinctions played into infringement contentions about which signals are present at the receiver’s analog‑to‑digital converter (ADC) and what the Nokia implementation actually multiplies.

## Jury verdict

The case proceeded to a jury trial in the Eastern District of Texas. The jury found all asserted claims of both patents valid and infringed. The jury awarded a lump-sum damages verdict of $166,303,391 to compensate Finesse for the remaining life of the ’134 and ’775 patents.

## Post-trial motions in the district court

After the verdict, Appellants moved for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement and JMOL on damages, and alternatively sought a new trial on damages. The district court denied both JMOL motions and denied the motion for a new trial on damages. Those denials are the rulings on appeal to the Federal Circuit.

The key factual and claim‑construction disputes that drove the post‑trial motions were:

- For the ’134 patent: whether the accused Nokia radios’ RF ADC samples a passband that includes both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals” as required by claim 1[a]. Finesse’s expert relied heavily on a Nokia technical document; Appellants argued that the signal Finesse identified as the “interference generating signal” (the modeled PIM path) is generated downstream of the RF ADC and therefore could not have been sampled, defeating that claim limitation.

- For the ’775 patent: whether the claim element requiring “three signals S1, S2 and S3” was limited to three distinct (unique) signals or instead could be satisfied by three separately identifiable signals that may include copies, and crucially whether the accused radios performed the seven distinct third‑order multiplications recited in the claim. Appellants argued the accused radios use only two unique signals (x1 and x2) and that the Nokia documents disclose at most three distinct multiplications (and not the seven required by the claims).

The district court rejected Appellants’ JMOLs and new‑trial request; the Federal Circuit’s opinion reviews those denials.

## Appellate jurisdiction

The Federal Circuit had jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1) to review the final decision of the district court following the jury verdict and the post‑trial orders denying JMOL and a new trial.

## Standards of review applied by the Federal Circuit

Understanding the standards of review the Federal Circuit applied is essential because they determine how the appellate court evaluates the district court’s denials and the sufficiency of the trial evidence:

- Denial of JMOL of noninfringement: Because the case arose in the Fifth Circuit, the Federal Circuit applies the regional‑circuit law for review of a district court’s denial of JMOL. Under Fifth Circuit law (as applied by this court), the denial is reviewed de novo, but a JMOL denial will be reversed only if, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence “points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” The Federal Circuit also states the related standard that jury infringement findings are reviewed for substantial evidence—i.e., whether a reasonable jury could have found for the prevailing party given the evidence presented.

- Substantial-evidence review of factual findings: For the jury’s factual findings about infringement, the Federal Circuit applies substantial‑evidence review—asking whether a reasonable jury could have reached the verdict based on the record.

- Claim construction: The Federal Circuit reviews claim construction de novo (i.e., without deference), but reviews any subsidiary factual findings made by the district court in support of the construction for clear error.

- Denial of a new trial on damages: The Federal Circuit applies the law of the regional circuit for reviewing denials of new trials. Under Fifth Circuit law, the denial of a new trial is reviewed for abuse of discretion.

These standards shaped the court’s analysis: the court asked whether the record contained substantial evidence to support the jury’s infringement findings for each asserted patent and whether the district court abused its discretion in denying a new trial on damages. Because the Federal Circuit concluded the jury’s infringement verdicts for both patents lacked substantial evidentiary support, it reversed the district court’s JMOL denials and vacated the damages award (rendering the new‑trial issue on damages moot).

This procedural posture and the standards of review set the framework for the court’s holdings and reasoning discussed in the subsequent sections of the opinion.

# Technical and Claim Frame: IMP/PIM Mitigation and the Accused Architecture

This chapter sets out the technical backdrop and the claim structure the Federal Circuit used to evaluate infringement in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC. Understanding the radio-interference concepts and the architecture in the Nokia materials cited at trial is necessary to follow why the court concluded the jury’s verdict on infringement was not supported by substantial evidence.

## IMP and PIM concepts

Intermodulation products (IMPs) are new frequencies that result when two or more transmitted signals combine non‑linearly. The patents and the trial materials focus on a particular practical problem: when two transmit signals mix—because of nonlinearity at some point in the system—they can generate frequency components that fall “in‑band” with signals that a receiver is trying to receive. Those in‑band IMPs interfere with reception of the intended signals.

The opinion draws an important technical distinction that appears repeatedly in the patents and the accused‑device architecture: internal (or internal-to-the-radio) PIM versus external (or passive/external) PIM. “Passive intermodulation” (PIM) is a subset of IMPs caused by passive elements such as metal connectors, rusty bolts, loose cable joints, or other physical discontinuities. Passive PIM can be generated either outside the radio (external PIM) or inside the radio (internal PIM, from mechanical issues inside the radio). The patents at issue are aimed at mitigating PIM (passive IMPs) by estimating and canceling those unwanted components in the receiver.

Keeping clear which signals exist where and when is critical in this case because the asserted claims require the receiver to sample a passband that contains both (a) the “signals of interest” and (b) the “interference generating signals.” If an element that a plaintiff’s expert identifies as an “interference generating signal” is actually produced after the receiver’s analog‑to‑digital conversion (i.e., after sampling), it cannot satisfy a claim that requires sampling those interference inputs.

## ’134 patent overview (receiver-side method)

The ’134 patent claims a method performed in a receiver for reducing IMP interference. The representative claim recited a sequence of steps that the court singled out as dispositive for infringement:

- Over‑sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, where the received signals include “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” capable of producing in‑band IMPs. (This is limitation 1[a].)
- Isolating the signals of interest and separately isolating the source signals that generate the in‑band IMPs by using decimating filters (limitations 1[b]–1[c]).
- Computing estimates of the intermodulation products from those source signals (1[d]).
- Canceling the in‑band IMPs using those estimates (1[e]).
- Performing closed‑loop phase and amplitude adjustment of the IMP estimations, including sub‑sample phase shifts (1[f]).

Two features of the claim are central to the Federal Circuit’s analysis. First, the claim places an affirmative burden on the patentee to prove that the receiver’s sampling operation actually captures both categories of signals in the claimed passband—the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Second, the claim requires that cancellation be based on computed estimates of the IMPs derived from the source signals, followed by closed‑loop adjustments including sub‑sample phase shifts. The timing and locus of the “interference generating signals” thus matter: if the accused architecture generates modeled PIM signals downstream of the receiver’s analog‑to‑digital conversion, they are not part of the passband that the RF ADC sampled and therefore do not satisfy limitation 1[a].

The opinion focuses on where the parties’ expert testimony said the various labels in Nokia’s technical materials mapped onto the claimed categories. The dispute over whether the modeled PIM path was an “interference generating signal” that the RF ADC sampled is dispositive for the ’134 claims. Because Finesse’s expert repeatedly identified the modeled PIM path as the interference‑generating signal, and the record showed that modeled PIM is generated later (in the GROOT FPGA) after sampling, the court concluded the expert’s testimony was internally inconsistent or insufficient to support a finding that the receiver sampled the requisite interference signals.

## ’775 patent overview (third‑order power‑series multiplications)

The ’775 patent claims a distinct but related receiver‑side method for cancelling passive IMPs by generating intermodulation product cancellation signals (ICSs). The representative claim requires, among other things:

- Generating ICSs continuously and near real time using copies of transmitter signals.
- The passive IMPs addressed are those generated in passive transmitter and receiver components that occur after the high‑power amplifier (HPA) and the transmitter filter, with the transmitter filter coupled between the HPA and the antenna.
- The generation of ICSs must be “based on a power series description of a non‑linear process” for generating the IMPs, and specifically the claim describes how an n‑th order ICS is formed from three signals S1, S2, and S3 by digitally multiplying them in particular third‑order combinations. For third order, the claim lists seven distinct triple products—S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3.

Two issues drove the court’s handling of the ’775 patent. First, the district court construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to require that the signals be separately identifiable; it did not import a requirement that they be three different (unique) signals. The Federal Circuit agreed the prosecution history did not clearly and unambiguously disclaim combinations that effectively use two unique inputs and a copy of one of them, so the claim construction did not categorically exclude implementations that use two unique signals plus a copy.

Second, and dispositive on appeal, the court required proof that the accused system actually performs the seven specified third‑order multiplications (or at least produces them in a way that maps onto the claim). The opinion emphasizes that the claim limitation is not satisfied by showing only some other set of distinct multiplications or by post‑hoc mappings that were not presented to the jury. At trial, Finesse’s expert pointed to Nokia materials and identified three distinct multiplications in the accused system (involving combinations of a signal and its modulus or its complex conjugate). The court concluded there was no evidence those three distinct operations corresponded to the seven claimed triple multiplications, and there was no evidence that the accused system repeats or produces the particular triple products required by the claim. The presence of modulus (|x|) and complex conjugate (x') operations in the Nokia documents further complicated any attempt to map those operations onto the claimed literal multiplications of S1, S2, and S3.

Thus, for the ’775 claims the decisive failure was evidentiary: the patentee did not present reliable testimony or documentary proof at trial that the accused radios performed the seven explicit triple products the claim requires.

## Key accused components/processes from Nokia materials

The jury and the court relied heavily on a set of Nokia technical documents that the patentee’s expert used to support infringement. Understanding the components the documents and the expert refer to helps explain why the Federal Circuit found the evidence deficient.

- RF ADC (radio‑frequency analog‑to‑digital converter). The RF ADC is the receiver’s front‑end that samples a passband of analog signals and produces a digital bitstream. If a claimed limitation requires sampling both the signals of interest and the interference‑generating signals, those interference‑generating signals must exist in the analog passband before the RF ADC samples.

- DL(TX) reference. In the Nokia diagram and legend, the “DL (TX) reference” is labeled on a red path and was identified by Finesse’s expert as corresponding to the claimed “signals of interest” (the desired downlink signal that must be protected from IMP interference).

- Modeled PIM path and GROOT FPGA. The Nokia materials also label a “modeled PIM path” on that same red path. The modeled PIM signal is generated inside the digital processing chain—specifically, the document and trial testimony placed the model PIM generation in a digital block referred to as the GROOT FPGA. The key timing point is that, according to the trial record, the modeled PIM path is generated after the RF ADC (i.e., downstream of sampling). If that modeled PIM path is what the patentee’s expert mapped to the claim term “interference generating signals,” the claim’s over‑sampling limitation is not satisfied because the modeled PIM was not in the sampled analog passband.

- Signals x1 and x2. The Nokia materials and the expert testimony also refer to signals labeled x1 and x2. These are treated in the trial record as the actual distinct inputs that the accused system uses in generating cancellation products. Much of the dispute turns on whether x1 and x2 are the two signals that are sampled by the RF ADC and whether they were the actual signals the expert meant to identify when he referenced the red path in the Nokia diagram. The expert’s testimony is internally inconsistent about whether the “red path” refers directly to x1 and x2 or whether it refers to the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path.

- Use of complex conjugates and moduli. The Nokia document the expert relied on lists multiplication‑like operations that involve a signal multiplied by its complex conjugate (x') or its modulus (|x|). Those operations are not literal multiplications of three distinct signals in the form S1×S2×S3; they are mathematical operations on x1 and x2 that produce different products (for example x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|). The court emphasized that the presence of conjugates and moduli means the Nokia document does not literally show the seven specific triple products recited in the ’775 claim, and the patentee’s expert did not explain at trial how to map the documented operations to the seven claimed multiplications.

Together, these architectural facts explain why the Federal Circuit reversed: for the ’134 patent the patentee’s expert repeatedly tied the “interference generating signals” to a modeled PIM path that was created after sampling, which cannot satisfy the claim’s sampling requirement; and for the ’775 patent the expert relied on Nokia documents that show only three distinct multiplication‑type operations (and use conjugates and moduli) without mapping them to the seven third‑order products the claim requires. Because the patentee bore the burden of proving these claim elements and the testimony was inconsistent or incomplete on these points, the court concluded no reasonable jury could have found literal infringement of either patent based on the trial record.

Understanding these technical relationships—what is sampled, where modeled signals are generated, which algebraic operations are actually performed, and how the claim language ties to those operations—is essential to reading the opinion’s legal reasoning: the court applied regional‑circuit JMOL and substantial‑evidence principles to reverse where the evidentiary record could not support the jury’s findings on critical, fact‑intensive claim limitations.

# ’134 Patent: Why Substantial Evidence Was Lacking

This chapter explains why the Federal Circuit concluded the jury verdict of infringement of the ’134 patent was not supported by substantial evidence as to claim 1[a], the oversampling limitation. That limitation requires the accused receiver to oversample “a passband of received signals” that includes both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” The court’s reversal turned on the way Finesse’s expert attempted to map components of Nokia’s technical documents onto those two required categories and the internal contradictions and timing problems in his testimony.

## The legal standard governing JMOL and substantial evidence

Before addressing the factual record, recall the legal lenses applied on JMOL. The Fifth Circuit standard governs the denial of JMOL in this appeal: a denial of JMOL is reviewed de novo, but reversal is appropriate only if, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly for one party that reasonable jurors could not have arrived at a contrary conclusion. Separately, infringement findings by a jury are reviewed for substantial evidence — meaning a reasonable jury could have found for the patentee based on the evidence actually presented. These standards require the patentee (here, Finesse) to present clear, non-contradictory evidence that the accused devices met each claim limitation, including the sampling limitation of claim 1[a].

## The disputed limitation: what claim 1[a] requires

Claim 1[a] of the ’134 patent requires “[o]ver-sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals, the interference generating signals capable of generating intermodulation products inband of the signals of interest.” Put simply, to prove infringement of this limitation, Finesse had to show the accused radio’s RF analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC) actually samples a passband that contains both the signals of interest and the signals that generate the in-band intermodulation products (the interference‑generating signals). If the alleged interference-generating signals do not exist at the ADC input (for example, because they are generated later inside the radio), they cannot satisfy claim 1[a].

## Finesse’s trial mapping through Dr. Wells

Finesse’s case at trial rested substantially on Dr. Jonathan Wells’s testimony tied to a Nokia technical diagram. Dr. Wells repeatedly identified the “DL (TX) reference” as the signal of interest and identified a “modeled PIM path” shown on the same red path of the diagram as the interference-generating signals. His expert report echoed that mapping, stating the DL(TX) reference meets the construction of “signal(s) of interest” and the modeled PIM signal is the “interference generating signal.”

That mapping, if accepted, would support infringement: both the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path were depicted on the red path feeding the RF ADC in Nokia’s diagram, so Finesse argued the ADC samples a passband containing both required categories.

## The temporal impossibility: modeled PIM path is produced after sampling

Appellants exposed a critical temporal problem on cross-examination and at JMOL: the “modeled PIM path” that Dr. Wells identified is actually generated inside the GROOT FPGA, downstream of the RF ADC, i.e., after sampling has already occurred. In other words, the modeled PIM path is an internal, computed signal produced later in the processing chain; it is not a received analog signal present at the ADC input at the time of sampling.

Because claim 1[a] requires sampling a passband of received signals that includes the interference‑generating signals, any “interference generating signals” that are only generated after sampling cannot satisfy that limitation. Appellants therefore argued (and the court acknowledged) that if the modeled PIM path is what Dr. Wells mapped to the interference‑generating signals, then the sampling limitation is not met and there can be no infringement.

## The cross-examination pivot to x1 and x2 — and why it failed

Faced with the timing point, Dr. Wells attempted to pivot on cross-examination by pointing out the red path contains two “signals” labeled x1 and x2. He suggested there are “two signals on that red path” and later said x1 and x2 are “the two inputs.” However, crucially, Dr. Wells never clearly and directly testified that x1 corresponds to the “signal of interest” and x2 corresponds to the “interference generating signal” (or vice versa) as those claim categories require.

Throughout his testimony, he continued to refer to the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path when asked about the claimed categories, and he repeatedly affirmed that the modeled PIM path was the interference‑generating signals. At most, Finesse asks the court to infer — by piecing together testimony from different parts of the record — that Dr. Wells meant x1 and x2 when he said “DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path.” The Federal Circuit found that inference both strained and unsupported by clear testimony.

## The district court’s reasoning and why the Federal Circuit disagreed

At the JMOL stage the district court accepted Finesse’s construction of Dr. Wells’s testimony. The district court concluded Dr. Wells “is clearly referring to x1 and x2” when he testified the RF ADC receives two signals (the signals of interest and the interference generating signals), and thus found sufficient evidence for the jury to conclude both categories were sampled.

The Federal Circuit reversed that conclusion. The appellate court emphasized the testimony was confusing and self‑contradictory: on the one hand Dr. Wells explicitly mapped the interference‑generating signals to the modeled PIM path (which is generated after sampling), and on the other hand he later referenced x1 and x2 without ever clearly tying them to the claim categories. The court explained that Finesse’s strategy amounted to piecing together fragments of testimony from across the transcript to create the necessary mapping, rather than pointing to a clear, consistent statement from the expert tying x1/x2 to “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” That combination — explicit mapping to a post‑ADC modeled signal plus ambiguous, indirect references to x1/x2 — left the record internally inconsistent as to what the expert actually meant.

## The controlling principle: self‑contradictory expert testimony cannot supply substantial evidence

The Federal Circuit relied on precedent holding that when the patentee’s proof rests on an expert’s contradictory testimony, a reasonable jury could not base a finding of infringement on that testimony. Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp. is instructive: there, a patentee’s expert gave internally inconsistent views about whether a device met a limitation, and this contradiction undermined the sufficiency of the evidence. Applying that principle here, the court concluded that Dr. Wells’s inconsistent testimony — explicitly mapping the interference generator to a signal created after sampling while also gesturing to two inputs without a clear mapping — was not substantial evidence that the ADC sampled a passband containing both required categories.

## The holding and its practical lesson

The Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL for the asserted claims of the ’134 patent because Finesse failed to present substantial evidence that the accused radios met claim 1[a]. The crucial failure was the absence of clear, non‑contradictory testimony tying concrete signals present at the ADC input (e.g., x1 and x2) to the claim’s “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” together with the temporal impossibility that the modeled PIM path (the expert’s explicit mapping for the interference generator) could have been sampled because it is generated after the ADC.

For law students reading opinions on infringement challenges, this part of the opinion underscores two practical points. First, each claim limitation must be tied to concrete, admissible evidence showing the accused device practices that limitation at the relevant timepoint (here, at sampling). Second, expert testimony must be coherent and consistent: a jury cannot be permitted to base a verdict on an expert’s internally contradictory account where one strand of that account undermines the necessary factual predicate for infringement.

# ’775 Patent: Three Signals and Seven Multiplications

## Overview of the contested claim limitations

The central dispute for the ’775 patent concerned two linked limitations in the representative claim: (1) the claim requires “three signals S1, S2 and S3” that are “separately identifiable,” and (2) the claim requires generating an n‑th order intermodulation cancellation signal by performing particular third‑order multiplications—the claim explicitly lists seven distinct triple products (e.g., S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, and four others). The district court’s construction accepted that the three signals must be “separately identifiable” but need not be “unique,” and the jury found infringement. On appeal the Federal Circuit reversed: it concluded that (a) the jury had no substantial evidence that the accused radios actually carry out all seven of the claimed third‑order multiplications, and (b) the prosecution history did not clearly and unambiguously surrender systems with only two unique inputs, so the claim construction itself was not the reversible error—the fault was the evidentiary showing at trial.

Understanding why the Federal Circuit reached that result requires keeping two threads separate: (i) what the claim means (construction and prosecution history issues) and (ii) whether the evidence at trial proved the accused devices met the claim’s seven‑multiplication requirement.

## Claim construction and the “separately identifiable” requirement

At summary judgment the district court construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to require that the three signals be “separately identifiable,” but the court expressly declined to read into the claim a requirement that the three be distinct or unique. The court relied on intrinsic record and credited extrinsic inventor testimony to the effect that “S1, S2 and S3” could represent the same signal or different signals so long as they are distinguishable in the processing chain (i.e., separately identifiable).

This construction matters because it shapes whether the accused system—allegedly using two unique underlying signals (x1 and x2) and copies or derived forms of them—could fall within the claim. If the claim required three different (unique) inputs, the defendants’ two‑input system would clearly miss the limitation; under the district court’s construction, however, a system that produces three separately identifiable signal streams derived from two unique inputs might meet the limitation.

The Federal Circuit did not disturb that construction on appeal; it accepted that the intrinsic/extrinsic record did not amount to a clear disavowal requiring uniqueness. Instead, the court focused on whether the proof at trial established that the accused radios actually performed the seven specifically claimed multiplications.

## Prosecution-history (surrender) argument and the court’s response

Appellants argued that Finesse had surrendered claim scope during prosecution when it amended claims from language that had allowed “two or three signals” to explicit recitation of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in order to overcome the Filipovic prior art. Appellants’ theory was that the amendment narrowed the claim to exclude arrangements using only two unique inputs—so the accused radios (allegedly using two inputs) would be outside the claim.

The Federal Circuit applied the familiar rule that prosecution disclaimer or surrender requires “clear and unambiguous” disavowal. The court reviewed the prosecution record and the applicant’s arguments to the examiner and concluded that the amendment and arguments did not amount to the kind of unmistakable relinquishment necessary to support a finding of surrender of systems with two unique inputs. The applicant had distinguished Filipovic on grounds that Filipovic addressed active IMPs and not passive IMPs, and the prosecution statements did not clearly state that three unique inputs were required. Thus the court declined to read a prohibition on two‑input systems into the claim construction; at most the amendment required three separately identifiable signals, not three unique ones.

This ruling preserved the district court’s construction and left open the possibility that a two‑unique‑input system could infringe if it produced three separately identifiable streams that corresponded to S1, S2, S3. But that doctrinal result did not resolve the case; the dispositive problem on appeal was the evidentiary showing about the seven multiplications.

## What the evidence actually showed about the multiplications

The core factual failing identified by the Federal Circuit concerns the proof that the accused radios performed the seven third‑order multiplications recited in the claim. At trial Finesse’s expert, Dr. Wells, relied principally on Nokia technical documents describing the signal processing performed by the accused radios. Those documents did show certain triple‑product‑like operations, but they did not plainly disclose the seven explicitly claimed multiplications in the form the claim requires.

Specifically, the Nokia document listed three distinct forms corresponding to the accused implementation: x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2' (x2' being the complex conjugate of x2), and x1·|x2|·|x2|. Those operations involve modulus and complex‑conjugate manipulations rather than literal multiplication of three raw signals S1, S2, S3 as listed in the claim. The document also includes a broader set of terms (the opinion references up to ten multiplication‑like terms in the document), but the document did not identify a clear mapping from those terms to the seven specific triple products recited in the claim.

The district court accepted Dr. Wells’ testimony that the accused system used three separately identifiable signals and that the three distinct multiplications listed could be equated in effect to the seven claimed multiplications. On appeal the Federal Circuit found this evidentiary basis insufficient as a matter of law: the document’s operations were not shown to correspond to the seven claimed products, and the expert did not tie the three distinct expressions to the seven claimed multiplications.

## Mapping failure: the expert’s shortfall and the improper appellate mapping

Two related evidentiary problems drove the Court’s holding.

First, at trial Dr. Wells did not provide a concrete, step‑by‑step mapping of accused‑device operations onto each of the seven claimed multiplications. He identified three distinct multiplication‑type expressions in the Nokia material but did not explain how those three expressions satisfy all seven recited triple products (including any necessary repetitions or substitutions). A jury verdict requires that the patentee satisfy its burden of proof with admissible evidence presented at trial; the patentee cannot defer creating the necessary mapping until briefing on appeal.

Second, on appeal Finesse attempted to reconcile the discrepancy by offering new mappings and arguments—that some entries in the Nokia document imply ten multiplications and that an appropriate subset corresponds to the seven claimed products. The Federal Circuit rejected that effort because the mappings and explanations relied on evidence and inferences that were not presented to the jury. The appellate court emphasized that it cannot affirm infringement based on a mapping that was not before the jury, nor on a strained reinterpretation that mixes signals with their complex conjugates and moduli in ways the expert did not articulate at trial.

The court also stressed the practical problem with Finesse’s proposed mapping: it would require treating, for example, S3 as simultaneously equal to x2, the complex conjugate x2', and the modulus |x2|—an inconsistent identification that Dr. Wells did not justify. Because the patent claim requires multiplication of the three identified signals in particular combinations, the lack of proof that the accused system actually implements those specific combinations (and in the numerosity required) was fatal.

## Role of the Nokia document’s ten‑multiplication suggestion

At oral argument and on appeal Finesse pointed to portions of the Nokia document suggesting up to ten multiplication terms. Even if the document lists ten candidate terms, the Federal Circuit found two problems: (1) the record did not identify which subset of those ten map to the seven claimed products, and (2) the expert never presented that mapping to the jury. The court refused to supply that gap by creating the mapping itself. Under the appellate standard (substantial evidence review of a jury verdict), the court asked whether a reasonable jury, viewing the trial record, could have found that the accused radios perform each of the seven claimed multiplications. Without the mapping in the record, the court answered no.

## Legal standard and application to the seven‑multiplication requirement

The panel reviewed the denial of JMOL under the Fifth Circuit’s standard (de novo for the denial, but with the familiar view-evidence-in-the-light-most-favorable‑to‑the‑verdict rule). For infringement, the court asks whether the jury’s verdict is supported by substantial evidence—i.e., whether a reasonable jury could have found for the patentee based on the evidence presented. Here the Federal Circuit concluded that reasonable jurors could not have found all seven multiplications performed because the evidence showed only three distinct multiplication‑type expressions, lacked a trial‑level mapping to the seven claimed products, and relied on manipulations (conjugates/moduli) that were not shown to correspond to the multiplicative combinations the claim requires.

The court distinguished the claim‑construction outcome (which allowed three separately identifiable signals) from the separate factual question of whether the accused product met the claim’s multiplication limitation. Even accepting the district court’s non‑uniqueness construction, the patentee still had to prove that the accused device executed the seven specific triple products required by the claim. Failing that, there was not substantial evidence to support the jury verdict of infringement.

## Result and takeaways

Because the jury’s verdict on the ’775 asserted claims rested on insufficient evidence of the seven required multiplications, the Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the ’775 patent. The court’s decision turned not on rewriting the claim construction or finding prosecution disclaimer, but on the patentee’s failure at trial to present adequate evidence tying accused‑device operations to each of the seven claimed multiplication combinations. The appellate court vacated the damages award consistent with reversal of infringement.

For a student learning to read opinions, the key lessons are: (1) distinguish claim‑construction issues from evidence of infringement under that construction; (2) prosecution amendments must amount to clear and unambiguous disavowal to limit claim scope beyond the plain claim language; and (3) at trial the patentee must present concrete, admissible mappings from accused‑device operations to each claim limitation—an expert’s conclusory or post hoc assertions, or appellate‑only mappings not presented to the jury, will not sustain an infringement verdict on appeal.

# Damages and Final Disposition

This final portion of the opinion explains what happened to the jury’s monetary award after the court reversed the infringement findings, the standards the appellate court applied to the district court’s rulings on JMOL and a new trial, and the formal disposition entered by the Federal Circuit.

Damages posture: the jury verdict and post-trial motions
At trial the jury found all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents both valid and infringed and awarded a single lump‑sum damages award of $166,303,391 for the remaining life of those patents. After the verdict, Appellants moved in the district court for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement and for JMOL on damages or, alternatively, a new trial on damages. The district court denied the JMOL and denied the motion for a new trial. Those denials were the rulings appealed to this court.

Standards of review applicable to the post‑trial rulings
The opinion applies the standard-of-review rules that govern appellate review of district court rulings on JMOL and on motions for a new trial. Denial of JMOL is reviewed under regional-circuit law (here, the Fifth Circuit), but the Federal Circuit explains its substantive review of the infringement finding under the substantial-evidence standard: a jury finding is supported if a reasonable jury could have so found in light of the trial evidence. By contrast, denials of a new trial are reviewed for abuse of discretion under Fifth Circuit law. The court notes this distinction but did not need to defer to an abuse-of-discretion standard for the infringement issues because it reversed the jury verdicts under the substantial‑evidence analysis.

Effect of the noninfringement rulings on damages
Because the Federal Circuit concluded that no reasonable jury could have found infringement of the asserted claims of either patent—i.e., the infringement verdicts were not supported by substantial evidence—the predicate for the damages award was eliminated. Damages in a patent case flow from a finding of liability; if the asserted claims are not infringed, there is no basis for an award of past or future royalties or lump‑sum damages tied to those claims. Accordingly, after reversing the denial of JMOL on noninfringement for both the ’134 and ’775 patents, the court vacated the jury’s $166.3 million lump‑sum damages award. The panel did not reach or otherwise alter the district court’s prior rulings on damages under the abuse‑of‑discretion standard because reversing noninfringement made the damages award legally unsupportable and required vacatur.

Final judgment and costs
The court’s disposition is succinct: it reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of both patents and vacated the damages award. The opinion concludes by awarding costs to the defendants‑appellants (AT&T and Nokia). The Federal Circuit thus returned judgment in favor of the appellants on the issues decided on appeal—eliminating liability and the associated damages award—and directed the usual cost award to the prevailing appellants.
