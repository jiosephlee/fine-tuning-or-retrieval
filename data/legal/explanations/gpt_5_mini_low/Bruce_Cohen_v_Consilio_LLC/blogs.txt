Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

## Commissioner-Only Penalties under Minnesota’s MPWA: The Eighth Circuit Reads § 181.101 Narrowly

Why this matters: The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC (No. 24-2079, Oct. 6, 2025) narrows the private enforcement toolkit available to Minnesota wage plaintiffs. By holding that the “average daily wage” penalties described in Minn. Stat. § 181.101 are enforceable only by the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry (the Commissioner), not by private plaintiffs in civil suits, the court removes a commonly litigated leverage point that employees and their lawyers have used to obtain recoveries—or to extract settlements—when employers change pay practices or short the last paycheck.

What the court did: The panel applied a straightforward text-first statutory interpretation. Section 181.171—Minnesota’s private right of action provision—permits an aggrieved party to bring civil suit for MPWA violations and ties available remedies to “the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.” But § 181.101, the subsection at issue, expressly contemplates the Commissioner serving a demand, collecting unpaid wages and a penalty if the employer fails to pay within ten days, and remitting recovered money to the employee. The Eighth Circuit relied on that language, on Minnesota precedent about giving plain statutory language effect, and on the legislature’s demonstrated ability elsewhere in the MPWA to authorize employee-initiated penalties (e.g., Minn. Stat. §§ 181.13 and 181.14), to conclude that the statute vests penalty authority with the Commissioner rather than private litigants. The district court’s grant of summary judgment on the MPWA penalty claim was therefore affirmed.

The court anchored its conclusion in two textual moves worth noting. First, the statute’s procedural framework gives the Commissioner a central, active role in demanding and collecting penalties; the text says that money collected “must be paid to the employee concerned,” which the court read as consistent with a government-initiated enforcement mechanism rather than a private suit. Second, the panel invoked the canonical interpretive point that where the legislature knew how to empower private plaintiffs to collect penalties in adjacent provisions (the discharged-employee and quitting-employee penalty provisions), its silence in § 181.101 indicates a deliberate choice.

How this fits with prior decisions: The opinion squarely aligns with Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., a Minnesota federal-district decision the Eighth Circuit cited that reached the same outcome under § 181.101. By treating Hull as consistent authority and reinforcing a text-driven approach, the panel sends a signal to lower courts to respect statutory allocation of enforcement authority unless the text says otherwise. The decision also dovetails with Minnesota caselaw emphasizing that when statutory language is plain and unambiguous, courts should give it effect.

Practical consequences for wage plaintiffs and litigators: This is a practical ruling with predictable consequences.

- Reduced leverage in fully compensated cases. When employers pay the base wages and even liquidated damages demanded by a plaintiff (as Consilio did here), plaintiffs often seek statutory civil penalties to preserve leverage—both to cover counsel’s time and to deter ongoing or future violations. If § 181.101 penalties must be pursued through the Commissioner, private plaintiffs lose a directly accessible penalty remedy in these circumstances. That matters particularly where the Commissioner may have limited resources, be slow to act, or decline to pursue enforcement on any given fact pattern.

- Settlement dynamics shift. Defendants facing MPWA claims may be less incentivized to pay a premium in settlement where plaintiffs cannot use § 181.101 penalties directly in private suits. Plaintiffs’ counsel will need to pivot to other levers—claims under different statutes that permit private penalties, requests for liquidated damages under the MFLSA (federal or state analogues), or seeking injunctive relief or attorney’s fees where available. This can reduce plaintiffs’ bargaining power and change how plaintiffs value cases where unpaid wages have already been remedied.

- More attention to alternative statutory avenues. Litigants will scrutinize other state and federal provisions for penalty or fee-shifting features. The Eighth Circuit’s opinion notes the legislature did authorize private penalties elsewhere in the MPWA; plaintiffs’ counsel may increasingly rely on those provisions or on parallel federal-law theories when possible. Where the lawyer’s principal goal is deterrence or systemic change, the ruling steers them toward public-enforcement channels.

- Potential for reduced class-action utility. For putative classes seeking statutory penalties under § 181.101, the decision means the remedy is unavailable in private class litigation. That reduces one common statutory stick that makes class litigation attractive for low-dollar wage claims.

Doctrine and jurisdictional ripples: The opinion keeps careful doctrinal boundaries. It does not collapse the distinction between penalties payable to the government and damages payable to a private plaintiff; rather, it enforces the statutory allocation. The panel also distinguished the MPWA penalty question from the separate standing/mootness concerns the plaintiff raised under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA). The court vacated the MFLSA penalty ruling and remanded for the district court to resolve mootness and standing, recognizing that statutory penalties paid to a government official can still sometimes confer Article III standing (Friends of the Earth), depending on whether the plaintiff demonstrates continued personal stake and the risk of recurrence. The concurrence emphasized uncertainty on standing for MFLSA penalties—underscoring that the MPWA holding is doctrinally discrete from standing questions that arise when the remedy benefits the public purse rather than a private litigant.

Policy and legislative considerations: Should the legislature fix this? Two competing considerations arise.

- Arguments for a fix: If policymakers are worried about under-deterrence—especially where employers can avoid meaningful legal pressure by making belated wholesale payments—authorizing private plaintiffs to recover statutory penalties in more contexts would enhance enforcement, expand private enforcement capacity, and help litigants obtain meaningful remedies for historically undercompensated work. Private enforcement often operates as the principal enforcement mechanism in wage-and-hour law; allowing private penalties can correct for under-resourced government enforcement.

- Arguments against a fix: Public enforcement serves different objectives and is not costless. Vesting penalty authority in the Commissioner allows for centralized prioritization, consistent enforcement, and perhaps measured use of penalties to avoid multiplicative or duplicative sanctions. Congress and state legislatures sometimes prefer that fines and penalties be assessed through administrative apparatuses that can consider systemic impacts. Expanding private penalties risks over-deterrence, inconsistent application, or settlements that leave the government out of the enforcement calculus.

From a pragmatic standpoint, a legislative fix is possible but not inevitable. If Minnesota policymakers want to empower private litigants to seek the particular “average daily wage” penalty at issue, they can amend § 181.101 to say so explicitly; the Eighth Circuit’s reading makes clear what language change would be necessary. Whether that will happen likely depends on lobbying by employee-side bar groups and political appetite in the legislature.

What plaintiffs should do now: Lawyers representing wage plaintiffs in Minnesota should (1) audit statutory bases for penalties early in a case and identify where private recovery is actually available; (2) consider parallel MFLSA claims or other state statutes that permit private penalties or liquidated damages; (3) factor the Commissioner’s role into settlement strategy and consider referring matters to the Commissioner when public enforcement may be a path to penalties; and (4) preserve injunctive and broader equitable claims where ongoing policy changes (rather than isolated nonpayment) are at stake, because deterrence and prevention remain viable forms of relief.

Bottom line: Cohen v. Consilio is a reminder that statutory text still matters. The Eighth Circuit declined to read § 181.101 as implicitly granting private plaintiffs the power to recover the Commissioner’s penalty, particularly where the statute’s structure vests initial enforcement authority with the Commissioner and adjacent MPWA provisions explicitly grant private penalties. The decision reduces one private-enforcement tool in Minnesota wage litigation, shifts bargaining leverage, and points litigants and policymakers to the legislature (or to the Commissioner) if they want a different allocation of enforcement authority.



# MFLSA Penalties Meet Article III: Tender, Voluntary Cessation, and the Laidlaw Question

Why this matters — and fast
The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC (Oct. 6, 2025) does a careful, practically important thing: it refuses to paper over Article III when a defendant pays the core monetary relief mid‑litigation but the plaintiff seeks statutory penalties that — by statute — flow to the government. Employers regularly respond to wage suits by tendering unpaid wages (and sometimes liquidated damages) to blunt or moot cases; courts and litigants need clear guidance on when such tenders actually defeat federal jurisdiction. The panel affirms dismissal of Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) penalties (because only the state commissioner can pursue those penalties), but it stops short of disposing of Cohen’s Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) penalties claim and vacates that part of the summary‑judgment ruling for a jurisdictional reexamination. The court’s approach highlights the tricky boundary between a plaintiff’s continuing interest in injunctive and deterrent relief and the constitutional requirement that a plaintiff must have a personal stake in each form of relief sought.

What the court actually did
There are three main points from the opinion.

First, on the MPWA (Minn. Stat. § 181.101), the Eighth Circuit follows a straightforward statutory reading: average‑daily‑wage penalties are available only to the Commissioner. Section 181.101 conditions the collection of penalties on a prior demand and collection process the Commissioner controls; the statute explicitly directs the Commissioner to pay any collected money to the employee, but it does not confer on private plaintiffs a separate right to secure those penalties. Because the statute itself channels enforcement to the commissioner, the court affirmed summary judgment for Consilio on that claim.

Second, on the MFLSA penalties, the posture was different. Consilio had paid Cohen the overtime and liquidated damages that Cohen himself sought and stipulated had been satisfied. But the MFLSA also authorizes statutory civil penalties that, unlike overtime and liquidated damages, are payable to the state. The court recognized that penalties payable to government coffers can nonetheless serve a private plaintiff’s Article III interest in Laidlaw — namely, deterrence and prospective assurance that unlawful practices stop — but the key jurisdictional question remains: is there still a live controversy? Because the record was unclear whether Consilio continued to employ the same overtime policy (the very policy Cohen challenged), the court vacated the district court’s grant of summary judgment on the MFLSA penalty claim and remanded for the district court to determine whether it still has jurisdiction (mootness/standing). Put differently, if the policy persists, Cohen may have a live interest in penalties that deter or eradicate ongoing violations; if the policy is gone and its cessation is “absolutely clear,” the claim may be moot.

Third, the court brushed aside Cohen’s MWTA claim (for notice violations) for lack of developed argument and left the injunctive‑relief request unresolved because Cohen had altered his requested equitable relief without properly amending the complaint.

The Laidlaw anchor — when do penalties vindicate Article III?
Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw supplies the doctrinal anchor here: a plaintiff can have standing to seek civil penalties paid to government coffers if those penalties redress an Article III injury by deterring or stopping ongoing unlawful conduct. Laidlaw’s logic is not limitless. A plaintiff pressing penalties must show a continuing or threatened injury tied to the challenged practice at the time jurisdiction is assessed. The Eighth Circuit correctly insists on that temporal hook: standing and mootness are not satisfied merely because a statute authorizes penalties payable to the state; there must be a concrete, ongoing stake for the private litigant in securing those penalties.

Two common fact patterns illustrate the friction: (1) tender of relief (defendant pays wages and liquidated damages while litigation is pending), and (2) voluntary cessation (defendant changes the contested policy or practice during litigation). Tendered wages often eliminate the plaintiff’s personal monetary injury, and voluntary cessation can render injunctive or deterrent relief unnecessary — but voluntary cessation does not automatically resolve Article III questions. The defendant must show that the cessation renders the wrongful conduct incapable of repetition; otherwise, courts will often preserve jurisdiction because penalties can still serve a prospective, deterrent function.

What the Eighth Circuit emphasized — and what it left open
The opinion does three sensible things: (1) it enforces the statutory text that channels certain penalties to the commissioner; (2) it recognizes that penalties payable to the state can still vindicate a private plaintiff’s Article III interest under Laidlaw; and (3) it refuses to decide the Laidlaw/mootness question on an underdeveloped record. That posture is modest but correct: whether an employer’s mid‑suit payments moot a penalties claim depends on contemporaneous facts about the allegedly wrongful policy and the likelihood the conduct will recur.

Judge Kobes’s concurrence adds a cautionary voice: he questions whether Laidlaw’s reasoning fully supports private‑plaintiff standing for MFLSA penalties and urges the district court to address jurisdiction early on remand. That separate concurrence underscores an open doctrinal question: Laidlaw was an environmental enforcement case in which the penalties addressed continuing pollution. Courts have since applied Laidlaw across contexts, but applying that remedial logic to statutory penalties that the legislature has expressly made payable to the state raises conceptual and prudential concerns that deserve thorough litigation.

Practical and doctrinal takeaways

For defendants: Tender responsibly, and don’t assume payment buys mootness.
- Paying a plaintiff’s claimed wages and liquidated damages may neutralize the plaintiff’s personal monetary claim, but it does not automatically extinguish claims for statutory penalties or injunctive relief — especially where the challenged policy may persist. If the goal is to eliminate litigation exposure, defendants should consider (i) promptly and transparently rescinding the challenged policy, (ii) documenting the rescission and the durability of the change, and (iii) seeking a covenant not to reapply the policy or an explicit settlement resolving all statutory claims (including penalties) if feasible. Absent that, courts will remand and scrutinize whether the defendant’s voluntary cessation is genuine and durable.

For plaintiffs: Preserve the Article III link to deterrence.
- Plaintiffs who seek penalties payable to the state should develop and preserve evidence showing a continuing or threatened injury — for example, proof that the policy remains, that the employer retains an incentive or capacity to revert to the old practice, or that the challenged conduct affects multiple employees in a manner likely to recur. Plaintiffs should also brief Laidlaw squarely and explain how penalties will provide prospective relief by deterring future violations, not merely furnish a windfall to the state.

For courts and future doctrine: Laidlaw’s fit will keep being tested.
- The Eighth Circuit’s remand invites lower courts to engage carefully with Laidlaw’s standing analysis in the wage‑law context. Will judges require plaintiffs to show a realistic risk of recurrence beyond mere theoretical possibility? How much weight will courts give a defendant’s unilateral policy change? And should courts require a stricter showing when statutory penalties are payable to a state official rather than to the private plaintiff? Those are live questions. Judge Kobes’s separate doubts signal that some judges want a more skeptical posture before permitting private litigants to pursue government‑directed penalties under Article III doctrines derived from Laidlaw.

Bottom line
Cohen v. Consilio is not a headline‑grabbing doctrinal shift; it is a practical, careful application of existing Article III principles to common wage‑and‑hour litigation tactics. The decision reaffirms that statutory structure matters (the MPWA channels penalties to the commissioner), and it preserves judicial oversight of jurisdictional thresholds where defendants tender relief or change policies mid‑suit. Expect more litigation on the margins — and expect that defendants who treat payment alone as a jurisdictional trump will find the courts asking whether their policy changes truly remove the prospect of future harm.



# The Vanishing MWTA Claim: Notice Violations, Penalties, and Waiver on Appeal

Why this matters: Employers and employee-side lawyers sometimes focus on notice provisions in state wage statutes as an easy route to statutory penalties. The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC (Oct. 6, 2025) shows the limits of that strategy under Minnesota law. Cohen’s Wage Theft Act (MWTA) claim—grounded in an alleged failure to provide statutorily required notice of an overtime-policy change—was affirmed as dismissed because he had been paid the wages he sought and pointed to no statutory authority allowing a private party to recover civil penalties under the specific MWTA provision at issue. The opinion is a useful reminder that (1) remedy provisions matter as much as substantive violations, (2) appellate briefing must identify statutory footing for remedies, and (3) employers still have good reason to document compliance even where private penalties are unlikely.

## What the court held (and why)

Cohen sued Consilio under multiple Minnesota statutes after the company abolished its overtime premium pay for certain licensed-attorney reviewers. He alleged, among other things, that Consilio violated the MWTA’s notice requirements—Minn. Stat. § 181.032(d)(4) and (f)—by failing to give statutorily required notice of the change, and sought penalties.

The Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s dismissal of Cohen’s MWTA claim. Two discrete strands of reasoning drove that result. First, Cohen had stipulated that he had received all unpaid overtime and liquidated damages he claimed—there was no unpaid wage claim left. Second, and dispositive on appeal, Cohen failed to identify any statutory authority that would permit a private litigant to recover civil penalties under the specific MWTA provision he invoked. Because his brief offered no meaningful argument or authority supporting entitlement to penalties, the court treated the issue as waived and declined to disturb the dismissal. The opinion cites the settled appellate principle that issues unsupported by argument and authority are forfeited (Milligan v. City of Red Oak).

The practical takeaway from the panel’s treatment: when a statute ties remedial power to an enforcement mechanism (for example, designating the Commissioner as the actor who may assess penalties), private plaintiffs cannot bootstrap civil penalties into their claims without a clear textual or statutory hook.

## The statutory landscape: why remedy language controls

Minnesota’s wage statutes, like many states’, separate substantive obligations (pay this, provide that notice) from remedies and enforcement channels. The MWTA and related wage provisions contain a mixed bag: some sections expressly authorize the Commissioner of Labor to demand and collect penalties or direct that certain sums be paid to the Commissioner; other sections provide an explicit private right to recover wages and liquidated damages. The Eighth Circuit’s opinion emphasizes that remedy allocation matters.

Here, Cohen pointed to the MWTA notice provision but failed to point to a sub-section that creates a private cause of action for penalties payable to the employee. Unlike other Minnesota provisions that explicitly enable an aggrieved employee to “charge and collect a penalty” upon making a demand (see, e.g., Minn. Stat. § 181.13), the MWTA language at issue ties penalty collection to the Commissioner or is silent about a private penalty remedy. The court relied on the canon that where the legislature knows how to authorize private penalties in one statute and did not do so in another, courts should not read a private penalty into the latter.

This doctrinal posture is predictable: courts require more than a showing of breach; they require a textual basis for the specific remedies sought. The Eighth Circuit applied that principle in short order, and Cohen’s brief failure to marshal statutory support made the argument untenable.

## Waiver on appeal: argumentation matters

A nontrivial part of why the MWTA claim did not survive appeal was procedural: Cohen did not meaningfully brief or cite statutory authority showing a private penalty remedy under the MWTA provision he invoked. The court explicitly invoked Milligan to explain that an issue lacking legal argument and supporting authority is waived. That is a practical but important lesson: even meritorious-looking statutory claims can be lost on appeal—or never gain traction in the district court—if litigants do not tie the alleged violation to the specific remedial language that permits relief.

This element of the decision reinforces the division of labor between advocacy and adjudication: judges and appellate panels expect advocates to map the remedies landscape, not ask courts to do heavy statutory-interpretive lifting without briefing.

## Compliance lessons for employers (and cautionary notes)

Employers should not read this opinion as a free pass to ignore notice duties. The court’s holding is narrow: it affirms dismissal in an instance where the employee had been paid the wages owed and pointed to no private penalty authority under the MWTA provision. Employers still face regulatory enforcement by the Commissioner, statutory claims under other provisions that do authorize private penalties, class claims in other circumstances, and reputational and contractual consequences.

Practical steps for employers:

- Document all policy changes and the notices provided to affected employees (retain copies of emails, acknowledgment receipts, and dated policy documents).
- When making policy changes that affect pay structure or overtime, coordinate with counsel to ensure any statutorily required notice language and timing are satisfied—do not assume absence of private penalties equals absence of risk.
- Be alert to statute-specific remedial schemes: what one statute authorizes (private recovery of penalties) another may assign to an administrative agency. Treat each statutory duty on its own terms.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

The opinion underscores a few doctrinal themes likely to matter across wage-and-hour litigation. First, remedial specificity matters. Courts will not invent private penalties where the statute allocates enforcement to an agency or is silent. Second, the decision highlights the practical interplay between mootness/standing and remedies: an employee who has been paid the wages at issue may lack a continuing personal interest in certain forms of relief (especially where penalties, not wages, would be payable to the government). The Eighth Circuit remanded separately on MFLSA penalty questions for the district court to examine mootness/continuing injury, showing the court’s readiness to police Article III limits.

Finally, the case is a reminder of the limits of penalty-focused litigation tactics. Plaintiffs’ counsel who seek statutory penalties should be prepared to show more than a policy breach: they must identify the statutory mechanism that makes penalties payable to them (or otherwise justify an equitable remedy). Defendants, for their part, get some short-term comfort from the ruling, but the practical and administrative remedies available to regulators and the possibility of other statutory claims mean that compliance remains the safer course.

## Bottom line

Cohen v. Consilio is a cautionary tale about reading remedies into statutes and the perils of unmoored appellate briefing. Minnesota’s MWTA notice provisions can be enforced, but who enforces them—and who receives any penalty—is dictated by statutory text. Employers should continue to document and follow notice rules; plaintiffs should be rigorous in tying injunctive or penalty requests to explicit remedial language. The district court’s dismissal of the MWTA claim stands, not because notice didn’t matter, but because the remedy Cohen sought was unsupported and unbriefed.



## Remedies Must Be Pleaded: Injunctive Relief, Rule 15, and the Late Pivot Problem

Why this case matters
Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC (8th Cir. Oct. 6, 2025) is a useful, pragmatic reminder that in wage-and-hour litigation, the form of relief a plaintiff seeks matters procedurally and strategically. The panel affirmed most of the district court’s rulings but expressly refused to consider a new request—raised for the first time in summary‑judgment briefing—that Consilio be ordered to rescind its 2019 overtime policy. The court reiterated the familiar rule that new claims and new affirmative relief should be pleaded through an amended complaint under Rule 15, not sprung on the court and the defendant at the summary‑judgment stage. The opinion also highlights jurisdictional and mootness traps when plaintiffs seek statutory penalties that primarily benefit the government (or the state’s commissioner), and it contains a concurring note flagging attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest as likely issues on remand. Together these rulings should inform counsel’s timing, pleading choices, and evidentiary strategy when seeking prospective relief in wage cases.

What the court held and why
Cohen sued after his employer announced a change eliminating overtime premium pay. He sought money damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief. The district court granted summary judgment for Consilio on all claims. On appeal the Eighth Circuit affirmed except as to the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) claim, which it vacated and remanded for the district court to resolve whether it still has jurisdiction given that Consilio had paid the overtime and liquidated damages Cohen previously claimed. On the injunctive‑relief point, the panel refused to consider Cohen’s summary‑judgment argument that Consilio should be ordered to rescind the 2019 policy because that remedy was not pleaded; the court treated the late‑raised demand as an improper new claim and cited Rule 15 principles and precedent that new claims belong in an amended complaint.

The court’s rationale is straightforward: a complaint frames the litigation, gives fair notice to defendants, and defines the universe of discovery and relief. A litigant cannot wait until summary judgment to ask the court for an affirmative, prospective order that was not part of the operative pleading. The panel relied on binding district‑court practice and its own prior instruction that “new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs.” The district court’s omission of an analysis on the MWTA notice/penalty claim was similarly unavailing to the appellant because Cohen did not marshal a meaningful argument on appeal.

Practical implications for plaintiffs seeking prospective relief
Cohen is not a novel application of Rule 15, but it provides a crisp checklist of procedural pitfalls to avoid when a litigant wants injunctive or other forward‑looking relief in wage disputes:

- Plead prospective relief early. If you want an injunction (including a remedy that rescinds a written policy or requires affirmative corrective measures), include a clear, specific request for that relief in the complaint. Label the request as injunctive and state the factual and legal bases for prospective relief. That creates defendant notice and preserves the issue through discovery and summary judgment.

- Use Rule 15 proactively, not defensively. If the need for prospective relief arises after filing—because you discover a wider or continuing pattern of violations—move to amend under Rule 15(a) as soon as practicable. The Eighth Circuit’s admonition suggests courts will be unreceptive to raising an injunction for the first time in summary‑judgment briefing.

- Tailor your proposed injunction to what you can prove. Courts ask whether the plaintiff faces a real, ongoing injury or a threat of repeated injury sufficient to justify prospective relief. That requires factual development: (1) evidence the policy is still in effect or has concrete ongoing effects; (2) proof of the likelihood of future harm to the plaintiff or the class; and (3) evidence of attempts to obtain relief administratively where required (for example, any pre‑suit notices to a state commissioner if the statute conditions remedies on agency demand). Vague or categorical requests to “rescind the policy” are weak without supporting facts showing the policy’s continuing operation and impact.

- Coordinate injunctive requests with penalty theories. The Eighth Circuit’s opinion emphasizes that some statutory penalties (notably under Minnesota law at issue here) either are payable only to the state commissioner or otherwise implicate public enforcement mechanisms. When penalties primarily benefit the government, plaintiffs must think through standing and mootness: a defendant’s voluntary cessation or payment of monetary relief can deprive a court of a live case unless there is a credible showing the conduct will recur. If you seek penalties that go to the state or its commissioner, plead and develop parallel claims that justify prospective relief on traditional equitable grounds (e.g., likelihood of future violations, public‑interest considerations) so a court has a basis to enter a forward‑looking remedy even if monetary relief has been tendered.

- Preserve discovery targeted at ongoing practices. If you anticipate jurisdictional or mootness arguments, obtain documentary and deposition evidence showing the employer’s current pay practices, policy revisions, communications to employees, and any internal decision‑making about overtime policy. That evidence will be essential both to oppose mootness and to support a well‑pleaded injunction.

Mootness, penalties payable to the state, and standing
A central wrinkle in Cohen is that Consilio paid the overtime and liquidated damages the plaintiff had originally demanded, and the statutes at issue sometimes channel penalties to the commissioner, not the employee. The panel sensibly vacated the MFLSA ruling and remanded for the district court to address whether it retains jurisdiction over Cohen’s penalty claim. The court correctly recognized that obtaining fines payable to the government does not automatically confer standing for a private plaintiff absent a continuing injury; Friends of the Earth permits prospective relief where plaintiffs show ongoing or imminently threatened injury, but the plaintiff bears the burden of showing that the challenged conduct is likely to recur.

This is a common problem in wage cases: defendants can blunt litigation by tendering backpay and liquidated damages, and plaintiffs can be left seeking only penalties that benefit the state. To avoid that, plaintiffs should anchor their cases in concrete allegations of continuing or systemic practices that place them at risk of future violations, and litigate both the factual question of recurrence and the legal question of whether penalties payable to the state can redress their injury or are ancillary to an equitable remedy.

What to expect on remand — fees, costs, and interest
Judge Kobes’s separate concurrence is short but noteworthy: he flags attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest as issues he would vacate and let the district court reconsider on remand. That is an important signal. Fee entitlement under many fee‑shifting statutes often depends on securing non‑moot relief; courts sometimes deny fees when the plaintiff’s victory is ephemeral or when the relief sought is not within the plaintiff’s live interest. If the district court concludes it has no jurisdiction to award penalties or prospective relief, entitlement to fees and interest may be affected. Conversely, if the court finds the policy remains in effect or that Cohen retains a live interest in injunctive relief, fee applications will be live and vigorously litigated.

Counsel should therefore preserve the record on reasonableness and necessity of fees, tie fee requests to particular claims and the relief obtained, and be prepared to brief the interplay between mootness, equitable relief, and fee eligibility. On the defense side, employers who tender backpay should anticipate fee hearings and be prepared to argue that voluntary cessation or full tender mooted the litigation and therefore undercuts fee awards.

Bottom line
Cohen is a practical lesson: if you want more than money, say so early and build the factual record for prospective relief. Don’t try to spring a rescission or other affirmative injunction from the summary‑judgment podium without having pleaded it and developed the evidence to justify it. And be mindful that penalties payable to a state official raise standing and mootness concerns that must be addressed by pleading and proof of ongoing or likely future injury. Finally, remember that fee, interest, and costs issues will often ride the coattails of whether a plaintiff secures non‑moot, prospective relief — a fact that should shape litigation strategy from the outset.
