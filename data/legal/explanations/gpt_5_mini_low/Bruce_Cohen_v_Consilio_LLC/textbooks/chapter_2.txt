Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 2: # Appellate Standards and Jurisdictional Frame

This chapter explains the appellate standards the Eighth Circuit applied in Cohen v. Consilio, LLC and the jurisdictional (standing and mootness) principles that shaped the court’s treatment of Cohen’s claim for statutory penalties under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA). The opinion centers on two doctrinal axes: (1) the familiar summary‑judgment framework the court uses on appeal; and (2) the constitutional requirement that a plaintiff maintain a live case or controversy throughout the litigation, which governs whether a claim for civil penalties remains justiciable after the defendant has taken corrective or mitigating action.

## Summary judgment standard and viewing the evidence

The court began by stating the standard of review that controls an appeal from a district court’s grant of summary judgment. Summary judgment is appropriate only when there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. On de novo review, the appellate court independently applies that standard. In doing so the court must view the evidence and all reasonable inferences drawn from it in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party—in this case, Cohen. Those principles are procedural fundamentals: they tell us both the burden a movant faces at summary judgment and how an appellate court assesses whether the district court correctly concluded that no triable issue remained.

In Cohen the Eighth Circuit relied on its prior decisions (for example, Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc.) to restate these rules before assessing whether the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims survived summary judgment. Practically, that meant the court measured Consilio’s statutory defenses and Cohen’s proofs against the record, but interpreted unresolved facts and reasonable inferences in Cohen’s favor. Where statutory interpretation, rather than factual dispute, controlled the outcome (for example, whether a statute permits an employee to obtain a particular penalty), summary judgment hinges on legal analysis rather than contested facts.

## Standing must be shown for each claim and each form of relief

A separate constitutional threshold sits above summary judgment: the plaintiff must have Article III standing for each claim and for each form of relief sought. The Eighth Circuit quoted the Supreme Court’s clear instruction that standing must be demonstrated “for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought” (Town of Chester v. Laroe Estates, Inc.). That rule means that a plaintiff cannot rely on a past injury alone to press a request for equitable or injunctive relief or to seek remedies that do not redress that injury. When the availability or character of relief depends on continued injury, the plaintiff must show a continuing or imminent injury that the requested relief will remedy.

Applied here, Cohen had stipulated that Consilio already paid him the overtime wages and liquidated damages he sought under the MFLSA. Because statutory penalties under the MFLSA would not be paid to Cohen but to the state Commissioner, the appellate court recognized that Cohen had to show some continuing or threatened injury that statutory penalties would redress in order to have standing to pursue that form of relief.

## Continuing case‑or‑controversy requirement; mootness parallels standing

Standing is not only required at the outset; it must persist throughout the litigation. The opinion cited the settled principle that the “requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)” (Sisney v. Kaemingk, quoting Arizonans for Official English). This continuity requirement transforms the standing inquiry into a recurring jurisdictional question: even if a plaintiff had standing when filing suit, subsequent events can render the claim moot if those events eliminate any real, live controversy.

In Cohen, this meant the Eighth Circuit had to consider whether subsequent developments—principally Consilio’s payments and the parties’ stipulation that Cohen had been paid all MFLSA wages and liquidated damages—had eradicated the injury that gave rise to the penalties claim. If the employer’s conduct could not reasonably be expected to recur, the claim for penalties might be moot despite earlier standing.

## Civil penalties, deterrence, and redress (Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw)

The court invoked Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw to explain why civil penalties can sometimes supply Article III redress for private plaintiffs even though the penalties themselves are payable to the government. Laidlaw recognized that penalties and enforcement mechanisms that deter ongoing violations and encourage defendants to discontinue wrongful conduct can confer a cognizable interest on plaintiffs who seek to end or forestall continuing harms. Thus, civil penalties may further the plaintiff’s interest in having the defendant stop unlawful behavior and in preventing future injury to the plaintiff or others in the plaintiff’s position.

In Cohen the Eighth Circuit applied this reasoning cautiously. Although MFLSA penalties would go to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen, the court acknowledged that such penalties could still redress Cohen’s interests by incentivizing Consilio to stop a policy that caused the alleged harm. That doctrinal insight explains why a claim for penalties is not automatically moot when back pay has been paid.

## Voluntary cessation and the “absolutely clear” no‑recurrence standard

The flip side of Laidlaw is the doctrine that a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not automatically render a case moot. A defendant cannot defeat jurisdiction merely by stopping the offending conduct temporarily. The crucial test is whether it is “absolutely clear” that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur (a standard the Eighth Circuit tied to Laidlaw and earlier precedent such as Concentrated Phosphate Export Ass’n).

Applied to this case, that means the district court and, on remand, the Eighth Circuit must determine whether Consilio’s elimination of overtime premium pay (and its payments to Cohen) left any reasonable likelihood that the policy or similar conduct will be reinstated. If it is “absolutely clear” that the policy will not recur, the penalties claim is moot; if not, the claim may still present a live controversy. The voluntary‑cessation rule thus requires a searching factual and legal inquiry into the employer’s current practices and motives, and it is why the panel vacated the summary judgment disposition on the MFLSA penalties question and remanded for the district court to address jurisdictional facts.

## How these principles structured the court’s disposition

Putting the elements together explains the court’s mixed outcome. The Eighth Circuit applied the summary‑judgment standard to the statutory issues that were purely legal (for example, whether the MPWA authorizes employee‑initiated average daily wage penalties) and affirmed dismissal where statutory text did not support Cohen’s position. By contrast, where the jurisdictional posture depended on whether the employer’s conduct remained an ongoing harm or risk (the MFLSA penalties), the court treated standing and mootness as threshold questions that could not be assumed away by stipulation about back pay. Because the record left open whether Consilio’s overtime policy remained in force or was likely to recur, and because civil penalties can sometimes serve a deterrent and redressive function under Laidlaw, the court vacated the MFLSA portion of the summary‑judgment order and remanded for the district court to resolve whether a live controversy remains.

In short: on appeal, legal questions about statutory entitlement were resolved under de novo review and settled textual canons, while the justiciability of a penalties claim required a focused standing/mootness analysis—one attentive to whether penalties would meaningfully redress a continuing threat of harm and whether it was absolutely clear that the employer would not revert to the challenged practice.
