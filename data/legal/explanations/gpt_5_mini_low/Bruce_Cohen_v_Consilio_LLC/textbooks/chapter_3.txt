Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 3: # MPWA Claim: Who May Seek Average Daily Wage Penalties

This section explains the Eighth Circuit’s analysis and holding on whether an employee may recover “average daily wage penalties” under Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), specifically through the interplay of Minn. Stat. §§ 181.171 and 181.101. The court concludes that the MPWA’s plain text vests authority to assess and collect the statutory penalty in the state Commissioner, not in private employees pursuing civil actions; therefore Cohen cannot obtain average daily wage penalties under § 181.101 and the district court’s grant of summary judgment on that point was affirmed.

## Statutory framework: § 181.171 and the scope of private civil actions

Section 181.171 is the MPWA provision that authorizes private civil enforcement: it permits “a person [to] bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specified MPWA sections, and provides that when an employer “is found to have violated” those provisions the employer is “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” The key question in this case is whether that authorization permits an employee, suing under § 181.171, to recover the particular “average daily wage” penalty described in § 181.101.

The court frames § 181.171 as a general grant of a civil remedy but reads that grant against the specific language of the MPWA provision that defines the penalty and the procedure for obtaining it. Understanding how broad grants of civil enforcement interact with detailed statutory penalty schemes is central to the court’s reasoning.

## Text of § 181.101(a): penalties and the Commissioner’s exclusive role

Section 181.101(a) is the provision that actually sets out the average daily wage penalty process. The statute provides that the Commissioner (of Labor and Industry) may, upon being served a demand for unpaid wages by an employee and after wages remain unpaid for ten days, “charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” It also expressly directs that money collected by the Commissioner “must be paid to the employee concerned.”

Two textual points are decisive for the court. First, the statute conditions availability of the penalty on the Commissioner’s action—service of a demand by the Commissioner and collection by the Commissioner. Second, the statute expressly contemplates distribution of collected sums to the employee, which supports the inference that the statutory mechanism is administrative and governmental, not a private enforcement right. Because the statute places the mechanism and the authority to impose and collect the penalty in the Commissioner, the court concluded the penalty recovery is not something an aggrieved employee may independently pursue through a § 181.171 civil action.

## Employee right preserved: prosecuting a claim for wages but not penalties

Although § 181.101 emphasizes the Commissioner’s role in assessing penalties, the statute explicitly preserves an employee’s ability to “prosecute a claim for wages.” The court reads this language as confirming a distinction: employees retain a private cause of action to recover unpaid wages via lawsuits, but that private cause of action does not extend to exercising the Commissioner’s statutory power to assess the average daily wage penalty. In short, private suits can vindicate wage claims; they do not duplicate the Commissioner’s penalty-collection procedure.

This interpretive point keeps in view the different remedies the statute separately contemplates: wages (recoverable by the employee directly) versus penalties (collected under the Commissioner’s administrative power and then paid to the employee).

## Plain-language canon and Minnesota precedent: give effect to the statute’s text

Because the court found the language of § 181.101 plain and unambiguous, it applied the ordinary rule of statutory interpretation in Minnesota: give effect to the statute’s manifest language and legislative intent as expressed through that language. The opinion cites Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co. for the proposition that unambiguous statutory text controls. It also cites Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch. to reinforce that money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee, which supports reading § 181.101 as allocating the penalty mechanism to the Commissioner rather than to private litigants.

The court therefore relies primarily on textual analysis rather than on extrinsic aids; because the statute clearly describes a Commissioner-driven process, that plain text resolves the question.

## Comparative statutes and legislative choice: § 181.13, § 181.14, and the “knows how” inference

To bolster its conclusion that the legislature did not intend to authorize employees to seek the § 181.101 penalty directly, the court points out other provisions in the MPWA where the legislature expressly grants employees the ability to recover penalties. For example, §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14, subdivision 2, allow discharged or quitting employees, upon demand, to recover unpaid wages “and . . . may charge and collect a penalty.” The court treats these provisions as evidence that the legislature knows how to create a private right to recover penalties when it intends to do so.

Citing Lagasse v. Horton and In re Hubbard, the court applies the familiar interpretive inference that when the legislature includes a remedy in one statutory provision but omits it in another, the omission is meaningful. That comparative statutory analysis supports the conclusion that the legislature did not, in § 181.101, intend to authorize private recovery of average daily wage penalties.

## Persuasive authority: Hull v. ConvergeOne

The opinion references district-court authority (Hull v. ConvergeOne) that reached the same conclusion: under § 181.101(a), the enforcement power for the penalty is limited to the Commissioner, with any collected funds going to the employee through the Commissioner. The Eighth Circuit treats Hull as persuasive support for its textual reading, showing that lower courts have interpreted § 181.101 consistently with the Commissioner-focused mechanism.

## Holding and consequence for the MPWA claim

Bringing together the plain statutory text, the preserved private right to recover wages (but not penalties), the legislature’s recognition elsewhere of private penalty rights, and persuasive district-court authority, the Eighth Circuit holds that only the Commissioner may seek the average daily wage penalties under § 181.101. Because Cohen sought those penalties in his private civil action, the district court’s dismissal of that aspect of his MPWA claim was correct, and the court affirms summary judgment in favor of Consilio on the MPWA penalties issue.

This holding is narrow and statutory: it does not bar the Commissioner from bringing a claim for the penalty, nor does it affect an employee’s separate private claims for unpaid wages. It resolves only whether an individual employee can, via § 181.171, recover the specific average daily wage penalty created and administered under § 181.101—and answers that question in the negative.
