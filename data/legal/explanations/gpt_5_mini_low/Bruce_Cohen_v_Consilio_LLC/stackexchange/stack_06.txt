Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### Can a district court reach the merits on summary judgment without first resolving a serious question about mootness and Article III jurisdiction?
Question:
Here, the Eighth Circuit vacates the summary-judgment ruling on the MFLSA penalties issue and remands for the district court to determine its jurisdiction first. Is it error for a district court to grant summary judgment on the merits when mootness remains unresolved, or is vacatur the standard corrective measure? Are there narrow circumstances where a court can assume jurisdiction to reach the merits (e.g., hypothetical jurisdiction) in the Eighth Circuit, or is that categorically prohibited?
Answer:
Short answer: In the Eighth Circuit, a district court should not resolve the merits of a claim if there is a serious, live question about Article III jurisdiction (standing/mootness) that could defeat the claim. If the record leaves the court unsure whether a plaintiff’s interest continues, the proper appellate remedy is vacatur and remand for the district court to decide jurisdiction first — exactly what the Eighth Circuit did in Cohen v. Consilio.

Why: legal doctrine and what the opinion says
- Article III requires a continuing personal stake for each claim and each form of relief. Standing must exist at filing and mootness must not later extinguish the plaintiff’s interest. (Opinion cites Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Sisney v. Kaemingk, and Friends of the Earth.)
- The Court explained that civil penalties can sometimes confer prospective relief value (deterrence), but whether that value persists depends on whether the defendant’s wrongful conduct can reasonably be expected to recur. If it cannot, the claim may be moot. (Citing Friends of the Earth, 528 U.S. at 186, 189.)
- Because Cohen conceded he received all overtime wages and liquidated damages, but there remained a disputed factual question whether Consilio’s overtime policy remained in effect (i.e., whether wrongful conduct might recur), the Eighth Circuit concluded there was a “serious concern” about jurisdiction and therefore vacated the district court’s summary judgment on the MFLSA penalty claim and remanded for the district court to determine mootness/jurisdiction first. (Opinion Parts II.B and III.)

Practical rule from Cohen
- If jurisdictional facts are genuinely in dispute or could be outcome-determinative (here, whether the policy remains), a district court should not proceed to decide the merits on summary judgment without first resolving jurisdiction. Otherwise the judgment on the merits may be vacated on appeal and the case sent back for the threshold jurisdictional inquiry — again, what this Court did in Cohen.
- The appellate court’s use of vacatur and remand is the appropriate corrective measure where the district court reached the merits while the jurisdictional question remained unresolved. That is the remedy applied in Cohen.

Are there narrow exceptions (hypothetical jurisdiction)?
- The opinion does not authorize a categorical rule allowing courts to assume “hypothetical” jurisdiction and decide the merits when a real mootness question remains. Cohen emphasizes the need to decide whether the controversy continues before final adjudication of the merits and remands for that determination.
- In practice, courts sometimes address the merits where jurisdictional defects are factually impossible or when the parties have stipulated away jurisdictional objections, but Cohen shows the Eighth Circuit will not permit merits adjudication where a genuine dispute about recurrence (and thus mootness) exists. The concurrence likewise notes the district court “may decide the mootness question first” on remand, reinforcing the threshold nature of the inquiry.

Bottom line
Cohen stands for the straightforward proposition that when a live question remains about Article III jurisdiction (here, whether the defendant’s wrongful policy still exists and thus whether penalty claims remain justiciable), a district court should not resolve the merits without first resolving that jurisdictional question. If it does, the typical corrective measure is vacatur and remand so the district court can decide jurisdiction.
