Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### What is the pathway for attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest when substantive wage claims are paid but penalty and mootness issues remain?
Question:
Judge Kobes notes vacating the district court’s ruling as to attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. Under MFLSA/MPWA/MWTA fee-shifting provisions, can a plaintiff be a "prevailing party" if the defendant pays wages and liquidated damages mid-litigation without judgment? If penalties are jurisdictionally barred or denied, does that affect fee eligibility for the earlier, now-satisfied wage components? How do Minnesota statutes and federal case law treat fees where relief is obtained via voluntary payment rather than a court order?
Answer:
Short answer
The Eighth Circuit in Cohen v. Consilio vacated the district court’s award of attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest and sent the case back for the district court to decide whether it still has jurisdiction over Cohen’s remaining MFLSA penalty claim. The practical pathway is therefore two-step:

1) the district court must first resolve standing/mootness (i.e., whether any live claim remains after Consilio’s voluntary payments and any change (or not) to its policy); and
2) only after resolving jurisdiction/mootness can the court decide whether Cohen is a “prevailing party” under the fee-shifting provisions and award fees, costs, or prejudgment interest.

Why the Court did this (legal doctrine and reasoning in the opinion)
- Standing and mootness control whether a federal court may decide a claim at all. The panel relied on Friends of the Earth and related Supreme Court precedent (and Town of Chester) to explain that a plaintiff must have standing at the outset and must maintain a continuing personal interest throughout the litigation; if a defendant’s voluntary payment or change of conduct makes it “absolutely clear” the wrongful conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur, the claim may be moot. Cohen, slip op. § II.B (discussing Friends of the Earth, Town of Chester, Sisney).
- Because Cohen stipulated that he had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages, the only remaining contested relief was statutory penalties (which the parties agree would be payable to the Commissioner, not Cohen). The panel expressed “serious concern” about jurisdiction over that penalty claim depending on whether Consilio’s policy remains in effect and whether the claim is genuinely threatened or already moot. Cohen, § II.B.
- Given that potential mootness, the court vacated the district court’s award of fees, costs, and prejudgment interest and remanded so the district court can determine jurisdiction first and then address fee entitlement. Cohen, § III; Kobes, J., concurring specially (vacating fees while remanding mootness).

How that maps onto fee eligibility under MFLSA/MPWA/MWTA as reflected in the opinion
- The opinion does not adopt a new rule about whether a plaintiff becomes a “prevailing party” when a defendant pays wages (or liquidated damages) before judgment; instead, it focuses on jurisdiction. Because the Eighth Circuit could not be sure whether any live claim remained, it declined to let the prior fee order stand and sent the matter back for the district court to decide that threshold issue. Cohen, § II.B; § III.
- If, on remand, the district court concludes Cohen lacks a continuing interest (i.e., the penalty claim is moot and no other relief remains), then there would be no live controversy and the basis for treating Cohen as a prevailing party under the statutes (and thus awarding fees) would be undermined; that is the practical consequence the panel sought to avoid by vacating the fee award pending the jurisdictional ruling. See Cohen, § II.B; Kobes concurrence.
- Conversely, if the district court finds that a live claim remains (for example, because the challenged policy remains in effect so a penalty claim is not moot), then Cohen may proceed on the merits of that claim and the district court can then determine whether he is a “prevailing party” for purposes of attorney’s fees, costs, or prejudgment interest under the relevant statutes. The opinion leaves that determination to the district court after resolving standing/mootness. Cohen, § II.B; § III.

Specific points the opinion makes about statutory penalties and who receives them
- For the MPWA average-daily-wage penalty, the court held the statute reserves enforcement to the Commissioner (not a private plaintiff), and therefore Cohen could not recover those MPWA penalties as a private right. Cohen, § II.A.
- For MFLSA and MWTA penalties, the panel recognized those penalties are payable to the Commissioner (the parties agreed), which implicated the standing/mootness inquiry and contributed to the court’s decision to vacate the fee award and remand. Cohen, § II.B–C.

What the district court must do on remand (practical checklist)
- Decide whether Consilio’s policy still poses a real, ongoing injury such that Cohen’s claim for MFLSA penalties is not moot (apply Friends of the Earth’s “absolutely clear” standard and City of Mesquite anti-cessation principles as discussed in the opinion). Cohen, § II.B.
- If the court finds no live claim, dismiss the case as moot; in that event the court will then consider whether any fee award is appropriate (the Eighth Circuit has already vacated the prior fee order because the jurisdictional posture is unsettled). Cohen, § III, Kobes concurrence.
- If the court finds a live claim remains, adjudicate the merits of that claim and then decide whether Cohen is a prevailing party under the applicable statutory fee-shifting provision and, if so, award fees, costs, and any allowable prejudgment interest consistent with statute and precedent.

Bottom line
Cohen controls the process but does not announce a substantive rule that voluntary payment of wages automatically makes a plaintiff a prevailing party (or bars fees). Instead, it requires the district court first to resolve the jurisdictional (standing/mootness) question created by the voluntary payments and the status of the employer’s policy; only after that threshold is decided may the court properly determine whether the plaintiff prevailed and is entitled to fees, costs, or prejudgment interest. The Eighth Circuit vacated the fee order precisely because that jurisdictional question was unresolved. Cohen, §§ II.B–III; Kobes concurrence.
