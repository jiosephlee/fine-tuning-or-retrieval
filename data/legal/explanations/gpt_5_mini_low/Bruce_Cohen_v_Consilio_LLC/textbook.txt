Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Parties and roles: Bruce Cohen vs. Consilio, LLC

This case pits Bruce Cohen, an hourly licensed-attorney document reviewer, against his employer, Consilio, LLC, a nationwide legal-document-review company with offices in Minnesota. Cohen worked for Consilio beginning in 2018 performing document-review work that was paid on an hourly basis. The dispute arises from how Consilio compensated (or failed to compensate) Cohen and other similarly situated Minnesota-based hourly licensed-attorney reviewers for overtime work and from related statutory notice and penalty provisions in Minnesota law.

## The 2019 overtime-policy change email

A central factual predicate is a company-wide policy change communicated in July 2019. Consilio sent an email to Cohen and other Minnesota-based hourly licensed-attorney reviewers informing them that, effective August 2019, Consilio would institute a new overtime policy. Under the new policy the company eliminated the overtime premium and stated that those employees would be paid “at [their] base pay rate” for all hours worked. That email and the policy change are the conduct Cohen challenges as violating Minnesota wage laws.

## Claims filed in 2020 and the statutory framework invoked

Cohen filed suit in August 2020 on behalf of himself and a putative class. He alleged that Consilio’s elimination of the overtime premium violated three separate Minnesota wage statutes. First, he relied on the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), principally Minn. Stat. §§ 181.101 and 181.171. Second, he asserted claims under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), citing Minn. Stat. §§ 177.25 and 177.27. Third, he alleged violations of the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), invoking Minn. Stat. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), along with related provisions cited for remedies. The complaint therefore pressed both pay-substantive claims (unpaid overtime wages and related liquidated damages) and claims tied to statutory notice and penalty schemes.

## Relief sought: wages, damages, penalties, and injunctive relief

Cohen sought multiple forms of relief. He claimed unpaid overtime wages and the liquidated damages available under the MFLSA. He also sought statutory penalties available under the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA, plus injunctive relief to prevent or reverse the challenged overtime policy. Thus the complaint combined individual wage recovery with requests for penalties (some payable to the state or commissioner) and equitable relief aimed at stopping the employer’s practices going forward.

## Consilio’s payments and stipulations about wages and liquidated damages

Before summary judgment, Consilio made substantial payments tied to Cohen’s MFLSA overtime and liquidated-damages claims. The company paid a total of $256,010.01 to cover overtime wages and liquidated damages for the Minnesota licensed-attorney reviewers identified by Cohen’s claim. Of that sum, Cohen personally received $3,225.21 in overtime and $462.00 in liquidated damages. The parties stipulated to these payments and expressly agreed that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that he “does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” Those stipulations effectively eliminated any live dispute over unpaid overtime wages and MFLSA liquidated damages for Cohen personally.

## The remaining dispute: availability of statutory penalties

Although wages and liquidated damages were paid and stipulated resolved, the parties could not agree on the availability of statutory penalties. Cohen sought substantial penalties: $172,080 in average-daily-wage penalties under the MPWA and $42,000 each under the MFLSA and the MWTA. Consilio contended that the statutory schemes did not permit such penalties to be recovered by Cohen (or at all in the manner he sought). Thus, after the payments, the litigation narrowed to the legal question whether the statutes at issue authorized the penalties sought.

## Procedural agreement before the district court: summary judgment first

Given the narrowed dispute, the parties jointly proposed and the district court accepted an expedited path: the parties agreed the most efficient approach was for the district court to decide Consilio’s summary-judgment motion on the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims before engaging in further discovery or classwide proceedings. Both sides acknowledged that if Consilio prevailed on summary judgment in full, the case would be over. That stipulation set the procedural posture for the district-court ruling that followed.

## District court ruling and Cohen’s appeal

The district court granted summary judgment in favor of Consilio on all three statutory claims—the MPWA, the MFLSA, and the MWTA—and dismissed Cohen’s request for injunctive relief without addressing a later-asserted form of relief Cohen sought in summary-judgment briefing. Cohen appealed the adverse summary-judgment decision to the Eighth Circuit. On appeal the Eighth Circuit reviewed the district court’s grant of summary judgment de novo, recounting the facts and stipulations and resolving legal questions about whether the statutory penalty provisions at issue could be pursued by Cohen or were limited to the Commissioner or otherwise implicated by mootness and standing concerns. The panel affirmed in part, vacated and remanded in part, and left intact the district court’s rulings it found supported by the statutes and the record.

# Appellate Standards and Jurisdictional Frame

This chapter explains the appellate standards the Eighth Circuit applied in Cohen v. Consilio, LLC and the jurisdictional (standing and mootness) principles that shaped the court’s treatment of Cohen’s claim for statutory penalties under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA). The opinion centers on two doctrinal axes: (1) the familiar summary‑judgment framework the court uses on appeal; and (2) the constitutional requirement that a plaintiff maintain a live case or controversy throughout the litigation, which governs whether a claim for civil penalties remains justiciable after the defendant has taken corrective or mitigating action.

## Summary judgment standard and viewing the evidence

The court began by stating the standard of review that controls an appeal from a district court’s grant of summary judgment. Summary judgment is appropriate only when there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. On de novo review, the appellate court independently applies that standard. In doing so the court must view the evidence and all reasonable inferences drawn from it in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party—in this case, Cohen. Those principles are procedural fundamentals: they tell us both the burden a movant faces at summary judgment and how an appellate court assesses whether the district court correctly concluded that no triable issue remained.

In Cohen the Eighth Circuit relied on its prior decisions (for example, Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc.) to restate these rules before assessing whether the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims survived summary judgment. Practically, that meant the court measured Consilio’s statutory defenses and Cohen’s proofs against the record, but interpreted unresolved facts and reasonable inferences in Cohen’s favor. Where statutory interpretation, rather than factual dispute, controlled the outcome (for example, whether a statute permits an employee to obtain a particular penalty), summary judgment hinges on legal analysis rather than contested facts.

## Standing must be shown for each claim and each form of relief

A separate constitutional threshold sits above summary judgment: the plaintiff must have Article III standing for each claim and for each form of relief sought. The Eighth Circuit quoted the Supreme Court’s clear instruction that standing must be demonstrated “for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought” (Town of Chester v. Laroe Estates, Inc.). That rule means that a plaintiff cannot rely on a past injury alone to press a request for equitable or injunctive relief or to seek remedies that do not redress that injury. When the availability or character of relief depends on continued injury, the plaintiff must show a continuing or imminent injury that the requested relief will remedy.

Applied here, Cohen had stipulated that Consilio already paid him the overtime wages and liquidated damages he sought under the MFLSA. Because statutory penalties under the MFLSA would not be paid to Cohen but to the state Commissioner, the appellate court recognized that Cohen had to show some continuing or threatened injury that statutory penalties would redress in order to have standing to pursue that form of relief.

## Continuing case‑or‑controversy requirement; mootness parallels standing

Standing is not only required at the outset; it must persist throughout the litigation. The opinion cited the settled principle that the “requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)” (Sisney v. Kaemingk, quoting Arizonans for Official English). This continuity requirement transforms the standing inquiry into a recurring jurisdictional question: even if a plaintiff had standing when filing suit, subsequent events can render the claim moot if those events eliminate any real, live controversy.

In Cohen, this meant the Eighth Circuit had to consider whether subsequent developments—principally Consilio’s payments and the parties’ stipulation that Cohen had been paid all MFLSA wages and liquidated damages—had eradicated the injury that gave rise to the penalties claim. If the employer’s conduct could not reasonably be expected to recur, the claim for penalties might be moot despite earlier standing.

## Civil penalties, deterrence, and redress (Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw)

The court invoked Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw to explain why civil penalties can sometimes supply Article III redress for private plaintiffs even though the penalties themselves are payable to the government. Laidlaw recognized that penalties and enforcement mechanisms that deter ongoing violations and encourage defendants to discontinue wrongful conduct can confer a cognizable interest on plaintiffs who seek to end or forestall continuing harms. Thus, civil penalties may further the plaintiff’s interest in having the defendant stop unlawful behavior and in preventing future injury to the plaintiff or others in the plaintiff’s position.

In Cohen the Eighth Circuit applied this reasoning cautiously. Although MFLSA penalties would go to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen, the court acknowledged that such penalties could still redress Cohen’s interests by incentivizing Consilio to stop a policy that caused the alleged harm. That doctrinal insight explains why a claim for penalties is not automatically moot when back pay has been paid.

## Voluntary cessation and the “absolutely clear” no‑recurrence standard

The flip side of Laidlaw is the doctrine that a defendant’s voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not automatically render a case moot. A defendant cannot defeat jurisdiction merely by stopping the offending conduct temporarily. The crucial test is whether it is “absolutely clear” that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur (a standard the Eighth Circuit tied to Laidlaw and earlier precedent such as Concentrated Phosphate Export Ass’n).

Applied to this case, that means the district court and, on remand, the Eighth Circuit must determine whether Consilio’s elimination of overtime premium pay (and its payments to Cohen) left any reasonable likelihood that the policy or similar conduct will be reinstated. If it is “absolutely clear” that the policy will not recur, the penalties claim is moot; if not, the claim may still present a live controversy. The voluntary‑cessation rule thus requires a searching factual and legal inquiry into the employer’s current practices and motives, and it is why the panel vacated the summary judgment disposition on the MFLSA penalties question and remanded for the district court to address jurisdictional facts.

## How these principles structured the court’s disposition

Putting the elements together explains the court’s mixed outcome. The Eighth Circuit applied the summary‑judgment standard to the statutory issues that were purely legal (for example, whether the MPWA authorizes employee‑initiated average daily wage penalties) and affirmed dismissal where statutory text did not support Cohen’s position. By contrast, where the jurisdictional posture depended on whether the employer’s conduct remained an ongoing harm or risk (the MFLSA penalties), the court treated standing and mootness as threshold questions that could not be assumed away by stipulation about back pay. Because the record left open whether Consilio’s overtime policy remained in force or was likely to recur, and because civil penalties can sometimes serve a deterrent and redressive function under Laidlaw, the court vacated the MFLSA portion of the summary‑judgment order and remanded for the district court to resolve whether a live controversy remains.

In short: on appeal, legal questions about statutory entitlement were resolved under de novo review and settled textual canons, while the justiciability of a penalties claim required a focused standing/mootness analysis—one attentive to whether penalties would meaningfully redress a continuing threat of harm and whether it was absolutely clear that the employer would not revert to the challenged practice.

# MPWA Claim: Who May Seek Average Daily Wage Penalties

This section explains the Eighth Circuit’s analysis and holding on whether an employee may recover “average daily wage penalties” under Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), specifically through the interplay of Minn. Stat. §§ 181.171 and 181.101. The court concludes that the MPWA’s plain text vests authority to assess and collect the statutory penalty in the state Commissioner, not in private employees pursuing civil actions; therefore Cohen cannot obtain average daily wage penalties under § 181.101 and the district court’s grant of summary judgment on that point was affirmed.

## Statutory framework: § 181.171 and the scope of private civil actions

Section 181.171 is the MPWA provision that authorizes private civil enforcement: it permits “a person [to] bring a civil action seeking redress for violations” of specified MPWA sections, and provides that when an employer “is found to have violated” those provisions the employer is “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” The key question in this case is whether that authorization permits an employee, suing under § 181.171, to recover the particular “average daily wage” penalty described in § 181.101.

The court frames § 181.171 as a general grant of a civil remedy but reads that grant against the specific language of the MPWA provision that defines the penalty and the procedure for obtaining it. Understanding how broad grants of civil enforcement interact with detailed statutory penalty schemes is central to the court’s reasoning.

## Text of § 181.101(a): penalties and the Commissioner’s exclusive role

Section 181.101(a) is the provision that actually sets out the average daily wage penalty process. The statute provides that the Commissioner (of Labor and Industry) may, upon being served a demand for unpaid wages by an employee and after wages remain unpaid for ten days, “charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty.” It also expressly directs that money collected by the Commissioner “must be paid to the employee concerned.”

Two textual points are decisive for the court. First, the statute conditions availability of the penalty on the Commissioner’s action—service of a demand by the Commissioner and collection by the Commissioner. Second, the statute expressly contemplates distribution of collected sums to the employee, which supports the inference that the statutory mechanism is administrative and governmental, not a private enforcement right. Because the statute places the mechanism and the authority to impose and collect the penalty in the Commissioner, the court concluded the penalty recovery is not something an aggrieved employee may independently pursue through a § 181.171 civil action.

## Employee right preserved: prosecuting a claim for wages but not penalties

Although § 181.101 emphasizes the Commissioner’s role in assessing penalties, the statute explicitly preserves an employee’s ability to “prosecute a claim for wages.” The court reads this language as confirming a distinction: employees retain a private cause of action to recover unpaid wages via lawsuits, but that private cause of action does not extend to exercising the Commissioner’s statutory power to assess the average daily wage penalty. In short, private suits can vindicate wage claims; they do not duplicate the Commissioner’s penalty-collection procedure.

This interpretive point keeps in view the different remedies the statute separately contemplates: wages (recoverable by the employee directly) versus penalties (collected under the Commissioner’s administrative power and then paid to the employee).

## Plain-language canon and Minnesota precedent: give effect to the statute’s text

Because the court found the language of § 181.101 plain and unambiguous, it applied the ordinary rule of statutory interpretation in Minnesota: give effect to the statute’s manifest language and legislative intent as expressed through that language. The opinion cites Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co. for the proposition that unambiguous statutory text controls. It also cites Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch. to reinforce that money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee, which supports reading § 181.101 as allocating the penalty mechanism to the Commissioner rather than to private litigants.

The court therefore relies primarily on textual analysis rather than on extrinsic aids; because the statute clearly describes a Commissioner-driven process, that plain text resolves the question.

## Comparative statutes and legislative choice: § 181.13, § 181.14, and the “knows how” inference

To bolster its conclusion that the legislature did not intend to authorize employees to seek the § 181.101 penalty directly, the court points out other provisions in the MPWA where the legislature expressly grants employees the ability to recover penalties. For example, §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14, subdivision 2, allow discharged or quitting employees, upon demand, to recover unpaid wages “and . . . may charge and collect a penalty.” The court treats these provisions as evidence that the legislature knows how to create a private right to recover penalties when it intends to do so.

Citing Lagasse v. Horton and In re Hubbard, the court applies the familiar interpretive inference that when the legislature includes a remedy in one statutory provision but omits it in another, the omission is meaningful. That comparative statutory analysis supports the conclusion that the legislature did not, in § 181.101, intend to authorize private recovery of average daily wage penalties.

## Persuasive authority: Hull v. ConvergeOne

The opinion references district-court authority (Hull v. ConvergeOne) that reached the same conclusion: under § 181.101(a), the enforcement power for the penalty is limited to the Commissioner, with any collected funds going to the employee through the Commissioner. The Eighth Circuit treats Hull as persuasive support for its textual reading, showing that lower courts have interpreted § 181.101 consistently with the Commissioner-focused mechanism.

## Holding and consequence for the MPWA claim

Bringing together the plain statutory text, the preserved private right to recover wages (but not penalties), the legislature’s recognition elsewhere of private penalty rights, and persuasive district-court authority, the Eighth Circuit holds that only the Commissioner may seek the average daily wage penalties under § 181.101. Because Cohen sought those penalties in his private civil action, the district court’s dismissal of that aspect of his MPWA claim was correct, and the court affirms summary judgment in favor of Consilio on the MPWA penalties issue.

This holding is narrow and statutory: it does not bar the Commissioner from bringing a claim for the penalty, nor does it affect an employee’s separate private claims for unpaid wages. It resolves only whether an individual employee can, via § 181.171, recover the specific average daily wage penalty created and administered under § 181.101—and answers that question in the negative.

# MFLSA Penalties: Stipulations, Redressability, and Mootness Remand

This chapter explains the Eighth Circuit’s treatment of appellant Bruce Cohen’s claim for statutory penalties under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) after he stipulated that he had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages. The court affirmed summary judgment in part, but it vacated and remanded the MFLSA penalty ruling because Article III jurisdiction (standing and mootness) remained uncertain. The analysis centers on the procedural posture created by the stipulation, the distinctive nature of civil penalties that are payable to the state, and the legal standard governing whether a request for penalties remains a live controversy.

## Stipulation: wages and liquidated damages paid; penalties alone remain

Before the district court’s summary judgment ruling, the parties jointly stipulated that Consilio had paid Cohen all overtime wages and liquidated damages he alleged were owed under the MFLSA. That stipulation narrowed the live dispute: Cohen no longer sought the back-pay or liquidated damages he had initially demanded; what was left on appeal was only his request for statutory penalties under the MFLSA. The stipulation therefore changed the injury Cohen could plausibly assert in federal court and is central to the Article III inquiry the panel undertook.

## Who would receive any penalties: the Commissioner, not Cohen

Both parties agreed (and the panel accepted) that any MFLSA statutory penalties at issue would not be payable to Cohen personally, but instead would be paid to the state Commissioner. That allocation matters for redressability. If the only relief sought would flow to the government rather than to the plaintiff, a court must carefully assess whether awarding that relief would nevertheless provide the plaintiff with a cognizable, concrete benefit sufficient to confer standing. The panel recognized this point as a threshold concern even though penalties can sometimes vindicate private interests indirectly.

## Redressability of civil penalties: Laidlaw and the deterring function

The court relied on the Supreme Court’s reasoning in Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw to explain how civil penalties—even when paid to government authorities—can still afford redress to private plaintiffs. Laidlaw held that civil penalties may vindicate private plaintiffs’ interests because imposing penalties can encourage a defendant to stop ongoing unlawful conduct and deter future violations, thereby alleviating the plaintiff’s injury or threat of injury. The Eighth Circuit therefore acknowledged that civil penalties are not categorically outside Article III redressability simply because the money is paid to the government; they may still remedy a plaintiff’s interest in preventing recurrence of unlawful behavior.

## The critical factual uncertainty: is the unlawful policy still in effect?

Even recognizing that penalties can provide redress, the court emphasized a key factual issue that affects whether redress is possible: whether Consilio’s allegedly violative overtime policy remains operative. If the policy remains in place, an award of penalties could plausibly deter continued violations and thus redress Cohen’s interest in avoiding future injury. But if Consilio has abandoned the policy and it is “absolutely clear” that the wrongful conduct will not recur, then no form of relief—penalties or otherwise—would redress a present or ongoing injury, and the claim could be moot.

## Mootness analysis and the voluntary cessation doctrine

The panel framed the problem within the well-established mootness principles. A plaintiff must have standing at the outset and maintain that personal interest throughout the litigation; if subsequent events make it “absolutely clear” that the wrongful behavior cannot reasonably be expected to recur, the claim becomes moot. The court also noted the voluntary cessation doctrine: a defendant’s voluntary discontinuation of a challenged practice does not necessarily render a case moot if the defendant could resume the practice. Under Laidlaw and related precedents, the plaintiff may still have a live claim unless the defendant shows that recurrence is impossible and the cessation is genuine and permanent. The Eighth Circuit applied these principles to the MFLSA penalty claim and found the state of the record inadequate to determine whether the policy remained in effect and therefore whether the claim survived mootness scrutiny.

## Procedural consequence: vacatur and remand to decide jurisdiction first

Because jurisdictional viability depended on unresolved factual questions (principally whether the policy remained in force and thus whether penalties could redress Cohen’s interests), the court vacated the district court’s grant of summary judgment on the MFLSA claim and remanded for further proceedings. The panel instructed the district court to determine its jurisdiction—standing and/or mootness—before reaching the merits of the penalties claim. The special concurrence echoed this course, noting that the parties had not briefed standing in full and that the district court could address mootness first on remand.

## Takeaway for litigators and students

When a plaintiff stipulates that core monetary relief has been paid, remaining requests for civil penalties (especially those payable to a government entity) trigger a focused Article III inquiry. Courts will ask whether the penalties can still provide redress by deterring ongoing or future misconduct. If the defendant’s allegedly unlawful policy may still be in effect, the claim may survive; if the defendant has credibly and permanently ceased the conduct, the claim may be moot. Where facts about the defendant’s current practices are unsettled, an appellate court may vacate a merits ruling and remand so the trial court can resolve jurisdictional questions before addressing the substance of the penalty claim.

# MWTA Claim and Injunctive Relief: Preservation and Statutory Limits

This chapter explains the Eighth Circuit’s treatment of Cohen’s Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) claim and his request for injunctive relief. The court affirmed the district court’s dismissal of the MWTA claim and declined to consider Cohen’s newly framed request for an affirmative injunction rescinding Consilio’s 2019 overtime policy. Understanding these rulings requires attention to (1) the statutory theory Cohen advanced under the MWTA, (2) the factual stipulation that he had received all money he claimed to be owed, (3) the absence of legal authority permitting the civil penalties he sought under the MWTA in the posture of this case, and (4) basic preservation principles governing appellate review of undeveloped arguments and new claims.

MWTA theory: failure to provide required notice of overtime policy change (§ 181.032(f), (d)(4))
Cohen’s MWTA claim rested on the allegation that Consilio failed to give statutorily required notice of its change in overtime policy—an asserted violation of Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f) and subsection (d)(4). The MWTA provision allegedly implicated requires employers to provide certain notices when employment terms change. Cohen’s legal theory was that lack of that notice triggered statutory penalties against Consilio.

Stipulation: all owed pay received; absence of cited authority for civil penalties under MWTA in this posture
A key factual and procedural backdrop is the parties’ stipulation that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that he does not dispute the liquidated damages he received under the MFLSA. Because Cohen had been paid for the monetary relief he initially sought, the remaining MWTA claim turned on whether the statute independently authorized civil penalties or other relief in these circumstances. On appeal, Cohen did not point to statutory authority that would support civil penalties available to him under the MWTA given the stipulated payments. The panel therefore had no meaningful legal basis before it to reverse the dismissal on the MWTA penalty claim.

Waiver on appeal for undeveloped arguments (Milligan)
The court disposed of Cohen’s MWTA argument in part on preservation grounds. Citing Milligan v. City of Red Oak, the Eighth Circuit reiterated that an appellant who presses an issue without developing argument or citation of authority on appeal effectively waives the point. Cohen’s brief offered little substantive legal argument or statutory interpretation to show that the MWTA conferred the penalties he sought in this posture. Because he failed to support the appellate contention with meaningful argument or legal authority, the court declined to disturb the district court’s dismissal.

Injunctive relief: complaint sought specific relief; later request to rescind 2019 policy raised only in summary judgment briefing
Cohen also sought injunctive relief, but the form of relief he asked for shifted during the case. His complaint requested particular injunctive remedies, and only later—during briefing on summary judgment—did he pivot and expressly ask the district court to order Consilio to rescind its 2019 overtime policy. The district court understandably did not address this new formulation because it was not pleaded as a claim for relief.

Procedural rule: new claims or materially different injunctive theories must be raised by amended complaint (InfoDeli; Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a))
The court relied on basic pleading and amendment rules in rejecting Cohen’s attempt to obtain a materially different injunction without an amended complaint. The opinion cites InfoDeli, LLC for the proposition that new claims should be raised through an amended complaint, not by a party’s brief in opposition to summary judgment. This is consistent with Rule 15(a) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, which governs amendment of pleadings and protects the defendant’s right to know and respond to the precise relief sought. Because Cohen sought a different form of injunctive relief only in his summary-judgment papers and did not amend his complaint, the issue was not properly before the district court or the appellate court.

Holding: district court’s dismissal of MWTA and refusal to consider new injunctive request affirmed
The net result is straightforward. Given the parties’ stipulation that Cohen had been paid all wages and liquidated damages he claimed, the absence of developed appellate argument or statutory authority authorizing the MWTA penalties he sought, and the failure to plead an amended request for rescission of the policy, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s dismissal of Cohen’s MWTA claim and refused to consider his newly asserted injunctive remedy. The opinion emphasizes preservation: undeveloped appellate arguments are waived, and new claims or materially different theories of relief must be presented through proper pleading, not briefed for the first time at summary judgment.

# Disposition, Concurring Views, and Practical Takeaways

## Judgment: what the court decided and why

The Eighth Circuit issued a split disposition. It affirmed the district court’s summary-judgment dismissal of Bruce Cohen’s claims under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. § 181.101, and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), but it vacated and remanded the district court’s summary-judgment ruling on Cohen’s Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) penalties claim for further jurisdictional analysis. The court declined to reach Cohen’s request for injunctive relief because that relief was not properly presented in the district court.

On the MPWA claim, the court held that the statutory text of § 181.101 limits the right to obtain “average daily wage” penalties to the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry (the Commissioner). The opinion rests on straightforward statutory-interpretation principles: the statute assigns the Commissioner the procedural role of serving a demand for unpaid wages and then charging and collecting penalties if the employer fails to comply. The statute also specifies that “money collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned,” emphasizing that the enforcement mechanism is administrative and commissioner-driven. Because the statute does not give private employees an independent right to recover the specified penalty, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the dismissal of Cohen’s MPWA penalty claim.

On the MWTA claim, Cohen alleged failure to provide statutorily required notice of a change in the overtime policy. The court observed that Cohen had stipulated he had been paid all wages owed and that he pointed to no statutory authority that authorized private recovery of civil penalties under the specific MWTA provisions at issue. Because Cohen offered no meaningful argument or authority to support reversal, the court deemed the issue waived and affirmed the district court’s dismissal.

By contrast, the MFLSA claim raised a jurisdictional (mootness/standing) question that could not be resolved on the record before the court. Cohen had stipulated that Consilio paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages owed to him. The parties agreed that statutory penalties under the MFLSA, if imposed, would be payable to the government (the Commissioner) rather than to Cohen. The Eighth Circuit explained that coercive civil penalties—even when payable to the government—can still provide redress to private plaintiffs and thus support standing in some circumstances because penalties may deter ongoing or recurring violations (citing Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw). But whether Cohen had a continuing personal stake depended on whether Consilio’s overtime policy remained in effect. If the policy persisted, Cohen’s penalty claim might not be moot; if the policy was abandoned and there was no reasonable expectation of recurrence, the claim could be moot. Because the record did not resolve whether Consilio’s policy continued, the court vacated the MFLSA summary judgment and remanded for the district court to determine whether it retained jurisdiction.

Finally, Cohen’s renewed request for an affirmative injunction rescinding Consilio’s policy was not considered because he did not seek that specific relief in his operative pleadings and raised it for the first time in summary-judgment briefing. The court treated that as an improper new claim and declined to address it.

## Special concurrence (Judge Kobes): narrower view on standing and remand instructions

Judge Kobes concurred specially, agreeing with the result to vacate and remand the MFLSA ruling but expressing hesitation about whether Cohen has Article III standing to seek MFLSA statutory penalties under Laidlaw. The concurrence notes that Laidlaw involved environmental penalties and that differences may exist between those penalties and MFLSA penalties; neither party briefed the standing issue in depth. Judge Kobes therefore suggested prudence: on remand, the district court may appropriately address the mootness question first and then, if necessary, decide standing or other threshold issues. The concurrence also votes to vacate the district court’s rulings concerning attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest so that the district court can address those matters in light of the remand.

Key takeaways from the concurrence:
- The Laidlaw framework may not map perfectly onto all statutory penalty schemes; courts should evaluate standing and redressability carefully when penalties flow to the government.
- It is procedurally sensible for the district court to resolve mootness before reaching merits-based or damages-adjacent questions on remand.

## Practice pointers for practitioners in Minnesota wage-and-hour cases

1. Plead and pursue injunctive relief clearly and early.
   - If you seek prospective relief (e.g., an order requiring the employer to rescind an unlawful policy), plead that relief in the complaint and preserve it through motion practice. Raising new forms of injunctive relief only in opposition briefs risks forfeiture, as the court refused to consider Cohen’s revised injunction request because it was not pleaded.

2. Know which actors can seek statutory penalties under the MPWA.
   - Section 181.101’s enforcement mechanism is structured around the Commissioner’s authority to serve a demand and collect penalties. When a statute expressly vests penalty-enforcement power in an administrative official and specifies that collected funds are paid to the employee, courts may interpret that scheme as precluding private suits for the same penalty. Before asserting MPWA penalty claims in litigation, confirm whether the statute grants private litigants an express right to that penalty; if it does not, expect courts to treat the penalty as a commissioner-only remedy.

3. Preserve and brief statutory bases for MWTA penalties.
   - If you rely on the MWTA for penalties or notice-based relief, identify the precise statutory subsection that authorizes civil penalty recovery and brief why private enforcement is available. The Eighth Circuit declined to consider Cohen’s MWTA penalty theory because he did not point to statutory authority or develop argument on appeal.

4. When an employer pays wages or stipulates to payment, anticipate Article III implications.
   - Stipulations or voluntary payments that eliminate the plaintiff’s remedial interest can generate standing and mootness questions. Even where penalties are payable to the government, plaintiffs must show a continuing personal stake (e.g., ongoing or reasonably likely repetition of the defendant’s conduct) to avoid mootness. Preserve and marshal evidence about whether the employer’s allegedly unlawful policy remains in effect; that evidence can make the difference between a live penalty claim and a moot one.

## Strategic implications: litigation posture and evidence focus

This opinion teaches several strategic lessons about how payments, stipulations, and record evidence shape wage-and-hour litigation:

- Payments and stipulations can narrow the dispute to constitutional or jurisdictional questions. Once the employer pays wages and liquidated damages (as here), the litigation often turns on whether non-monetary relief, penalties, or deterrence-based remedies remain available. Counsel should anticipate Article III challenges and plan factual development accordingly.

- For penalty claims grounded in deterrence (where the plaintiff’s interest is prevention of recurrence), contemporaneous evidence about the employer’s current policy and practice is crucial. Courts assessing standing and mootness will look for whether the defendant’s unlawful conduct is ongoing or likely to recur. Obtain discovery or affidavits showing the employer’s policy status, communications to similarly situated employees, or credible steps taken to abandon or maintain the challenged practice.

- Distinguish between remedies payable to the employee and remedies payable to the government. Remedies that go to the government do not automatically eliminate private standing, but plaintiffs must be prepared to explain how those remedies nonetheless provide personal redress (e.g., through deterrence, cessation of conduct, or other forward-looking benefits).

- Consider administrative enforcement as an alternative avenue when the statute centralizes penalty authority in an administrative agency. Where a statute gives the Commissioner exclusive or primary enforcement authority, engaging the agency (or at least preserving that option) may be necessary to obtain the specific statutory penalty.

In short, when wages have been paid and plaintiffs seek penalties or injunctive relief, plaintiffs must be proactive in pleading relief, developing evidence about ongoing practices, and briefing statutory enforcement schemes. Defendants facing wage claims should consider whether limited remedial payments or policy changes will moot claims or reduce exposure, but they should be aware that voluntary cessation does not necessarily defeat jurisdiction unless it is clear that the wrongful conduct will not recur.
