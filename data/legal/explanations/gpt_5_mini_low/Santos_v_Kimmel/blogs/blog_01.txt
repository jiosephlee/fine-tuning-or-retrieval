Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

## ‘Will Santos Say It?’ and the Reasonable Observer: Transformative Use After Warhol

Why this matters: The Second Circuit’s decision in Santos v. Kimmel (2d Cir. Oct. 6, 2025) is an important application of post-Warhol fair use doctrine to the very modern problem of user-created short-form media reused in broadcast commentary. At the core of the panel’s reasoning is a firm embrace of the “reasonable observer” test from Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith and a careful parsing of older language about “good faith” from Harper & Row. The opinion clarifies how courts should evaluate transformation when the secondary user (here, a late-night show) solicits original material from the purported copyright holder under a platform like Cameo, and it signals how allegations of deception will be treated in the fair-use balance going forward.

The facts and the Court’s holding, briefly: Santos, a former congressman, sold personalized videos through Cameo. Under fictitious names, Jimmy Kimmel requested videos, which Santos provided; Kimmel then broadcasted them on Jimmy Kimmel Live! in a recurring satirical segment, “Will Santos Say It?” The district court dismissed Santos’s complaint at the pleadings stage, holding the JKL segments were fair use; the Second Circuit affirmed. The panel concluded the use was transformative—aimed at commentary and ridicule of Santos’s willingness to say outrageous things for payment—and that the other fair-use factors (nature of the work, amount used, and market effect) did not weigh against defendants. The court also rejected Santos’s argument that Kimmel’s deceptive means of procuring the videos defeated the defense.

Why the “reasonable observer” test matters here: The Second Circuit foregrounds the Warhol principle that transformation turns on how a reasonable observer would perceive the secondary use, not on the subjective intentions of the creator or the reuser. Santos argued that Kimmel “instigated” the creation of the videos, and so the segments were not transformative because they supposedly fulfilled Kimmel’s original purpose. The court dismissed that contention: the complaint itself alleges the videos, as created by Santos, conveyed messages of encouragement and positivity. A reasonable observer, however, would view the JKL segments as using those videos for distinct critical and satirical ends—to lampoon Santos and to interrogate what he would say for money. That divergence between the original expressive content and the late-night commentary is the classic sign of transformation under Warhol and the Second Circuit’s precedents.

This reasoning has two practical implications. First, the court reaffirms that transformation is a public-perception inquiry. When the secondary work recasts or repurposes the original in a way that communicates a new message or purpose, transformation can be found even if the secondary user played a role in the original work’s creation. Second, content solicited from a copyright owner is not categorically untransformative because the solicitor “instigated” the work; the ultimate question remains how the reused material functions in the new context.

Harper & Row’s “good faith” language: alive in history, attenuated in application. Santos leaned on Harper & Row’s remark that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” arguing that deceptive procurement of the videos (using fake names) undercut fair use. The Second Circuit gave this argument short shrift for two reasons.

First, the panel notes that the Supreme Court has moved away from treating Harper & Row’s “good faith” statement as a talismanic requirement. Subsequent decisions and circuits have not treated alleged bad faith as an automatic fatal blow to fair use; rather, bad faith is a contextual consideration that can inform, but does not necessarily control, the first-factor transformation inquiry or the overall balance.

Second, on the record before the court—fact-pleading on the face of the complaint—the district court’s factual findings were that defendants used the videos for satire and commentary, not to supplant Santos’s market. The Second Circuit observed that the complaint itself negated a plausible claim that defendants intended to usurp Santos’s market in the videos. In short, even if deception was alleged, Santos’s pleadings did not show the kind of bad-faith purpose that would convert remedial commentary into an impermissible market-substituting use.

Doctrinal takeaways and caveats: The opinion does several useful doctrinal jobs. It reiterates that (1) transformation is outcome-focused and judged by reasonable perception; (2) solicitation of original material by a defendant does not foreclose a finding of transformation; and (3) allegations of deceit in obtaining copyrighted material do not automatically defeat fair use—bad faith can matter, but it must be plausibly tied to the statutory factors (especially whether the defendant intended to supplant the original’s market).

A few caveats. This is a pleading-stage disposition. The Court emphasized that the relevant videos and the JKL segments alone sufficed to decide fair use as a matter of law on the complaint; that is a narrow posture. Had the complaint alleged more particularized facts about intent, market harm, or other relevant circumstances, a different outcome might have been possible. Moreover, the court’s treatment of deception is careful but not absolute: if plaintiffs in future cases can plausibly allege that deception was undertaken to produce a commercially competing substitute or to leverage the material into the same market as the original, that allegation could carry weight in the fair-use calculus.

Market effect and the commercial angle: The Second Circuit’s discussion of the fourth factor follows conventional lines: courts look not to whether the secondary work merely undercuts the original’s value in some general sense, but whether it is a market substitute. Here, the court found it implausible that creators of “imaginative works” like Santos’s would license parody or satire of their own productions; therefore, there was no realistic licensing market for the type of use Kimmel made. That analysis tracks Campbell and later Second Circuit cases and will be familiar to practitioners, but it’s worth noting that in tightly commercial contexts—especially where the secondary use might plausibly supplant downstream licensing—market effect can decisively sway the balance.

Broader implications for media, satire, and platform-solicited content: Santos v. Kimmel sits at an intersection of celebrity, monetization platforms, and broadcast commentary. Platforms like Cameo create a hybrid marketplace of bespoke speech, and content creators may both monetize and repurpose that speech. The Second Circuit’s approach gives comfort to commentators and satirists who repurpose user-produced content for critical or comedic ends, even when that content was originally commissioned by the commentator in some form. It also suggests that plaintiffs must plead more than mere allegations of deceit to overcome fair use—they will need to tie deceptive procurement to a non-transformative purpose or to concrete market-harm allegations.

In short: Santos does not announce a broad license to solicit and reuse copyrighted material, but it does confirm that courts will assess transformation by asking what a reasonable observer would perceive, not by cataloging subjective motives. Allegations of bad faith remain relevant but are neither conclusive nor dispositive. For broadcasters, hosts, and satire-makers, the lesson is familiar and now reinforced: the new expressive context matters; the way the material is used and perceived will often determine the fair-use outcome. For copyright owners monetizing bespoke content, the decision signals that they should expect use in commentary to be scrutinized under the reasonable observer standard—and that contract and state-law remedies, not copyright, may be the more viable route when they object to such reuse.
