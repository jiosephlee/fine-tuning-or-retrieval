Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Markets, Mockery, and Cameo: Why the Fourth Factor Cut Against Santos

## Why this case matters

Santos v. Kimmel is another useful Second Circuit application of fair use to modern media mash-ups: a public figure sold brief personalized videos on a commercial platform, a late-night show repurposed those videos for a mocking recurring segment, and the court was asked to decide whether that use was copyright infringement. At first glance the dispute reads like an ugly collision of celebrity commerce and satire. But the doctrinal heart of the opinion is familiar and significant: the Court reaffirms that the critical inquiry for the fourth fair-use factor is market substitution — not merely whether the secondary use “devalues” the original work — and that mocking, critical uses are unlikely to usurp a licensable market for the original creative content. That determination proved fatal to Santos’s claim at the pleading stage.

Understanding how the court reached that conclusion matters beyond this one dispute. We are increasingly seeing short-form, user-requested content (Cameo-style videos, TikTok shout-outs, personalized greetings) reused in commentary-driven media. Santos confirms that courts will not reflexively treat such secondary uses as market harms simply because they may sour the commercial appeal of the original to its creator. The decision also illuminates how the other fair-use factors — nature and amount — fit into these modern, short-form contexts.

## How the court reasoned

The Second Circuit affirmed dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6), agreeing with the district court that fair use bars Santos’s copyright claims. The opinion walks through the four statutory factors, but places particular weight on the first and fourth factors as dispositive.

For the first factor — purpose and character — the court focused on transformativeness. Even though Kimmel used the Cameo platform to obtain the videos, the broadcast segments served a different communicative purpose: criticism, mockery, and commentary about Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. The court reiterates a settled point from Warhol and Andy Warhol Found. v. Goldsmith: subjective intent of the user or the author is not the determinative metric; rather, the reasonable-observer test controls. Santos’s complaint itself described the videos’ original expressive content as conveying hope and encouragement; Kimmel’s segments recast those same clips as objects of ridicule — a paradigmatic transformative use designed to comment on Santos’s conduct.

On the fourth factor — market effect — the court relied on the familiar and important distinction drawn in Campbell v. Acuff-Rose: the inquiry is whether the secondary work usurps the market for the original by acting as a substitute, not whether the parody diminishes the original’s value. The court emphasized that creators of imaginative works rarely, if ever, would license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions; thus skeptical licensing markets are not the kind of cognizable market harm the statute protects. The complaint itself undermined any plausible claim of market usurpation — the videos were one-off, published on the day they were made, and the broadcast segments were not reasonably substitutable for the Cameo product. Accordingly, the fourth factor favored fair use.

The opinion also addressed the second (nature) and third (amount) factors and found them consistent with a fair-use outcome. The videos were published work (diminishing the protective force of the second factor), and the amount taken was limited to what was necessary for the commentary to succeed — brief clips used to make the joke intelligible. Taken together — and with the first and fourth factors strongly supporting transformation and no market usurpation — the court held that fair use could be resolved in favor of defendants at the pleading stage.

## Why the fourth factor is decisive here

Two interlocking points make the fourth factor dispositive in cases like this.

First, market substitution is a functional test: it asks whether the secondary use serves as a meaningful economic substitute for the original. A late-night television segment that stitches together brief Cameo videos to lampoon a subject has a different audience expectation and use-pattern than an individual purchasing a personalized Cameo greeting. Even if the segment reduces Santos’s reputational or commercial desirability for future Cameo gigs, that reputational devaluation is not equivalent to the secondary work offering the same good in place of the original. The court follows Campbell’s observation that parody and critical uses are unlikely to have a sensible licensing market — the creators of the original typically would not license commentary lampooning their work.

Second, practical realities about the market for these videos matter. Cameo-style clips are bespoke, paid-for messages. They are transactional, tied to the celebrity’s willingness to participate. The court correctly recognized that permitting a copyright owner to convert reputational effects into a cognizable licensing harm would substantially broaden the fourth factor’s reach, threatening to chill critical commentary everywhere. If every broadcast that mocked a personalized video were treated as a substitute that damages a market, parody and journalistic criticism would face a legal regime inviting expensive suits and uncertain liability.

This is not to say market effects are always irrelevant when satire or criticism is involved. If a secondary use were to functionally replace a licensable market — for example, if a television program rebroadcasted entire personalized videos as an alternative product consumers would buy instead of the originals, or if the secondary use made available a competing compilation that purchasers would pay for in place of commissioning original Cameo messages — the fourth factor might cut the other way. But those are empirically distinct scenarios from what the Santos complaint alleged and the JKL segments presented.

## The role of the nature and amount factors here

The opinion’s treatment of the second and third factors is worth noting because those factors sometimes get short shrift in popularized accounts of fair use.

On the second factor (nature of the work), the court placed weight on the fact that Santos’s videos were published on the day of creation. Publication weakens the copyright holder’s claim to protection against repurposing for commentary; unpublished works enjoy heightened protection precisely because the author’s right to control first public appearance is an important interest. The registration materials and complaint made publication status clear, so the second factor did not weigh strongly against fair use.

On the third factor (amount and substantiality), the Court applied the familiar balancing test: use only as much as necessary for the transformative purpose. Late-night mockery needs little more than short clips to make the joke intelligible. The segments did not, on their face, reproduce more of the videos than needed. That limited, purposive copying reinforced the finding of transformative purpose and diminished the likelihood of market substitution.

Taken together, these factors show how the fair-use calculus coheres in short-form commentary contexts: published, short, quote-like uses designed to comment or criticize will often clear the fair-use bar.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Santos v. Kimmel reaffirms and operationalizes several doctrinal lines that matter going forward. First, it reinforces Campbell’s market-substitution lens for factor four and echoes the Supreme Court’s insistence that courts consider what a “reasonable observer” would think about purpose and transformation. That synthesis guards against elevating an author’s speculative or reputational harms into statutory market harms.

Second, the opinion underscores that fair use can be decided at the motion-to-dismiss stage when the pleadings and incorporated documents plainly establish the relevant facts — published status, briefness of use, and the secondary work’s clearly different communicative purpose. That procedural point matters: permitting early resolution avoids protracted discovery and the chilling litigation costs that can suppress commentary.

Third, the decision may influence how content creators think about monetizing short, personalized works. While creators retain remedies in contract and platform governance, they cannot necessarily weaponize copyright law against critical reuse that does not, in realistic terms, replace the market for the original. This tension pushes creators toward strengthening contractual protections (platform terms, bespoke licenses restricting reuse) if they want to control downstream uses, though the opinion also shows the limits of implied-contract claims when third-party beneficiaries are not clearly contemplated.

Finally, media outlets and commentators should take reassurance: using brief clips from commercially distributed personalized content to make a critical point is likely to be viewed through a fair-use-friendly lens, especially where the secondary use is plainly transformative and not a substitute product.

## A final thought

Copyright doctrine in the digital age must balance creators’ economic interests with robust protection for commentary and criticism. Santos v. Kimmel is a predictable, sensible application of that balance. It shows the courts are attentive to market realities and wary of letting copyright doctrine become a backdoor for silencing mockery or journalistic critique. For creators frustrated by mockery, the decision points toward contract law and platform policy as the more promising — and appropriate — avenues for control, not copyright suits premised on generalized reputational harm.
