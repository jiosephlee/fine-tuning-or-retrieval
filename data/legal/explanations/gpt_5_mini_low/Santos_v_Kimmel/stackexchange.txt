Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### How can fair use be decided on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion without discovery?
Question:
The panel affirms dismissal at the pleading stage on fair use, citing TCA Television and Cariou. I'm confused about what materials a court can consider at 12(b)(6) to resolve fair use as a matter of law. Here, the court relied on the complaint, exhibits, incorporated videos, and judicially noticed copyright registrations. What are the limits on considering the actual videos (original Cameos and JKL segments) at this stage? Is there a risk the court is resolving disputed facts about purpose/market harm that normally would require evidence?
Answer:
Short answer
A court may decide fair use on a 12(b)(6) motion when the complaint and the materials properly before the court (including the original works at issue, exhibits attached or incorporated by reference, and facts subject to judicial notice such as Copyright Office registrations) plainly show the use is fair. That is what the Second Circuit did in Santos: it relied on the complaint, the attached exhibits and incorporated videos, and judicially noticed copyright registrations to conclude as a matter of law that fair use barred the claim. But the doctrine has limits: a court cannot grant a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal if resolution of the fair‑use factors would require resolving contested factual disputes not settled by the pleadings or judicially noticeable materials.

What the opinion says and the governing standards
- What the court may consider: The Court reviewed “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference in the complaint,” and it also took judicial notice of “copyright registrations . . . as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.” (Opinion ¶ DISCUSSION, citing DiFolco and Island Software/TCA Televison.) The Court expressly said it needed only “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue” to decide fair use. (Opinion ¶ I, quoting Cariou.)

- Why that can be sufficient: Fair use is a mixed legal-and-factual inquiry, but some fair‑use questions turn on the comparison of the two works themselves (e.g., whether the secondary use is transformative, how much of the work was used, and whether the secondary use is a market substitute). The Second Circuit has recognized that where the complaint and the works before the court make the fair‑use conclusion “so clearly established,” dismissal is appropriate. The Santos opinion follows that line (citing TCA Television and Cariou).

Limits and the risk of premature factual resolution
- Judicial notice and incorporated materials: The court can take judicial notice of objective public records (e.g., copyright registrations) and can examine works incorporated into the complaint or attached as exhibits. If the plaintiff’s claims rest on the content of those works and the complaint does not allege facts that plausibly create a contrary inference, the court may resolve fair use on the pleadings.

- But courts cannot resolve disputed factual questions on a 12(b)(6): If fair‑use resolution depends on contested fact issues (e.g., disputed intent, credibility, factual allegations about how the works were used, or market evidence not apparent from the works or the complaint), dismissal would be improper because the defendants would be asking the court to accept their version of contested facts. The opinion recognizes this boundary by grounding its decision in the materials actually before the court and in the complaint’s own allegations (for example, the court relied on Santos’s own allegation about the videos’ apparent purpose and on the registrations showing publication date).

How that applied in Santos
- The Court concluded the first (transformative‑use) factor strongly favored defendants because, as alleged in the complaint, a reasonable observer would view the Cameo videos as conveying “hope…encouragement” while the JKL segments used them to criticize/mock — a comparison the court could make from the complaint and the videos themselves. (Opinion ¶ I.)
- The Court also relied on judicially noticed registrations to resolve timing/publication facts relevant to the fourth factor (market harm), and on the complaint’s own allegations to find no plausible allegation of a licensing market that the JKL segments usurped. (Opinion ¶ I.)
- Because those materials sufficed to resolve the relevant fair‑use factors without factual development, the court affirmed dismissal.

Takeaway for future cases
- If the plaintiff’s claim depends only on the content of the works and on publicly noticeable facts (registrations, publication dates) or on the plaintiff’s own allegations, a court can decide fair use at the pleading stage.
- If fair‑use determination requires evidence outside the complaint (e.g., disputed facts about bad faith, consumer confusion, actual or likely market harm, or other extrinsic evidence), fair use is usually inappropriate for resolution on a 12(b)(6) and should await summary judgment or trial.

So there is a real risk only when the fair‑use factors turn on contested, extrinsic facts; the Santos court found no such unresolved factual disputes and relied on the complaint plus judicially noticed materials to resolve fair use as a matter of law.

### Does the fact that Kimmel instigated the Cameo scripts defeat transformativeness?
Question:
Santos argued Kimmel "instigated the creation" by submitting prompts under aliases, implying the secondary user designed the original purpose. The court rejects this, focusing on what a reasonable observer would perceive in the secondary use, and not on subjective intent (citing Warhol). Do courts ever consider who engineered the underlying content in assessing whether the later use is transformative, especially when the 'original' was created at the secondary user's direction? How does that square with cases like Campbell (parody) where the original wasn’t authored at the parodist’s behest?
Answer:
Short answer: No — at least under Santos v. Kimmel the fact that Kimmel “instigated” or prompted the creation of the Cameo videos does not defeat a finding of transformativeness. The Second Circuit rejected Santos’s argument that authorship or the engineer of the original purpose controls the fair‑use inquiry and instead applied the familiar “reasonable observer” / purpose‑and‑character test.

Why (doctrinally)
- Santos asked the court to treat Kimmel as the designer of the videos’ original purpose and argued that this undercuts any claim the later use was transformative. The court squarely rejected that approach. It held that whether a secondary use is transformative “turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user,” and cited the Supreme Court’s Warhol decision for the same principle. (Op. at pp. ___ (citing Warhol, 598 U.S. at 544).)
- The fair‑use “purpose and character” analysis focuses on whether the secondary use “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character” such that it does not merely supersede the original. The court found the JKL segments’ purpose — sarcastic criticism/commentary — different from the videos’ communicated persona (as alleged), and therefore the first factor strongly favored fair use. (Op. at § I.)

How that relates to situations like Campbell (parody)
- Campbell is consistent with this approach: the key question is the nature and purpose of the secondary use (e.g., parody/criticism), not whether the defendant engineered the original work. Santos tried to distinguish by saying he created the videos with the same ostensible purpose as Kimmel’s use, but the court responded that plaintiff’s and defendant’s subjective intents are not the controlling inquiry — the reasonable observer’s perception of the works is. The court therefore resolved the issue by comparing the original videos (as published) to the JKL segments and concluding the segments were transformative. (Op. at ¶¶ discussing factor one and citing Campbell on market effect.)

Bottom line
Under the Second Circuit’s ruling in Santos v. Kimmel, who “engineered” or prompted creation of the original content is not dispositive. Courts look to the objective character and purpose of the secondary use (how a reasonable observer would perceive it) to assess transformativeness, even when the secondary user played a role in creating the original.

### What role does bad faith or deception play in fair use after Harper & Row?
Question:
Santos claimed Kimmel’s false identities on Cameo showed bad faith that should vitiate fair use, citing Harper & Row’s "good faith" language. The court says the Supreme Court has distanced itself from that doctrine and focuses instead on non-supplanting commentary. What is the current Second Circuit (and Supreme Court) doctrine on bad faith in fair use? Can deception ever tip factor one or four against fair use, and under what circumstances would misrepresentation matter?
Answer:
Short answer
After Harper & Row the Supreme Court and the Second Circuit do not treat an actor’s “bad faith” or deception as an independent, dispositive freestanding requirement of fair use. Courts now evaluate the statutory §107 factors—especially (1) purpose/character (is the use transformative?) and (4) market effect (does the use usurp a market or licensing opportunity)—and will consider deception only to the extent it bears on those factors (e.g., shows the use was non‑transformative or was intended to supplant a market). Santos v. Kimmel illustrates that approach: the Second Circuit acknowledged Harper & Row’s “good faith” language but explained the Court has “distanced itself” from that doctrine and looked instead to whether the use was commentary and not a market‑supplanting substitute.

How the doctrine works (doctrinal framework)
- Harper & Row: the Court famously said “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.” But later precedent (and the Second Circuit in Santos) has backed away from treating good faith as an independent dispositive element. Instead, current analysis asks whether the challenged use is transformative (factor 1) and whether it usurps a potential market (factor 4), with the remaining factors considered in context.
- Transformation/Objective inquiry: courts ask what a reasonable observer would view the secondary use to be (see Andy Warhol Found. v. Goldsmith, cited in Santos). A user’s subjective intent or the copyright owner’s stated purpose is not dispositive.
- Market‑supplanting focus: factor 4 asks whether the secondary work is a substitute for the original (usurps licensing markets), not merely whether it harms reputation or reduces value through criticism or parody (see Campbell; quoted in Santos and Hachette).

When deception can matter
Deception is relevant only insofar as it illuminates one of the statutory factors:

1) Deception that bears on factor one (purpose/character)
   - If the deception shows the secondary work’s apparent purpose is not commentary/criticism or otherwise not transformative to a reasonable observer, it can weigh against fair use. Example: if someone procures a work by pretending to want a compliment but instead uses it unchanged in a context that competes with the original, that undermines a claimed transformative purpose.
   - In Santos the court found the complaint itself showed the reasonable‑observer view was commentary (transformative), so the alleged deceit did not negate factor one.

2) Deception that bears on factor four (market effect)
   - If deception was used to obtain access to an unpublished or unreleased work or to obtain rights in a way that enables the defendant to usurp the original’s licensing market (the classic Harper & Row concern), that can tip factor four against fair use. Harper & Row itself involved unauthorized publication of an unpublished presidential memoir excerpt obtained in a context that interfered with the owner’s right to first publication/licensing.
   - If the deception has no plausible link to creating a market substitute (e.g., it merely facilitated obtaining material used for parody or commentary that would not be licensed), courts will not treat it as dispositive.

3) Deception that creates independent legal liability
   - Even if deception does not kill fair use, it may support other legal claims (fraud, contract, wiretap statutes, platform terms violations). Santos addressed those state claims separately and rejected them on pleading grounds—its fair‑use holding turned on the statutory factors, not an independent “bad faith” rule.

Practical rule of thumb
- Allegations of deceit are not per se fatal to fair use in the Second Circuit or under current Supreme Court precedent.
- Courts will ask: does the deception make the use non‑transformative or enable a competing market substitute that would have been licensable? If yes, deception can push factors 1 or 4 against fair use. If no—if the use is genuinely critical/transformative and not a market substitute—deception alone usually won’t vitiate fair use (as the Second Circuit held in Santos).

Key textual anchors from Santos v. Kimmel
- Santos recognized Harper & Row’s “good faith” language but observed the Supreme Court “has since distanced itself from this statement.”
- The court framed the inquiry around whether the complaint showed the defendants’ purpose was to “supplant” Santos’s commercially valuable right; because the complaint showed sarcastic criticism/commentary, factor one supported fair use.
- The court reiterated that market‑usurpation, not mere value diminution, drives factor four—citing Campbell and Hachette.

Conclusion
Bad faith or deception is not a separate trumping doctrine after Harper & Row; it matters only insofar as it informs the §107 factors—primarily whether the use is transformative and whether it usurps a licensable market. Deception can tip factor one or four against fair use when it meaningfully supports a non‑transformative purpose or an intent/ability to supplant the original’s market (for example, procuring unpublished material to publish as a substitute). But mere deceit unconnected to those statutory concerns will not automatically vitiate fair use under current Second Circuit and Supreme Court precedent.

### Why doesn’t airing the Cameos on national TV cause cognizable market harm under factor four?
Question:
The court says the relevant question is whether JKL usurped the market by offering a competing substitute, not whether criticism devalues Santos’s videos. But if Santos sells these exact videos on Cameo, didn’t JKL’s broadcast supply them to a broader audience for free, potentially undermining demand? How does the analysis differentiate between substitutive use of the same content and non-substitutive commentary, and what evidentiary showing is required at the pleading stage to allege market usurpation?
Answer:
Short answer: Because the fair-use “market effect” inquiry looks to whether the secondary use usurps a cognizable, traditional market for the original — i.e., whether the secondary work is (or functions as) a competing substitute that purchasers would buy instead of the original — not whether the secondary use merely criticizes or “devalues” the original. The Second Circuit applied that settled rule here and concluded Santos’ allegations do not plausibly plead usurpation at the pleading stage.

Why the court framed the issue that way
- The court repeats the controlling rule: “we ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” (Opinion quoting Hachette, Campbell). That is the proper focus of factor four.
- The rationale is practical: fair use protects commentary and criticism even when it harms value; only when the secondary use displaces a normal market for licensing or sales is the fourth factor likely to weigh against fair use.

How substitutive use differs from non‑substitutive commentary
- Substitutive use: the secondary work performs the same function as the original in a way that consumers would use it instead of the original (e.g., reproducing and distributing identical content in a context that satisfies the same consumer demand, or entering a distinct licensing market that the rightsholder would reasonably exploit).
- Non‑substitutive commentary: the secondary work uses the original to add new purpose or meaning (criticism, commentary, parody, news reporting). Even if this use “devalues” the original’s reputation or reduces demand, courts treat that harm differently from market usurpation because creators are not expected to license parodies or critical takedowns of their own works (see Campbell’s point that creators are unlikely to license such uses).

What the Santos opinion says about pleading market usurpation
- The court treated the complaint’s own allegations as showing the JKL segments were critical/commentary uses (purpose was to mock Santos) and thus non‑transformative only in the sense relevant to the reasonable‑observer test — but expressly held the first factor favors fair use.
- On the fourth factor, the court concluded on the face of the complaint that Santos “has not suffered market harm within the meaning of the fourth fair use factor” because the relevant question is usurpation. The court relied on (a) the nature of JKL’s use (commentary/parody), and (b) the lack of any allegation that JKL offered a competing market or displaced a licensing market for Santos’s Cameo videos.

What a plaintiff must plead at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage to survive on market‑effect grounds
- Plausible, non‑conclusory factual allegations that the secondary work (or its distribution) actually targets and substitutes for a cognizable market the plaintiff could or would exploit. Examples of useful allegations (drawn from the governing standard applied in the opinion):
  - There exists (or reasonably could exist) a licensing or sales market for the specific kind of secondary use (e.g., licensing clips for broadcast), and defendants’ use is of the sort that rightsholders customarily license.
  - The defendant’s use was offered to the same audience and served the same function as the originals such that consumers would choose the defendant’s use instead of purchasing/viewing the originals.
  - Quantifiable or plausible allegations of lost sales, lost licensing opportunities, or concrete displacement (dates, audiences, distribution channels) that make substitution plausible rather than speculative.
- Bare assertions that the defendant’s broadcast “devalued” the works or reached a broader audience are insufficient absent facts showing competitive substitution. The Santos opinion affirms dismissal because the complaint lacked such factual plausibility.

Bottom line
The court applied the black‑letter fair‑use rule: factor four asks about usurpation (competing substitute), not mere devaluation by criticism. Santos’ complaint did not plausibly allege that Kimmel’s broadcast served as a market substitute for Santos’s Cameo videos or displaced a recognizable licensing market, so the fourth factor did not defeat fair use at the pleading stage.

### What did the court mean by treating the Cameos as 'published' for factor two?
Question:
The opinion notes the copyright registrations show the videos were published the same day they were created and cites 17 U.S.C. § 101. How does the court infer 'publication' from the registrations, and why does that matter to the nature-of-the-work factor? Are Cameo deliveries to a single purchaser 'publication,' or is there a separate distribution/broadcast requirement? How significant is the published/unpublished distinction after Harper & Row in modern digital contexts?
Answer:
Short answer
The Court treated the Cameo videos as “published” because the Copyright Office registrations included publication dates, and the Court took judicial notice of those registrations. That matters for the “nature of the work” (factor two) because publication status is part of the statutory definition and doctrinal inquiry into how close the work is to the author’s protected right of first public appearance; published works are generally less entitled to protection on factor two than unpublished works. The opinion does not resolve a separate rule for single-copy/single-customer deliveries; it simply relied on the registrations here and did not need to litigate whether a one‑to‑one Cameo delivery is a “publication.” And as the Court noted, Harper & Row’s language about “good faith” has been softened by later decisions, so that concern plays a narrower role in modern fair‑use analysis.

Explanation tied to the opinion

1) How the Court inferred publication from the registrations
- The opinion states that “the relevant copyright registrations establish that the videos were published on the same day they were created” and cites 17 U.S.C. § 101 and precedent allowing judicial notice of Copyright Office registrations (Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp.). App’x 160–69; see Island Software, 413 F.3d at 261 (quoted in the opinion). In short, the Court treated the registration entries — which include publication dates — as judicially noticeable evidence that the works were published.

2) Why publication status matters for factor two (nature of the work)
- The “nature of the copyrighted work” factor asks whether the work is more factual or imaginative and whether it is published or unpublished — publication status is part of the classic inquiry into how much the law should protect the author’s right of first public appearance. The opinion does not give an extended factor‑two analysis, but by identifying the works as published it placed them within the published‑work side of that inquiry rather than the unpublished‑work side, where factor two tends to weigh more strongly against fair use. The Court therefore treated publication as an operative classification relevant to fair‑use balancing.

3) Are Cameo deliveries to a single purchaser “publication”?
- The opinion does not adopt a rule on that specific factual question. It did not analyze whether single‑recipient Cameo deliveries alone would qualify as publication; instead, it relied on the registrations’ statement that the works were published the same day they were created. So for this case the Court did not need to decide (and does not say) whether a one‑to‑one Cameo delivery, standing alone, is legally a “publication” under § 101 — it simply treated the registration’s publication date as dispositive evidence on that point.

4) How Harper & Row figures into the modern analysis
- The opinion discusses Harper & Row only on the narrower point of whether bad faith precludes fair use. It notes that Harper & Row’s formulation that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing” has been “distanced” by subsequent decisions, and the Court declined to let alleged bad faith (Kimmel’s false representations) defeat fair use on the facts alleged. That indicates the Court sees Harper & Row’s good‑faith language as less determinative today; it does not repudiate Harper & Row’s other holdings about the role of publication and the first public appearance right, but it shows that alleged bad faith alone will not necessarily nullify a fair‑use defense post‑Harper & Row.

Bottom line
The Court relied on the Copyright Office registration entries (judicially noticed) to treat the Cameo videos as published. That classification matters because publishedness is a recognized part of factor two’s inquiry and generally makes the nature‑of‑work factor less protective of the copyright owner’s claim. The opinion does not resolve whether a single‑recipient Cameo delivery is publication in itself; it simply accepted the registrations’ publication dates as controlling for this case. Finally, Harper & Row’s “good faith” language has diminished force in the Court’s view, so bad faith allegations alone will not necessarily defeat fair use.

### Why did Santos lack standing to sue on the Cameo Terms of Service under Illinois law?
Question:
The direct breach claim fails because Santos isn’t a party to the user Terms of Service (which govern Kimmel’s account) and Illinois law has a strong presumption against third-party beneficiaries absent an 'almost express' declaration. What specific language in typical platform TOS would be sufficient to make a non-party creator like Santos an intended third-party beneficiary? Does the presence of third-party-beneficiary language elsewhere in the TOS weigh against implying beneficiary status for other provisions as a matter of Illinois contract interpretation?
Answer:
Short answer
Santos lacked standing because under the Cameo Terms (governed by Illinois law) he was not a contracting party and the complaint did not plead anything like the “almost express” declaration Illinois requires to overcome the strong presumption against third‑party beneficiaries. The opinion explains that “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration,” and it relied on the fact that the Terms do contain express third‑party‑beneficiary language in some places to show that Cameo knew how to make a beneficiary express when it intended to do so. Santos’s complaint pointed to no comparable express language, so he could not sue for breach.

What language would be sufficient under Illinois law (based on the Court’s reasoning)
The Circuit’s description of the Illinois rule is best read functionally: to be an intended third‑party beneficiary a non‑party must be identifiable in the contract in language that shows the contracting parties intended to confer an enforceable right on that person or class. Language that would meet that standard would include, for example:

- an express grant of rights to the non‑party (by name or by clearly defined class), e.g. “This Agreement is made for the benefit of X and X shall have the right to enforce the obligations herein”; or
- an explicit statement that a particular provision “shall be enforceable” by a non‑party or a defined class, e.g. “Creators who upload content to Cameo are intended third‑party beneficiaries of Sections 5–7 and may enforce those provisions”; or
- language stating the providers’ promise is “for the benefit of” a defined class that unambiguously includes the non‑party and that the class “may bring an action to enforce” the promise.

In short, language that is practically an express declaration — naming the beneficiary or expressly conferring enforcement rights — is what Illinois (as the opinion cites Barry) requires.

Does explicit third‑party language elsewhere in the TOS cut against implying beneficiary status for other provisions?
Yes — and the opinion relied on precisely that point. The court noted that “other provisions of the Terms of Service do contain third‑party beneficiary language, indicating that when the parties to that agreement intended to allow provisions of the contract to be enforced by third parties, they said so expressly.” Under ordinary contract interpretation, the presence of express third‑party‑beneficiary clauses in some provisions suggests the parties knew how to create such rights and did not intend to create them where they omitted that language. That comparative drafting choice therefore weighs against implying beneficiary status for different provisions that lack comparable language.

Takeaway for pleading or drafting
- For a creator like Santos to plead (or for platform drafters to create) enforceable third‑party rights under Illinois law, the TOS should include express beneficiary language (naming or clearly defining the class and stating enforcement rights).  
- If a TOS contains express beneficiary clauses in some places but not in the provision a plaintiff relies on, courts are likely to treat that as evidence against implying rights for the omitted provisions, as the Santos opinion demonstrates.

### What was missing from the New York implied-in-fact contract claim tied to the TOS?
Question:
The court says Santos failed to plead the essential terms and a meeting of the minds that Kimmel would adhere to the TOS vis-à-vis Santos. If Santos had alleged that he accepted Kimmel’s orders only on the condition that purchasers comply with Cameo’s usage restrictions, would that suffice to plead an implied-in-fact agreement under New York law? What kinds of conduct or communications (course of dealing, notices on profile pages, order confirmations) have courts treated as manifesting mutual assent in analogous platform contexts?
Answer:
Short answer
What the Second Circuit said Santos lacked was (1) allegations of the “essential terms” of any implied agreement and (2) facts showing a “meeting of the minds” that Kimmel would adhere to Cameo’s Terms of Service. Santos could have plausibly pleaded an implied‑in‑fact contract if he had alleged nonconclusory facts showing he accepted requests only on the condition that purchasers (and requesters) comply with Cameo’s usage restrictions and that Kimmel knew of and manifested assent to that condition. Whether those particular facts would carry the day would depend on concrete manifestations of mutual assent (communications, notice, course of dealing), not on bare legal conclusions.

What the opinion actually requires (doctrine / standard)
The court applied New York law about implied‑in‑fact contracts: the plaintiff must plead (with nonconclusory allegations) the essential terms of the putative contract and facts showing an indication of a meeting of the minds. The opinion cites Rocky Point Props. (intent and surrounding circumstances govern formation), Caniglia (plead essential terms including specific provisions invoked), and DG & A / Tjoa (need plausible indication of meeting of minds). In short — plead what the promise was, who made it, and nonconclusory facts showing mutual assent.

Would your hypothetical have helped?
Yes — but only if pled with factual detail rather than labels. Alleging “I accepted Kimmel’s orders only on the condition that purchasers comply with Cameo’s usage restrictions” could support an implied‑in‑fact contract claim under the court’s standard, provided the complaint also alleged facts showing:
- Which specific Cameo provisions were involved (the “essential terms”); and
- How Kimmel knew of those terms and agreed to them (facts evidencing his assent), not merely the legal conclusion that he did.

The opinion rejects Santos’s claim because his pleading did neither: it failed to identify the operative provisions and failed to allege a plausible meeting of minds that Kimmel would adhere to the TOS.

What kinds of conduct or communications courts look for (in the framework the opinion uses)
The Second Circuit’s opinion emphasizes intent and surrounding circumstances rather than listing a closed set of acceptable manifestations. That said, under the governing principle cited in the opinion, plausible factual allegations that typically support a finding of mutual assent include (when actually pled with supporting facts):
- Direct communications between the parties showing the conditional acceptance (e.g., requester and creator exchanging messages specifying permitted uses); 
- Notices or prominent statements that put a requester on notice that videos are subject to usage restrictions (e.g., profile pages or requester instructions that reference platform TOS), combined with evidence the requester read/acknowledged them; and
- Repeated course of dealing or consistent transaction practices demonstrating that both sides understood and acted on the same set of restrictions.

The opinion itself does not decide which of these would be dispositive here; it only requires that Santos plead specific contractual terms and facts indicating a meeting of minds. So factual allegations of the sorts above — shown with detail in the complaint rather than conclusory assertions — are the kind that could have cured the pleading defect identified by the court.

Bottom line
Under the Second Circuit’s reasoning, an implied‑in‑fact claim tied to platform TOS survives only if the complaint alleges (with factual particularity) the specific contractual provisions relied on and facts showing a meeting of the minds that the defendant would abide by those provisions. The conditional‑acceptance allegation you propose could be sufficient if accompanied by detailed facts demonstrating that Kimmel knew of and manifested assent to those usage restrictions.

### Why did the fraudulent inducement claim fail for lack of out-of-pocket loss under New York law?
Question:
The panel affirms dismissal because Santos didn’t allege any out-of-pocket pecuniary loss, citing Lama Holding and Connaughton. If Santos spent time creating the videos and received payment, could he allege a net loss (e.g., time/value of services exceeding the price, or lost opportunities) that qualifies as out-of-pocket damages? Are reputational harms or diminution of the videos’ value cognizable in fraud, or are those barred as benefit-of-the-bargain or consequential damages in New York?
Answer:
Short answer: The fraudulent-inducement claim failed because under New York law a deceit plaintiff must plead a concrete, out‑of‑pocket pecuniary loss — not mere subjective harms, lost opportunities, or the “value” of time — and Santos pleaded none. The opinion (citing Lama Holding and Connaughton) treats the required loss as tangible economic injury, and finds Santos’s own allegations (he made the videos and was paid; the videos were published the same day) do not allege any such loss.

Why that rule controls
- Lama Holding v. Smith Barney establishes that New York measures damages for common‑law fraud by out‑of‑pocket loss (the actual pecuniary loss suffered) rather than the benefit‑of‑the‑bargain (the gain the plaintiff expected). The Second Circuit here applies that principle. 
- Connaughton reaffirmed that a fraud plaintiff must allege an actual, non‑speculative pecuniary loss — mere indignity, reputational injury, or a theoretical diminution in value is not enough unless tied to a concrete monetary loss.

How the opinion applies those principles to Santos
- The court observed that Santos was paid for the Cameo videos and that the videos were promptly published, and that he did not allege he suffered any measurable, out‑of‑pocket monetary loss from Kimmel’s conduct. On that basis the panel affirmed dismissal of the fraud claim for failure to plead the required pecuniary injury (Opinion ¶II, citing Lama Holding and Connaughton).

Can Santos plead a “net loss” (time/value of services exceeding the price, or lost opportunities)?
- Possibly — but only if pled as a concrete pecuniary loss. New York fraud damages require monetary loss. Alleging that the fee he received was less than the fair value of his time or services could state out‑of‑pocket loss if pleaded with enough factual detail (e.g., unreimbursed expenses, costs incurred in reliance, quantifiable difference between market/licensing value and what he received). Similarly, lost licensing or other economic opportunities can qualify if plausibly pleaded as actual, non‑speculative monetary harm (e.g., demonstrable lost contracts or licensing fees caused by defendants’ misrepresentations).
- Bare assertions that “I lost time” or “the price was too low,” without tying those claims to a calculable pecuniary injury, will not survive under Lama/Connaughton as applied in the opinion.

Are reputational harms or diminution of the videos’ value cognizable?
- Not as the out‑of‑pocket fraud damages the court required here. Reputational injury or an abstract “diminution in value” is not the type of direct pecuniary loss New York’s out‑of‑pocket rule demands, absent allegations converting reputation/diminution into measurable economic loss (for example, lost contracts, lost licensing revenue, or other specific financial harm caused by the reputational hit). The opinion’s fair‑use discussion (videos published the same day; no market harm alleged) also undercuts a claim that the segments usurped a licensing market or caused measurable market loss.

What Santos would need to plead to survive
- Specific, non‑conclusory allegations of actual monetary loss caused by the defendants’ misrepresentations: unreimbursed costs incurred because of the inducement, a quantifiable shortfall between what he received and what he would have received absent the fraud (with factual support), or identifiable lost economic opportunities directly traceable to the defendants’ conduct.
- Allegations tying reputational or diminution claims to concrete economic consequences (e.g., demonstrable lost sales, cancelled engagements, or lost licensing deals) rather than asserting reputational harm in the abstract.

Bottom line: Under the Second Circuit’s reading of Lama Holding and Connaughton, New York fraud requires a tangible out‑of‑pocket pecuniary loss. Santos’s complaint, which alleges he produced the videos and was paid and does not quantify any actual monetary loss tied to the alleged deceit, fails that pleading requirement.
