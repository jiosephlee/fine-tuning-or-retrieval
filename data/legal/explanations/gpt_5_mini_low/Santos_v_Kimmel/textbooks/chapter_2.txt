Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 2: # Fair Use Framework and Pleading-Stage Adjudication

This chapter explains how the Second Circuit applied the fair use doctrine in Santos v. Kimmel and why the court resolved fair use at the Rule 12(b)(6) pleading stage. It focuses on the statutory factors of 17 U.S.C. § 107, the centrality of the transformative-use inquiry (and its objective/“reasonable observer” focus), the contextual and flexible nature of fair use, and the limited record the court relied upon — namely, the original Cameo videos, the Jimmy Kimmel Live! (JKL) segments that aired them, and the copyright registrations. Understanding these elements will help you see why a court can dismiss a copyright claim on fair use grounds before discovery when the complaint and the works themselves show the defense plainly applies.

## Fair use as an affirmative defense that furthers copyright’s goals

Fair use is an affirmative defense to copyright infringement; it permits uses that would otherwise be infringing when refusing permission would unduly restrain the creative and communicative purposes the Copyright Act aims to encourage. The Santos opinion frames fair use as a tool that avoids “rigid application of the Copyright Act when it would stifle the very creativity the Act is meant to promote.” That framing informs the Court’s willingness to consider fair use early in litigation: where a secondary use is plainly within the scope of fair use, the doctrine protects the secondary user's expressive activity (here, televised commentary and ridicule) even though the activity employs copyrighted material created by another.

## Section 107 factors — the familiar four-part inquiry

Section 107 sets out four non-exclusive factors courts weigh in fair use determinations: (1) the purpose and character of the use; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used; and (4) the effect of the use on the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. Santos reiterates that these factors are to be applied flexibly and contextually rather than as rigid formulae. In the opinion the Court examined each factor, but it emphasized the first — purpose and character — as the most probative here given the clear communicative purpose of the JKL segments.

## Transformative use and the reasonable-observer standard

Within the first factor, the Second Circuit centers analysis on whether the secondary use is transformative: does it merely supersede the original or instead add something new with a different purpose or character? Santos v. Kimmel follows the line of Second Circuit and Supreme Court authority holding that the transformative inquiry is primarily an objective question — what a reasonable observer would perceive — not a test of the parties’ subjective intent. The opinion cites Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith to emphasize that courts should not let the subjective intent of the user or the copyright holder determine the purpose of the secondary use. Applying that standard, the Court concluded that a reasonable observer would see the Cameo videos as original expressions conveying positivity and encouragement, whereas Kimmel’s repurposing of those videos on JKL plainly added critical and satirical commentary about Santos’s alleged willingness to say absurd things for money. That change in communicative purpose supported a finding of transformative use under the first factor.

## Flexibility of fair use and context-specific analysis

Santos underscores fair use’s contextual flexibility: the same use might be transformative in one setting and not in another depending on how the material is employed and presented. The Court reiterated that fair use can be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” when the complaint and the works themselves make the nature of the reuse evident. In other words, courts do not apply a single, mechanical test; they weigh the statutory factors in light of the specific expressive relationship between the original and secondary works. In Santos the satire and commentary evident in the JKL segments weighed heavily in favor of fair use.

## Pleading-stage resolution: when fair use is clear from the complaint and the works

A critical procedural point in Santos is the Court’s disposition of the copyright claims at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage. Normally fair use is an affirmative defense, and factual disputes may make summary judgment or trial appropriate. But the Second Circuit has long held that a fair use defense may be resolved on a motion to dismiss when fair use is apparent from the complaint and the works themselves. The opinion cites precedent (e.g., TCA Television Corp. and Cariou v. Prince) to show that when the original and secondary works — together with the complaint’s factual allegations and any properly incorporated documents — plainly demonstrate fair use, the court need not wait for discovery. In Santos the complaint expressly described the nature of the original videos, the way Kimmel republished them, and the communicative aim of the JKL segments; those allegations, paired with the videos themselves, made fair use clear as a matter of law.

## Permissible record at the pleading stage: videos and registrations

When deciding a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal that rests on fair use, courts must limit themselves to materials properly before them: the complaint’s factual allegations, documents attached to or incorporated in the complaint, and things of which the court may take judicial notice. Santos illustrates that approach. The Second Circuit considered the original Cameo videos and the JKL segments (either attached, incorporated, or subject to judicial notice) because those works are central to the fair use analysis and were before the court. The opinion also relied on the publicly available copyright registrations in the Copyright Office’s registry to establish that the works were published the same day they were created — a factual point relevant to the second and fourth factors (nature and market effect). By using only those materials, the Court avoided factual disputes inappropriate for a pleading-stage decision and nevertheless reached a definitive fair use holding.

## How these points come together in Santos v. Kimmel

Applied to the facts of Santos, the Court’s framework produced a straightforward result. The first-factor inquiry — whether Kimmel added new expression and meaning — weighed strongly for fair use because the JKL segments used the Cameo videos as fodder for satirical commentary, a different purpose than the motivational intent Santos allegedly had when making them. The Court rejected Santos’s reliance on Kimmel’s alleged deceit (using fictitious names) to defeat fair use: even accepting the complaint’s allegations about Kimmel’s conduct, the complaint itself showed no intent by defendants to supplant Santos’s market or to act in a way that would negate the fair use analysis. The Court also found no plausible market-harm theory because the JKL segments did not serve as a market substitute for the original videos. Because the complaint and the works made these points clear, the Court held that fair use barred the copyright claims at the pleading stage and affirmed the District Court’s dismissal.

In sum, Santos demonstrates when and why a court can decide fair use on a motion to dismiss: where the complaint’s allegations plus the original and secondary works (and any judicially noticeable registrations) make clear that the statutory factors — particularly transformation of purpose — favor the user. For students learning to read such opinions, the key takeaways are the primacy of the transformative inquiry evaluated objectively, the contextual nature of fair use, and the narrow but sufficient record that may justify an early, dispositive ruling.
