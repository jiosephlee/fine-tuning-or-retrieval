Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 3: # Applying the Fair Use Factors to Kimmel’s Broadcasts

This chapter explains how the Second Circuit applied the four statutory fair use factors to the videos that Congressman Santos created and to the way Jimmy Kimmel used them on his television show. The court affirmed dismissal of Santos’s copyright claims at the pleading stage because, viewing the complaint and incorporated materials, Kimmel’s broadcast use was a paradigmatic instance of fair use. To follow the court’s reasoning, you should keep two things in mind: the fair use inquiry is multi-factor and flexible, and the most important consideration here was whether Kimmel’s use was transformative when viewed objectively by a reasonable observer.

## Purpose and character: transformative commentary on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money

The court began with the first statutory factor—purpose and character—which it treated primarily as a question of transformation. Transformation asks whether the secondary use “merely supersedes” the original or instead “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character.” The District Court found, and the Second Circuit agreed, that Kimmel’s segments served a distinct communicative purpose: they used the personalized Cameo videos in a recurring, mocking television bit titled “Will Santos Say It?” The purpose of the JKL segments was to comment on and lampoon Santos’s apparent willingness to say outrageous things for money, not to replicate or replace the original videos’ messages.

Critically, the complaint itself described the original videos in emotive terms—conveying “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.” The court treated that description as establishing the original communicative character of the works. Because Kimmel’s broadcasts reframed the same footage for a different end (satire/criticism directed at Santos), the use added a new expressive meaning and thus was transformative for fair use purposes. The court concluded that the first factor therefore strongly favored defendants.

## Objective lens: the reasonable observer standard and the “designer” argument

Santos argued that Kimmel “instigated” the videos by posing scripted lines through Cameo and thus was, in effect, the designer of the originals’ purpose; on that account, the use could not be transformative. The court rejected this line of reasoning because transformation is judged objectively: what matters is how a reasonable observer would perceive the secondary use, not the subjective intent of the copyright owner or the secondary user. The Supreme Court and the Second Circuit emphasize that courts should not let the parties’ self-serving characterizations override the ordinary observer’s impression.

Applying that standard here, the court explained that a reasonable viewer would see Santos’s videos as sincere, encouraging messages (the original purpose) while viewing Kimmel’s on-air presentation as a critical, satirical reframing. The fact that Kimmel used fictitious names to request the videos did not alter the objective communicative difference between the originals and the televised segments.

## Bad faith argument: narrowing of Harper & Row and why the complaint undermines bad-faith inference

Santos invoked a line from Harper & Row that fair use “presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” arguing that Kimmel’s deceptive requests showed bad faith and defeated the defense. The Second Circuit rejected that argument for two reasons. First, subsequent Supreme Court guidance has limited the weight of Harper & Row’s dictum about an implicit good-faith requirement; bad intent is not automatically fatal to a fair use defense. Second, and dispositively at the pleading stage, Santos’s own allegations undermined any inference that Kimmel intended to supplant Santos’s market for the videos. The complaint portrayed the defendants as engaging in sarcastic criticism and commentary—classic fair use motivations—rather than trying to deprive Santos of any commercial market for his works. Because Santos pleaded no plausible theory that defendants sought to monetize or replace the original videos, the bad-faith argument did not defeat fair use on the face of the complaint.

## Nature of the work: published status and its impact on factor two

The second statutory factor asks about the nature of the copyrighted work—with unpublished, highly creative works normally getting stronger protection. The court relied on incorporated copyright registrations showing the videos were published the day they were created. That publication status diminishes the weight of factor two against fair use because courts treat published works as more susceptible to secondary uses that comment on them. The opinion therefore concluded that the second factor did not favor Santos in a way that would overcome the strong transformative character found under factor one.

## Amount and substantiality: use tailored to the commentary

The third factor looks to how much of the original was used and whether the “heart” of the work was appropriated. The court’s analysis tied this factor to the purpose of the defendant’s commentary. Kimmel aired the personalized videos in full (or at least substantial portions) as necessary to make the satirical point: the humor depended on viewers seeing Santos’s own words and tone. In fair use jurisprudence, copying a substantial portion can be acceptable when the amount copied is appropriate to the transformative purpose. Here, because the broadcasts sought to ridicule Santos’s willingness to utter certain lines for money, using the lines themselves was integral to the comment. Consequently, the third factor did not outweigh the strong support for fair use supplied by the first factor.

## Market effect: no usurpation of the market for the original videos

The fourth factor asks whether the secondary use harms the existing or potential market for the original. The court emphasized the distinction between devaluing a work through parody or criticism (which may harm perception but is not the kind of harm the fourth factor guards against) and usurpation—offering a competing substitute in the same market. The court cited precedent explaining that creators of imaginative works are unlikely to license others to produce critical reviews or lampoons of those works; therefore, such secondary uses are outside the sorts of licensing markets the fourth factor protects.

Applying that principle here, the court concluded that Kimmel’s broadcasts did not serve as a market substitute for Santos’s Cameo videos. Santos had no plausible allegation that people would purchase a JKL segment as a replacement for paid personalized videos or that the television use displaced any licensing market for such messages. Because the complaint itself demonstrated publication and offered no plausible market-harm theory, the fourth factor did not defeat fair use.

## Bottom line: fair use established on the pleadings

Weighing the factors together, the Second Circuit agreed with the District Court that the first factor—strongly favoring defendants because the use was transformative commentary—was decisive. The objective reasonable-observer inquiry, the lack of plausible allegations of marketplace usurpation, the published status of the videos, and the context-driven justification for the amount used all supported fair use. Because these conclusions were apparent from the complaint and incorporated registration materials, the court held that Santos’s copyright claims were barred by fair use as a matter of law and affirmed dismissal at the pleading stage.

For a law student, this opinion illustrates how courts may resolve fair use on a Rule 12(b)(6) record when the complaint and attached materials plainly show a use that reframes expressive content for commentary or critique and when the plaintiff fails to plead facts suggesting market substitution or other countervailing harms.
