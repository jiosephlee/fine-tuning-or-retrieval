Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 5: # Holding, Disposition, and Significance

## Holding: Affirmance of dismissal; fair use bars copyright claims; state-law claims fail on their elements

The Second Circuit affirmed the District Court’s dismissal of all of George Santos’s claims. On the copyright claims, the court held that Jimmy Kimmel’s airing of the Cameo videos on Jimmy Kimmel Live! was a fair use as a matter of law at the pleading stage. The court emphasized the first statutory fair-use factor — the purpose and character of the use — and found the JKL segments were transformative because they repurposed Santos’s videos for critical, comedic commentary rather than to serve the original expressive purpose that Santos alleged. The court rejected Santos’s argument that Kimmel’s role in soliciting the videos defeated a finding of transformativeness; rather, an objective reasonable-observer test governs whether a secondary use is transformative, not the subjective intent of the parties. The court also found the other fair-use factors did not defeat fair use on the face of the complaint, and in particular that there was no cognizable market-harm theory: the JKL segments did not usurp any plausible licensing market for Santos’s videos.

As to the state-law claims (breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement), the court affirmed dismissal on the merits. The court explained that Santos could not enforce Cameo’s Terms of Service because he was not a contracting party and pointed to the strong presumption against conferring contractual benefits on noncontracting third parties under Illinois law (which governs the Terms). For the implied-in-fact contract theory under New York law, the complaint failed to plead the essential terms and mutual assent required to create such a contract. And the fraudulent inducement claim failed because Santos did not allege any actual out-of-pocket loss, a necessary element under New York law for that claim.

## Disposition: Judgment of the District Court affirmed

The Second Circuit affirmed the District Court’s Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal in full. The court reviewed the dismissal de novo, considered the complaint and incorporated documents (including copyright registrations), and concluded that dismissal was proper at the pleading stage for both the copyright and state-law claims. The practical result is that Santos’s suit against Kimmel, ABC, and Disney was ended by affirmance.

## Key takeaways on fair use: Objective purpose controls; comedic commentary and critique can be transformative even where the defendant instigates content

The opinion teaches that courts focus on how a reasonable observer would perceive the secondary use when assessing transformativeness. The fact that a defendant solicited or instigated creation of the original material does not automatically preclude a finding of transformative use. Here, although Kimmel used fictitious names to request personalized Cameo videos, the segments broadcast on JKL clearly recontextualized those videos as objects of satire and commentary. The court relied on precedent emphasizing that subjective intent (of either the copyright owner or the user) is not the touchstone; instead, the analysis asks whether the secondary work adds new expressive content, purpose, or character that differs from the original.

This is an important instructional point for students: transformative use is an objective inquiry centered on the secondary work’s new purpose or meaning. Comedy and lampooning are paradigmatic examples of purposes that can be transformative because they alter the original’s communicative content.

## Market-harm clarification: Criticism/lampooning does not create a cognizable licensing market for the plaintiff

The court reiterated that the fourth fair-use factor (market effect) asks whether the secondary work acts as a market substitute for the original work — i.e., whether it usurps the original’s market — not whether it diminishes the original’s value by criticizing or parodying it. The opinion draws from Campbell and later Second Circuit authorities to explain that it is unlikely creators of imaginative or expressive works would license critical reviews or lampoons of their own output; therefore, market-harm theories premised on criticism or parody devaluing the original do not typically defeat fair use. In Santos’s case, the complaint itself showed no plausible market that the JKL segments could have supplanted, so the market-harm factor did not overcome the other considerations favoring fair use.

For students, this clarifies that alleged reputational or expressive harm from criticism is not the same thing as the kind of substitutive market harm that counts against fair use.

## Contract lessons: Third-party beneficiary hurdles under Illinois law; specificity and mutual assent required for implied contracts under New York law

The opinion provides concrete lessons about using platform terms in contract claims:

- Third-party beneficiary doctrine: Because Santos was not a party to Cameo’s Terms of Service, his direct breach-of-contract claim faced a significant obstacle under Illinois law — the law governing the Terms. The court pointed to the “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties” and required a near-explicit declaration in the contract that third parties may enforce it. The presence of express third-party beneficiary language elsewhere in the Terms reinforced that omission here was meaningful. This underscores that litigants trying to enforce platform agreements as nonparties must locate clear contractual language showing an intent to benefit and permit enforcement by third parties.

- Implied-in-fact contracts and mutual assent: For the related implied contract theory governed by New York law, the complaint failed because it did not allege essential terms or demonstrate a meeting of the minds. An implied-in-fact contract claim requires factual allegations showing offer, acceptance, consideration, and mutual assent to specific terms; conclusory assertions that the defendants “should have” followed the Terms are insufficient. The opinion shows that courts will dismiss implied contract claims that rest on speculation about nonparties’ obligations absent concrete facts establishing assent and defined contractual terms.

These points are especially relevant in cases involving interactions on multi-party platforms where the platform’s terms bind only some participants and where plaintiffs attempt to convert terms intended for platform users into rights enforceable by third parties.

## Fraud lesson: Necessity of pleading actual out-of-pocket loss, not reputational or expectancy harms

On fraudulent inducement under New York law, the court affirmed dismissal because Santos failed to allege actual out-of-pocket damages — the traditional measure required in New York for fraud claims. The opinion distinguishes between reputational or expectancy-type harms and the specific, tangible out-of-pocket loss New York law requires for a fraud claim to survive. Santos’s complaint did not allege that he suffered the kind of monetary loss traceable to the alleged misrepresentations that induced performance, so the fraudulent inducement claim could not proceed.

For students, this reinforces the lesson that pleading fraud requires plausible allegations of both actionable misrepresentation and actual, particularized economic loss caused by reliance; generalized harms or speculative damages are inadequate.

## Significance and instructional value

This opinion is useful pedagogically because it demonstrates how fair-use analysis can resolve a copyright case at the pleadings stage when the complaint and incorporated materials plainly show a transformative purpose and no plausible market usurpation. It also shows the interplay between federal copyright defenses and state-law contract and tort theories tied to platform terms: even if state-law claims are not preempted, they must still satisfy ordinary elements such as privity or third-party-beneficiary language, mutual assent for implied contracts, and concrete out-of-pocket damages for fraud. Together, these rulings illustrate practical pleading pitfalls—especially in litigation arising from platform-mediated interactions and comedic commentary—helpful for students learning to map fact patterns onto both copyright defenses and state-law pleading requirements.
