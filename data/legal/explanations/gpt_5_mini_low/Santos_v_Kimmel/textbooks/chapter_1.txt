Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 1: # Background, Facts, and Procedural Posture

This case pits former Congressman George Santos (the plaintiff and appellant) against a set of widely known media defendants: Jimmy Kimmel (the late‑night host), American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), which broadcasts Kimmel’s program, Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”), and ABC’s parent company, The Walt Disney Company (collectively the defendants or appellees). Santos sued after Kimmel obtained and publicly aired short personalized videos that Santos had recorded on the Cameo platform. The District Court dismissed Santos’s complaint under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6), and Santos appealed to the Second Circuit. The appeal focuses principally on whether the defendants’ use of those videos was protected by the fair use doctrine and whether Santos’s ancillary state‑law claims were adequately pleaded.

Factual core. The operative facts, as alleged in Santos’s complaint and reflected in materials the courts considered, are straightforward. Santos created and posted short, personalized videos on Cameo, a digital marketplace where content creators record paid messages on request. Using various fictitious names, Jimmy Kimmel submitted requests through Cameo for personalized videos from Santos. Santos fulfilled those requests and delivered the videos on the Cameo platform. Kimmel then incorporated the recorded videos into recurring televised segments on JKL titled “Will Santos Say It?”—segments that presented the Santos recordings as part of a mocking commentary series. The complaint itself describes the original tenor of Santos’s videos as conveying “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” while it characterizes the JKL segments as ridiculing Santos by highlighting his willingness to utter absurd statements for money.

Claims asserted. Santos sued in federal court asserting federal copyright infringement claims and several state‑law causes of action. The federal claim alleged that the defendants reproduced and publicly performed Santos’s copyrighted videos without authorization. The state‑law claims included breach of contract (premised on the Cameo Terms of Service), breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. Santos sought relief for the alleged unauthorized use and related harms.

District Court disposition. The District Court (Cote, J.) dismissed the complaint under Rule 12(b)(6). As to the copyright claims, the District Court concluded that defendants’ use of the Cameo videos on JKL was fair use as a matter of law at the pleading stage, and therefore the copyright counts failed to state a viable claim. For the state‑law claims, the District Court held alternatively that the contract‑based claims were preempted by the federal Copyright Act or, in the alternative, failed to state claims under applicable state law; the fraudulent inducement claim was dismissed for failure to allege the requisite out‑of‑pocket injury under New York law. Santos appealed.

Standard of review on appeal. The Second Circuit reviewed the District Court’s Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal de novo. In that posture the appellate court assumed the factual allegations in the complaint as true and asked whether those allegations, together with documents properly before the court, state a plausible claim for relief. The court reiterated the materials it may consider at the pleading stage: the allegations in the complaint, exhibits attached to the complaint, and documents incorporated by reference into the complaint. The court also noted its authority to take judicial notice of certain public records—most notably, copyright registrations published in the Copyright Office’s registry—and relied on that power here to establish facts about publication dates and registrations.

Judicial notice of registrations. The Second Circuit explicitly treated the Copyright Office entries as matters of which it could take judicial notice. Those registrations, which appear in the record and were cited by the parties, helped establish objective facts relevant to the fair use inquiry—most importantly that the videos were published on the same day they were created, a fact the court used in assessing the second and fourth fair use factors (nature of the work and market effect).

Appellate framing and issues presented. At the center of the appeal was whether fair use could be resolved against Santos at the pleading stage—i.e., whether the complaint itself, together with the incorporated materials and registrations, established fair use as a matter of law so that the copyright claims must be dismissed. The Second Circuit addressed that question by applying the statutory four‑factor fair use test set out in 17 U.S.C. § 107 and relevant precedents, focusing first on the “purpose and character” factor (and particularly on whether the defendants’ use was transformative). In parallel, the court considered whether Santos’s state‑law contract and fraud theories could survive dismissal on the pleadings. Although the District Court had relied in part on federal preemption to dispose of the contract claims, the Second Circuit resolved the appeal without deciding preemption: it concluded that, under the governing state‑law standards the District Court applied (Illinois law for the Cameo Terms of Service and New York law for the implied contract and fraud theories), Santos failed to allege the necessary elements of those claims in a nonconclusory and plausible manner.

Procedural outcome on appeal. Applying de novo review, the Second Circuit agreed that the copyright claims were barred by fair use at the pleadings stage and that the state‑law claims failed to state a claim under applicable state law (including failure to plead third‑party beneficiary/implied contract essentials and failure to allege actual out‑of‑pocket damages for fraudulent inducement). The court therefore affirmed the District Court’s dismissal in full.
