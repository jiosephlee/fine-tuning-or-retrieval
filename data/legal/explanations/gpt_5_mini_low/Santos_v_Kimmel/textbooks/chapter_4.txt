Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 4: # State Law Claims: Contract Theories and Fraud

This chapter explains why the Second Circuit affirmed the District Court’s dismissal of George Santos’s state-law claims—direct breach of contract (third‑party beneficiary theory), breach of an implied‑in‑fact contract, and fraudulent inducement—without deciding whether the Copyright Act preempted those claims. The court elected to avoid resolving preemption because it found each claim independently deficient as pleaded. Understanding the court’s analysis requires attention to (1) the procedural posture of avoiding preemption where a claim fails on the merits, and (2) the specific pleading rules and state‑law elements the court applied under the governing choice‑of‑law provisions.

## Preemption posture: affirming on failure‑to‑state‑a‑claim grounds

Although the District Court relied in part on federal copyright preemption to dismiss Santos’s contract claims, the Second Circuit declined to decide the preemption question. When a federal court can dispose of a case on an adequate independent ground—here, the plaintiff’s failure to plead the elements of state‑law claims—appellate courts often avoid addressing a potentially broader preemption issue. The panel therefore analyzed the state‑law claims under the applicable state standards (Illinois law for the Cameo Terms of Service and New York law for the implied contract and fraud claims) and affirmed dismissal because the complaint did not plausibly allege the necessary elements. This approach narrows the opinion’s holdings to pleading deficiencies rather than a ruling that the Copyright Act necessarily displaces Santos’s state claims.

## Direct breach of contract under the Cameo Terms of Service (Illinois law)

Santos alleged a breach of the Cameo Terms of Service (TOS). But he was not a party to the TOS; rather, the TOS governed the contractual relationship between Cameo and its users (including Kimmel). The controlling principle under Illinois law—applied because the TOS declared Illinois law governs—is a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” The Second Circuit relied on Illinois appellate authority stating that to allow third‑party enforcement, “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” In other words, a plaintiff who is not a contracting party must point to language or circumstances tantamount to an explicit third‑party‑beneficiary provision.

The court examined Santos’s complaint and the TOS and found no such express or practically express declaration. Indeed, the TOS itself contained explicit third‑party‑beneficiary language in other provisions—demonstrating that the drafter knew how to create enforceable third‑party rights when intended. That contrast undermined any inference that the parties to the TOS intended Santos to be an intended beneficiary. Because Santos identified no provisions that unequivocally conferred enforceable rights on nonparties, his direct breach claim failed as a matter of Illinois law.

Key takeaways:
- Nonparty beneficiaries face a high bar; courts look for express or effectively express intent to benefit third parties.
- The existence of explicit third‑party‑beneficiary language elsewhere in a contract weighs against finding implied third‑party rights where none are stated.

## Implied‑in‑fact contract under New York law

Santos also pleaded an implied‑in‑fact contract theory under New York law—arguing that Kimmel (or the defendants) should be bound by the Cameo TOS as a matter of tacit agreement. Under New York law, to state an implied‑in‑fact contract claim a plaintiff must plead the essential terms of the alleged contract and facts showing a “meeting of the minds” (i.e., mutual assent) sufficient to infer agreement and its scope.

The Second Circuit applied the New York standards and emphasized two pleading failures. First, the complaint did not allege the essential terms of any contract between Santos and the defendants. Courts require nonconclusory allegations specifying the material provisions on which liability is predicated; generic assertions that “a contract existed” are insufficient. Second, the complaint failed to plausibly allege a meeting of the minds that Kimmel would adhere to Cameo’s TOS. An implied contract claim cannot rest on a unilateral expectation or hope that a third party would follow another party’s contractual rules; there must be factual allegations showing reciprocal assent or circumstances from which assent can reasonably be inferred.

Because Santos’s allegations were conclusory and omitted the necessary specifics about terms and mutual assent, the court held his implied‑in‑fact contract claim was inadequately pleaded under New York law and therefore properly dismissed.

Key takeaways:
- Implied‑in‑fact contracts require allegations of essential terms and facts giving rise to mutual assent.
- Mere conclusory assertions of an agreement or reliance on a third party’s contract language are insufficient.

## Fraudulent inducement under New York law: out‑of‑pocket loss requirement

Santos’s final state claim accused the defendants of fraudulent inducement. New York law imposes particular requirements on fraud claims, including allegations of a misrepresentation, scienter, reasonable reliance, and damages. For fraudulent inducement claims specifically, New York follows the “out‑of‑pocket” rule articulated in Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney: the plaintiff must allege an actual pecuniary loss caused by the misrepresentation. The more recent decision in Connaughton v. Chipotle reaffirmed that plaintiffs must allege specific, actual out‑of‑pocket losses rather than speculative or nonpecuniary harms.

The Second Circuit agreed with the District Court that Santos failed to allege any actual pecuniary loss resulting from the alleged misrepresentations Kimmel made in obtaining the videos. Santos’s complaint did not plausibly connect any monetary harm to the defendants’ conduct in a way that satisfies New York’s out‑of‑pocket requirement. Consequently, the fraudulent inducement claim was also properly dismissed.

Key takeaways:
- New York fraudulent inducement claims require measurable, actual pecuniary loss (out‑of‑pocket).
- Allegations of reputational harm or nonpecuniary injury, without specific monetary loss tied to the fraud, will not satisfy the pleading standard.

## Disposition: dismissal of all state claims for failure to state a claim

Because each state‑law claim independently failed to state a claim under the applicable state standards—no plausible third‑party beneficiary claim under Illinois law, no adequately pleaded implied‑in‑fact contract under New York law, and no alleged out‑of‑pocket loss for fraudulent inducement—the Second Circuit affirmed the District Court’s dismissal of the state claims. The opinion left open the preemption issue, resolving the case on narrower pleading grounds. For students, this demonstrates a common appellate practice: courts will affirm on the simplest adequate ground—here, inadequate pleading—without reaching broader questions (like federal preemption) unless necessary.
