Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### What role does bad faith or deception play in fair use after Harper & Row?
Question:
Santos claimed Kimmel’s false identities on Cameo showed bad faith that should vitiate fair use, citing Harper & Row’s "good faith" language. The court says the Supreme Court has distanced itself from that doctrine and focuses instead on non-supplanting commentary. What is the current Second Circuit (and Supreme Court) doctrine on bad faith in fair use? Can deception ever tip factor one or four against fair use, and under what circumstances would misrepresentation matter?
Answer:
Short answer
After Harper & Row the Supreme Court and the Second Circuit do not treat an actor’s “bad faith” or deception as an independent, dispositive freestanding requirement of fair use. Courts now evaluate the statutory §107 factors—especially (1) purpose/character (is the use transformative?) and (4) market effect (does the use usurp a market or licensing opportunity)—and will consider deception only to the extent it bears on those factors (e.g., shows the use was non‑transformative or was intended to supplant a market). Santos v. Kimmel illustrates that approach: the Second Circuit acknowledged Harper & Row’s “good faith” language but explained the Court has “distanced itself” from that doctrine and looked instead to whether the use was commentary and not a market‑supplanting substitute.

How the doctrine works (doctrinal framework)
- Harper & Row: the Court famously said “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.” But later precedent (and the Second Circuit in Santos) has backed away from treating good faith as an independent dispositive element. Instead, current analysis asks whether the challenged use is transformative (factor 1) and whether it usurps a potential market (factor 4), with the remaining factors considered in context.
- Transformation/Objective inquiry: courts ask what a reasonable observer would view the secondary use to be (see Andy Warhol Found. v. Goldsmith, cited in Santos). A user’s subjective intent or the copyright owner’s stated purpose is not dispositive.
- Market‑supplanting focus: factor 4 asks whether the secondary work is a substitute for the original (usurps licensing markets), not merely whether it harms reputation or reduces value through criticism or parody (see Campbell; quoted in Santos and Hachette).

When deception can matter
Deception is relevant only insofar as it illuminates one of the statutory factors:

1) Deception that bears on factor one (purpose/character)
   - If the deception shows the secondary work’s apparent purpose is not commentary/criticism or otherwise not transformative to a reasonable observer, it can weigh against fair use. Example: if someone procures a work by pretending to want a compliment but instead uses it unchanged in a context that competes with the original, that undermines a claimed transformative purpose.
   - In Santos the court found the complaint itself showed the reasonable‑observer view was commentary (transformative), so the alleged deceit did not negate factor one.

2) Deception that bears on factor four (market effect)
   - If deception was used to obtain access to an unpublished or unreleased work or to obtain rights in a way that enables the defendant to usurp the original’s licensing market (the classic Harper & Row concern), that can tip factor four against fair use. Harper & Row itself involved unauthorized publication of an unpublished presidential memoir excerpt obtained in a context that interfered with the owner’s right to first publication/licensing.
   - If the deception has no plausible link to creating a market substitute (e.g., it merely facilitated obtaining material used for parody or commentary that would not be licensed), courts will not treat it as dispositive.

3) Deception that creates independent legal liability
   - Even if deception does not kill fair use, it may support other legal claims (fraud, contract, wiretap statutes, platform terms violations). Santos addressed those state claims separately and rejected them on pleading grounds—its fair‑use holding turned on the statutory factors, not an independent “bad faith” rule.

Practical rule of thumb
- Allegations of deceit are not per se fatal to fair use in the Second Circuit or under current Supreme Court precedent.
- Courts will ask: does the deception make the use non‑transformative or enable a competing market substitute that would have been licensable? If yes, deception can push factors 1 or 4 against fair use. If no—if the use is genuinely critical/transformative and not a market substitute—deception alone usually won’t vitiate fair use (as the Second Circuit held in Santos).

Key textual anchors from Santos v. Kimmel
- Santos recognized Harper & Row’s “good faith” language but observed the Supreme Court “has since distanced itself from this statement.”
- The court framed the inquiry around whether the complaint showed the defendants’ purpose was to “supplant” Santos’s commercially valuable right; because the complaint showed sarcastic criticism/commentary, factor one supported fair use.
- The court reiterated that market‑usurpation, not mere value diminution, drives factor four—citing Campbell and Hachette.

Conclusion
Bad faith or deception is not a separate trumping doctrine after Harper & Row; it matters only insofar as it informs the §107 factors—primarily whether the use is transformative and whether it usurps a licensable market. Deception can tip factor one or four against fair use when it meaningfully supports a non‑transformative purpose or an intent/ability to supplant the original’s market (for example, procuring unpublished material to publish as a substitute). But mere deceit unconnected to those statutory concerns will not automatically vitiate fair use under current Second Circuit and Supreme Court precedent.
