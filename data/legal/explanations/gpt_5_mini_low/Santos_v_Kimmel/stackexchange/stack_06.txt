Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why did Santos lack standing to sue on the Cameo Terms of Service under Illinois law?
Question:
The direct breach claim fails because Santos isn’t a party to the user Terms of Service (which govern Kimmel’s account) and Illinois law has a strong presumption against third-party beneficiaries absent an 'almost express' declaration. What specific language in typical platform TOS would be sufficient to make a non-party creator like Santos an intended third-party beneficiary? Does the presence of third-party-beneficiary language elsewhere in the TOS weigh against implying beneficiary status for other provisions as a matter of Illinois contract interpretation?
Answer:
Short answer
Santos lacked standing because under the Cameo Terms (governed by Illinois law) he was not a contracting party and the complaint did not plead anything like the “almost express” declaration Illinois requires to overcome the strong presumption against third‑party beneficiaries. The opinion explains that “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration,” and it relied on the fact that the Terms do contain express third‑party‑beneficiary language in some places to show that Cameo knew how to make a beneficiary express when it intended to do so. Santos’s complaint pointed to no comparable express language, so he could not sue for breach.

What language would be sufficient under Illinois law (based on the Court’s reasoning)
The Circuit’s description of the Illinois rule is best read functionally: to be an intended third‑party beneficiary a non‑party must be identifiable in the contract in language that shows the contracting parties intended to confer an enforceable right on that person or class. Language that would meet that standard would include, for example:

- an express grant of rights to the non‑party (by name or by clearly defined class), e.g. “This Agreement is made for the benefit of X and X shall have the right to enforce the obligations herein”; or
- an explicit statement that a particular provision “shall be enforceable” by a non‑party or a defined class, e.g. “Creators who upload content to Cameo are intended third‑party beneficiaries of Sections 5–7 and may enforce those provisions”; or
- language stating the providers’ promise is “for the benefit of” a defined class that unambiguously includes the non‑party and that the class “may bring an action to enforce” the promise.

In short, language that is practically an express declaration — naming the beneficiary or expressly conferring enforcement rights — is what Illinois (as the opinion cites Barry) requires.

Does explicit third‑party language elsewhere in the TOS cut against implying beneficiary status for other provisions?
Yes — and the opinion relied on precisely that point. The court noted that “other provisions of the Terms of Service do contain third‑party beneficiary language, indicating that when the parties to that agreement intended to allow provisions of the contract to be enforced by third parties, they said so expressly.” Under ordinary contract interpretation, the presence of express third‑party‑beneficiary clauses in some provisions suggests the parties knew how to create such rights and did not intend to create them where they omitted that language. That comparative drafting choice therefore weighs against implying beneficiary status for different provisions that lack comparable language.

Takeaway for pleading or drafting
- For a creator like Santos to plead (or for platform drafters to create) enforceable third‑party rights under Illinois law, the TOS should include express beneficiary language (naming or clearly defining the class and stating enforcement rights).  
- If a TOS contains express beneficiary clauses in some places but not in the provision a plaintiff relies on, courts are likely to treat that as evidence against implying rights for the omitted provisions, as the Santos opinion demonstrates.
