Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why did the fraudulent inducement claim fail for lack of out-of-pocket loss under New York law?
Question:
The panel affirms dismissal because Santos didn’t allege any out-of-pocket pecuniary loss, citing Lama Holding and Connaughton. If Santos spent time creating the videos and received payment, could he allege a net loss (e.g., time/value of services exceeding the price, or lost opportunities) that qualifies as out-of-pocket damages? Are reputational harms or diminution of the videos’ value cognizable in fraud, or are those barred as benefit-of-the-bargain or consequential damages in New York?
Answer:
Short answer: The fraudulent-inducement claim failed because under New York law a deceit plaintiff must plead a concrete, out‑of‑pocket pecuniary loss — not mere subjective harms, lost opportunities, or the “value” of time — and Santos pleaded none. The opinion (citing Lama Holding and Connaughton) treats the required loss as tangible economic injury, and finds Santos’s own allegations (he made the videos and was paid; the videos were published the same day) do not allege any such loss.

Why that rule controls
- Lama Holding v. Smith Barney establishes that New York measures damages for common‑law fraud by out‑of‑pocket loss (the actual pecuniary loss suffered) rather than the benefit‑of‑the‑bargain (the gain the plaintiff expected). The Second Circuit here applies that principle. 
- Connaughton reaffirmed that a fraud plaintiff must allege an actual, non‑speculative pecuniary loss — mere indignity, reputational injury, or a theoretical diminution in value is not enough unless tied to a concrete monetary loss.

How the opinion applies those principles to Santos
- The court observed that Santos was paid for the Cameo videos and that the videos were promptly published, and that he did not allege he suffered any measurable, out‑of‑pocket monetary loss from Kimmel’s conduct. On that basis the panel affirmed dismissal of the fraud claim for failure to plead the required pecuniary injury (Opinion ¶II, citing Lama Holding and Connaughton).

Can Santos plead a “net loss” (time/value of services exceeding the price, or lost opportunities)?
- Possibly — but only if pled as a concrete pecuniary loss. New York fraud damages require monetary loss. Alleging that the fee he received was less than the fair value of his time or services could state out‑of‑pocket loss if pleaded with enough factual detail (e.g., unreimbursed expenses, costs incurred in reliance, quantifiable difference between market/licensing value and what he received). Similarly, lost licensing or other economic opportunities can qualify if plausibly pleaded as actual, non‑speculative monetary harm (e.g., demonstrable lost contracts or licensing fees caused by defendants’ misrepresentations).
- Bare assertions that “I lost time” or “the price was too low,” without tying those claims to a calculable pecuniary injury, will not survive under Lama/Connaughton as applied in the opinion.

Are reputational harms or diminution of the videos’ value cognizable?
- Not as the out‑of‑pocket fraud damages the court required here. Reputational injury or an abstract “diminution in value” is not the type of direct pecuniary loss New York’s out‑of‑pocket rule demands, absent allegations converting reputation/diminution into measurable economic loss (for example, lost contracts, lost licensing revenue, or other specific financial harm caused by the reputational hit). The opinion’s fair‑use discussion (videos published the same day; no market harm alleged) also undercuts a claim that the segments usurped a licensing market or caused measurable market loss.

What Santos would need to plead to survive
- Specific, non‑conclusory allegations of actual monetary loss caused by the defendants’ misrepresentations: unreimbursed costs incurred because of the inducement, a quantifiable shortfall between what he received and what he would have received absent the fraud (with factual support), or identifiable lost economic opportunities directly traceable to the defendants’ conduct.
- Allegations tying reputational or diminution claims to concrete economic consequences (e.g., demonstrable lost sales, cancelled engagements, or lost licensing deals) rather than asserting reputational harm in the abstract.

Bottom line: Under the Second Circuit’s reading of Lama Holding and Connaughton, New York fraud requires a tangible out‑of‑pocket pecuniary loss. Santos’s complaint, which alleges he produced the videos and was paid and does not quantify any actual monetary loss tied to the alleged deceit, fails that pleading requirement.
