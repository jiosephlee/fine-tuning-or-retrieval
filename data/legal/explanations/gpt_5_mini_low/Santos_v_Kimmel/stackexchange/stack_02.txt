Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Does the fact that Kimmel instigated the Cameo scripts defeat transformativeness?
Question:
Santos argued Kimmel "instigated the creation" by submitting prompts under aliases, implying the secondary user designed the original purpose. The court rejects this, focusing on what a reasonable observer would perceive in the secondary use, and not on subjective intent (citing Warhol). Do courts ever consider who engineered the underlying content in assessing whether the later use is transformative, especially when the 'original' was created at the secondary user's direction? How does that square with cases like Campbell (parody) where the original wasn’t authored at the parodist’s behest?
Answer:
Short answer: No — at least under Santos v. Kimmel the fact that Kimmel “instigated” or prompted the creation of the Cameo videos does not defeat a finding of transformativeness. The Second Circuit rejected Santos’s argument that authorship or the engineer of the original purpose controls the fair‑use inquiry and instead applied the familiar “reasonable observer” / purpose‑and‑character test.

Why (doctrinally)
- Santos asked the court to treat Kimmel as the designer of the videos’ original purpose and argued that this undercuts any claim the later use was transformative. The court squarely rejected that approach. It held that whether a secondary use is transformative “turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user,” and cited the Supreme Court’s Warhol decision for the same principle. (Op. at pp. ___ (citing Warhol, 598 U.S. at 544).)
- The fair‑use “purpose and character” analysis focuses on whether the secondary use “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character” such that it does not merely supersede the original. The court found the JKL segments’ purpose — sarcastic criticism/commentary — different from the videos’ communicated persona (as alleged), and therefore the first factor strongly favored fair use. (Op. at § I.)

How that relates to situations like Campbell (parody)
- Campbell is consistent with this approach: the key question is the nature and purpose of the secondary use (e.g., parody/criticism), not whether the defendant engineered the original work. Santos tried to distinguish by saying he created the videos with the same ostensible purpose as Kimmel’s use, but the court responded that plaintiff’s and defendant’s subjective intents are not the controlling inquiry — the reasonable observer’s perception of the works is. The court therefore resolved the issue by comparing the original videos (as published) to the JKL segments and concluding the segments were transformative. (Op. at ¶¶ discussing factor one and citing Campbell on market effect.)

Bottom line
Under the Second Circuit’s ruling in Santos v. Kimmel, who “engineered” or prompted creation of the original content is not dispositive. Courts look to the objective character and purpose of the secondary use (how a reasonable observer would perceive it) to assess transformativeness, even when the secondary user played a role in creating the original.
