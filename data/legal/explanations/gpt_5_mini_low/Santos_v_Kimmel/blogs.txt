Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

## ‘Will Santos Say It?’ and the Reasonable Observer: Transformative Use After Warhol

Why this matters: The Second Circuit’s decision in Santos v. Kimmel (2d Cir. Oct. 6, 2025) is an important application of post-Warhol fair use doctrine to the very modern problem of user-created short-form media reused in broadcast commentary. At the core of the panel’s reasoning is a firm embrace of the “reasonable observer” test from Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith and a careful parsing of older language about “good faith” from Harper & Row. The opinion clarifies how courts should evaluate transformation when the secondary user (here, a late-night show) solicits original material from the purported copyright holder under a platform like Cameo, and it signals how allegations of deception will be treated in the fair-use balance going forward.

The facts and the Court’s holding, briefly: Santos, a former congressman, sold personalized videos through Cameo. Under fictitious names, Jimmy Kimmel requested videos, which Santos provided; Kimmel then broadcasted them on Jimmy Kimmel Live! in a recurring satirical segment, “Will Santos Say It?” The district court dismissed Santos’s complaint at the pleadings stage, holding the JKL segments were fair use; the Second Circuit affirmed. The panel concluded the use was transformative—aimed at commentary and ridicule of Santos’s willingness to say outrageous things for payment—and that the other fair-use factors (nature of the work, amount used, and market effect) did not weigh against defendants. The court also rejected Santos’s argument that Kimmel’s deceptive means of procuring the videos defeated the defense.

Why the “reasonable observer” test matters here: The Second Circuit foregrounds the Warhol principle that transformation turns on how a reasonable observer would perceive the secondary use, not on the subjective intentions of the creator or the reuser. Santos argued that Kimmel “instigated” the creation of the videos, and so the segments were not transformative because they supposedly fulfilled Kimmel’s original purpose. The court dismissed that contention: the complaint itself alleges the videos, as created by Santos, conveyed messages of encouragement and positivity. A reasonable observer, however, would view the JKL segments as using those videos for distinct critical and satirical ends—to lampoon Santos and to interrogate what he would say for money. That divergence between the original expressive content and the late-night commentary is the classic sign of transformation under Warhol and the Second Circuit’s precedents.

This reasoning has two practical implications. First, the court reaffirms that transformation is a public-perception inquiry. When the secondary work recasts or repurposes the original in a way that communicates a new message or purpose, transformation can be found even if the secondary user played a role in the original work’s creation. Second, content solicited from a copyright owner is not categorically untransformative because the solicitor “instigated” the work; the ultimate question remains how the reused material functions in the new context.

Harper & Row’s “good faith” language: alive in history, attenuated in application. Santos leaned on Harper & Row’s remark that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” arguing that deceptive procurement of the videos (using fake names) undercut fair use. The Second Circuit gave this argument short shrift for two reasons.

First, the panel notes that the Supreme Court has moved away from treating Harper & Row’s “good faith” statement as a talismanic requirement. Subsequent decisions and circuits have not treated alleged bad faith as an automatic fatal blow to fair use; rather, bad faith is a contextual consideration that can inform, but does not necessarily control, the first-factor transformation inquiry or the overall balance.

Second, on the record before the court—fact-pleading on the face of the complaint—the district court’s factual findings were that defendants used the videos for satire and commentary, not to supplant Santos’s market. The Second Circuit observed that the complaint itself negated a plausible claim that defendants intended to usurp Santos’s market in the videos. In short, even if deception was alleged, Santos’s pleadings did not show the kind of bad-faith purpose that would convert remedial commentary into an impermissible market-substituting use.

Doctrinal takeaways and caveats: The opinion does several useful doctrinal jobs. It reiterates that (1) transformation is outcome-focused and judged by reasonable perception; (2) solicitation of original material by a defendant does not foreclose a finding of transformation; and (3) allegations of deceit in obtaining copyrighted material do not automatically defeat fair use—bad faith can matter, but it must be plausibly tied to the statutory factors (especially whether the defendant intended to supplant the original’s market).

A few caveats. This is a pleading-stage disposition. The Court emphasized that the relevant videos and the JKL segments alone sufficed to decide fair use as a matter of law on the complaint; that is a narrow posture. Had the complaint alleged more particularized facts about intent, market harm, or other relevant circumstances, a different outcome might have been possible. Moreover, the court’s treatment of deception is careful but not absolute: if plaintiffs in future cases can plausibly allege that deception was undertaken to produce a commercially competing substitute or to leverage the material into the same market as the original, that allegation could carry weight in the fair-use calculus.

Market effect and the commercial angle: The Second Circuit’s discussion of the fourth factor follows conventional lines: courts look not to whether the secondary work merely undercuts the original’s value in some general sense, but whether it is a market substitute. Here, the court found it implausible that creators of “imaginative works” like Santos’s would license parody or satire of their own productions; therefore, there was no realistic licensing market for the type of use Kimmel made. That analysis tracks Campbell and later Second Circuit cases and will be familiar to practitioners, but it’s worth noting that in tightly commercial contexts—especially where the secondary use might plausibly supplant downstream licensing—market effect can decisively sway the balance.

Broader implications for media, satire, and platform-solicited content: Santos v. Kimmel sits at an intersection of celebrity, monetization platforms, and broadcast commentary. Platforms like Cameo create a hybrid marketplace of bespoke speech, and content creators may both monetize and repurpose that speech. The Second Circuit’s approach gives comfort to commentators and satirists who repurpose user-produced content for critical or comedic ends, even when that content was originally commissioned by the commentator in some form. It also suggests that plaintiffs must plead more than mere allegations of deceit to overcome fair use—they will need to tie deceptive procurement to a non-transformative purpose or to concrete market-harm allegations.

In short: Santos does not announce a broad license to solicit and reuse copyrighted material, but it does confirm that courts will assess transformation by asking what a reasonable observer would perceive, not by cataloging subjective motives. Allegations of bad faith remain relevant but are neither conclusive nor dispositive. For broadcasters, hosts, and satire-makers, the lesson is familiar and now reinforced: the new expressive context matters; the way the material is used and perceived will often determine the fair-use outcome. For copyright owners monetizing bespoke content, the decision signals that they should expect use in commentary to be scrutinized under the reasonable observer standard—and that contract and state-law remedies, not copyright, may be the more viable route when they object to such reuse.



## Fair Use at the Pleading Stage: The Second Circuit’s Growing Comfort with Early Dismissals

Why this matters: Santos v. Kimmel is the latest sign that the Second Circuit is increasingly willing to treat fair use as a dispositive legal question at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage when the plaintiff’s own pleadings and documents incorporated into the complaint make the key facts plain. For media defendants facing content-based suits, Santos reinforces that early dismissal is a realistic outcome where the alleged secondary use is unmistakably commentary or parody and the complaint — together with attached materials and judicially noticed registrations — undermines any plausible market-harm theory.

What the court did (and why it held): The panel affirmed dismissal of George Santos’s copyright claims because, on the face of the complaint and exhibits, Jimmy Kimmel’s use of Santos’s Cameo videos was squarely within fair use. The opinion applies the familiar four-factor framework of 17 U.S.C. § 107 and leans heavily on the transformative-use inquiry under the “purpose and character” factor. Drawing on Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith and more recent Second Circuit decisions (TCA Television, Cariou, Hachette), the court treated the question as one a reasonable observer can resolve without discovery: Kimmel recontextualized brief, self‑published cameos into a recurring mocking segment with an expressive purpose different from the original videos’ claimed inspirational content. The court also disposed of arguments about bad faith and market harm, finding the complaint inconsistent with an allegation that defendants sought to usurp any licensing market and noting that parody/criticism traditionally sits outside the realm of realistic licensing markets.

Procedure and proof: pleadings, exhibits, and judicial notice
Santos is procedural as much as substantive. The opinion underscores three tools that make fair-use dismissal at the pleading stage possible:

- The court repeatedly relied on the complaint’s own allegations about the videos’ content and purpose. That is, where plaintiffs describe both their own work and the secondary use in ways that foreclose a plausible claim of nontransformative copying, dismissal follows.
- The panel considered materials incorporated by reference and attached exhibits — here the actual Cameo videos (or transcript descriptions), the JKL segments, and the Cameo copyright registrations. Those materials let the court compare the works directly without reaching beyond the complaint.
- The court took judicial notice of copyright registrations in the Copyright Office. Registrations can establish publication dates and other formalities that bear on the fair-use factors (e.g., timing, whether the work was published), and the Second Circuit treats them as proper for notice at the pleading stage.

How precedent fits: continuity, not a break
Santos sits comfortably in a line of Second Circuit cases that have permitted early resolution of fair use where contextual facts are clear. TCA Television and Cariou recognized that transformation can be a legal conclusion when the secondary work’s character is apparent from the face of the works. Hachette (and the circuit’s more recent work) shows the court’s willingness to weigh market-harm arguments early when the complaint negates realistic licensing markets for critical or parodic uses. Santos adds nuance: the court reiterates that the “reasonable observer” — not the subjective intent of parties — controls the transformation inquiry, following the Supreme Court’s approach in Goldsmith.

Implications for plaintiffs
Santos is a cautionary tale. When a plaintiff’s own description of the works or attachments to the complaint reveal an obvious critical or parodic secondary use, courts may find fair use as a matter of law. Plaintiffs who want to avoid an early dismissal therefore must (a) plead facts that keep open a nontransformative characterization of the defendant’s use; (b) identify concrete, plausible markets that the defendant’s use could usurp; and (c) be wary that attaching the allegedly infringing material (or the defendant’s published segments) can enable the court to resolve transformation without discovery. In short, careful pleading matters: avoid admitting facts that foreclose alternative characterizations of the use and plead specific market-harm allegations grounded in realistic licensing contexts.

Implications for defendants and their litigators
For media defendants, Santos is an invitation to move early when the secondary use is plainly commentary, parody, or criticism. Well-drafted Rule 12 motions that incorporate or attach the underlying works and relevant registrations stand a good chance of success. Defendants should preserve documentary evidence (published segments, timestamps, registrations) that permit a court to view the works side-by-side at the pleading stage. The opinion also signals the tactical value of invoking judicial notice of registrations to establish publication and timing, which can be decisive on the fourth factor.

Doctrinal limits and potential downstream effects
Santos does not erase the fact-intensive nature of fair use. Many fair-use disputes will still require discovery because the transformative character or market effects are not obvious on the face of the works or where extrinsic factual disputes (e.g., about intent, context, or audience perception) matter. But Santos narrows the zone of cases that must proceed past pleading: where the alleged secondary use is manifestly critical and the complaint either admits or attaches the materials that demonstrate transformation and lack of market usurpation, courts can and will decide fair use as a legal matter.

One noteworthy doctrinal emphasis in Santos is the court’s reliance on the “reasonable observer” standard and apparent willingness to discount the plaintiff’s asserted original purpose when a plausible, differing interpretation is supported by the works themselves. That aligns the Second Circuit with the Supreme Court’s recent doctrinal posture in Goldsmith and suggests future panels will be cautious about letting subjective intent (on either side) defeat an otherwise clear finding of transformation.

Bottom line
Santos v. Kimmel continues a trend: the Second Circuit is increasingly comfortable resolving fair use at the pleading stage when the complaint and attached or judicially noticed materials make transformation and lack of market harm apparent. Plaintiffs must plead with care and avoid courtroom-ready admissions that enable early dismissal; defendants should not hesitate to test fair use early when the record is similarly clean. The decision doesn’t make fair-use adjudication easier across the board, but it sharpens the procedural battleground: in copyright litigation, the content you put into the complaint — and what you attach for the court to see — can determine whether the case ever gets off the ground.



# Markets, Mockery, and Cameo: Why the Fourth Factor Cut Against Santos

## Why this case matters

Santos v. Kimmel is another useful Second Circuit application of fair use to modern media mash-ups: a public figure sold brief personalized videos on a commercial platform, a late-night show repurposed those videos for a mocking recurring segment, and the court was asked to decide whether that use was copyright infringement. At first glance the dispute reads like an ugly collision of celebrity commerce and satire. But the doctrinal heart of the opinion is familiar and significant: the Court reaffirms that the critical inquiry for the fourth fair-use factor is market substitution — not merely whether the secondary use “devalues” the original work — and that mocking, critical uses are unlikely to usurp a licensable market for the original creative content. That determination proved fatal to Santos’s claim at the pleading stage.

Understanding how the court reached that conclusion matters beyond this one dispute. We are increasingly seeing short-form, user-requested content (Cameo-style videos, TikTok shout-outs, personalized greetings) reused in commentary-driven media. Santos confirms that courts will not reflexively treat such secondary uses as market harms simply because they may sour the commercial appeal of the original to its creator. The decision also illuminates how the other fair-use factors — nature and amount — fit into these modern, short-form contexts.

## How the court reasoned

The Second Circuit affirmed dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6), agreeing with the district court that fair use bars Santos’s copyright claims. The opinion walks through the four statutory factors, but places particular weight on the first and fourth factors as dispositive.

For the first factor — purpose and character — the court focused on transformativeness. Even though Kimmel used the Cameo platform to obtain the videos, the broadcast segments served a different communicative purpose: criticism, mockery, and commentary about Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. The court reiterates a settled point from Warhol and Andy Warhol Found. v. Goldsmith: subjective intent of the user or the author is not the determinative metric; rather, the reasonable-observer test controls. Santos’s complaint itself described the videos’ original expressive content as conveying hope and encouragement; Kimmel’s segments recast those same clips as objects of ridicule — a paradigmatic transformative use designed to comment on Santos’s conduct.

On the fourth factor — market effect — the court relied on the familiar and important distinction drawn in Campbell v. Acuff-Rose: the inquiry is whether the secondary work usurps the market for the original by acting as a substitute, not whether the parody diminishes the original’s value. The court emphasized that creators of imaginative works rarely, if ever, would license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions; thus skeptical licensing markets are not the kind of cognizable market harm the statute protects. The complaint itself undermined any plausible claim of market usurpation — the videos were one-off, published on the day they were made, and the broadcast segments were not reasonably substitutable for the Cameo product. Accordingly, the fourth factor favored fair use.

The opinion also addressed the second (nature) and third (amount) factors and found them consistent with a fair-use outcome. The videos were published work (diminishing the protective force of the second factor), and the amount taken was limited to what was necessary for the commentary to succeed — brief clips used to make the joke intelligible. Taken together — and with the first and fourth factors strongly supporting transformation and no market usurpation — the court held that fair use could be resolved in favor of defendants at the pleading stage.

## Why the fourth factor is decisive here

Two interlocking points make the fourth factor dispositive in cases like this.

First, market substitution is a functional test: it asks whether the secondary use serves as a meaningful economic substitute for the original. A late-night television segment that stitches together brief Cameo videos to lampoon a subject has a different audience expectation and use-pattern than an individual purchasing a personalized Cameo greeting. Even if the segment reduces Santos’s reputational or commercial desirability for future Cameo gigs, that reputational devaluation is not equivalent to the secondary work offering the same good in place of the original. The court follows Campbell’s observation that parody and critical uses are unlikely to have a sensible licensing market — the creators of the original typically would not license commentary lampooning their work.

Second, practical realities about the market for these videos matter. Cameo-style clips are bespoke, paid-for messages. They are transactional, tied to the celebrity’s willingness to participate. The court correctly recognized that permitting a copyright owner to convert reputational effects into a cognizable licensing harm would substantially broaden the fourth factor’s reach, threatening to chill critical commentary everywhere. If every broadcast that mocked a personalized video were treated as a substitute that damages a market, parody and journalistic criticism would face a legal regime inviting expensive suits and uncertain liability.

This is not to say market effects are always irrelevant when satire or criticism is involved. If a secondary use were to functionally replace a licensable market — for example, if a television program rebroadcasted entire personalized videos as an alternative product consumers would buy instead of the originals, or if the secondary use made available a competing compilation that purchasers would pay for in place of commissioning original Cameo messages — the fourth factor might cut the other way. But those are empirically distinct scenarios from what the Santos complaint alleged and the JKL segments presented.

## The role of the nature and amount factors here

The opinion’s treatment of the second and third factors is worth noting because those factors sometimes get short shrift in popularized accounts of fair use.

On the second factor (nature of the work), the court placed weight on the fact that Santos’s videos were published on the day of creation. Publication weakens the copyright holder’s claim to protection against repurposing for commentary; unpublished works enjoy heightened protection precisely because the author’s right to control first public appearance is an important interest. The registration materials and complaint made publication status clear, so the second factor did not weigh strongly against fair use.

On the third factor (amount and substantiality), the Court applied the familiar balancing test: use only as much as necessary for the transformative purpose. Late-night mockery needs little more than short clips to make the joke intelligible. The segments did not, on their face, reproduce more of the videos than needed. That limited, purposive copying reinforced the finding of transformative purpose and diminished the likelihood of market substitution.

Taken together, these factors show how the fair-use calculus coheres in short-form commentary contexts: published, short, quote-like uses designed to comment or criticize will often clear the fair-use bar.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Santos v. Kimmel reaffirms and operationalizes several doctrinal lines that matter going forward. First, it reinforces Campbell’s market-substitution lens for factor four and echoes the Supreme Court’s insistence that courts consider what a “reasonable observer” would think about purpose and transformation. That synthesis guards against elevating an author’s speculative or reputational harms into statutory market harms.

Second, the opinion underscores that fair use can be decided at the motion-to-dismiss stage when the pleadings and incorporated documents plainly establish the relevant facts — published status, briefness of use, and the secondary work’s clearly different communicative purpose. That procedural point matters: permitting early resolution avoids protracted discovery and the chilling litigation costs that can suppress commentary.

Third, the decision may influence how content creators think about monetizing short, personalized works. While creators retain remedies in contract and platform governance, they cannot necessarily weaponize copyright law against critical reuse that does not, in realistic terms, replace the market for the original. This tension pushes creators toward strengthening contractual protections (platform terms, bespoke licenses restricting reuse) if they want to control downstream uses, though the opinion also shows the limits of implied-contract claims when third-party beneficiaries are not clearly contemplated.

Finally, media outlets and commentators should take reassurance: using brief clips from commercially distributed personalized content to make a critical point is likely to be viewed through a fair-use-friendly lens, especially where the secondary use is plainly transformative and not a substitute product.

## A final thought

Copyright doctrine in the digital age must balance creators’ economic interests with robust protection for commentary and criticism. Santos v. Kimmel is a predictable, sensible application of that balance. It shows the courts are attentive to market realities and wary of letting copyright doctrine become a backdoor for silencing mockery or journalistic critique. For creators frustrated by mockery, the decision points toward contract law and platform policy as the more promising — and appropriate — avenues for control, not copyright suits premised on generalized reputational harm.



## Platform Terms Won’t Save You: The Collapse of Santos’s State-Law Claims

Why this matters: The Second Circuit’s decision in Santos v. Kimmel (2d Cir. Oct. 6, 2025) confirms an important limitation for public figures and creators who hope to use platform user agreements as a backstop against unwanted reuse or repurposing of content. Even though the court affirmed dismissal of the copyright claims on fair-use grounds, the more instructive part of the opinion is how each of Santos’s state-law theories—direct breach of the Cameo Terms of Service as a third‑party beneficiary, an implied-in-fact contract theory, and fraudulent inducement—failed at the pleading stage. The ruling underscores that platform terms do not magically create enforceable private rights for non-contracting bystanders, and that common-law contract and fraud doctrines still require traditional, often stringent, elements.

What the court held (in short): After agreeing that Kimmel’s broadcast use of Santos’s Cameo videos was protected by fair use, the Second Circuit affirmed dismissal of Santos’s state-law claims. The court explained that (1) Santos cannot enforce Cameo’s Terms of Service as a third‑party beneficiary under the governing law because there is no “express declaration” in the contract conferring rights on non‑contracting users; (2) his implied-in-fact contract claim under New York law failed for lack of alleged essential terms and any plausible “meeting of the minds” with the defendants; and (3) his fraudulent inducement claim failed because Santos did not plead the requisite out-of-pocket loss under New York law.

Walking through the reasoning

Third‑party beneficiary theory. Santos tried to make the Cameo Terms of Service, which govern the platform relationship between Cameo and purchasers/users, into a sword he could use against downstream users who obtained videos through the platform. The court disposed of that idea under Illinois law (which governs the Terms) by citing the well-settled presumption against conferring benefits on noncontracting third parties. The opinion emphasizes that, when drafters intend third-party enforcement, they typically say so expressly; here the Terms include clauses that expressly name third-party beneficiaries in other contexts, suggesting that the absence of such language for the provisions Santos invoked was intentional. That textual gap dooms an attempted third‑party beneficiary claim.

Implied‑in‑fact contract claim. The Second Circuit also rejected Santos’s fallback that some form of implied agreement existed between him and the defendants (ABC/the show). Under New York law the complaint was required to plead the essential terms of the alleged contract and facts showing mutual assent. The court found only conclusory allegations about an implied deal and insufficient factual detail about what the supposed promise was, when it was made, and how the parties manifested assent. Without that particularity, an implied-in-fact contract cannot survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion.

Fraudulent inducement and out‑of‑pocket loss. Finally, Santos’s fraud claim—that he was tricked into making videos under false pretenses—fell victim to New York’s requirement that a plaintiff allege actual out‑of‑pocket damages. The complaint did not plausibly identify any such loss traceable to the defendants’ conduct; the court relied on controlling New York precedent holding that speculative or non-economic harms will not suffice.

Analytical perspective: sensible doctrinal guardrails

The court’s approach is doctrinally conservative and pragmatically sensible. Platform Terms of Service are ubiquitous and often bilateral (platform ↔ user). Allowing noncontracting third parties to enforce such terms based on thin allegations would radically expand private enforcement and inject uncertainty into commonplace internet transactions. The Second Circuit’s insistence on either express third‑party beneficiary language or clear factual allegations of mutual assent preserves contract doctrine’s role in policing promises while preventing an end‑run around contractual privity doctrines.

Two features of the opinion deserve emphasis. First, the court reads the presence of express third‑party beneficiary clauses elsewhere in the Terms as evidence that the drafters knew how to create enforceable third‑party rights and simply did not do so for the provisions at issue. That context-driven contract interpretation aligns with ordinary contract principles but also gives drafters—platforms and users—notice about how to draft if they want third‑party enforcement. Second, the court’s dismissal of the implied‑in‑fact theory underscores that courts will not fill gaps with inferences absent concrete allegations of what was promised and how assent was reached. In other words, equitable intuition will not supplant the pleading requirements that protect defendants from speculative litigation.

Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Platforms and creators: The decision will embolden platforms and downstream users who repurpose user-generated content—especially in contexts where commentary, criticism, or parody is involved—because it curtails a likely avenue for claims that try to convert platform terms into broad rights for absent third parties. Platforms that want to empower content creators to police third-party uses should draft explicit third‑party beneficiary provisions or licensing assurances in a way that creates enforceable rights for the author or subject of content. Absent that drafting, creators cannot rely on a passive expectation that terms will be enforced by courts.

Public figures and subjects of content: Public figures like Santos should take away a pragmatic lesson: if you want contractual protection against certain downstream uses (for example, representation of a video being “private” or “not for broadcast”), you need an express agreement with the party who will control the distribution or a licensing arrangement that creates direct privity. Platforms’ boilerplate invites many expectations, but the law will often resist treating those expectations as enforceable by outsiders.

Litigation strategy: Plaintiffs facing an unfavorable copyright landscape (where fair use is likely) may be tempted to pursue state-law contract or tort theories tied to platform terms. Santos demonstrates how that strategy can fail at the pleading stage unless the complaint marshals concrete contractual language, clear evidence of assent, and traceable damages for fraud. Counsel should therefore assess early whether facts can support a plausible claim of enforceable contract rights or whether the case is better framed around copyright or statutory claims where the posture is different.

Free-speech and public-interest considerations: Though the opinion affirms fair use on the copyright claims (not the focus of this post), the disposition of the state-law claims dovetails with free-speech interests. If platform terms were routinely judicially enforceable by third parties to suppress critical or satirical reuse, that might chill commentary and undermine the fair-use balance. This ruling keeps a higher bar for private-law remedies that could otherwise be used to constrain broadcast criticism.

Practical takeaways

- Don’t assume platform terms are your shield. If you want enforceable rights against downstream users (including broadcasters), secure express contractual promises or licenses from the parties who will control distribution.

- Draft expressly if you mean to confer third‑party rights. Platforms that want creators—or subjects—to have enforceable protections should include clear third‑party beneficiary language and state the scope of the enforceable rights.

- Plead with particulars. Implied-in-fact contract and fraud claims require concrete allegations about essential terms, the manifestation of assent, and actual out-of-pocket loss; boilerplate allegations will not survive Rule 12 scrutiny.

- Consider licensing, not litigation. For creators concerned about downstream commercial exploitation, negotiated licenses that set permitted uses and remedies are a more reliable tool than relying on platform terms or post hoc litigation.

Conclusion

Santos v. Kimmel is a reminder that common-law contract and fraud doctrines retain their traditional boundaries even in the messy ecosystem of internet platforms. Where creators or public figures lack privity with platform users, courts will not casually transmute platform Terms of Service into enforceable private rights without clear textual or factual support. For those seeking to control how their content is used, thoughtful contracting and explicit licensing—not hopes about boilerplate terms—remain the most effective route.
