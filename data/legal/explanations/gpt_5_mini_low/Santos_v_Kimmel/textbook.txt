Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Background, Facts, and Procedural Posture

This case pits former Congressman George Santos (the plaintiff and appellant) against a set of widely known media defendants: Jimmy Kimmel (the late‑night host), American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), which broadcasts Kimmel’s program, Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”), and ABC’s parent company, The Walt Disney Company (collectively the defendants or appellees). Santos sued after Kimmel obtained and publicly aired short personalized videos that Santos had recorded on the Cameo platform. The District Court dismissed Santos’s complaint under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6), and Santos appealed to the Second Circuit. The appeal focuses principally on whether the defendants’ use of those videos was protected by the fair use doctrine and whether Santos’s ancillary state‑law claims were adequately pleaded.

Factual core. The operative facts, as alleged in Santos’s complaint and reflected in materials the courts considered, are straightforward. Santos created and posted short, personalized videos on Cameo, a digital marketplace where content creators record paid messages on request. Using various fictitious names, Jimmy Kimmel submitted requests through Cameo for personalized videos from Santos. Santos fulfilled those requests and delivered the videos on the Cameo platform. Kimmel then incorporated the recorded videos into recurring televised segments on JKL titled “Will Santos Say It?”—segments that presented the Santos recordings as part of a mocking commentary series. The complaint itself describes the original tenor of Santos’s videos as conveying “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” while it characterizes the JKL segments as ridiculing Santos by highlighting his willingness to utter absurd statements for money.

Claims asserted. Santos sued in federal court asserting federal copyright infringement claims and several state‑law causes of action. The federal claim alleged that the defendants reproduced and publicly performed Santos’s copyrighted videos without authorization. The state‑law claims included breach of contract (premised on the Cameo Terms of Service), breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. Santos sought relief for the alleged unauthorized use and related harms.

District Court disposition. The District Court (Cote, J.) dismissed the complaint under Rule 12(b)(6). As to the copyright claims, the District Court concluded that defendants’ use of the Cameo videos on JKL was fair use as a matter of law at the pleading stage, and therefore the copyright counts failed to state a viable claim. For the state‑law claims, the District Court held alternatively that the contract‑based claims were preempted by the federal Copyright Act or, in the alternative, failed to state claims under applicable state law; the fraudulent inducement claim was dismissed for failure to allege the requisite out‑of‑pocket injury under New York law. Santos appealed.

Standard of review on appeal. The Second Circuit reviewed the District Court’s Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal de novo. In that posture the appellate court assumed the factual allegations in the complaint as true and asked whether those allegations, together with documents properly before the court, state a plausible claim for relief. The court reiterated the materials it may consider at the pleading stage: the allegations in the complaint, exhibits attached to the complaint, and documents incorporated by reference into the complaint. The court also noted its authority to take judicial notice of certain public records—most notably, copyright registrations published in the Copyright Office’s registry—and relied on that power here to establish facts about publication dates and registrations.

Judicial notice of registrations. The Second Circuit explicitly treated the Copyright Office entries as matters of which it could take judicial notice. Those registrations, which appear in the record and were cited by the parties, helped establish objective facts relevant to the fair use inquiry—most importantly that the videos were published on the same day they were created, a fact the court used in assessing the second and fourth fair use factors (nature of the work and market effect).

Appellate framing and issues presented. At the center of the appeal was whether fair use could be resolved against Santos at the pleading stage—i.e., whether the complaint itself, together with the incorporated materials and registrations, established fair use as a matter of law so that the copyright claims must be dismissed. The Second Circuit addressed that question by applying the statutory four‑factor fair use test set out in 17 U.S.C. § 107 and relevant precedents, focusing first on the “purpose and character” factor (and particularly on whether the defendants’ use was transformative). In parallel, the court considered whether Santos’s state‑law contract and fraud theories could survive dismissal on the pleadings. Although the District Court had relied in part on federal preemption to dispose of the contract claims, the Second Circuit resolved the appeal without deciding preemption: it concluded that, under the governing state‑law standards the District Court applied (Illinois law for the Cameo Terms of Service and New York law for the implied contract and fraud theories), Santos failed to allege the necessary elements of those claims in a nonconclusory and plausible manner.

Procedural outcome on appeal. Applying de novo review, the Second Circuit agreed that the copyright claims were barred by fair use at the pleadings stage and that the state‑law claims failed to state a claim under applicable state law (including failure to plead third‑party beneficiary/implied contract essentials and failure to allege actual out‑of‑pocket damages for fraudulent inducement). The court therefore affirmed the District Court’s dismissal in full.

# Fair Use Framework and Pleading-Stage Adjudication

This chapter explains how the Second Circuit applied the fair use doctrine in Santos v. Kimmel and why the court resolved fair use at the Rule 12(b)(6) pleading stage. It focuses on the statutory factors of 17 U.S.C. § 107, the centrality of the transformative-use inquiry (and its objective/“reasonable observer” focus), the contextual and flexible nature of fair use, and the limited record the court relied upon — namely, the original Cameo videos, the Jimmy Kimmel Live! (JKL) segments that aired them, and the copyright registrations. Understanding these elements will help you see why a court can dismiss a copyright claim on fair use grounds before discovery when the complaint and the works themselves show the defense plainly applies.

## Fair use as an affirmative defense that furthers copyright’s goals

Fair use is an affirmative defense to copyright infringement; it permits uses that would otherwise be infringing when refusing permission would unduly restrain the creative and communicative purposes the Copyright Act aims to encourage. The Santos opinion frames fair use as a tool that avoids “rigid application of the Copyright Act when it would stifle the very creativity the Act is meant to promote.” That framing informs the Court’s willingness to consider fair use early in litigation: where a secondary use is plainly within the scope of fair use, the doctrine protects the secondary user's expressive activity (here, televised commentary and ridicule) even though the activity employs copyrighted material created by another.

## Section 107 factors — the familiar four-part inquiry

Section 107 sets out four non-exclusive factors courts weigh in fair use determinations: (1) the purpose and character of the use; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used; and (4) the effect of the use on the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. Santos reiterates that these factors are to be applied flexibly and contextually rather than as rigid formulae. In the opinion the Court examined each factor, but it emphasized the first — purpose and character — as the most probative here given the clear communicative purpose of the JKL segments.

## Transformative use and the reasonable-observer standard

Within the first factor, the Second Circuit centers analysis on whether the secondary use is transformative: does it merely supersede the original or instead add something new with a different purpose or character? Santos v. Kimmel follows the line of Second Circuit and Supreme Court authority holding that the transformative inquiry is primarily an objective question — what a reasonable observer would perceive — not a test of the parties’ subjective intent. The opinion cites Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith to emphasize that courts should not let the subjective intent of the user or the copyright holder determine the purpose of the secondary use. Applying that standard, the Court concluded that a reasonable observer would see the Cameo videos as original expressions conveying positivity and encouragement, whereas Kimmel’s repurposing of those videos on JKL plainly added critical and satirical commentary about Santos’s alleged willingness to say absurd things for money. That change in communicative purpose supported a finding of transformative use under the first factor.

## Flexibility of fair use and context-specific analysis

Santos underscores fair use’s contextual flexibility: the same use might be transformative in one setting and not in another depending on how the material is employed and presented. The Court reiterated that fair use can be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” when the complaint and the works themselves make the nature of the reuse evident. In other words, courts do not apply a single, mechanical test; they weigh the statutory factors in light of the specific expressive relationship between the original and secondary works. In Santos the satire and commentary evident in the JKL segments weighed heavily in favor of fair use.

## Pleading-stage resolution: when fair use is clear from the complaint and the works

A critical procedural point in Santos is the Court’s disposition of the copyright claims at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage. Normally fair use is an affirmative defense, and factual disputes may make summary judgment or trial appropriate. But the Second Circuit has long held that a fair use defense may be resolved on a motion to dismiss when fair use is apparent from the complaint and the works themselves. The opinion cites precedent (e.g., TCA Television Corp. and Cariou v. Prince) to show that when the original and secondary works — together with the complaint’s factual allegations and any properly incorporated documents — plainly demonstrate fair use, the court need not wait for discovery. In Santos the complaint expressly described the nature of the original videos, the way Kimmel republished them, and the communicative aim of the JKL segments; those allegations, paired with the videos themselves, made fair use clear as a matter of law.

## Permissible record at the pleading stage: videos and registrations

When deciding a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal that rests on fair use, courts must limit themselves to materials properly before them: the complaint’s factual allegations, documents attached to or incorporated in the complaint, and things of which the court may take judicial notice. Santos illustrates that approach. The Second Circuit considered the original Cameo videos and the JKL segments (either attached, incorporated, or subject to judicial notice) because those works are central to the fair use analysis and were before the court. The opinion also relied on the publicly available copyright registrations in the Copyright Office’s registry to establish that the works were published the same day they were created — a factual point relevant to the second and fourth factors (nature and market effect). By using only those materials, the Court avoided factual disputes inappropriate for a pleading-stage decision and nevertheless reached a definitive fair use holding.

## How these points come together in Santos v. Kimmel

Applied to the facts of Santos, the Court’s framework produced a straightforward result. The first-factor inquiry — whether Kimmel added new expression and meaning — weighed strongly for fair use because the JKL segments used the Cameo videos as fodder for satirical commentary, a different purpose than the motivational intent Santos allegedly had when making them. The Court rejected Santos’s reliance on Kimmel’s alleged deceit (using fictitious names) to defeat fair use: even accepting the complaint’s allegations about Kimmel’s conduct, the complaint itself showed no intent by defendants to supplant Santos’s market or to act in a way that would negate the fair use analysis. The Court also found no plausible market-harm theory because the JKL segments did not serve as a market substitute for the original videos. Because the complaint and the works made these points clear, the Court held that fair use barred the copyright claims at the pleading stage and affirmed the District Court’s dismissal.

In sum, Santos demonstrates when and why a court can decide fair use on a motion to dismiss: where the complaint’s allegations plus the original and secondary works (and any judicially noticeable registrations) make clear that the statutory factors — particularly transformation of purpose — favor the user. For students learning to read such opinions, the key takeaways are the primacy of the transformative inquiry evaluated objectively, the contextual nature of fair use, and the narrow but sufficient record that may justify an early, dispositive ruling.

# Applying the Fair Use Factors to Kimmel’s Broadcasts

This chapter explains how the Second Circuit applied the four statutory fair use factors to the videos that Congressman Santos created and to the way Jimmy Kimmel used them on his television show. The court affirmed dismissal of Santos’s copyright claims at the pleading stage because, viewing the complaint and incorporated materials, Kimmel’s broadcast use was a paradigmatic instance of fair use. To follow the court’s reasoning, you should keep two things in mind: the fair use inquiry is multi-factor and flexible, and the most important consideration here was whether Kimmel’s use was transformative when viewed objectively by a reasonable observer.

## Purpose and character: transformative commentary on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money

The court began with the first statutory factor—purpose and character—which it treated primarily as a question of transformation. Transformation asks whether the secondary use “merely supersedes” the original or instead “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character.” The District Court found, and the Second Circuit agreed, that Kimmel’s segments served a distinct communicative purpose: they used the personalized Cameo videos in a recurring, mocking television bit titled “Will Santos Say It?” The purpose of the JKL segments was to comment on and lampoon Santos’s apparent willingness to say outrageous things for money, not to replicate or replace the original videos’ messages.

Critically, the complaint itself described the original videos in emotive terms—conveying “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.” The court treated that description as establishing the original communicative character of the works. Because Kimmel’s broadcasts reframed the same footage for a different end (satire/criticism directed at Santos), the use added a new expressive meaning and thus was transformative for fair use purposes. The court concluded that the first factor therefore strongly favored defendants.

## Objective lens: the reasonable observer standard and the “designer” argument

Santos argued that Kimmel “instigated” the videos by posing scripted lines through Cameo and thus was, in effect, the designer of the originals’ purpose; on that account, the use could not be transformative. The court rejected this line of reasoning because transformation is judged objectively: what matters is how a reasonable observer would perceive the secondary use, not the subjective intent of the copyright owner or the secondary user. The Supreme Court and the Second Circuit emphasize that courts should not let the parties’ self-serving characterizations override the ordinary observer’s impression.

Applying that standard here, the court explained that a reasonable viewer would see Santos’s videos as sincere, encouraging messages (the original purpose) while viewing Kimmel’s on-air presentation as a critical, satirical reframing. The fact that Kimmel used fictitious names to request the videos did not alter the objective communicative difference between the originals and the televised segments.

## Bad faith argument: narrowing of Harper & Row and why the complaint undermines bad-faith inference

Santos invoked a line from Harper & Row that fair use “presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” arguing that Kimmel’s deceptive requests showed bad faith and defeated the defense. The Second Circuit rejected that argument for two reasons. First, subsequent Supreme Court guidance has limited the weight of Harper & Row’s dictum about an implicit good-faith requirement; bad intent is not automatically fatal to a fair use defense. Second, and dispositively at the pleading stage, Santos’s own allegations undermined any inference that Kimmel intended to supplant Santos’s market for the videos. The complaint portrayed the defendants as engaging in sarcastic criticism and commentary—classic fair use motivations—rather than trying to deprive Santos of any commercial market for his works. Because Santos pleaded no plausible theory that defendants sought to monetize or replace the original videos, the bad-faith argument did not defeat fair use on the face of the complaint.

## Nature of the work: published status and its impact on factor two

The second statutory factor asks about the nature of the copyrighted work—with unpublished, highly creative works normally getting stronger protection. The court relied on incorporated copyright registrations showing the videos were published the day they were created. That publication status diminishes the weight of factor two against fair use because courts treat published works as more susceptible to secondary uses that comment on them. The opinion therefore concluded that the second factor did not favor Santos in a way that would overcome the strong transformative character found under factor one.

## Amount and substantiality: use tailored to the commentary

The third factor looks to how much of the original was used and whether the “heart” of the work was appropriated. The court’s analysis tied this factor to the purpose of the defendant’s commentary. Kimmel aired the personalized videos in full (or at least substantial portions) as necessary to make the satirical point: the humor depended on viewers seeing Santos’s own words and tone. In fair use jurisprudence, copying a substantial portion can be acceptable when the amount copied is appropriate to the transformative purpose. Here, because the broadcasts sought to ridicule Santos’s willingness to utter certain lines for money, using the lines themselves was integral to the comment. Consequently, the third factor did not outweigh the strong support for fair use supplied by the first factor.

## Market effect: no usurpation of the market for the original videos

The fourth factor asks whether the secondary use harms the existing or potential market for the original. The court emphasized the distinction between devaluing a work through parody or criticism (which may harm perception but is not the kind of harm the fourth factor guards against) and usurpation—offering a competing substitute in the same market. The court cited precedent explaining that creators of imaginative works are unlikely to license others to produce critical reviews or lampoons of those works; therefore, such secondary uses are outside the sorts of licensing markets the fourth factor protects.

Applying that principle here, the court concluded that Kimmel’s broadcasts did not serve as a market substitute for Santos’s Cameo videos. Santos had no plausible allegation that people would purchase a JKL segment as a replacement for paid personalized videos or that the television use displaced any licensing market for such messages. Because the complaint itself demonstrated publication and offered no plausible market-harm theory, the fourth factor did not defeat fair use.

## Bottom line: fair use established on the pleadings

Weighing the factors together, the Second Circuit agreed with the District Court that the first factor—strongly favoring defendants because the use was transformative commentary—was decisive. The objective reasonable-observer inquiry, the lack of plausible allegations of marketplace usurpation, the published status of the videos, and the context-driven justification for the amount used all supported fair use. Because these conclusions were apparent from the complaint and incorporated registration materials, the court held that Santos’s copyright claims were barred by fair use as a matter of law and affirmed dismissal at the pleading stage.

For a law student, this opinion illustrates how courts may resolve fair use on a Rule 12(b)(6) record when the complaint and attached materials plainly show a use that reframes expressive content for commentary or critique and when the plaintiff fails to plead facts suggesting market substitution or other countervailing harms.

# State Law Claims: Contract Theories and Fraud

This chapter explains why the Second Circuit affirmed the District Court’s dismissal of George Santos’s state-law claims—direct breach of contract (third‑party beneficiary theory), breach of an implied‑in‑fact contract, and fraudulent inducement—without deciding whether the Copyright Act preempted those claims. The court elected to avoid resolving preemption because it found each claim independently deficient as pleaded. Understanding the court’s analysis requires attention to (1) the procedural posture of avoiding preemption where a claim fails on the merits, and (2) the specific pleading rules and state‑law elements the court applied under the governing choice‑of‑law provisions.

## Preemption posture: affirming on failure‑to‑state‑a‑claim grounds

Although the District Court relied in part on federal copyright preemption to dismiss Santos’s contract claims, the Second Circuit declined to decide the preemption question. When a federal court can dispose of a case on an adequate independent ground—here, the plaintiff’s failure to plead the elements of state‑law claims—appellate courts often avoid addressing a potentially broader preemption issue. The panel therefore analyzed the state‑law claims under the applicable state standards (Illinois law for the Cameo Terms of Service and New York law for the implied contract and fraud claims) and affirmed dismissal because the complaint did not plausibly allege the necessary elements. This approach narrows the opinion’s holdings to pleading deficiencies rather than a ruling that the Copyright Act necessarily displaces Santos’s state claims.

## Direct breach of contract under the Cameo Terms of Service (Illinois law)

Santos alleged a breach of the Cameo Terms of Service (TOS). But he was not a party to the TOS; rather, the TOS governed the contractual relationship between Cameo and its users (including Kimmel). The controlling principle under Illinois law—applied because the TOS declared Illinois law governs—is a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” The Second Circuit relied on Illinois appellate authority stating that to allow third‑party enforcement, “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” In other words, a plaintiff who is not a contracting party must point to language or circumstances tantamount to an explicit third‑party‑beneficiary provision.

The court examined Santos’s complaint and the TOS and found no such express or practically express declaration. Indeed, the TOS itself contained explicit third‑party‑beneficiary language in other provisions—demonstrating that the drafter knew how to create enforceable third‑party rights when intended. That contrast undermined any inference that the parties to the TOS intended Santos to be an intended beneficiary. Because Santos identified no provisions that unequivocally conferred enforceable rights on nonparties, his direct breach claim failed as a matter of Illinois law.

Key takeaways:
- Nonparty beneficiaries face a high bar; courts look for express or effectively express intent to benefit third parties.
- The existence of explicit third‑party‑beneficiary language elsewhere in a contract weighs against finding implied third‑party rights where none are stated.

## Implied‑in‑fact contract under New York law

Santos also pleaded an implied‑in‑fact contract theory under New York law—arguing that Kimmel (or the defendants) should be bound by the Cameo TOS as a matter of tacit agreement. Under New York law, to state an implied‑in‑fact contract claim a plaintiff must plead the essential terms of the alleged contract and facts showing a “meeting of the minds” (i.e., mutual assent) sufficient to infer agreement and its scope.

The Second Circuit applied the New York standards and emphasized two pleading failures. First, the complaint did not allege the essential terms of any contract between Santos and the defendants. Courts require nonconclusory allegations specifying the material provisions on which liability is predicated; generic assertions that “a contract existed” are insufficient. Second, the complaint failed to plausibly allege a meeting of the minds that Kimmel would adhere to Cameo’s TOS. An implied contract claim cannot rest on a unilateral expectation or hope that a third party would follow another party’s contractual rules; there must be factual allegations showing reciprocal assent or circumstances from which assent can reasonably be inferred.

Because Santos’s allegations were conclusory and omitted the necessary specifics about terms and mutual assent, the court held his implied‑in‑fact contract claim was inadequately pleaded under New York law and therefore properly dismissed.

Key takeaways:
- Implied‑in‑fact contracts require allegations of essential terms and facts giving rise to mutual assent.
- Mere conclusory assertions of an agreement or reliance on a third party’s contract language are insufficient.

## Fraudulent inducement under New York law: out‑of‑pocket loss requirement

Santos’s final state claim accused the defendants of fraudulent inducement. New York law imposes particular requirements on fraud claims, including allegations of a misrepresentation, scienter, reasonable reliance, and damages. For fraudulent inducement claims specifically, New York follows the “out‑of‑pocket” rule articulated in Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney: the plaintiff must allege an actual pecuniary loss caused by the misrepresentation. The more recent decision in Connaughton v. Chipotle reaffirmed that plaintiffs must allege specific, actual out‑of‑pocket losses rather than speculative or nonpecuniary harms.

The Second Circuit agreed with the District Court that Santos failed to allege any actual pecuniary loss resulting from the alleged misrepresentations Kimmel made in obtaining the videos. Santos’s complaint did not plausibly connect any monetary harm to the defendants’ conduct in a way that satisfies New York’s out‑of‑pocket requirement. Consequently, the fraudulent inducement claim was also properly dismissed.

Key takeaways:
- New York fraudulent inducement claims require measurable, actual pecuniary loss (out‑of‑pocket).
- Allegations of reputational harm or nonpecuniary injury, without specific monetary loss tied to the fraud, will not satisfy the pleading standard.

## Disposition: dismissal of all state claims for failure to state a claim

Because each state‑law claim independently failed to state a claim under the applicable state standards—no plausible third‑party beneficiary claim under Illinois law, no adequately pleaded implied‑in‑fact contract under New York law, and no alleged out‑of‑pocket loss for fraudulent inducement—the Second Circuit affirmed the District Court’s dismissal of the state claims. The opinion left open the preemption issue, resolving the case on narrower pleading grounds. For students, this demonstrates a common appellate practice: courts will affirm on the simplest adequate ground—here, inadequate pleading—without reaching broader questions (like federal preemption) unless necessary.

# Holding, Disposition, and Significance

## Holding: Affirmance of dismissal; fair use bars copyright claims; state-law claims fail on their elements

The Second Circuit affirmed the District Court’s dismissal of all of George Santos’s claims. On the copyright claims, the court held that Jimmy Kimmel’s airing of the Cameo videos on Jimmy Kimmel Live! was a fair use as a matter of law at the pleading stage. The court emphasized the first statutory fair-use factor — the purpose and character of the use — and found the JKL segments were transformative because they repurposed Santos’s videos for critical, comedic commentary rather than to serve the original expressive purpose that Santos alleged. The court rejected Santos’s argument that Kimmel’s role in soliciting the videos defeated a finding of transformativeness; rather, an objective reasonable-observer test governs whether a secondary use is transformative, not the subjective intent of the parties. The court also found the other fair-use factors did not defeat fair use on the face of the complaint, and in particular that there was no cognizable market-harm theory: the JKL segments did not usurp any plausible licensing market for Santos’s videos.

As to the state-law claims (breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement), the court affirmed dismissal on the merits. The court explained that Santos could not enforce Cameo’s Terms of Service because he was not a contracting party and pointed to the strong presumption against conferring contractual benefits on noncontracting third parties under Illinois law (which governs the Terms). For the implied-in-fact contract theory under New York law, the complaint failed to plead the essential terms and mutual assent required to create such a contract. And the fraudulent inducement claim failed because Santos did not allege any actual out-of-pocket loss, a necessary element under New York law for that claim.

## Disposition: Judgment of the District Court affirmed

The Second Circuit affirmed the District Court’s Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal in full. The court reviewed the dismissal de novo, considered the complaint and incorporated documents (including copyright registrations), and concluded that dismissal was proper at the pleading stage for both the copyright and state-law claims. The practical result is that Santos’s suit against Kimmel, ABC, and Disney was ended by affirmance.

## Key takeaways on fair use: Objective purpose controls; comedic commentary and critique can be transformative even where the defendant instigates content

The opinion teaches that courts focus on how a reasonable observer would perceive the secondary use when assessing transformativeness. The fact that a defendant solicited or instigated creation of the original material does not automatically preclude a finding of transformative use. Here, although Kimmel used fictitious names to request personalized Cameo videos, the segments broadcast on JKL clearly recontextualized those videos as objects of satire and commentary. The court relied on precedent emphasizing that subjective intent (of either the copyright owner or the user) is not the touchstone; instead, the analysis asks whether the secondary work adds new expressive content, purpose, or character that differs from the original.

This is an important instructional point for students: transformative use is an objective inquiry centered on the secondary work’s new purpose or meaning. Comedy and lampooning are paradigmatic examples of purposes that can be transformative because they alter the original’s communicative content.

## Market-harm clarification: Criticism/lampooning does not create a cognizable licensing market for the plaintiff

The court reiterated that the fourth fair-use factor (market effect) asks whether the secondary work acts as a market substitute for the original work — i.e., whether it usurps the original’s market — not whether it diminishes the original’s value by criticizing or parodying it. The opinion draws from Campbell and later Second Circuit authorities to explain that it is unlikely creators of imaginative or expressive works would license critical reviews or lampoons of their own output; therefore, market-harm theories premised on criticism or parody devaluing the original do not typically defeat fair use. In Santos’s case, the complaint itself showed no plausible market that the JKL segments could have supplanted, so the market-harm factor did not overcome the other considerations favoring fair use.

For students, this clarifies that alleged reputational or expressive harm from criticism is not the same thing as the kind of substitutive market harm that counts against fair use.

## Contract lessons: Third-party beneficiary hurdles under Illinois law; specificity and mutual assent required for implied contracts under New York law

The opinion provides concrete lessons about using platform terms in contract claims:

- Third-party beneficiary doctrine: Because Santos was not a party to Cameo’s Terms of Service, his direct breach-of-contract claim faced a significant obstacle under Illinois law — the law governing the Terms. The court pointed to the “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties” and required a near-explicit declaration in the contract that third parties may enforce it. The presence of express third-party beneficiary language elsewhere in the Terms reinforced that omission here was meaningful. This underscores that litigants trying to enforce platform agreements as nonparties must locate clear contractual language showing an intent to benefit and permit enforcement by third parties.

- Implied-in-fact contracts and mutual assent: For the related implied contract theory governed by New York law, the complaint failed because it did not allege essential terms or demonstrate a meeting of the minds. An implied-in-fact contract claim requires factual allegations showing offer, acceptance, consideration, and mutual assent to specific terms; conclusory assertions that the defendants “should have” followed the Terms are insufficient. The opinion shows that courts will dismiss implied contract claims that rest on speculation about nonparties’ obligations absent concrete facts establishing assent and defined contractual terms.

These points are especially relevant in cases involving interactions on multi-party platforms where the platform’s terms bind only some participants and where plaintiffs attempt to convert terms intended for platform users into rights enforceable by third parties.

## Fraud lesson: Necessity of pleading actual out-of-pocket loss, not reputational or expectancy harms

On fraudulent inducement under New York law, the court affirmed dismissal because Santos failed to allege actual out-of-pocket damages — the traditional measure required in New York for fraud claims. The opinion distinguishes between reputational or expectancy-type harms and the specific, tangible out-of-pocket loss New York law requires for a fraud claim to survive. Santos’s complaint did not allege that he suffered the kind of monetary loss traceable to the alleged misrepresentations that induced performance, so the fraudulent inducement claim could not proceed.

For students, this reinforces the lesson that pleading fraud requires plausible allegations of both actionable misrepresentation and actual, particularized economic loss caused by reliance; generalized harms or speculative damages are inadequate.

## Significance and instructional value

This opinion is useful pedagogically because it demonstrates how fair-use analysis can resolve a copyright case at the pleadings stage when the complaint and incorporated materials plainly show a transformative purpose and no plausible market usurpation. It also shows the interplay between federal copyright defenses and state-law contract and tort theories tied to platform terms: even if state-law claims are not preempted, they must still satisfy ordinary elements such as privity or third-party-beneficiary language, mutual assent for implied contracts, and concrete out-of-pocket damages for fraud. Together, these rulings illustrate practical pleading pitfalls—especially in litigation arising from platform-mediated interactions and comedic commentary—helpful for students learning to map fact patterns onto both copyright defenses and state-law pleading requirements.
