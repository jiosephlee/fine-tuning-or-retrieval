Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

# Domestic Violence Call and On-Scene Facts

This chapter reconstructs, from the body-camera record the district court and Seventh Circuit relied upon, what happened at the Coleman residence on April 20, 2023. The narrative emphasizes the communications between Officer Jamie Kizer and Lisa Coleman that the courts treated as supplying consent to enter, and it traces how the officers’ welfare check evolved into a search warrant that produced firearms. The video serves as the foundational source for the factual findings the courts reviewed.

At roughly 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Marshfield, Wisconsin police received a 911 call reporting a domestic dispute in which Lisa said her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother and was still inside the home. Officer Kizer arrived and knocked on the front door. The body-camera footage captures the entire interaction the courts examined, and the Seventh Circuit relied on the video to resolve disputes about what was said and how it was said.

When Lisa first answered, she stepped onto the porch and identified that her son and daughter were inside the house. She was reluctant to say whether the children were safe; at first she only said they were in her bedroom and then responded “I hope so” when asked if they were going to be okay. The footage shows an officer attempting to determine where Coleman was located. When Lisa said she did not know his location, Officer Kizer asked if he could come inside. On that first request, Lisa explicitly refused, saying “um … I’d prefer you not.” That initial refusal mattered to the courts: it demonstrated that Lisa understood she could deny entry and that the officers had asked for permission rather than simply asserting authority.

After declining, Lisa told the officer she needed to go back inside to get her shoes and went into the house. Several minutes passed with no apparent return. Concerned, Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the home to check on her. About a minute later Lisa reemerged. At that point the conversation shifted and produced the dialogue the courts treated as decisive.

Officer Kizer asked whether Coleman was calm and whether the children were okay. The pivotal exchange then unfolded, captured verbatim on the video and quoted by the Seventh Circuit:
- Lisa: “I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].”
- Officer Kizer: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.”
- Lisa: “Okay.”
- Officer Kizer: “Okay, um ….”
- Lisa: “They’re in my bedroom.”
- Officer Kizer: “Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….”
- Lisa: “You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.”
- Officer Kizer: “Okay.”
- Lisa: “They’re not gonna bite.”
- Officer Kizer: “Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?”
- Lisa: “I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.”
- Officer Kizer: “Okay, alright.”

The court focused on two features of that exchange. First, Officer Kizer’s language expressed concern for the children’s welfare and used the word “need” to convey urgency: “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Second, Lisa’s immediate “Okay” and subsequent provision of information—identifying where the children were and reassuring the officers about the dogs—were treated as affirmative acquiescence to entry. The court read the totality of the interaction, including the earlier refusal, as showing that Lisa had the capacity and opportunity to refuse but chose to let the officers in after a second, uncoercive request.

Following that exchange Lisa opened the door and physically assisted the officers’ entry by brushing her dogs away. She led them to one of the back bedrooms and pointed out where the children were. The officers then conducted the welfare-oriented tasks they had stated as their purpose: a brief scan to locate people in the house and to assess any immediate danger. In that process the officers located Coleman in a separate bedroom. He consented to a limited protective pat-down, during which the officers were still acting under the welfare-check premise.

Once the officers spoke with the children, additional facts emerged that changed the nature of the encounter. The children reported that their parents had been fighting and characterized Coleman as loud and aggressive. Critically, both children told officers that Coleman had pointed a gun at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and threatened to burn the house down. They said they had seen the gun that night and on other occasions but did not know its precise location at the moment. Those statements supplied facts beyond the immediate welfare check: threats, a firearm had been displayed, and potential danger remained in the residence.

Using the information gathered during the entry and the children’s statements, the officers sought and obtained a warrant to search the home. The subsequent warrant search uncovered several firearms. Those firearms formed the basis for the federal indictment charging Coleman with unlawful possession of a firearm as a felon under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1).

Throughout its factual reconstruction the Seventh Circuit emphasized that the body-camera footage showed a short, conversational interaction in which the officers’ tone was concerned rather than coercive, and in which Lisa demonstrated an ability to refuse before ultimately permitting entry. The court treated the officers’ initial activities inside the home as appropriately within the scope of a welfare check—locating occupants, briefly assessing danger, and asking questions—which then led to probable cause sufficient to support a warrant and the later discovery of firearms.

# Procedural History and Issues on Appeal

This chapter follows the case from investigation to appellate disposition and frames the specific legal questions the Seventh Circuit resolved. The path is straightforward: a federal indictment triggered a Fourth Amendment contest over entry into a private home; a magistrate and district court denied suppression; the defendant pleaded guilty while preserving his suppression claim; and the Seventh Circuit reviewed the narrow legal issues presented on appeal.

The case began when officers responding to a 911 domestic-violence call entered the Coleman home and, after questioning, obtained a warrant that led to discovery of firearms. Those firearms formed the basis of a federal indictment charging Jaison Coleman under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) — the felon-in-possession statute that makes it a crime for a person previously convicted of a felony to possess firearms. The indictment placed Coleman in the ordinary posture of a criminal defendant facing evidence obtained during police activity inside his home.

Coleman moved to suppress the firearms, arguing two related Fourth Amendment defects. First, he contended that his wife, Lisa Coleman, never consented to the officers’ entry into the house; second, he argued that even if she had given any consent, the officers exceeded the permissible scope of that consent once inside. These are distinct legal inquiries: voluntariness addresses whether the consent exception to the warrant requirement applies at all, while scope asks what the officers were permitted to do consistent with whatever consent was actually given.

A magistrate judge conducted a suppression hearing, principally relying on body-worn camera footage recorded by the officers, and issued a report recommending that the suppression motion be denied. The magistrate found that Lisa had in fact consented to the entry and that the officers did not exceed the consent’s scope. The district court adopted the magistrate’s report and issued an order denying suppression. Important to the district court’s decision were (1) the magistrate’s credibility and factual findings informed by the video record and (2) the legal application that consent, if freely and voluntarily given under the totality of the circumstances, permits warrantless entry.

After the district court’s adverse ruling, Coleman entered a conditional guilty plea to the § 922(g)(1) charge. A conditional plea allowed him to accept the benefits of pleading guilty while preserving the right to appeal the district court’s denial of his motion to suppress; if he prevailed on appeal, he would be able to seek relief inconsistent with the guilty plea’s factual basis. This procedural choice confined the appeal to the specific suppression ruling rather than presenting a broad re-litigation of other trial issues.

On appeal, Coleman presented two discrete questions for the Seventh Circuit to resolve: (1) whether Lisa’s consent to the officers’ entry was voluntary under the Fourth Amendment, and (2) whether the officers’ actions inside the house exceeded the scope of any consent she gave. Those issues called for mixed factual and legal analysis. The voluntariness determination turns on the totality of the circumstances and is therefore a factual finding reviewed for clear error. Similarly, scope-of-consent issues are assessed objectively — what a reasonable person would have understood — and the district court’s factual findings about what occurred are likewise reviewed under the clear-error standard.

A procedural and evidentiary feature of the record played a central role on appeal: the body-camera video. The Seventh Circuit emphasized that the contemporaneous video “evaporate[d] any factual dispute” about the interaction at the door, allowing the appellate court to confidently review the district court’s factual findings for clear error. The video shows the sequence of questions and answers between Officer Kizer and Lisa, including Lisa’s initial refusal, the later exchange in which Officer Kizer said he “almost need[ed] to come inside to make sure” the children were okay, Lisa’s reply of “Okay,” and her subsequent actions facilitating entry. The court treated the footage as dispositive of the factual record relevant to consent and scope.

The Seventh Circuit’s review was narrowly framed. It did not reach alternative justifications the government had advanced at the district court — namely, exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery — because the court affirmed on the consent ground. The appellate court applied settled Fourth Amendment and consent law principles (voluntariness under the totality of the circumstances; scope judged by objective reasonableness) and reviewed the district court’s factual findings for clear error in light of the video record. Concluding that the magistrate and district court did not clearly err in finding voluntary consent and a search within the consent’s scope, the Seventh Circuit affirmed Coleman’s conviction.

# Governing Fourth Amendment Principles

This section explains the legal principles the Seventh Circuit relied on in assessing whether Officer Kizer’s entry into the Coleman home was constitutional. The court’s analysis turns on several discrete but related doctrines: consent as an exception to the warrant requirement, third‑party consent when privacy interests are shared, the voluntariness inquiry under the Fourth Amendment, the limits of the scope of consent, the standard of appellate review for the factual findings that underlie those conclusions, and the evidentiary value of contemporaneous video. Each doctrine is described briefly and then tied to how the court applied it in United States v. Coleman.

Consent as a categorical exception to the warrant requirement
The starting point is the settled rule that consent is a recognized exception to the Fourth Amendment’s requirement that police generally obtain a warrant before entering and searching a home. The court cites Schneckloth v. Bustamonte for the general proposition that consent renders a search reasonable even without a warrant, and it relies on more recent Seventh Circuit precedent (United States v. Banks) to place the Coleman dispute within that framework. In practical terms, once valid consent to enter is shown, the initial entry is lawful and the reasonableness inquiry moves to whether the consent was voluntary and whether officers stayed within its scope.

Third‑party consent where shared privacy interests exist
The opinion next explains that consent can come from someone other than the defendant when that third party possesses actual authority to allow entry. The Supreme Court’s Matlock rule and Seventh Circuit authority in Cellitti recognize that a third party who shares common authority over the premises may consent to a search binding on a co‑occupant. The court in Coleman applies that principle to Lisa’s decision to allow officers inside: her status as a co‑resident who controlled access to the home put her in the position to give the consent that the officers relied on.

Voluntariness: “freely and voluntarily” under the totality of the circumstances
Even when a third party gives permission, the court must ensure the permission was given “freely and voluntarily.” The opinion quotes Bumper for the well‑known requirement that consent not be the product of coercion. The Seventh Circuit’s more recent decisions (e.g., Han) frame voluntariness as a totality‑of‑the‑circumstances inquiry: no single factor is dispositive, and the court must weigh everything known to the officers and the person who gave consent.

Common factors bearing on voluntariness
To operationalize the totality‑of‑the‑circumstances test, the court points to recurring factors in Seventh Circuit cases. Figueroa‑Espana and Santiago identify common considerations: the person’s age, intelligence, and education; whether Miranda or other constitutional rights were recited; the length of any detention before consent; whether consent was immediate or the product of repeated requests; the presence of physical coercion; and whether the person was in custody when consent was given. The Coleman opinion uses these factors as a checklist to evaluate Lisa’s interaction with Officer Kizer: she was not in custody, there was no physical coercion, the interaction was brief, she had previously declined entry once, and the officers asked for permission rather than forcibly entering.

Scope of consent: objective‑reasonableness and what a typical person would understand
Obtaining voluntary consent does not license an unlimited search. The court invokes Florida v. Jimeno to explain the proper inquiry into scope: an objective‑reasonableness test that asks what the typical reasonable person would have understood the consenting party to permit. The Seventh Circuit’s precedents (Saucedo, Jackson) reinforce that the factual content and stated purpose of the officers’ request shape the permissible extent of their actions once inside. In Coleman the officers told Lisa they were there to check on the welfare of the children and to ensure Coleman did not present a danger; the court viewed the officers’ subsequent movements—scanning rooms, locating the occupants, asking questions, and conducting a protective pat‑down—as objectively within the reasonable scope of that consent.

Appellate standard of review: clear error for voluntariness and scope findings
Because voluntariness and scope are fact‑intensive inquiries, the opinion emphasizes the applicable standard of review. The Seventh Circuit reviews a district court’s factual finding that consent was voluntary, and its finding on the scope of consent, for clear error. The court cites Jones for this principle and, consistent with the standard, gives deference to the district court’s credibility and factual determinations unless they are unsupported by the record.

Use of contemporaneous video evidence to resolve factual disputes
Finally, the opinion notes the evidentiary significance of body‑camera footage. Citing Scott v. Harris and the court’s own Norville decision, the panel explains that an objective video record can resolve factual disputes that might otherwise be subject to competing narratives. In Coleman the full body‑camera recording allowed the district court—and this court on review—to observe tone, timing, the content of the questions, Lisa’s initial refusal, her later acquiescence, and the short duration and noncoercive nature of the interaction. That visual record undergirded the district court’s voluntariness finding and supported the conclusion that the officers remained within the scope of consent.

How the principles fit together in Coleman
Taken together, these principles define the analytical path the court followed: (1) consent is a recognized exception to the warrant requirement; (2) a co‑resident (Lisa) may give valid consent for entry; (3) that consent must be voluntary under the totality of the circumstances, evaluated by familiar factors; (4) the search must be limited to the objectively reasonable scope of the consent; (5) factual findings on voluntariness and scope are reviewed only for clear error; and (6) contemporaneous video evidence can be decisive in resolving disputed facts. Applying that framework to the body‑camera record, the court affirmed the district court’s findings that Lisa voluntarily consented to the officers’ entry and that the officers’ conduct inside the home comported with the scope of her consent.

# Application: Voluntariness and Scope in a Welfare Check

This section walks through how the Seventh Circuit applied Fourth Amendment consent principles to the bodycam-recorded encounter between Officer Kizer and Lisa Coleman. The court’s analysis is fact-driven and relies on the totality of circumstances to conclude (1) that Lisa voluntarily consented to officers entering the home and (2) that the officers’ initial movements inside the house remained within the scope of that consent. Keep in mind the standard of review that frames the analysis: the district court’s factual finding that consent was voluntary and that the search stayed within the scope of consent is reviewed for clear error, so the appellate court asks whether the district court’s conclusions were plausibly supported by the video record and controlling law.

Tone and demeanor: concern versus coercion
The court places significant weight on the posture and tone of Officer Kizer. Throughout the recorded interaction he speaks calmly and respectfully, conveys urgency about child safety, and does not threaten, intimidate, or physically restrain Lisa. The Seventh Circuit treats those demeanor features as evidentiary: a concerned (rather than aggressive) officer is less likely to have elicited compelled acquiescence. The court therefore views the interaction as consistent with a welfare check—an officer expressing the need to ensure the children’s safety—rather than coercive law-enforcement pressure intended to override a resident’s free choice.

Evidence of agency: initial refusal as proof of capacity to deny
A central factual point the court highlights is Lisa’s initial, explicit refusal when Kizer first asked to enter. That refusal demonstrates she understood she could say no, and it shows the presence of genuine choice at that point in time. Under the totality-of-the-circumstances test for voluntariness, an affirmative earlier denial is probative evidence that a later “yes” was not compelled. The court emphasizes that Lisa’s responses in the interaction show comprehension and the ability to make decisions: she hesitates, answers questions, and later articulates where the children and the husband are. These behaviors support the district court’s finding that her later assent was voluntary.

The second request and the “need” language
The critical exchange on appeal is Officer Kizer’s second solicitation: “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay],” followed by Lisa’s “Okay.” Coleman argued that the use of “need” rendered the request coercive. The appellate court rejects constraining the analysis to that single word. Instead it situates the phrase in context: the request came after a short, calm conversation; Lisa had already demonstrated she could refuse; and the officer did not deploy force, threats, or commands. The court thus treats the “need” language as an expression of the officer’s concern and urgency, not as an illegitimate assertion of authority that vitiated consent.

Why “need” did not make consent involuntary (Hicks and the totality of circumstances)
The court applies the well-known totality-of-the-circumstances approach and draws on circuit precedent (including Hicks) to explain why the phrasing did not invalidate consent. Hicks stands for the principle that an officer’s expressions of urgency or statements of necessity do not automatically equate to coercion; voluntariness depends on the overall interaction. Here, the court notes multiple facts that tip the balance: (1) there was no physical coercion or custody; (2) the exchange was brief; (3) the officer asked for permission rather than ordering entry; and (4) Lisa’s immediate, responsive behavior (identifying where the kids and dogs were, assuring the officer about dog behavior) is consistent with a voluntary consent given to address safety concerns. Under that holistic view, the use of “need” was not enough to render Lisa’s assent involuntary.

Absence of custody, threats, or physical coercion; brief duration; implied awareness of choice
The court specifically points to factors commonly considered in voluntariness analysis: Lisa was not in custody, she was not physically restrained, there were no threats or show of force, and the interaction was short—only a few minutes from the first knock to the opening of the door. While the officer did not expressly say “you don’t have to let us in,” the court finds that his asking for permission and his earlier compliance with Lisa’s denial implied that she knew a choice existed. These facts reinforce the district court’s finding that consent was given freely.

Post-consent conduct signaling understanding
The appellate court emphasizes the actions Lisa took immediately after saying “Okay” as corroborative of a voluntary choice informed by understanding. She told officers where the children were, described the dogs’ likely behavior, physically opened the door, brushed the dogs aside, and led officers to a back bedroom to show where the children were. Those specific, facilitating acts are powerful indicia that Lisa intended to allow the officers in for the welfare-check purpose she had heard described, rather than merely acquiescing under duress or confusion.

Scope analysis: what a reasonable person would understand
Having accepted that Lisa consented, the court then applies the objective “scope” test from Jimeno—what would a reasonable person have understood the consent to permit? The stated purpose of the entry was to check on the children’s welfare and to see whether Coleman posed a danger. The court finds it objectively reasonable that a typical person who allowed officers in for that welfare check would expect them to scan rooms to locate occupants, verify the children’s condition, and ask basic, safety-focused questions. That expectation defines the outer bounds of reasonable consent in these circumstances.

Permissible actions within that scope
Under the court’s view of the reasonable-person understanding, the officers’ actions—entering, moving through the house to find the children and any potential threat, asking the children and adults questions, and conducting a brief protective pat-down of Coleman—were all within the consented scope. The pat-down was a limited, safety-oriented intrusion. The court treats the sweep and the minimal investigative steps as consistent with the welfare-check purpose rather than an impermissible search for evidence unrelated to safety.

Causal chain to a warrant and recovery of firearms
The information obtained during that permitted welfare check—primarily the children’s statements that Coleman had pointed a gun at their mother and threatened to shoot her—gave officers probable cause to seek and obtain a search warrant. The court accepts that lawful warrant as the means by which the officers could then recover firearms. Because the initial entry and sweep were valid under the consent exception and the subsequent warrant was supported by information lawfully obtained during that entry, the evidentiary chain leading to the firearms was not tainted by Fourth Amendment error.

No need to reach exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery
Finally, because the court affirms that Lisa’s consent was voluntary and that the officers acted within its scope, the panel declines to reach the government’s alternative arguments—exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery. Those doctrines would have provided independent grounds for admission, but they are unnecessary once valid consent and a lawful warrant-based search have been established.

Conclusion: facts drive the voluntariness and scope determinations
The Seventh Circuit’s analysis shows how closely courts weigh video-recorded interactions and how the voluntariness and scope inquiries are tethered to specific, observable facts: tone, phrasing, presence or absence of coercion, demonstrable agency by the consenting party, and post-consent behavior. Here, the bodycam footage supplied a clear record that supported the district court’s factual findings. Given that record and applying the totality-of-the-circumstances test, the appellate court concluded there was no clear error in finding Lisa’s consent voluntary and the officers’ actions within the consent’s scope.

# Holding, Disposition, and Practical Significance

## Holding: No clear error in finding voluntary third‑party consent; officers stayed within the scope

The Seventh Circuit’s holding is straightforward: the district court did not clearly err in finding that Lisa, a third party with authority over the home, voluntarily consented to Officer Kizer and his colleagues entering the residence, and the officers’ actions inside the house remained within the scope of that consent. The panel relied on the totality-of-the-circumstances test for voluntariness, emphasizing the noncoercive tone and demeanor of the officer, Lisa’s demonstrated ability to refuse entry (she initially did so), the short duration and nature of the interaction, and the fact she was not in custody. The court rejected Coleman’s argument that the officer’s statement—“I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure [the kids are okay]”—was coercive. Read in context, the comment was a safety-motivated expression of urgency, not a compulsion that vitiated voluntary consent. Once inside, the officers’ conduct—checking on the children, locating occupants, and asking basic questions—was objectively reasonable under the scope of Lisa’s consent.

## Disposition: Conviction affirmed; suppression properly denied

Because the district court’s factual finding of voluntary consent was not clearly erroneous and the officers did not exceed the consent’s scope, the Seventh Circuit affirmed the denial of the motion to suppress and, consequently, affirmed Coleman’s conviction for unlawful possession of a firearm. The court expressly declined to reach the government’s alternative theories (exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery) because the consent ruling disposed of the case.

## Significance for policing domestic‑violence calls: balancing welfare checks and constitutional limits

This opinion illustrates a practical, fact-sensitive approach courts take when reviewing welfare-check entries in domestic-violence contexts. Officers may lawfully enter a home without a warrant when a co-resident with authority freely and voluntarily consents, including where officers express concern about the safety of vulnerable people (children or alleged victims). Courts will weigh the officer’s words and demeanor, the third party’s responses (including any initial refusal), and the surrounding context (time, length of interaction, whether the person was free to refuse) rather than treating particular words as dispositive. The case signals that expressions of urgency tied to welfare (e.g., concern for children) do not automatically render consent involuntary, provided the interaction remains noncoercive.

## Importance of precise, noncoercive phrasing and calm demeanor in consent requests

The court’s reasoning underscores that how officers ask matters. Officer Kizer’s calm, respectful tone and his choice to pose a question—rather than a command—supported voluntariness. Conversely, coercive language, direct orders, or implied threats can undermine consent. Practically, officers should:

- Frame entry as a request: ask for permission rather than asserting that entry is necessary without explanation.
- Avoid language that could be reasonably perceived as a command, threat, or ultimatum (e.g., “We will come in if you don’t open the door”).
- Convey the purpose (welfare/checking on children) transparently and briefly.
- Allow the resident a genuine opportunity to refuse and record that refusal if it occurs.

The opinion also highlights that a single word (like “need”) is not dispositive; the surrounding conduct and tone control the voluntariness assessment.

## Body camera footage as decisive evidence in consent and scope disputes

A central evidentiary lesson from this case is the evidentiary power of body-worn camera footage. The court relied on the video to resolve credibility and clarify the sequence, tone, and content of exchanges; the footage “evaporate[d]” factual disputes about whether consent was coerced or whether the officers exceeded the scope of consent. For practitioners, this reinforces two points:

- For prosecutors and defense counsel: body-camera footage can be outcome-determinative on Fourth Amendment questions that turn on demeanor and context.
- For officers: recording interactions preserves contemporaneous evidence of how consent was requested and given, and of the limits of any consent granted.

Courts will treat video as a reliable, often controlling, source for reconstructing interactions.

## Practical practice pointers

From the opinion, practitioners—police trainers, prosecutors, defense counsel, and judges—can extract concrete practices to reduce Fourth Amendment risk and to strengthen subsequent litigation positions:

- Articulate purpose of entry: When seeking consent, state the specific, limited purpose (e.g., “We want to check on your children because of a 911 call”) so the scope of any consent is clear.
- Confirm consent affirmatively: Obtain an explicit verbal yes or no; a clear affirmative response helps establish voluntariness on later review. If feasible, officers should ask a follow-up question (e.g., “Is it okay if we come in to check on them?”).
- Limit actions to stated objectives: Once inside under consent, confine initial actions to what a reasonable person would understand the entry to permit—welfare checks, locating people, cursory safety inquiries. Avoid unrelated searches or prolonged investigative activities beyond what is necessary to accomplish the stated purpose.
- Transition promptly to warrants when probable cause develops: If officers acquire information inside that gives rise to probable cause (for instance, children report seeing a gun), they should secure a warrant to conduct an extended search, preserving the legality of any evidence obtained after the initial consensual entry. The Coleman court approved the officers’ obtaining a warrant after the initial welfare-check activity.
- Preserve and use recordings: Ensure bodycams capture the entirety of the contact, and preserve footage. Good recordings are persuasive evidence of noncoercive conduct and of the consent’s scope.
- Document refusals and voluntary choices: If a resident initially refuses and later consents, noting the sequence and any intervening explanations or circumstances helps show voluntariness.

Taken together, these practices protect constitutional rights while allowing police to respond effectively to urgent domestic-safety concerns.

Conclusion

United States v. Jaison Coleman reaffirms that voluntary third‑party consent to enter a home during a domestic‑violence welfare check turns on the totality of the circumstances, with courts giving weight to demeanor, tone, the ability to refuse, and clear affirmative consent. The case illustrates the decisive role of body-camera footage and provides actionable guidance for officers about phrasing, limiting the scope of their actions, and promptly seeking warrants once probable cause arises.
