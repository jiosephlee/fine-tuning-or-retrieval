Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

Chapter 1: # Domestic Violence Call and On-Scene Facts

This chapter reconstructs, from the body-camera record the district court and Seventh Circuit relied upon, what happened at the Coleman residence on April 20, 2023. The narrative emphasizes the communications between Officer Jamie Kizer and Lisa Coleman that the courts treated as supplying consent to enter, and it traces how the officers’ welfare check evolved into a search warrant that produced firearms. The video serves as the foundational source for the factual findings the courts reviewed.

At roughly 4:00 a.m. on April 20, 2023, Marshfield, Wisconsin police received a 911 call reporting a domestic dispute in which Lisa said her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother and was still inside the home. Officer Kizer arrived and knocked on the front door. The body-camera footage captures the entire interaction the courts examined, and the Seventh Circuit relied on the video to resolve disputes about what was said and how it was said.

When Lisa first answered, she stepped onto the porch and identified that her son and daughter were inside the house. She was reluctant to say whether the children were safe; at first she only said they were in her bedroom and then responded “I hope so” when asked if they were going to be okay. The footage shows an officer attempting to determine where Coleman was located. When Lisa said she did not know his location, Officer Kizer asked if he could come inside. On that first request, Lisa explicitly refused, saying “um … I’d prefer you not.” That initial refusal mattered to the courts: it demonstrated that Lisa understood she could deny entry and that the officers had asked for permission rather than simply asserting authority.

After declining, Lisa told the officer she needed to go back inside to get her shoes and went into the house. Several minutes passed with no apparent return. Concerned, Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the home to check on her. About a minute later Lisa reemerged. At that point the conversation shifted and produced the dialogue the courts treated as decisive.

Officer Kizer asked whether Coleman was calm and whether the children were okay. The pivotal exchange then unfolded, captured verbatim on the video and quoted by the Seventh Circuit:
- Lisa: “I … I … I don’t … They’re … They’re okay right this second … [unintelligible].”
- Officer Kizer: “Okay, um, I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.”
- Lisa: “Okay.”
- Officer Kizer: “Okay, um ….”
- Lisa: “They’re in my bedroom.”
- Officer Kizer: “Are we gonna get bit by dogs if we come in or are they like ….”
- Lisa: “You shouldn’t, they’re just gonna jump all over you.”
- Officer Kizer: “Okay.”
- Lisa: “They’re not gonna bite.”
- Officer Kizer: “Okay. Alright, where do you think [Coleman] is right now?”
- Lisa: “I’m pretty sure he’s in the back bedroom.”
- Officer Kizer: “Okay, alright.”

The court focused on two features of that exchange. First, Officer Kizer’s language expressed concern for the children’s welfare and used the word “need” to convey urgency: “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Second, Lisa’s immediate “Okay” and subsequent provision of information—identifying where the children were and reassuring the officers about the dogs—were treated as affirmative acquiescence to entry. The court read the totality of the interaction, including the earlier refusal, as showing that Lisa had the capacity and opportunity to refuse but chose to let the officers in after a second, uncoercive request.

Following that exchange Lisa opened the door and physically assisted the officers’ entry by brushing her dogs away. She led them to one of the back bedrooms and pointed out where the children were. The officers then conducted the welfare-oriented tasks they had stated as their purpose: a brief scan to locate people in the house and to assess any immediate danger. In that process the officers located Coleman in a separate bedroom. He consented to a limited protective pat-down, during which the officers were still acting under the welfare-check premise.

Once the officers spoke with the children, additional facts emerged that changed the nature of the encounter. The children reported that their parents had been fighting and characterized Coleman as loud and aggressive. Critically, both children told officers that Coleman had pointed a gun at their mother, threatened to shoot her, and threatened to burn the house down. They said they had seen the gun that night and on other occasions but did not know its precise location at the moment. Those statements supplied facts beyond the immediate welfare check: threats, a firearm had been displayed, and potential danger remained in the residence.

Using the information gathered during the entry and the children’s statements, the officers sought and obtained a warrant to search the home. The subsequent warrant search uncovered several firearms. Those firearms formed the basis for the federal indictment charging Coleman with unlawful possession of a firearm as a felon under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1).

Throughout its factual reconstruction the Seventh Circuit emphasized that the body-camera footage showed a short, conversational interaction in which the officers’ tone was concerned rather than coercive, and in which Lisa demonstrated an ability to refuse before ultimately permitting entry. The court treated the officers’ initial activities inside the home as appropriately within the scope of a welfare check—locating occupants, briefly assessing danger, and asking questions—which then led to probable cause sufficient to support a warrant and the later discovery of firearms.
