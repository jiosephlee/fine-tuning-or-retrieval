Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### On a motion to dismiss an indictment, what facts can the court consider and why is review de novo?
Question:
The panel says sufficiency of an indictment is reviewed de novo and that the court must take the allegations as true (citing Fontenot). I'm confused about the boundary here. Can a district court consider extrinsic evidence (like trading records) to test sufficiency, or is it strictly limited to the four corners of the indictment? And why is the appellate standard de novo rather than abuse of discretion when the district court dismissed?
Answer:
Short answer
- On a motion to dismiss an indictment for failure to state an offense, the district court evaluates the legal sufficiency of the charging document and generally must accept the indictment’s factual allegations as true — i.e., it is confined to the “four corners” of the indictment (and matters properly incorporated or judicially noticeable). It should not resolve disputed facts by considering extrinsic proof such as trading records or other evidence going to the merits.
- The court of appeals reviews that ruling de novo because sufficiency of an indictment presents a question of law (whether the allegations, taken as true, plead each material element), not a discretionary factual determination. United States v. Constantinescu applies these principles (citing Rafoi and Fontenot).

Why the district court is normally limited to the indictment
- Constantinescu reiterates the controlling rule: “the court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated.” (quoting Fontenot). That is the paradigmatic Rule 12(b)(3) posture: the motion challenges the legal adequacy of the pleading, so the judge asks whether the pleading, assumed true, supplies the elements and notice required by the statutes and case law (see the Gaytan/Guzman‑Ocampo formulation the panel cites).
- Practical consequence: the district court should not use trading records or other extrinsic evidence to “test” the sufficiency of the indictment because doing so would convert the Rule 12 legal inquiry into a merits contest. Factual disputes over what really happened are for trial (or for resolution on a properly supported Rule 29/Rule 33 motion or at summary judgment where appropriate).

Limited exceptions
- Although Constantinescu does not catalogue every exception, the governing approach allows the court to consider (a) documents that are expressly incorporated into the indictment or central to the charges and (b) matters of public record or facts subject to judicial notice without converting the motion into a merits hearing. Those narrow categories permit the court to consult certain extrinsic materials when they are properly part of the charging context, but not to resolve contested evidentiary questions via evidentiary proof. (The panel’s reliance on Fontenot and Crow underscores the “take allegations as true” rule.)

Why appellate review is de novo
- Constantinescu cites Rafoi for the proposition that “We review the sufficiency of an indictment de novo.” That follows from the character of the issue: whether the indictment, assumed true, alleges each material element of the offense is a legal question. De novo review is appropriate for legal questions and statutory or constitutional interpretation; it asks anew whether the pleading meets the legal standard (e.g., the elements discussed in Guzman‑Ocampo, Gaytan, and the securities‑fraud elements the panel applies).
- By contrast, abuse‑of‑discretion review applies where the district court exercised judgment over procedural or discretionary matters (sanctions, discovery disputes, evidentiary rulings). A dismissal for failure to state an offense is not primarily a discretionary factual ruling but a legal determination about the pleading’s adequacy, so the court of appeals reviews it de novo.

How that played out in Constantinescu
- The panel reversed because the indictment, taken as true, alleged a fraudulent‑inducement theory (victims were induced to buy securities and part with money/property), which satisfies the elements of § 1348 under governing Supreme Court precedents (Ciminelli distinguished; Kousisis construed). The panel treated the indictment’s allegations as true and resolved the legal question whether those allegations, if true, state an offense — the precise de novo legal inquiry the authorities require.

Bottom line for practice
- On a motion to dismiss an indictment, stick to the indictment’s allegations (and only extrinsic materials that are incorporated or judicially noticeable). If the defense wants to dispute facts in trading records, that is better raised in discovery, at trial, or in a motion that litigates factual issues with an appropriate record (not by asking the court to go beyond the indictment on a Rule 12 sufficiency challenge).

### How does the government prove a 'scheme to defraud' under § 1348 when victims received market-value securities?
Question:
The opinion relies on Kousisis to say actual economic loss isn't required and that obtaining money or property can be the object even if victims get equivalent value. In a pump-and-dump, if followers bought at market price and later could resell, what exactly is the 'property' deprivation? Is it the cash they parted with at the moment of purchase regardless of resale, or must the government tie the cash transfer specifically to the defendants (as counterparties) rather than to the market generally?
Answer:
Short answer
Under the Fifth Circuit’s reading of § 1348 in Constantinescu (drawing on Kousisis and Ciminelli), the government proves a “scheme to defraud” in a pump‑and‑dump by showing the defendant fraudulently induced investors to part with money or property (here, cash used to buy securities). The prosecution does not have to show the victims suffered a net pecuniary loss, nor that the victims received nothing of value; what matters is that the defendant sought to “obtain” the victims’ money or property through deception. The government must, however, link the fraudulent inducement to the transfers that enabled the defendants to obtain money or property (for example, by tracing how the defendants’ dumping realized cash or other profits).

Relevant legal standard from the opinion
- Constantinescu adopts the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory described in Kousisis: a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he uses a material misstatement “to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property,” even if the fraudster provides something of equal value and even if the victim is not left economically worse off. Constantinescu ¶ III.A (quoting Kousisis).
- The opinion emphasizes the distinction from Ciminelli: depriving someone only of valuable economic information (a “right‑to‑control” theory) is not a protected property interest, but inducing a person to hand over money or property is. Constantinescu ¶ III.A.
- The Court also makes clear that the government need not prove actual economic loss — only that obtaining the victim’s money or property was an object of the fraud. Constantinescu ¶ III.B (citing Kousisis).

What the government must prove in a pump‑and‑dump (and what it need not)
- What counts as the “property” deprivation: the money the follower parted with when purchasing the security (i.e., the cash paid to enter the position) is a recognized “money or property” interest for the fraudulent‑inducement theory. Constantinescu holds that inducing purchases is itself the deprivation the statute protects (¶¶ III.A–B).
- No requirement that the investor be left with no market‑value asset: receipt of market‑value securities does not defeat the charge. The Fifth Circuit explicitly says followers’ receipt of market‑value securities “does not obviate the fraud charges” because the statute protects the act of obtaining the money/property via deception (¶ III.B; citing Kousisis).
- Causation/connection the government must show: although the statute and Constantinescu do not demand net loss, the indictment (and at trial the proof) must tie the defendants’ misrepresentations to the purchases — i.e., show the deception induced the victims to hand over cash (or otherwise part with property). In practice that means proving the false/misleading communications and that reasonable investors relied on them in deciding to buy so that money was transferred as a result of the fraud (see ¶¶ I, III.A–B).
- Does the cash have to have flowed directly to the defendants as counterparty? Not necessarily in form. Constantinescu and Kousisis recognize that a defendant can “obtain” money or property even where the fraudster gives something in return or uses pass‑through arrangements; courts have rejected a strict “mirror‑image” requirement tying every dollar lost to a specific defendant’s receipt. Constantinescu cites Baker for that proposition (¶ III.B). But the government still must show that the transactions enabled the defendants to realize profits tied to the fraud (e.g., by dumping into the inflated market and thereby converting the artificial price increase into cash or other proceeds). In short: the government must trace a causal and economic link between the fraudulent inducement and the defendants’ gains, but it need not show that each investor’s payment went straight into the defendant’s pocket as a bilateral purchase.

Practical evidentiary points the opinion implies
- Pleading/proving deceptive communications and their timing relative to purchases is central (Constantinescu ¶ I, describing posts that “induced” purchases).
- Evidence that defendants bought before pumping and sold after pumping (and realized profits) supports the inference that obtaining money was the object of the scheme (¶ I, ¶ III.B).
- Proof that investors bought because of defendants’ statements — trading patterns, contemporaneous messages, and witness testimony — supplies the causal link the statute requires under the fraudulent‑inducement theory (¶ III.A–B).

Bottom line
Under Constantinescu the “property” the government prosecutes is the money (or property) victims were induced to part with when they purchased securities. It does not matter that the victims received market‑value securities or that they could later resell; what matters is that the defendants used deception to induce transfers and that those transfers enabled the defendants to obtain money or property (e.g., by selling into an artificially inflated market). The government must tie the fraudulent inducement to the transfers and to the defendants’ ability to profit from them, but it need not show a direct bilateral cash transfer from each victim to the defendant as a precondition to liability.

### What distinguishes the rejected 'right-to-control' theory from the accepted 'fraudulent-inducement' theory here?
Question:
The panel says this case is unlike Ciminelli because it's not about depriving victims of valuable economic information, but about inducing them to part with money. Factually, the indictment emphasizes misstatements about the defendants' positions and price targets. Those are 'information' too. What makes those allegations cross the line from 'valuable economic information' (not property) to fraudulent inducement to transfer money (property)? Is it the causal linkage to an actual purchase obligation?
Answer:
Short answer: The difference is functional, not semantic. Under Ciminelli the government relied only on a “right-to-control” theory—that a defendant deprived a victim of valuable economic information (a nontraditional “right” to control assets)—which the Supreme Court rejected. Here the indictment alleges a classic fraudulent-inducement scheme: the defendants used materially false statements to cause followers to buy securities (i.e., to hand over money/property). The critical line is whether the misrepresentations are alleged to have induced a transfer of money or property; if so, the fraudulent‑inducement theory (protected by the fraud statutes) applies.

Why (and how) the opinion draws that line
- Ciminelli: the Court held the fraud statutes protect traditional property interests, not a generalized “right to valuable economic information.” A prosecution that rests solely on depriving someone of information therefore fails. (Constantinescu, slip op. III.A (discussing Ciminelli).)
- Kousisis/fraudulent-inducement: by contrast, the fraudulent‑inducement theory covers schemes that use material misstatements to trick a victim into a contract or purchase that requires handing over money or property. The Supreme Court confirmed this is not a repackaged right‑to‑control theory and protects money/property even if the victim doesn’t suffer a net pecuniary loss. (Constantinescu, slip op. III.A–B (citing Kousisis).)

Applying those principles here
- The indictment does more than say the defendants withheld or distorted “information.” It alleges they posted false and misleading statements about their positions and price potential “so that other investors were induced to purchase the security,” and that defendants then sold into that inflation to realize $114 million. Indictment ¶ 13–14; Constantinescu, slip op. I, III.B.
- That allegation satisfies the “scheme to defraud” element: a material misrepresentation intended to obtain money or property (the followers’ purchases). The panel therefore treats the posts as the means of inducing transfers of money/property—not merely as the withholding of valuable information. (Constantinescu, slip op. III (citing Evans, Greenlaw, Kousisis).)

Two concise takeaways for your reading of the opinion
1. The same underlying factual act (posting false claims) can be either an impermissible “information‑deprivation” theory or a permissible fraudulent‑inducement theory depending on what the indictment alleges the defendant sought to obtain. If the alleged objective is to cause victims to part with money/property, it is fraudulent inducement. (See Indictment ¶ 13 and the panel’s discussion, Constantinescu, III.A–B.)
2. The government need not allege that victims suffered a net economic loss—only that the defendant intended to obtain the victims’ money or property by deception. (Constantinescu, slip op. III.B (discussing Kousisis).)

So yes: the causal linkage to inducing an actual purchase/transfer of money or property is what crosses the line from an impermissible “deprivation of information” theory (Ciminelli) into a valid fraudulent‑inducement prosecution (as the panel found here).

### What counts as a 'material misrepresentation' in a social-media pump-and-dump: puffery vs. actionable lie?
Question:
The indictment alleges posts 'misrepresenting their trading positions and the potential price of securities.' I understand lying about your current holdings or intent to sell could be material, but what about optimistic price targets or hype—could those be dismissed as puffery or opinion? Does § 1348 adopt the Rule 10b-5 materiality framework (reasonable investor standard), and how would that apply to casual tweets vs. formal statements?
Answer:
Short answer
- Under the Fifth Circuit’s opinion in United States v. Constantinescu, a “material misrepresentation” for a § 1348 pump‑and‑dump is a false or misleading statement that is part of a scheme “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value” — i.e., a misstatement that is used to induce victims to hand over money or property (here, to buy securities). Op. § III (quoting Greenlaw/Evans) and Part III.A–B.
- That means clear factual lies about holdings, trades, or intent to sell (or concealed sales) are prototypically material and actionable. Hype or generic optimistic commentary (classic puffery/opinion) is less likely to be material unless it is presented or understood as a factual assertion that plausibly induces purchases.
- The opinion does not say that § 1348 formally “adopts” the Rule 10b‑5 reasonable‑investor test; it analyzes § 1348 under the scheme‑to‑defraud / fraudulent‑inducement framework (drawing on Ciminelli and Kousisis) — materiality is tied to whether the misstatement was used to induce a purchase of securities (fraudulent‑inducement), not the right‑to‑control theory rejected in Ciminelli. See Parts III and III.A–B.

Why — legal doctrine in the opinion
- The court frames § 1348 liability in two elements: (1) scheme to defraud (which requires a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value”), and (2) intent to defraud. Op. § III (citing Greenlaw and Evans).
- The opinion distinguishes two theories:
  - Right‑to‑control theory (rejected by Ciminelli): protecting only a person’s “right to valuable economic information” is not a traditional property interest and cannot alone sustain fraud. Op. III.A (discussing Ciminelli).
  - Fraudulent‑inducement theory (upheld here via Kousisis): using a material misstatement to trick a victim into a transaction that causes the victim to part with money or property. The court says that is precisely what the indictment alleges (inducing purchases), and that theory protects money/property even if the victim is not left economically worse off. Op. III.A–B (citing Kousisis).
- Materiality, as the court treats it, operates in service of the fraudulent‑inducement theory: a misstatement is material if it is a misstatement that was used to induce the follower to purchase the security (and thereby part with money/property). Op. III (scheme requires “material misrepresentation ... intended to deceive ... to obtain something of value”).

Puffery / opinion vs. actionable lies — how the opinion guides analysis
- Actionable: factual misstatements that directly bear on transactional decisions are squarely within the indictment the court revived. The opinion repeatedly points to allegations that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information” and “induced … to purchase the security” (Indictment ¶13) as the paradigmatic fraudulent‑inducement. Op. I, III.A–B.
  - Examples from that frame: lying about current holdings, falsely stating that one will not sell when secretly planning to dump, claiming fictitious trades or fabricated demand to create urgency — these are the sort of factual misrepresentations the opinion treats as material because they are alleged to have induced purchases.
- Puffery/opinion: the court’s reasoning leaves room for non‑actionability of generic hype. The opinion endorses Ciminelli’s principle that not all economic information is a protected property interest and treats the fraudulent‑inducement materiality question by asking whether the misstatement was used to obtain money/property. Thus, generalized bullish statements, vague optimism, or subjective price targets framed as opinion are less likely to satisfy the indictment’s requirement unless they are presented as or rest on factual assertions that induce purchases.
- Context matters: the same language can be actionable in one context and puffery in another. The opinion’s focus on the defendant’s intent and whether the misstatement actually induced purchases suggests courts will look to:
  - specificity and factual content (specific statements about holdings, trades, intent, pipelines of buyers, etc. are more likely material),
  - whether the speaker is a purported expert/influencer who presents statements as factual trading guidance (the defendants “held themselves out to be skilled stock traders” and posted trading activity — Op. I), and
  - whether the statement plausibly induced actual purchases (the indictment alleges induced purchases and profits).

Does § 1348 use the Rule 10b‑5 “reasonable investor” materiality test?
- The opinion does not say § 1348 adopts Rule 10b‑5’s reasonable‑investor formulation verbatim. Instead, it analyzes § 1348 under the well‑established fraudulent‑inducement framework (and relies on Supreme Court decisions interpreting related fraud statutes — Ciminelli and Kousisis). See Op. III and III.A–B.
- Practically that means courts will assess whether the misstatement was a material misrepresentation insofar as it was used to induce purchases (an objective inducement/use‑of‑misstatement inquiry), which overlaps with—but is not described in the opinion as identical to—the Rule 10b‑5 “would an average investor consider the statement important?” inquiry. The opinion grounds materiality in the role the statement played in inducing a transaction (fraudulent inducement), not the separate right‑to‑control theory Ciminelli rejected.

Practical takeaways for fact patterns like tweets vs. formal statements
- Casual tweets can be actionable if they convey false factual claims that reasonably induce followers to buy (e.g., “I just bought 100k shares; you should buy now,” when false, or “I’m not selling” while secretly dumping). The opinion highlights defendants who “held themselves out to be skilled stock traders” and used social media posts to induce purchases. Op. I.
- Casual bullish commentary, vague predictions, or hyperbolic slogans are more likely to be treated as non‑actionable puffery unless tied to deceptive factual claims or intended to create a false impression about facts material to the buying decision.
- The more the communicator presents statements as factual, specific, and actionable trading information (and the more the audience reasonably treats them that way), the more the statement fits the fraudulent‑inducement materiality the Fifth Circuit describes.

Bottom line
- Under Constantinescu, § 1348 liability turns on whether the alleged misstatements were used to fraudulently induce followers to part with money or property. Clear factual lies about holdings, trades, or intent are prototypical material misrepresentations; generic hype and opinion are less likely to be material unless framed or understood as factual claims that induce purchases. The court analyzes materiality in the fraudulent‑inducement frame (citing Kousisis and distinguishing Ciminelli) rather than announcing a wholesale adoption of the Rule 10b‑5 reasonable‑investor test.

### Does § 1348 borrow all the scienter elements from mail/wire fraud, or is there a securities-specific gloss?
Question:
The court says § 1348 'borrows key concepts' from mail and wire fraud and uses the Evans/Greenlaw framework (scheme + intent to defraud). Is the required mens rea identical to mail/wire fraud (intent to deceive + intent to obtain money/property), or does the 'in connection with any security' language import anything akin to Rule 10b-5's scienter or reliance concepts? In prosecutions under § 1348, is investor reliance ever an element or just evidentiary?
Answer:
Short answer: The Fifth Circuit in Constantinescu treats § 1348 as borrowing the core mens rea framework from mail/wire fraud (the Evans/Greenlaw “scheme to defraud” + “intent to defraud” formulation) — it does not import Rule 10b‑5’s separate reliance or securities‑specific scienter doctrines as additional, distinct elements. Reliance (or investors’ reliance) can be evidence of deception and intent to obtain money/property, but it is not a separate statutory element under § 1348 as the opinion frames the offense.

Why (grounded in the opinion)

- The court explicitly says § 1348 “borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes,” and that courts “have given the terms similar treatment.” It then sets out the operative elements using the Evans/Greenlaw framework: (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” (Op. § III, quoting Greenlaw and Evans.)

- The opinion defines those mens rea components in the mail/wire‑fraud terms: a scheme to defraud requires a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived,” and intent to defraud requires an intent “(1) to deceive, and (2) [to] cause some harm to result from the deceit.” (Op. § III.)

- The court relies on Supreme Court authority (Ciminelli and Kousisis) to explain what kinds of theories fit under § 1348. It distinguishes Ciminelli’s rejection of a right‑to‑control theory (which attempted to make deprivation of valuable information into a protected property interest) from the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory (used here), which the Court has recognized protects “money and property” when a victim is tricked into handing over money/property. The Fifth Circuit therefore treats the relevant inquiry as whether the indictment alleges deception and an object of obtaining the victim’s money/property. (Op. §§ III.A–B.)

- Notably, the opinion frames the statute’s mens rea around deception and the object of obtaining money/property, citing Kousisis: a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’” and the statutes do not require evidence that the defendant intended to leave the victim worse off economically. (Op. § III.B, quoting Kousisis.)

What that means doctrinally for § 1348 prosecutions

- § 1348 uses the mail/wire fraud mens rea vocabulary (scheme + intent to defraud). The court did not adopt Rule 10b‑5’s specific scienter doctrines (for example, the separate elements used in civil securities law like “scienter” narrowly defined for private suits or a distinct element of “reliance” as required in Rule 10b‑5 private actions). Instead, the court evaluates whether the indictment alleges deception and an object of obtaining money/property — the classic fraudulent‑inducement model.

- Investor reliance is not treated as a freestanding element of the § 1348 offense in this opinion. Allegations that investors were induced to buy (i.e., acted in reliance on defendants’ misrepresentations) are pleaded to show deception and that obtaining investors’ money/property was the object of the scheme. Thus reliance functions as evidence (and part of the government’s factual theory of fraudulent inducement), not an extra statutory element over and above scheme + intent to defraud as the court describes them.

Bottom line: Under Constantinescu, prosecutors proceed under the familiar mail/wire‑fraud mens rea framework adapted to securities (scheme to defraud + intent to defraud; object to obtain money/property). Rule 10b‑5 style reliance/scienter doctrines are not imported as separate statutory elements; reliance is relevant evidentiary proof of deception/intent, not a discrete element the indictment must plead beyond the Evans/Greenlaw components the opinion adopts.

### How do conspiracy (§ 1349) and aiding-and-abetting (§ 2) apply if some defendants didn’t personally sell or profit?
Question:
The indictment charges conspiracy and substantive § 1348 counts with § 2. Suppose a defendant only posted misleading content but didn’t sell into the spike or directly receive funds from followers. Is it enough for conspiracy that he agreed to the scheme and took overt acts, even if he didn’t profit? For aiding and abetting, what level of participation and intent must be shown—does merely amplifying others’ posts qualify?
Answer:
Short answer
- Conspiracy (§ 1349): Yes — an agreement to participate in the pump‑and‑dump and an overt act (e.g., posting misleading content) can support a conspiracy charge even if that defendant did not personally sell into the spike or directly receive funds, so long as the indictment pleads that the defendant shared the fraudulent purpose and took steps in furtherance of it.
- Aiding‑and‑abetting (§ 2): Liability requires purposeful, knowing assistance (and the requisite intent for the underlying securities fraud). Mere accidental or passive amplification is less likely to suffice; purposeful reposting, amplifying, or other conduct that meaningfully facilitates the fraudulent inducement can qualify.

Why (doctrine tied to the opinion)
1) Conspiracy under § 1349
- The Fifth Circuit in United States v. Constantinescu reversed dismissal because the indictment adequately alleged a “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud” under § 1348, and the defendants there were charged with conspiracy to commit securities fraud. The court treated the securities‑fraud theory as a “fraudulent‑inducement” theory — i.e., using material misstatements to trick victims into parting with money or property (Kousisis and Ciminelli discussion). The opinion emphasizes that the fraud statutes protect money/property obtained by inducement, not only a “right to economic information.” Slip op. §§ III.A–B.
- Basic conspiracy law (applied to § 1349 in the court’s reasoning) is met where the indictment alleges (1) an agreement to commit the substantive offense, (2) the defendant’s knowing participation in that agreement with the requisite intent, and (3) an overt act in furtherance of the conspiracy. The opinion treats as sufficient allegations that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security” and that defendants “purchased a security,” “pumped” the price, and “dumped” it for profit. Indictment ¶¶13–14; see slip op. § I.
- Applied to your fact: a defendant who agreed to the pump‑and‑dump and who posted misleading content can be charged with conspiracy even if he didn’t personally sell or directly receive funds, because the allegations that he shared the fraudulent purpose and took overt acts (the posts) can be enough to allege the conspiracy elements. The opinion expressly rejects the notion that a defendant must have intended to leave victims worse off economically; it is enough that the object was to obtain money/property by inducement (Kousisis, discussed at slip op. § III.B).

2) Aiding and abetting under § 2
- The opinion frames the underlying offense as securities fraud requiring (1) a scheme by material misrepresentation to obtain money/property, and (2) intent to deceive and to cause harm (as the circuits have construed those elements). The court cites Kousisis for the point that a fraud is complete when the defendant induced the victim to relinquish money/property under materially false pretenses, regardless of net economic loss to the victim (slip op. §§ III and III.B).
- Aiding‑and‑abetting criminal liability (18 U.S.C. § 2) requires proof that the defendant (a) knowingly and intentionally associated with the criminal venture, and (b) took some action that reasonably could assist or facilitate the offense, with the mental state required for the substantive offense. The Constantinescu opinion’s focus on the knowingly deceptive postings as the means by which followers were induced indicates that communications that intentionally further the fraudulent inducement are treated as culpable acts in the fraud.
- Applied to your fact: merely reposting or occasionally amplifying content without knowledge of the fraud or without intent to help the scheme is a weak basis for § 2 liability. But purposeful amplification or reposting made with knowledge of the scheme and with the intent to induce purchases (i.e., to help “pump” the price) is the kind of conduct the indictment alleges and that the court found sufficient to state offenses. In short: the conduct must be purposeful and knowing, and must meaningfully facilitate the fraudulent inducement that seeks to obtain money/property.

Practical points for charging/proof (as reflected in the opinion)
- The government need not allege that each defendant personally pocketed proceeds or caused a net pecuniary loss to victims. It is enough to allege the fraudulent‑inducement object — that defendants used misrepresentations to induce followers to hand over money/property (slip op. §§ III.A–B, relying on Kousisis).
- For non‑seller or non‑recipient defendants, the government should (and here did) plead facts showing agreement and purposeful conduct in furtherance (e.g., coordinated posting, synchronized timing, directions to buy, knowledge of others’ selling) so that their posts are more than passive speech.
- On the defense side, distinguishing passive speech from knowing facilitation (lack of knowledge, lack of intent to induce purchases, or mere innocuous amplification) is the principal pathway to attack aiding/abetting or to challenge individual culpability in a conspiracy.

Bottom line: under Constantinescu, an indictment that alleges a defendant agreed to a pump‑and‑dump and took overt acts (like posting misleading material) can support conspiracy liability even absent proof that that defendant personally sold or personally received proceeds. For aiding/abetting, the government must show knowing, intentional assistance that meaningfully furthers the fraudulent inducement; mere passive or unwitting amplification is unlikely to duplicate the culpability the panel found sufficient here.

### Are the defendants’ social-media statements protected by the First Amendment, and how does the fraud exception operate here?
Question:
The posts are speech. The opinion doesn’t address First Amendment issues, but are misrepresentations about holdings/intent and false price predictions categorically unprotected as fraudulent commercial speech, or does the government still need to show knowing falsity and materiality to avoid First Amendment problems? Does it matter that the speakers held themselves out as traders rather than issuers or analysts?
Answer:
Short answer
The Fifth Circuit did not decide any First Amendment question, but its opinion makes clear the theory the government must plead and that ordinary First Amendment protections do not shield traditional fraud allegations: when social‑media posts are alleged to be material misrepresentations knowingly made to induce followers to hand over money or property (the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory), those statements fall within the fraud statutes the court sustains here. By contrast, mere withholding of “valuable economic information” (the right‑to‑control theory condemned in Ciminelli) is not a federal fraud offense and would implicate First Amendment concerns.

What the opinion actually holds and why it matters
- The court reversed because the indictment alleges a classic fraudulent‑inducement scheme: defendants (who “held themselves out to be skilled stock traders” and posted trading activity) allegedly bought securities, posted false or misleading statements to induce followers to buy, and then sold at an inflated price. That pleading alleges a “scheme to defraud” and the requisite “intent to defraud.” (See pp. 1–3, III & III.A–B.)

- The opinion relies on two controlling propositions from the Supreme Court:
  - Ciminelli: the fraud statutes do not criminalize deprivation of merely “valuable economic information” (the right‑to‑control theory). So you cannot obtain a fraud conviction based only on withholding or skewing information that causes someone to make a discretionary economic choice. (Opinion § III.A, discussing Ciminelli.)
  - Kousisis: the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory is valid and protects money/property — a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he schemes to obtain a victim’s money or property by material misstatement, even if the victim is not left economically worse off overall. Kousisis also emphasizes that fraudulent inducement is different from the right‑to‑control theory. (Opinion § III.A–B.)

What the government must allege/prove (per this opinion)
- Material misrepresentation: the indictment must allege a “material misstatement” used to induce a purchase. The opinion repeatedly frames the scheme in terms of “material misstatement” and cites Kousisis and Evans on that element. (See III, III.A.)
- Intent to deceive/intent to obtain money or property: the opinion treats mens rea as necessary — the government must allege the defendants acted with intent to deceive and that obtaining money/property was an object of the fraud (the court accepts the indictment’s allegations that defendants “had money in mind”). (See III, III.B and definitions quoted from Greenlaw/Evans.)
- The opinion does not explicitly recite an evidentiary standard for knowing falsity at trial, but its reliance on intent-to-defraud jurisprudence (and the phrase “material misstatement” and “intent to deceive”) shows the government must allege and ultimately prove that the statements were false/misleading and made with fraudulent intent (i.e., scienter). In other words, the allegation of knowing falsity is embedded in the “intent to defraud” requirement the court upholds.

Role of speaker’s identity (trader vs. issuer/analyst)
- The court focused on what the speakers did and how they represented themselves: they “held themselves out to be skilled stock traders” and used that posture to induce followers to act. The opinion treats those self‑representations as the basis for alleging deception and does not condition liability on whether the speaker was a corporate issuer or a formal securities analyst. Thus, according to the court’s reasoning, a social‑media trader who falsely represents positions or prospects to induce purchases can be charged under the fraudulent‑inducement theory just as an issuer or analyst could be, so long as the government pleads the requisite materiality and intent to deceive. (See I & III.A.)

What the opinion does not decide
- The court did not squarely resolve First Amendment defenses: it did not hold, for example, that all false statements on social media are categorically unprotected or that negligence-level falsity would satisfy the statute. It only held the indictment, as pleaded, sufficiently alleges a traditional fraud (fraudulent inducement) — a paradigmatic unprotected category under existing fraud law. (See opening paragraph and IV.)

Practical takeaway for indictments and First Amendment concerns
- If the government can plead (and later prove) that: (1) the defendant made material false or misleading statements; (2) did so with fraudulent intent (scienter) to deceive followers; and (3) induced followers to hand over money or property (buy securities), then those statements are prosecutable under the fraud statutes and are not saved by the Ciminelli right‑to‑control limitation.
- Conversely, where the government relies only on allegations that a speaker withheld or distorted "economic information" without alleging inducement of a purchase or other transfer of money/property, Ciminelli (and the First Amendment’s protection of some speech) will bar a fraud prosecution.

Concise answer to your explicit questions
- Are the social‑media posts protected? Not if they are pleaded and proved to be material falsehoods knowingly used to induce purchases (fraudulent inducement). If instead the claim rests only on depriving followers of “valuable economic information,” those allegations are insufficient under Ciminelli.
- Does the government still need to show knowing falsity and materiality? Yes — the opinion treats “material misstatement” and “intent to deceive” as core elements the government must allege; scienter (knowing or at least intentional deception) is part of the “intent to defraud” requirement the court upholds.
- Does it matter that speakers were traders (not issuers/analysts)? Not for the basic analysis — the court rests liability on what the speakers represented and intended (they “held themselves out to be skilled stock traders” and allegedly used those representations to induce purchases), so the trader role does not immunize false inducements from fraud law.

### Must the government show that the defendants sought to obtain the followers’ money specifically, or just 'someone’s' money?
Question:
Kousisis emphasizes that obtaining the victim’s money or property must be an object of the scheme. In a pump-and-dump, trades clear through the market; the defendants may not know who their counterparties are, and followers may buy from other sellers. Is it sufficient that the object was to obtain money from market purchasers generally, or must the indictment tie the object of the fraud to obtaining money from the deceived followers in particular?
Answer:
Short answer: The government need not tie the object of the fraud to particular, identified counterparties. Under the Fifth Circuit’s opinion, it is enough to allege a scheme to induce market purchasers (here, social‑media followers) to part with money or property generally — i.e., a fraudulent‑inducement to buy — so long as the indictment pleads a scheme to obtain money or property by materially false statements.

Why: the opinion rests on two doctrinal points.

- Fraudulent‑inducement vs. right‑to‑control. Ciminelli rejected prosecutions that rest solely on a supposed “right to control” (deprivation of valuable economic information), because that theory does not protect a traditional property interest. By contrast, the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory targets a defendant who uses misrepresentations to trick victims into contracts (or purchases) that require handing over money or property. The court held the indictment here alleges a fraudulent‑inducement: defendants posted false information to induce followers to buy securities, thereby causing those followers to part with money (Indictment ¶ 13; Op. III.A).

- No requirement that the fraudster intend to leave the victim net worse off or that the government identify the exact person who suffered a net loss. Kousisis makes clear the statute reaches schemes whose object is “to obtain” the victim’s money or property even if the victim later receives something of equal value or the fraudster does not aim to cause a net pecuniary loss. The Fifth Circuit applied that principle: the allegation that defendants “pumped and dumped to gain a profit” shows they “had money in mind,” and that fraudulent inducement to purchase securities is sufficient to allege the required injury (Op. III.B).

What that means for pump‑and‑dump facts: markets clear through many counterparties and sellers often do not know the identities of buyers. The opinion treats an allegation that defendants induced their followers (or other market purchasers) to buy as alleging the defendants’ object was to obtain money from those purchasers generally. The indictment need not name the specific market counterparties; it must allege (1) material misrepresentations intended to deceive purchasers and (2) an object to obtain money/property by inducing purchases. The Fifth Circuit found those allegations present here and reversed dismissal (Op. III.A–B, IV).

Bottom line: The government can prosecute on a fraudulent‑inducement theory by alleging defendants sought to obtain money from purchasers in the market (including their followers as a class). It need not show that defendants targeted or specifically intended to obtain money from individually identified followers.
