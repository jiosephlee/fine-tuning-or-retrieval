Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# From Right-to-Control to Fraudulent-Inducement: The Fifth Circuit’s Post-Ciminelli Roadmap in Securities Fraud

## Why this case matters
United States v. Constantinescu is the Fifth Circuit’s clear answer to a question that has loomed since the Supreme Court’s decision in Ciminelli (and now under the shadow of Kousisis): when does a fraud prosecution based on misleading speech about investments run afoul of the prohibition on prosecuting the mere deprivation of “valuable economic information”? The decision matters because it supplies a practical, doctrinal roadmap for prosecutors and defense counsel in securities-and-markets prosecutions. By distinguishing the disallowed “right-to-control” theory in Ciminelli from a permissible “fraudulent-inducement” theory embraced in Kousisis, the panel revives a straightforward theory of liability for classic “pump-and-dump” schemes—so long as the indictment alleges that the defendant induced victims to part with money or property.

Put simply: Constantinescu tells us that you can be charged under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 for lying to followers about a stock to get them to buy it, not merely for withholding or misusing information. The opinion is a practical reassurance to prosecutors who must craft indictments that survive Ciminelli’s limits, and an important doctrinal marker for courts grappling with whether deception about investments targets a protected property interest.

## What the Fifth Circuit did
The Fifth Circuit reversed a district-court dismissal of a superseding indictment against several social-media “influencers” accused of orchestrating a pump-and-dump. The indictment alleged that the defendants bought securities, posted false or misleading information to induce followers to buy (pumping the price), then sold their holdings (dumping) to realize profits of roughly $114 million. The district court had concluded the indictment alleged only a scheme to deprive followers of “valuable economic information” — the exact theory rejected in Ciminelli — and therefore failed to plead a deprivation of a traditional property interest.

The Fifth Circuit disagreed. It began from two settled points as shaped by recent Supreme Court decisions. Section 1348 forbids schemes to “defraud” in connection with securities and schemes to obtain money or property by false pretenses; courts should apply concepts from mail and wire fraud doctrine in interpreting the statute. The elements require a scheme to defraud—a material misrepresentation intended to deceive to obtain something of value—and an intent to defraud, meaning an intent both to deceive and to cause harm (but, crucially, not requiring net pecuniary harm to the victim).

Against that backdrop, the panel distinguished Ciminelli on the ground that Ciminelli condemned a “right-to-control” theory—one that treats a victim’s right to make an informed decision as the protected property interest. By contrast, the indictment in Constantinescu charged a fraudulent-inducement theory: defendants allegedly used materially false statements to trick followers into buying securities, i.e., to part with money or property. The court relied on Kousisis, which explicitly validated the fraudulent-inducement route and emphasized that the fraud statutes protect money and property obtained through deception even if the victim is not left economically worse off. Thus, because the indictment alleged the defendants actively induced purchases (the victim handed over money/property), it adequately pleaded deprivation of a traditional property interest and the requisite intent to defraud.

## Analytical perspective: a sensible, careful line — with some open questions
The Fifth Circuit’s approach is doctrinally tidy and practically helpful. It respects Ciminelli’s limit on prosecuting the loss of informational control while preserving long-standing fraud liability for schemes that induce victims to transfer money or other property. That distinction tracks the Supreme Court’s recent signals: not all harms tied to misinformation are constitutionally equivalent, but obtaining money or property by false pretenses remains classic fraud.

Two features make the opinion persuasive. First, it adheres to the statutory text: § 1348 targets schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses … any money or property.” When the government alleges that false statements caused investors to hand over money in exchange for securities, it is pleading precisely what the statute protects. Second, by leaning on Kousisis’ rejection of a net-loss requirement, the court avoids a formalistic trap where a defendant who provides market-value securities or a “pass-through” benefit escapes liability merely because victims end up not strictly worse off.

Still, a few doctrinal seams remain. Ciminelli emphasized that the right-to-control theory risked criminalizing garden-variety political or commercial persuasion and decisionmaking. Constantinescu avoids that pitfall because the alleged deception resulted in a transfer of money. But future cases will test the boundary when the line between “information” and “inducement” blurs—say, where a speaker provides ambiguous endorsements or selective information without an explicit call to buy, or where the causal chain between statements and purchases is attenuated by other market factors. The Fifth Circuit’s analysis presumes a fairly direct nexus—posts that “induce purchase”—and may be less comfortable in borderline factual scenarios.

Another question arises about proof and the indictment stage. The panel appropriately evaluated only whether the indictment alleges the elements, treating those allegations as true for sufficiency review. The government may face more difficult questions at trial: proving that specific posts were materially misleading, that followers actually relied in a way that caused transfers of money, and that defendants acted with the requisite intent rather than mere enthusiasm or puffery. Constantinescu won’t eliminate those evidentiary challenges; it simply keeps the claims in play.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects
For prosecutors: Constantinescu provides a template for drafting indictments post-Ciminelli. Charging documents should (1) avoid alleging only a “right-to-control” deprivation, (2) expressly plead that false communications induced victims to buy or sell securities (i.e., to part with money/property), and (3) allege intent to obtain money or property—showing that the defendant had “money in mind” via purchases, trades, and secret sales. The opinion implicitly sanctions use of fraudulent-inducement theory in social-media and influencer-driven market manipulations, an area where regulators and criminal enforcers have been particularly active.

For defense counsel: the ruling underscores the value of early, fact-driven attacks on the sufficiency of the government’s proof of inducement and causation. Defenses that the posts were non-actionable puffery, opinion, or not materially misleading may find traction at dismissal or, more realistically, at trial. The opinion also leaves room to contest scienter and reliance at later stages—sections where Ciminelli’s concerns about criminalizing ordinary informational asymmetries might still be pressed.

For courts: Constantinescu will likely be cited as a useful post-Ciminelli precedent that preserves a narrow but potent route for fraud prosecutions. Courts will need to police the line between fraudulent inducement (properly criminal) and prosecutions premised on informational control (improper under Ciminelli). That policing will require careful factual parsing—asking whether the government’s theory rests on an allegation of induced transfers of money/property or merely on deception about information that influenced discretionary decisions.

For markets and platforms: the decision signals increased legal risk for influencers and social-media traders who coordinate misleading promotions tied directly to trading activity. Platforms and compliance officers may see pressure to enhance disclosures and moderation around paid promotions and trading advice to limit potential criminal exposure.

## Conclusion
Constantinescu performs a useful doctrinal balancing act. It confines Ciminelli’s prohibition on right-to-control prosecutions without collapsing the fraudulent-inducement theory into something indistinguishable from informational regulation. For prosecutors it restores a viable path to charge traditional pump-and-dump schemes conducted via social media; for defendants it preserves substantial evidentiary and First Amendment defenses at trial. The key lesson is straightforward: after Ciminelli, the government must allege (and later prove) that false statements induced victims to hand over money or property—not merely that the defendant deprived them of valuable economic information. Where that link is adequately pleaded, Constantinescu shows the fraud statutes still reach classic market-manipulation schemes.



# No Net-Loss Needed: Intent to Profit as Intent to Defraud in Pump‑and‑Dump Prosecutions

## Why this case matters

United States v. Constantinescu is a short but consequential Fifth Circuit opinion that clears a significant procedural hurdle for prosecutors pursuing “pump-and-dump” prosecutions under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. At issue was a narrow but potent question: must an indictment alleging securities fraud plead that defendants intended to cause their followers a net pecuniary loss, or is an intent to obtain money (to “profit”) by fraudulently inducing purchases enough? The Fifth Circuit answered decisively that it is enough. Drawing heavily on two recent Supreme Court decisions—Ciminelli and Kousisis—the court reversed the district court’s dismissal of the indictment, holding that an allegation that influencers posted false information to induce followers to buy securities adequately alleges both a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud, even if followers later held market‑priced shares and may not have suffered a net loss.

That holding matters because it reaffirms the reach of the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory as the primary way to litigate many digital-era market manipulation schemes, and it limits the scope of Ciminelli’s restriction of the “right‑to‑control” theory. For social‑media driven trading schemes—where defendants might argue that victims received something of value (shares) and therefore suffered no compensable property loss—Constantinescu provides prosecutors with a path forward at the indictment stage by focusing on the defendant’s object: obtaining money by deception.

## What the Fifth Circuit held and why

The factual posture is archetypal: defendants with large social‑media followings allegedly bought low, hyped certain securities with false and misleading posts to induce followers to buy (pumping the price), then sold their holdings (dumping), reaping roughly $114 million. The district court dismissed, reasoning that the indictment only alleged deprivation of “valuable economic information” (a Ciminelli‑style right‑to‑control theory) and thus failed to plead a protected property interest.

The Fifth Circuit reversed. It first recognized Ciminelli’s warning that federal fraud statutes do not protect an abstract “right to valuable economic information.” But the court distinguished this case from Ciminelli because the indictment here was framed as a fraudulent‑inducement scheme: defendants allegedly used material misrepresentations to trick followers into parting with money or property—namely, purchasing securities. That, the court emphasized, is a paradigmatic protection of “money or property,” not an attempt to recast informational harms as property deprivations.

On intent, the defendants argued that their motive was to profit—not to harm followers—and that Kousisis requires proof of a desire to hurt victims’ bottom lines. The Fifth Circuit read Kousisis the other way: the Supreme Court had rejected a requirement that a fraudster intend to leave the victim economically worse off. Kousisis holds that inducing a victim to hand over money or property by deception satisfies the intent‑to‑defraud element even if the victim receives something of equal value or is not left with a net loss. Applying Kousisis, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment’s allegation that defendants posted lies “so that other investors were induced to purchase the security” adequately alleges an intent to obtain money via deceit.

Put simply: where false statements cause people to buy securities—and the defendant’s object is to sell into that inflated demand—alleging an intent to profit is functionally alleging the requisite intent to defraud under § 1348.

## Analytical perspective: strengths and limits of the opinion

The opinion is careful and doctrinally conservative: it does not expand the scope of protected interests beyond the established “money or property” baseline, and it hews closely to Supreme Court guidance. That caution helps the decision withstand scrutiny: by distinguishing Ciminelli’s right‑to‑control theory and leaning on Kousisis to dispel any “no‑net‑loss” requirement, the Fifth Circuit avoids a frontal clash with the Supreme Court while still preserving prosecutorial viability in cases that look like classic inducement frauds.

At the same time, the opinion leaves some lines to be drawn later. The court decided only the sufficiency of the indictment under the well‑established standard of accepting allegations as true and asking whether an offense has been stated. It did not, and quite properly could not, resolve factual questions that will arise at trial—e.g., whether alleged representations were materially false, whether followers actually relied on them, or whether the transactions constituted transfers of money or property in the legally meaningful sense once market mechanics and the mutuality of trading are unfolded.

That restraint matters because the prosecution’s theory can look different in operation than on paper. Online market dynamics, the presence of contrarian traders, and secondary market behavior can complicate notions of direct causal transfer. While Kousisis allows conviction without proof of net victim loss, it still requires a fraudulent inducement that results in the victim’s parting with money or property. Proving that causal chain at trial—rather than merely alleging it at the indictment stage—will be the prosecutor’s work.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Constantinescu signals a practical doctrinal posture: courts will treat fraudulent‑inducement theories as viable in modern market‑manipulation prosecutions, and they will not impose a judicially created “victim‑loss” gloss onto § 1348. That has several foreseeable effects.

First, prosecutors are likely to continue bringing § 1348 charges against social‑media influencers and coordinated groups that hype securities for personal gain. The opinion reduces the chance that such indictments will be dismissed at the pleading stage on the grounds that victims received marketable shares.

Second, defense strategies premised on the absence of net pecuniary harm will have less force at motions to dismiss. Defendants will increasingly need to press these arguments at summary judgment or trial, converting them into factual disputes about causation, reliance, materiality, and whether alleged misstatements were puffery versus materially misleading.

Third, the decision preserves a meaningful boundary drawn in Ciminelli: courts will still police attempts to recast informational harms as property deprivation. But that boundary operates largely as a limit on creative prosecutorial theories based solely on informational control, not as a shelter for actors who allegedly lie to induce transactions.

Finally, there are policy stakes. Treating intent to obtain money via deception as sufficient to satisfy § 1348’s mens rea aligns criminal liability with intuitive wrongdoing in the securities context, but it also raises classic concerns about overcriminalization in markets where transactions are mutual and noisy. Those concerns will animate defenses and appellate scrutiny as these cases progress.

## Bottom line

Constantinescu is a clear win for the government on pleading standards in pump‑and‑dump cases: allege a fraudulent inducement that causes followers to purchase securities and allege the defendant’s object to obtain money, and you have adequately pled a scheme and intent to defraud under § 1348. The Fifth Circuit respected Ciminelli’s limits while relying on Kousisis to dispel any requirement of intended victim net loss. The real fights—to prove causation, materiality, and reliance—remain for trial, but prosecutors seeking to hold social‑media traders criminally accountable now have firmer footing at the gate.



# Criminal § 1348 as Mail/Wire Fraud’s Twin: Doctrinal Borrowing and the Edges of Overlap with Rule 10b-5

## Why United States v. Constantinescu matters

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Constantinescu is a compact but consequential ruling about how courts should read 18 U.S.C. § 1348 — the federal criminal securities fraud statute — in light of the doctrinal contours developed under the mail and wire fraud statutes and recent Supreme Court decisions like Ciminelli and Kousisis. At stake is whether prosecutors can deploy § 1348 as a broadly applicable fraud tool in securities markets, and how far criminal liability can reach conduct that looks more like deceptive communications to investors than the theft of a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit says: treat § 1348 much like mail/wire fraud. That answer vindicates the indictment in a social‑media–enabled “pump and dump” prosecution, but it also raises familiar questions about overlap with Rule 10b‑5 and about the statute’s bounds.

## What the court held, in plain terms

The defendants were accused of using social media followings to “pump” microcap stocks with false statements and then “dump” shares for profit. The district court dismissed, saying the indictment alleged only the deprivation of valuable economic information — a theory foreclosed by Ciminelli because the right‑to‑control or information‑as‑property theory is not a protected interest under the fraud statutes. The Fifth Circuit reversed. It accepted the government’s framing that the indictment alleged a fraudulent‑inducement scheme — i.e., misrepresentations designed to induce followers to buy securities, thereby parting with money or property. Relying on § 1348’s textual language and on the Supreme Court’s recent clarifications in Kousisis, the panel treated § 1348 as importing the same “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud” concepts used in mail/wire fraud jurisprudence, and held that an allegation of inducing purchases sufficed to state an offense even if victims ultimately received securities of market value (because criminal fraud turns on obtaining money/property by deception, not on causing a net pecuniary loss).

## How the opinion borrows from mail/wire fraud — and why that matters

The Fifth Circuit’s approach is methodologically straightforward: § 1348 criminalizes schemes to “defraud” in connection with securities and schemes to obtain “money or property” by false pretenses. Because the statute is textually analogous to the mail and wire fraud statutes, the court reasoned that courts may look to the well‑developed mail/wire fraud doctrine for concepts like material misrepresentation, fraudulent inducement, and the dual prongs of intent (to deceive and to cause harm or obtain value). That borrowing yields predictable results: a fraudulent inducement — a false statement intended to cause a person to give up money or property — is a paradigmatic § 1348 offense.

This alignment matters for prosecutors and defense counsel alike. On one hand, it preserves prosecutorial flexibility: prosecutors can charge online pump‑and‑dump schemes, tipsters, and coordinated disinformation efforts under a criminal securities fraud statute without needing to shoehorn a Rule 10b‑5 theory into criminal law or prove reliance in the way civil securities claims sometimes require. On the other hand, it imports into § 1348 some of the doctrinal guardrails that mail/wire fraud decisions have supplied in recent years (for example, the rejection of purely informational right‑to‑control theories post‑Ciminelli).

## Where § 1348 overlaps — and diverges — from Rule 10b‑5

There is significant practical overlap between criminal § 1348 prosecutions and civil actions under Rule 10b‑5. Both target deceptive schemes in connection with the purchase or sale of securities. But a few operational and doctrinal differences are important:

- Reliance and causation: Civil Rule 10b‑5 often requires, depending on the claim and jurisdiction, some showing of reliance or loss causation (and private plaintiffs generally must show actual losses to recover damages). Criminal § 1348, as the Fifth Circuit emphasized through Kousisis, does not require proof that the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss or even that the defendant intended to leave victims worse off; it is sufficient that the defendant schemed to obtain money or property by deception. That lowers the threshold for criminal liability relative to some civil formulations.

- Mens rea and intent: Criminal § 1348 requires proof of specific criminal intent — intent to defraud — which is a higher bar than negligence or recklessness in many civil contexts. But in practice intent can be inferred from the deceptive scheme and circumstantial evidence (e.g., secret sales, coordinated timing), making prosecutions feasible even when civil plaintiffs might struggle to establish scienter.

- Criminal tools: § 1348 enables conspiracy and aiding‑and‑abetting charges, joinder of co‑conspirators, and the full panoply of criminal discovery and enforcement mechanisms (subpoenas, grand juries, forfeiture). Those tools can be more attractive to prosecutors when the conduct implicates broad market misrepresentation schemes or coordinated campaigns across social platforms.

- Remedies and stakes: Criminal prosecutions bring incarceration, fines, and forfeiture; civil Rule 10b‑5 claims produce damages, disgorgement, and injunctive relief. The different stakes shape enforcement choices and defense strategies.

So while doctrinally akin, § 1348 and Rule 10b‑5 serve different institutional roles. The Fifth Circuit’s decision underscores that § 1348 can be used where misleading statements are the means by which victims are induced to part with money, independent of civil proof frameworks.

## Potential doctrinal frictions and fair‑notice concerns

Treating § 1348 as mail/wire fraud’s twin helps prosecutors fill enforcement gaps, but it also invites two interrelated concerns.

First, overbreadth. If courts continue to treat § 1348 as a general-purpose fraud statute for securities markets, it risks sweeping in a wide range of deceptive communications that, while wrong or manipulative, might better be policed by regulatory, administrative, or civil regimes. The social‑media context in Constantinescu crystallizes this risk: influencers, tipsters, and commentators operate in a noisy marketplace of opinion, and criminalization of borderline or ambiguous statements could chill speech and interfere with legitimate market commentary. Ciminelli’s rejection of a pure right‑to‑control information theory is an important boundary, but it does not answer all close cases where the line between dishonest inducement and puffery or opinion is fuzzy.

Second, fair notice. Criminal statutes require that people have adequate notice of what conduct is prohibited. Broad reliance on analogies to mail/wire fraud terminologies like “scheme to defraud” may leave actors uncertain whether particular social‑media posts, coordinated trading strategies, or promotional arrangements fall within criminal reach. The Fifth Circuit’s insistence that the indictment allege that followers were induced to part with money or property is an important notice signal, but prosecutors can often plead inducement in many factual circumstances, so courts will need to ensure indictments (and later, jury instructions) provide sufficient specificity about the alleged misrepresentations and the elements of the scheme.

These concerns counsel for careful gatekeeping at the indictment and jury‑instruction stages. Judges should demand clear pleading of the alleged misrepresentations, the mechanism by which victims were induced to part with money or property, and the connection between the deceit and the alleged economic transfer. Absent that, there is a risk that criminal law becomes a broad enforcement backstop for every manipulative market tactic.

## Downstream effects and open questions

Constantinescu will likely encourage prosecutors to pursue social‑media pump‑and‑dump cases under § 1348 rather than (or in addition to) SEC civil actions or other criminal statutes. It also signals that courts will read § 1348 through mail/wire fraud categories (fraudulent inducement, scheme to defraud, intent to obtain money/property) while applying Ciminelli/Kousisis limits to bar purely informational theories.

That posture raises several open questions courts will soon face: How granular must an indictment be about the allegedly deceptive statements and the timing of trades to satisfy fair notice? How will courts distinguish actionable false statements from protected opinions or market commentary, particularly in the unregulated landscape of social‑media influencers? And how will jury instructions clarify that § 1348 requires an object of obtaining money or property while not reinstating a slippery “information as property” theory?

Finally, coordination with SEC enforcement will be important. Civil and administrative remedies can sometimes address market misconduct with flexible tools and lower burdens of proof; criminalization should remain reserved for cases where the government can prove intentional, material deception designed to procure money or property. Constantinescu reinforces that standard while expanding prosecutors’ practical ability to proceed — a development that courts, practitioners, and market participants will need to monitor closely.

## Conclusion

United States v. Constantinescu reaffirms the convergence between § 1348 and mail/wire fraud doctrines and clarifies that criminal securities fraud reaches fraudulent inducement to buy securities even where victims may not suffer a net pecuniary loss. That clarity will empower enforcement against modern pump‑and‑dump schemes conducted over social platforms. But it also sharpens longstanding concerns about breadth and notice when prosecutors treat § 1348 as a general fraud statute for market speech. The next wave of cases will determine whether courts can preserve the statute’s utility without allowing criminal law to overreach into the contours of market commentary and information flows.



# Influencer Hype Meets Criminal Fraud: Speech, Social Media, and the Line Between Puffs and Deception

## Why this case matters

United States v. Constantinescu is the Fifth Circuit’s blunt reminder that social-media trading hype can cross from protected puffery into criminal securities fraud when it is tied to a fraudulent inducement scheme. At bottom, the court held that an indictment alleging that high-profile social-media traders “pumped” securities with false and misleading posts and then “dumped” into followers’ purchases sufficiently pleads a scheme to defraud and the requisite intent under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. That answer is straightforward under the Supreme Court’s recent clarifications rejecting a right-to-control theory (Ciminelli) and confirming the viability of a fraudulent-inducement theory even where victims are not left economically worse off (Kousisis). But the decision also raises practical and doctrinal questions about how ordinary promotional speech in fast-moving online markets will be treated by prosecutors, and where the constitutional and regulatory lines should be drawn.

## The court’s reasoning — concise walkthrough

The Fifth Circuit framed the issue as one of indictment sufficiency. The district court had dismissed the indictment on the ground that the alleged misconduct—depriving followers of valuable economic information—did not target a “traditional property interest” and so could not support fraud under Ciminelli. The Fifth Circuit reversed, identifying two critical points.

First, the indictment did not rest on the right-to-control theory the Supreme Court rejected in Ciminelli. Rather, it pleaded a classic fraudulent-inducement theory: defendants made material misrepresentations to induce followers to buy securities, thereby causing followers to part with money or property. Because fraudulent inducement targets money or property transferred under false pretenses, Ciminelli’s restriction on information-as-property did not doom the indictment.

Second, the court relied on Kousisis to reject the defendants’ contention that intent to harm victims’ economic interests is necessary. Kousisis holds that a fraudster can be guilty when the object of the scheme is to obtain money or property—even if the victim is not left worse off in net terms. Thus, according to the Fifth Circuit, allegations that the defendants recruited followers via false posts to purchase securities, and then sold into that demand to realize $114 million in profits, sufficiently allege both a scheme and the intent to defraud.

Taken together, the court concluded the indictment adequately alleged each material element of securities fraud and remanded for trial.

## Analytical perspective: what the opinion gets right — and what it leaves open

The Fifth Circuit correctly applied the Supreme Court’s recent doctrinal signals. Ciminelli narrowed the universe of cognizable property interests; Kousisis clarified that fraudulent inducement remains a valid pathway to liability and that prosecutors need not prove victims were left with net losses. Reading the two decisions together, the panel’s conclusion—that inducing purchases through deception is classic fraud—fits neatly within current precedent.

But the opinion is also modest: it resolves an indictment-sufficiency question, not the merits of any conviction, and it does not try to map a bright-line rule between permissible promotional speech and criminal deception. That restraint matters. Social-media-driven markets turn on speech that often mixes bullish hyperbole, selectively disclosed positions, and aggressive self-promotion. How courts will separate criminally actionable lies from routine “puffs” remains a fact-intensive question to be resolved at trial (or on summary judgment), not on a facial reading of an indictment.

Two doctrinally important uncertainties follow. First, what degree of specificity must an indictment (or later proof at trial) show about the defendant’s awareness of falsity and specific intent to induce purchases? The panel leaned on allegations that the influencers “post[ed] false and misleading information . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase,” and on the profit motive; but proving scienter in a world of aspirational claims and shifting positions can be hard. Second, the decision does not address how intermediary speech (retweets, algorithmic boosts, or coordinated but not centrally orchestrated hype) will be treated, leaving open how conspiracy and aiding-and-abetting theories will fare as prosecutors pursue sophisticated networks of promoters.

## First Amendment friction: will speech be chilled?

The opinion increases the exposure of prominent online traders and “finfluencers” to criminal prosecution when their posts are part of an allegation that they intended to induce purchases under materially false pretenses. That exposure raises legitimate free-speech concerns.

However, the opinion does not criminalize opinion, advocacy, or harmless exaggeration. The fraudulent-inducement theory targets deceptive misrepresentations of fact—for example, statements falsely claiming an executed buy or invented inside information when the post is designed to cause others to buy. Courts traditionally distinguish protected opinion and puffery from materially deceptive factual assertions. Under existing doctrine, robust advocacy about a security’s prospects remains protected unless it crosses into knowingly false or materially misleading factual claims that are used to obtain money or property.

Still, the line is blurrier on social media where a post can be both a recommendation and a representation about one’s trading position, and where followers often rely on the influencer’s stated “track record.” The risk is real that overbroad or aggressive prosecutions could chill legitimate commentary or disclosure-poor hype. To avoid that chilling effect, prosecutions will need to be careful to allege and prove objective falsity and scienter, not mere sophistication or success-based envy.

## Compliance risks for online traders and finfluencers

Practitioners and market participants should treat Constantinescu as a clear signal that prosecutors view social-media-driven market manipulation as within the reach of federal securities fraud statutes when easily-pleaded indicia of a pump-and-dump exist. Specific compliance lessons:

- Be transparent about positions and timing. Saying “I bought X today” when you did not, or obscuring undisclosed plans to sell while encouraging purchases, is a red flag. Disclosures should be contemporaneous and accurate.
- Avoid affirmative misrepresentations of fact. Opinion and analysis are less risky than demonstrably false statements (e.g., fabricated trades, invented insider information).
- Disclose conflicts and compensation. Paid promotions or undisclosed coordination with other market actors who will buy or sell should be disclosed to avoid allegations of deceptive intent.
- Keep records. Screenshots, trading logs, and contemporaneous notes about why a post was made can help rebut allegations of knowing falsity or fraudulent intent.
- Beware of coordinated activity. Coordinated campaigns to drive demand—especially when followed by sell-offs—invite conspiracy and aiding-and-abetting scrutiny.
- Consult counsel before paid endorsements or coordinated campaigns, especially for securities with low liquidity, where falsely induced purchases can move prices more dramatically.

## Downstream effects and prosecution strategy

Expect prosecutors to lean on the fraudulent-inducement framework in future pump-and-dump cases grounded in social-media activity. The Constantinescu panel emphasizes that prosecutors do not need to prove victims ended up worse off, only that the defendants intended to induce purchases and obtained money or property. That lowers one hurdle for the government—but other evidentiary challenges remain: establishing materiality, falsity, reliance (if required at trial), and scienter.

Defendants will likely focus on showing that statements were opinion or puffery, that followers’ purchases were independent, or that the alleged misstatements were immaterial. They may also press First Amendment defenses and urge heightened scienter standards where expression and advocacy overlap. Defense counsel will push factual distinctions: did the influencer genuinely hold a position and express belief, or fabricate positions and conceal sell orders? The factual texture will matter enormously.

Regulators (SEC) and civil plaintiffs may also be encouraged by the decision. Criminal indictments often spur parallel civil enforcement and private damages claims; an indictment that survives dismissal can embolden follow-on actions, settlements, and regulatory enforcement.

## Bottom line

Constantinescu is a reminder that modern pump-and-dump schemes can be prosecuted under the fraudulent-inducement theory even when the misconduct plays out on Twitter, Instagram, Discord, or TikTok. The Fifth Circuit applied recent Supreme Court principles to draw a sensible boundary: information itself is not property under Ciminelli, but false statements that induce the transfer of money or securities remain classic fraud. That framework protects legitimate advocacy while preserving a tool to police deceptive, profit-driven manipulation of retail investors.

For finfluencers and online traders, the practical takeaway is simple: treat public trading commentary as potentially consequential legal conduct. Accurate disclosures, careful separation of opinion from asserted facts, and transparent handling of compensation and positions are the best prophylactics against criminal and regulatory exposure. For courts and policymakers, the hard work remains: policing deception without chilling speech, and developing fact-sensitive standards that reflect how marketplace communication now occurs in real time and at scale.
