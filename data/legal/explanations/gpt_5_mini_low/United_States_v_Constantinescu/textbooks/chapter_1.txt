Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Alleged conduct: coordinated purchase, promotional ‘pump’ via false social‑media posts, and secret ‘dump’ for profit

The indictment alleges a classic “pump‑and‑dump” scheme carried out through coordinated trading and social‑media promotion. According to the charging document, the defendants purchased shares of target securities and then used their social‑media presences to “pump” prices by posting false and misleading statements about those securities. Those posts were calculated to induce followers and other retail investors to buy the promoted stocks, driving up market prices. Once the price was inflated, the defendants allegedly “dumped” their holdings—selling into the artificially elevated market—realizing substantial gains. The government frames these acts as a single fraudulent scheme in which misrepresentations on social media were an integral means of causing victims to part with money by purchasing securities.

## Defendants and platforms; public persona as skilled traders; alleged $114 million in profits

The indictment names eight defendants—Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight (who later pleaded guilty). It emphasizes that each defendant maintained large followings across multiple social‑media platforms and cultivated a public persona as a skilled trader who regularly posted trading activity and investment advice. The government alleges that, by leveraging those followings and reputations, the defendants induced followers to buy target securities. The indictment quantifies the alleged benefit to the defendants at approximately $114 million in profits derived from the scheme.

## Charges: 18 U.S.C. § 1349 (conspiracy) and 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2 (securities fraud and aiding/abetting)

The indictment charges the remaining defendants with conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and with substantive securities‑fraud offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, together with aiding and abetting liability under 18 U.S.C. § 2 where applicable. Section 1348 criminalizes schemes to defraud in connection with any security and schemes to obtain money or property by means of false or fraudulent pretenses in connection with the purchase or sale of securities. The conspiracy count alleges agreement among the defendants to carry out the pump‑and‑dump scheme; the substantive counts allege the underlying fraudulent acts by which the defendants induced purchases of securities.

## District court ruling: indictment fails to allege deprivation of traditional property; focuses only on loss of valuable economic information

The district court dismissed the indictment. Its dismissal rested on the view that the indictment did not allege deprivation of a “traditional property interest” protected by the federal fraud statutes. The district court treated the government’s theory as effectively alleging the defendants deprived their followers of valuable economic information—i.e., by concealing material facts about the defendants’ trading positions or intentions—rather than alleging that the defendants obtained victims’ money or property. Relying on Supreme Court precedent excluding a “right‑to‑control” theory that treats the right to economic information as property, the district court concluded the indictment failed to state an offense because it charged only a deprivation of information rather than a cognizable property interest.

## Government’s timely appeal

The government appealed the dismissal to the Fifth Circuit. Daniel Knight, one of the original defendants, pleaded guilty before the appeal; the remaining defendants pursued the district court’s dismissal order. The government’s appeal challenged the district court’s legal conclusion that the indictment did not sufficiently allege a scheme and intent to defraud within the meaning of § 1348.

## Standard of review: de novo; taking indictment’s allegations as true; requirements for indictment sufficiency (enumerate elements, fair notice, double‑jeopardy protection)

On appeal, the Fifth Circuit reviewed the sufficiency of the indictment de novo. Under settled circuit law, the appellate court accepts the facts alleged in the indictment as true for purposes of the sufficiency inquiry and asks whether those allegations, so taken, state an offense. An indictment must meet three core requirements to be legally sufficient: it must enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; it must fairly inform the defendant of the charges so the defendant can prepare a defense; and it must provide the defendant with protection against double jeopardy in any future prosecution for the same conduct. In practice, this means the indictment must allege the material elements of the securities fraud and conspiracy statutes—here, a scheme to defraud (including material misrepresentations), the requisite intent to defraud, and the object of obtaining money or property through the scheme—such that the defendants receive fair notice of the conduct at issue and the court can determine whether the charge fits within the fraud statutes’ scope.
