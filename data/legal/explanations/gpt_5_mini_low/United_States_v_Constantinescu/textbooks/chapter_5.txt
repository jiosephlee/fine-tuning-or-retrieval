Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 5: # Disposition and Practical Significance

## Holding and disposition
The Fifth Circuit reversed the district court’s dismissal of the superseding indictment and remanded for further proceedings. The court held that, as pleaded, the indictment sufficiently alleges both (1) a “scheme to defraud” under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and (2) the requisite “intent to defraud.” The panel concluded that the indictment does not rely on the disallowed “right‑to‑control” theory rejected in Ciminelli, but instead alleges a classic fraudulent‑inducement scheme: defendants made material misrepresentations to induce followers to hand over money (by buying securities), and defendants’ object was to obtain money by selling their positions at inflated prices. Because the pleadings, taken as true at this stage, allege deception plus an object of obtaining money or property, the indictment survives a facial sufficiency challenge.

## What survives to trial
Reversal of the dismissal returns the case to the ordinary criminal process: the government must still prove the charged offenses beyond a reasonable doubt at trial. Important elements the government must ultimately establish with evidence are the same elements whose pleading the court found sufficient:

- That defendants made material misrepresentations or omissions in connection with the purchase or sale of securities;
- That those misrepresentations were part of a scheme to defraud (i.e., a plan to induce purchases so defendants could profit);
- That defendants acted with the requisite intent to deceive and to obtain money or property (the opinion treats obtaining money as the object of the fraud, even if the victims do not suffer net pecuniary loss);
- That the alleged acts occurred in connection with the purchase or sale of securities.

The opinion does not adjudicate the factual merits. It treats the indictment’s allegations as true for purposes of sufficiency review; factual disputes, credibility contests, and evidentiary questions remain for the district court and jury.

## Doctrinal takeaways
Several doctrinal points emerge from the court’s reasoning, grounded in Supreme Court precedent cited in the opinion:

- Fraudulent‑inducement theory remains a valid basis for prosecution under § 1348. If a defendant uses materially false statements to induce a victim to hand over money or property (for example, to buy securities), that theory targets a traditional property interest and is consistent with Ciminelli and Kousisis as interpreted here.
- There is no requirement that the defendant intend to leave the victim worse off in net economic terms. Following Kousisis, the Fifth Circuit reiterates that the offense can be complete when the defendant obtains the victim’s money or property by deceit, even if the victim receives something of market value in return or ultimately suffers no net loss.
- The right‑to‑control theory—treating the victim’s informational advantage or control over assets as the protected property interest—remains impermissible after Ciminelli. The panel distinguished that theory from fraudulent inducement and expressly found the indictment did not rely on right‑to‑control.

These doctrinal conclusions align § 1348 analysis with the line of mail/wire fraud cases the court cites, using the fraudulent‑inducement framework to define the protected property interest.

## Implications for social‑media promoters and market‑manipulation prosecutions
The decision signals several practical consequences for prosecutions (and for compliance) in the age of social‑media stock promotion:

- Posts by influencers that knowingly contain material falsehoods or misleading statements and are designed to cause followers to buy securities can form the basis for securities‑fraud indictments under § 1348 when the object is to obtain money or property (e.g., to generate a sell‑off profit).
- The form of the statement (social post, tweet, video) does not immunize the speaker from criminal liability; what matters is whether the alleged misrepresentations were material, connected to a purchase or sale, and intended to induce transactions that benefit the promoter.
- Evidence that promoters coordinated purchases, concealed their own sales, or otherwise structured communications to create artificial demand will be particularly relevant to proving scheme and intent.

For practitioners advising social‑media promoters, the decision underscores the importance of truthful, non‑misleading statements and proper disclosures of positions and conflicts; failing that can pose criminal as well as civil and regulatory risk.

## Limits and what this opinion does not change
The opinion is narrowly framed and should not be read to expand the elements of fraud or lower the government’s burden:

- The court resolved only a pleading sufficiency challenge. It did not find that the government has proved the elements, nor did it relax the beyond‑a‑reasonable‑doubt standard or the need for admissible evidence at trial.
- Materiality and scienter remain essential elements. The panel’s decision rests on the indictment’s allegations that the misstatements were material and made with deceptive intent; the government will still need to prove those elements at trial.
- The opinion does not resurrect right‑to‑control claims or otherwise authorize prosecutions based solely on deprivation of informational advantages. Ciminelli’s bar against treating valuable economic information as a protected property interest remains intact.

In short, United States v. Constantinescu clarifies that properly pleaded fraudulent‑inducement allegations rooted in social‑media market‑manipulation can survive a motion to dismiss under § 1348, but it preserves ordinary limits on the government’s proof obligations and the doctrinal boundary established by Ciminelli.
