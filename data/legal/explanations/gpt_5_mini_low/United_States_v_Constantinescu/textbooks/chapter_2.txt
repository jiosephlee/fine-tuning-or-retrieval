Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 2: # Governing Law: Securities Fraud under § 1348 and Indictment Sufficiency

This chapter explains the legal framework the Fifth Circuit applied in United States v. Constantinescu to evaluate whether the superseding indictment for securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 was legally sufficient. The court’s analysis rests on two linked inquiries: (1) what § 1348 requires (as informed by mail/wire fraud doctrine), and (2) whether the indictment, taken as true, alleged each material element sufficiently to give defendants notice and to bar future prosecutions for the same conduct.

## Text of § 1348: schemes to defraud and schemes to obtain money or property in connection with securities

Section 1348 criminalizes two closely related types of wrongs in the securities context. In plain terms, it reaches (A) schemes to “defraud any person in connection with . . . any security,” and (B) schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” The opinion treats those two formulations as tracking the classic fraud picture: an actionable scheme involves deception (a material misrepresentation or omission) and seeks to obtain money or property in connection with securities transactions. The Fifth Circuit framed the statute’s requirements in terms that closely parallel mail and wire fraud doctrine, which provides interpretive guidance where § 1348’s terms are not otherwise defined by circuit precedent.

## Borrowing from mail and wire fraud: Greenlaw and Evans as interpretive anchors

Because this circuit had relatively little independent securities-fraud precedent, the court relied on prior Fifth Circuit mail-and-wire fraud decisions as interpretive anchors. The opinion cites United States v. Greenlaw and United States v. Evans for the two-part framing used throughout: a fraud under § 1348 requires (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” Those earlier cases supply vocabulary and tests—what counts as a “scheme,” what makes a misrepresentation “material,” and what constitutes the requisite mental state—so the court treated mail/wire fraud doctrine as controlling interpretive guidance, consistent with how other courts have analyzed § 1348.

## Elements: scheme to defraud (material misrepresentation to obtain value) and intent to deceive plus causing harm

The opinion sets out the operative elements with precision drawn from Evans and Greenlaw:

- Scheme to defraud: The indictment must allege a material misrepresentation (or omission) that is intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value—typically money or property. The court emphasizes that the misrepresentation must be material and that the object of the scheme must be to obtain value from the deceived party.

- Intent to defraud: The government must allege that the defendant acted with intent to deceive and with an objective to cause some harm to result from the deceit. The panel borrowed the two-part formulation used in prior decisions—intent to deceive and an intent that deceit produce a harmful result—while recognizing recent Supreme Court refinements (discussed below) about what kinds of harms or transfers satisfy the statute.

The opinion applies those elements to the indictment’s allegations that defendants purchased shares, published false and misleading posts to induce followers to buy, and then sold into the resulting price rise—allegedly obtaining $114 million in proceeds. In the Fifth Circuit’s view, those allegations plausibly describe a scheme in which defendants used material misrepresentations to obtain money or property (followers’ purchase proceeds, or transfers of value in the securities market) and did so with fraudulent intent.

## Supreme Court synthesis: how Ciminelli and Kousisis refine the scope of “fraud” under federal statutes

Because recent Supreme Court decisions clarified the difference between permissible fraud theories and theories that extend the statutes too far, the panel closely considered Ciminelli and Kousisis and applied their teachings to the indictment.

- Ciminelli (wire‑fraud context): The Court rejected the so‑called “right-to-control” theory of fraud—i.e., a theory that treats the victim’s “right to valuable economic information” as a protected property interest. Ciminelli holds that the federal fraud statutes protect traditional property interests and do not reach mere deprivation of valuable information needed to make discretionary economic decisions. The Fifth Circuit read Ciminelli as a limit: fraud cannot be based solely on the loss of information or control over decision-making; there must be a protected property or money interest at stake.

- Kousisis (fraudulent‑inducement theory): Kousisis clarifies that the fraudulent-inducement theory—where a defendant uses material falsehoods to induce another to enter a transaction that hands over money or property—is a valid basis for fraud liability. Crucially, Kousisis rejects the notion that the government must show the defendant intended to make the victim net worse off. It is enough that the defendant intended to obtain the victim’s money or property through deceit, even if the victim ostensibly received something of market value in return. Kousisis also explains that the fraudulent-inducement theory is not a repackaged right-to-control theory because it directly protects money and property, not merely information rights.

Applying those precedents, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the Constantinescu indictment did not rely on a forbidden right-to-control theory. Instead, it charged a classic fraudulent-inducement scheme—social-media posts that induced followers to buy securities (i.e., to part with money or transfer property) on false premises so defendants could sell at inflated prices. Under Kousisis, the government need not allege that victims were left economically worse off; it is enough that defendants intended to and did obtain money or property through the deception.

## Indictment sufficiency: pleading each material element; fair notice; double-jeopardy protection

The court reviewed the indictment’s sufficiency de novo and applied the conventional standards that govern challenges under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 12(b) to factual sufficiency of an indictment:

- Pleading each material element: An indictment must set out the prima facie elements of the offense. The Fifth Circuit relied on cases such as United States v. Gaytan and United States v. Guzman-Ocampo to restate this baseline requirement. For § 1348 offenses, that means the indictment must allege the scheme (including the material misrepresentations) and the defendant’s intent to obtain money or property by fraud.

- Fair notice to defendants: The indictment must fairly inform defendants of the charges so they can prepare a defense. The court treated the indictment’s allegations—the purchases, the false social-media posts, the induced purchases by followers, and the defendants’ secret sales—as sufficiently specific to inform each defendant of the charged conduct.

- Double jeopardy protection: The indictment must provide enough detail to prevent later prosecutorial duplication for the same conduct. Alleging the material elements and the essential facts of the alleged scheme satisfies this requirement per the cited precedents (Fontenot; Crow).

Taken together, the panel concluded that the superseding indictment satisfied these pleading requirements: it alleged a scheme to fraudulently induce followers to buy securities (a deprivation of money/property), alleged defendants acted with intent to deceive and to obtain money (and indeed profited), and thereby fairly informed defendants of the charges while identifying the material elements necessary to bar duplicative prosecutions.

Conclusion tied to the opinion’s holding

Grounded in the mail/wire fraud framework and informed by the Supreme Court’s limiting and clarifying decisions in Ciminelli and Kousisis, the Fifth Circuit held that an indictment alleging a pump-and-dump scheme by social-media influencers adequately pleads a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud under § 1348. It therefore reversed the district court’s dismissal for insufficiency, finding that the indictment did more than allege a mere deprivation of economic information—it alleged fraudulent inducement to part with money or property.
