Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 4: # Issue 2: Intent to Defraud—Must the Government Allege Intended Economic Harm?

## Defendants’ argument: intent to enrich themselves, not to harm followers; followers received market‑value securities
The defendants argued that the indictment should be dismissed because it does not allege an intent to cause economic harm to their followers. Their position rests on two linked propositions: first, the indictment shows they sought to enrich themselves by selling shares at inflated prices, not to injure followers; second, followers who bought the promoted securities received market‑value shares, so there was no necessarily cognizable pecuniary loss. From this perspective, the defendants contend that the fraud statutes require proof that the scheme aimed to diminish a victim’s economic position, and that an absence of intended or actual net loss should defeat the government’s case at the pleading stage.

## Kousisis’s rule: fraud is complete when defendant seeks to obtain victim’s money or property, even absent intended or actual net economic loss
The court rejected that line of argument by applying the Supreme Court’s decision in Kousisis. Kousisis clarifies the fraudulent‑inducement theory: a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he uses a material misstatement to induce a victim to hand over money or property as part of a transaction, even if the fraudster supplies something of equivalent value or even if the victim does not suffer a net pecuniary loss. In short, the critical inquiry is whether the defendant schemed to “obtain” the victim’s money or property; actual or intended net economic harm to the victim is not a necessary element. The Fifth Circuit therefore treats a fraudulent inducement that secures a victim’s money or property as a completed fraud irrespective of whether the victim is left worse off overall.

## Supporting Fifth Circuit precedent: Baker rejects mirror‑image loss‑gain requirement
The opinion anchors its ruling in existing Fifth Circuit authority, particularly United States v. Baker. Baker stands for the proposition that the fraud statutes do not impose a “mirror image” requirement—i.e., they do not demand that the victim’s loss match the defendant’s gain. The Fifth Circuit relies on that principle to show that an indictment need not allege that defendants sought to leave victims economically worse off; it is sufficient to allege an object of the scheme was to obtain the victims’ money or property by deception.

## Application to the indictment: allegations of false posts designed to induce purchases show intent to obtain followers’ money; ‘had money in mind’
Applying Kousisis and Baker to the indictment, the court finds the government sufficiently alleged intent to defraud. The indictment alleges defendants purchased securities, then posted false and misleading information on social media “so that other investors were induced to purchase the security,” and then sold their holdings at inflated prices. Those allegations directly describe an attempt to induce followers to part with money in exchange for securities. Because the indictment alleges that defendants acted with the object of obtaining money through deception, the court concludes defendants “had money in mind”—the requisite intent to obtain victims’ money or property.

## Rejection of harm requirement: receipt of market‑value securities does not negate fraudulent inducement
The court expressly rejects the notion that a victim’s receipt of market‑value securities defeats the fraudulent‑inducement theory. Under Kousisis, even when a fraudster provides something of value in return (for example, securities that are worth the purchase price at the time), the fraud is nonetheless complete if the defendant induced the victim, through materially false statements, to hand over money or property. Thus, the fact that followers received tradable shares does not negate the allegation that defendants intended to and did obtain their money by fraud.

## Persuasive authority: Seventh Circuit’s Smith applying Kousisis in similar context
To reinforce its conclusion, the court cites a recent Seventh Circuit decision, United States v. Smith, which applied Kousisis in a closely analogous fact pattern and upheld indictments premised on fraudulent inducement despite the absence of alleged net loss to victims. The Fifth Circuit views that decision as persuasive support for the proposition that a scheme designed to induce purchases through false statements suffices to allege the intent-to‑defraud element at the indictment stage.

Conclusion
Taken together, Kousisis, Baker, and the indictment’s specific allegations foreclose dismissal on the theory that the government must plead an intent to cause victims net economic harm. The indictment sufficiently alleges that defendants intended to obtain their followers’ money by deception, and the absence of an allegation that followers were left worse off economically does not defeat the securities‑fraud charges at the pleading stage.
