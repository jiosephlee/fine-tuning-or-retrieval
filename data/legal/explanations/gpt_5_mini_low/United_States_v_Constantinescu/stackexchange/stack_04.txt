Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What counts as a 'material misrepresentation' in a social-media pump-and-dump: puffery vs. actionable lie?
Question:
The indictment alleges posts 'misrepresenting their trading positions and the potential price of securities.' I understand lying about your current holdings or intent to sell could be material, but what about optimistic price targets or hype—could those be dismissed as puffery or opinion? Does § 1348 adopt the Rule 10b-5 materiality framework (reasonable investor standard), and how would that apply to casual tweets vs. formal statements?
Answer:
Short answer
- Under the Fifth Circuit’s opinion in United States v. Constantinescu, a “material misrepresentation” for a § 1348 pump‑and‑dump is a false or misleading statement that is part of a scheme “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value” — i.e., a misstatement that is used to induce victims to hand over money or property (here, to buy securities). Op. § III (quoting Greenlaw/Evans) and Part III.A–B.
- That means clear factual lies about holdings, trades, or intent to sell (or concealed sales) are prototypically material and actionable. Hype or generic optimistic commentary (classic puffery/opinion) is less likely to be material unless it is presented or understood as a factual assertion that plausibly induces purchases.
- The opinion does not say that § 1348 formally “adopts” the Rule 10b‑5 reasonable‑investor test; it analyzes § 1348 under the scheme‑to‑defraud / fraudulent‑inducement framework (drawing on Ciminelli and Kousisis) — materiality is tied to whether the misstatement was used to induce a purchase of securities (fraudulent‑inducement), not the right‑to‑control theory rejected in Ciminelli. See Parts III and III.A–B.

Why — legal doctrine in the opinion
- The court frames § 1348 liability in two elements: (1) scheme to defraud (which requires a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value”), and (2) intent to defraud. Op. § III (citing Greenlaw and Evans).
- The opinion distinguishes two theories:
  - Right‑to‑control theory (rejected by Ciminelli): protecting only a person’s “right to valuable economic information” is not a traditional property interest and cannot alone sustain fraud. Op. III.A (discussing Ciminelli).
  - Fraudulent‑inducement theory (upheld here via Kousisis): using a material misstatement to trick a victim into a transaction that causes the victim to part with money or property. The court says that is precisely what the indictment alleges (inducing purchases), and that theory protects money/property even if the victim is not left economically worse off. Op. III.A–B (citing Kousisis).
- Materiality, as the court treats it, operates in service of the fraudulent‑inducement theory: a misstatement is material if it is a misstatement that was used to induce the follower to purchase the security (and thereby part with money/property). Op. III (scheme requires “material misrepresentation ... intended to deceive ... to obtain something of value”).

Puffery / opinion vs. actionable lies — how the opinion guides analysis
- Actionable: factual misstatements that directly bear on transactional decisions are squarely within the indictment the court revived. The opinion repeatedly points to allegations that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information” and “induced … to purchase the security” (Indictment ¶13) as the paradigmatic fraudulent‑inducement. Op. I, III.A–B.
  - Examples from that frame: lying about current holdings, falsely stating that one will not sell when secretly planning to dump, claiming fictitious trades or fabricated demand to create urgency — these are the sort of factual misrepresentations the opinion treats as material because they are alleged to have induced purchases.
- Puffery/opinion: the court’s reasoning leaves room for non‑actionability of generic hype. The opinion endorses Ciminelli’s principle that not all economic information is a protected property interest and treats the fraudulent‑inducement materiality question by asking whether the misstatement was used to obtain money/property. Thus, generalized bullish statements, vague optimism, or subjective price targets framed as opinion are less likely to satisfy the indictment’s requirement unless they are presented as or rest on factual assertions that induce purchases.
- Context matters: the same language can be actionable in one context and puffery in another. The opinion’s focus on the defendant’s intent and whether the misstatement actually induced purchases suggests courts will look to:
  - specificity and factual content (specific statements about holdings, trades, intent, pipelines of buyers, etc. are more likely material),
  - whether the speaker is a purported expert/influencer who presents statements as factual trading guidance (the defendants “held themselves out to be skilled stock traders” and posted trading activity — Op. I), and
  - whether the statement plausibly induced actual purchases (the indictment alleges induced purchases and profits).

Does § 1348 use the Rule 10b‑5 “reasonable investor” materiality test?
- The opinion does not say § 1348 adopts Rule 10b‑5’s reasonable‑investor formulation verbatim. Instead, it analyzes § 1348 under the well‑established fraudulent‑inducement framework (and relies on Supreme Court decisions interpreting related fraud statutes — Ciminelli and Kousisis). See Op. III and III.A–B.
- Practically that means courts will assess whether the misstatement was a material misrepresentation insofar as it was used to induce purchases (an objective inducement/use‑of‑misstatement inquiry), which overlaps with—but is not described in the opinion as identical to—the Rule 10b‑5 “would an average investor consider the statement important?” inquiry. The opinion grounds materiality in the role the statement played in inducing a transaction (fraudulent inducement), not the separate right‑to‑control theory Ciminelli rejected.

Practical takeaways for fact patterns like tweets vs. formal statements
- Casual tweets can be actionable if they convey false factual claims that reasonably induce followers to buy (e.g., “I just bought 100k shares; you should buy now,” when false, or “I’m not selling” while secretly dumping). The opinion highlights defendants who “held themselves out to be skilled stock traders” and used social media posts to induce purchases. Op. I.
- Casual bullish commentary, vague predictions, or hyperbolic slogans are more likely to be treated as non‑actionable puffery unless tied to deceptive factual claims or intended to create a false impression about facts material to the buying decision.
- The more the communicator presents statements as factual, specific, and actionable trading information (and the more the audience reasonably treats them that way), the more the statement fits the fraudulent‑inducement materiality the Fifth Circuit describes.

Bottom line
- Under Constantinescu, § 1348 liability turns on whether the alleged misstatements were used to fraudulently induce followers to part with money or property. Clear factual lies about holdings, trades, or intent are prototypical material misrepresentations; generic hype and opinion are less likely to be material unless framed or understood as factual claims that induce purchases. The court analyzes materiality in the fraudulent‑inducement frame (citing Kousisis and distinguishing Ciminelli) rather than announcing a wholesale adoption of the Rule 10b‑5 reasonable‑investor test.
