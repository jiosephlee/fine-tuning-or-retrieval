Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Must the government show that the defendants sought to obtain the followers’ money specifically, or just 'someone’s' money?
Question:
Kousisis emphasizes that obtaining the victim’s money or property must be an object of the scheme. In a pump-and-dump, trades clear through the market; the defendants may not know who their counterparties are, and followers may buy from other sellers. Is it sufficient that the object was to obtain money from market purchasers generally, or must the indictment tie the object of the fraud to obtaining money from the deceived followers in particular?
Answer:
Short answer: The government need not tie the object of the fraud to particular, identified counterparties. Under the Fifth Circuit’s opinion, it is enough to allege a scheme to induce market purchasers (here, social‑media followers) to part with money or property generally — i.e., a fraudulent‑inducement to buy — so long as the indictment pleads a scheme to obtain money or property by materially false statements.

Why: the opinion rests on two doctrinal points.

- Fraudulent‑inducement vs. right‑to‑control. Ciminelli rejected prosecutions that rest solely on a supposed “right to control” (deprivation of valuable economic information), because that theory does not protect a traditional property interest. By contrast, the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory targets a defendant who uses misrepresentations to trick victims into contracts (or purchases) that require handing over money or property. The court held the indictment here alleges a fraudulent‑inducement: defendants posted false information to induce followers to buy securities, thereby causing those followers to part with money (Indictment ¶ 13; Op. III.A).

- No requirement that the fraudster intend to leave the victim net worse off or that the government identify the exact person who suffered a net loss. Kousisis makes clear the statute reaches schemes whose object is “to obtain” the victim’s money or property even if the victim later receives something of equal value or the fraudster does not aim to cause a net pecuniary loss. The Fifth Circuit applied that principle: the allegation that defendants “pumped and dumped to gain a profit” shows they “had money in mind,” and that fraudulent inducement to purchase securities is sufficient to allege the required injury (Op. III.B).

What that means for pump‑and‑dump facts: markets clear through many counterparties and sellers often do not know the identities of buyers. The opinion treats an allegation that defendants induced their followers (or other market purchasers) to buy as alleging the defendants’ object was to obtain money from those purchasers generally. The indictment need not name the specific market counterparties; it must allege (1) material misrepresentations intended to deceive purchasers and (2) an object to obtain money/property by inducing purchases. The Fifth Circuit found those allegations present here and reversed dismissal (Op. III.A–B, IV).

Bottom line: The government can prosecute on a fraudulent‑inducement theory by alleging defendants sought to obtain money from purchasers in the market (including their followers as a class). It need not show that defendants targeted or specifically intended to obtain money from individually identified followers.
