Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What distinguishes the rejected 'right-to-control' theory from the accepted 'fraudulent-inducement' theory here?
Question:
The panel says this case is unlike Ciminelli because it's not about depriving victims of valuable economic information, but about inducing them to part with money. Factually, the indictment emphasizes misstatements about the defendants' positions and price targets. Those are 'information' too. What makes those allegations cross the line from 'valuable economic information' (not property) to fraudulent inducement to transfer money (property)? Is it the causal linkage to an actual purchase obligation?
Answer:
Short answer: The difference is functional, not semantic. Under Ciminelli the government relied only on a “right-to-control” theory—that a defendant deprived a victim of valuable economic information (a nontraditional “right” to control assets)—which the Supreme Court rejected. Here the indictment alleges a classic fraudulent-inducement scheme: the defendants used materially false statements to cause followers to buy securities (i.e., to hand over money/property). The critical line is whether the misrepresentations are alleged to have induced a transfer of money or property; if so, the fraudulent‑inducement theory (protected by the fraud statutes) applies.

Why (and how) the opinion draws that line
- Ciminelli: the Court held the fraud statutes protect traditional property interests, not a generalized “right to valuable economic information.” A prosecution that rests solely on depriving someone of information therefore fails. (Constantinescu, slip op. III.A (discussing Ciminelli).)
- Kousisis/fraudulent-inducement: by contrast, the fraudulent‑inducement theory covers schemes that use material misstatements to trick a victim into a contract or purchase that requires handing over money or property. The Supreme Court confirmed this is not a repackaged right‑to‑control theory and protects money/property even if the victim doesn’t suffer a net pecuniary loss. (Constantinescu, slip op. III.A–B (citing Kousisis).)

Applying those principles here
- The indictment does more than say the defendants withheld or distorted “information.” It alleges they posted false and misleading statements about their positions and price potential “so that other investors were induced to purchase the security,” and that defendants then sold into that inflation to realize $114 million. Indictment ¶ 13–14; Constantinescu, slip op. I, III.B.
- That allegation satisfies the “scheme to defraud” element: a material misrepresentation intended to obtain money or property (the followers’ purchases). The panel therefore treats the posts as the means of inducing transfers of money/property—not merely as the withholding of valuable information. (Constantinescu, slip op. III (citing Evans, Greenlaw, Kousisis).)

Two concise takeaways for your reading of the opinion
1. The same underlying factual act (posting false claims) can be either an impermissible “information‑deprivation” theory or a permissible fraudulent‑inducement theory depending on what the indictment alleges the defendant sought to obtain. If the alleged objective is to cause victims to part with money/property, it is fraudulent inducement. (See Indictment ¶ 13 and the panel’s discussion, Constantinescu, III.A–B.)
2. The government need not allege that victims suffered a net economic loss—only that the defendant intended to obtain the victims’ money or property by deception. (Constantinescu, slip op. III.B (discussing Kousisis).)

So yes: the causal linkage to inducing an actual purchase/transfer of money or property is what crosses the line from an impermissible “deprivation of information” theory (Ciminelli) into a valid fraudulent‑inducement prosecution (as the panel found here).
