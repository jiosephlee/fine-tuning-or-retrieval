Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### How do conspiracy (§ 1349) and aiding-and-abetting (§ 2) apply if some defendants didn’t personally sell or profit?
Question:
The indictment charges conspiracy and substantive § 1348 counts with § 2. Suppose a defendant only posted misleading content but didn’t sell into the spike or directly receive funds from followers. Is it enough for conspiracy that he agreed to the scheme and took overt acts, even if he didn’t profit? For aiding and abetting, what level of participation and intent must be shown—does merely amplifying others’ posts qualify?
Answer:
Short answer
- Conspiracy (§ 1349): Yes — an agreement to participate in the pump‑and‑dump and an overt act (e.g., posting misleading content) can support a conspiracy charge even if that defendant did not personally sell into the spike or directly receive funds, so long as the indictment pleads that the defendant shared the fraudulent purpose and took steps in furtherance of it.
- Aiding‑and‑abetting (§ 2): Liability requires purposeful, knowing assistance (and the requisite intent for the underlying securities fraud). Mere accidental or passive amplification is less likely to suffice; purposeful reposting, amplifying, or other conduct that meaningfully facilitates the fraudulent inducement can qualify.

Why (doctrine tied to the opinion)
1) Conspiracy under § 1349
- The Fifth Circuit in United States v. Constantinescu reversed dismissal because the indictment adequately alleged a “scheme to defraud” and “intent to defraud” under § 1348, and the defendants there were charged with conspiracy to commit securities fraud. The court treated the securities‑fraud theory as a “fraudulent‑inducement” theory — i.e., using material misstatements to trick victims into parting with money or property (Kousisis and Ciminelli discussion). The opinion emphasizes that the fraud statutes protect money/property obtained by inducement, not only a “right to economic information.” Slip op. §§ III.A–B.
- Basic conspiracy law (applied to § 1349 in the court’s reasoning) is met where the indictment alleges (1) an agreement to commit the substantive offense, (2) the defendant’s knowing participation in that agreement with the requisite intent, and (3) an overt act in furtherance of the conspiracy. The opinion treats as sufficient allegations that defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security” and that defendants “purchased a security,” “pumped” the price, and “dumped” it for profit. Indictment ¶¶13–14; see slip op. § I.
- Applied to your fact: a defendant who agreed to the pump‑and‑dump and who posted misleading content can be charged with conspiracy even if he didn’t personally sell or directly receive funds, because the allegations that he shared the fraudulent purpose and took overt acts (the posts) can be enough to allege the conspiracy elements. The opinion expressly rejects the notion that a defendant must have intended to leave victims worse off economically; it is enough that the object was to obtain money/property by inducement (Kousisis, discussed at slip op. § III.B).

2) Aiding and abetting under § 2
- The opinion frames the underlying offense as securities fraud requiring (1) a scheme by material misrepresentation to obtain money/property, and (2) intent to deceive and to cause harm (as the circuits have construed those elements). The court cites Kousisis for the point that a fraud is complete when the defendant induced the victim to relinquish money/property under materially false pretenses, regardless of net economic loss to the victim (slip op. §§ III and III.B).
- Aiding‑and‑abetting criminal liability (18 U.S.C. § 2) requires proof that the defendant (a) knowingly and intentionally associated with the criminal venture, and (b) took some action that reasonably could assist or facilitate the offense, with the mental state required for the substantive offense. The Constantinescu opinion’s focus on the knowingly deceptive postings as the means by which followers were induced indicates that communications that intentionally further the fraudulent inducement are treated as culpable acts in the fraud.
- Applied to your fact: merely reposting or occasionally amplifying content without knowledge of the fraud or without intent to help the scheme is a weak basis for § 2 liability. But purposeful amplification or reposting made with knowledge of the scheme and with the intent to induce purchases (i.e., to help “pump” the price) is the kind of conduct the indictment alleges and that the court found sufficient to state offenses. In short: the conduct must be purposeful and knowing, and must meaningfully facilitate the fraudulent inducement that seeks to obtain money/property.

Practical points for charging/proof (as reflected in the opinion)
- The government need not allege that each defendant personally pocketed proceeds or caused a net pecuniary loss to victims. It is enough to allege the fraudulent‑inducement object — that defendants used misrepresentations to induce followers to hand over money/property (slip op. §§ III.A–B, relying on Kousisis).
- For non‑seller or non‑recipient defendants, the government should (and here did) plead facts showing agreement and purposeful conduct in furtherance (e.g., coordinated posting, synchronized timing, directions to buy, knowledge of others’ selling) so that their posts are more than passive speech.
- On the defense side, distinguishing passive speech from knowing facilitation (lack of knowledge, lack of intent to induce purchases, or mere innocuous amplification) is the principal pathway to attack aiding/abetting or to challenge individual culpability in a conspiracy.

Bottom line: under Constantinescu, an indictment that alleges a defendant agreed to a pump‑and‑dump and took overt acts (like posting misleading material) can support conspiracy liability even absent proof that that defendant personally sold or personally received proceeds. For aiding/abetting, the government must show knowing, intentional assistance that meaningfully furthers the fraudulent inducement; mere passive or unwitting amplification is unlikely to duplicate the culpability the panel found sufficient here.
