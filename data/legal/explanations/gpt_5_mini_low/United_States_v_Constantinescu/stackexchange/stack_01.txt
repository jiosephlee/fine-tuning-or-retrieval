Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### On a motion to dismiss an indictment, what facts can the court consider and why is review de novo?
Question:
The panel says sufficiency of an indictment is reviewed de novo and that the court must take the allegations as true (citing Fontenot). I'm confused about the boundary here. Can a district court consider extrinsic evidence (like trading records) to test sufficiency, or is it strictly limited to the four corners of the indictment? And why is the appellate standard de novo rather than abuse of discretion when the district court dismissed?
Answer:
Short answer
- On a motion to dismiss an indictment for failure to state an offense, the district court evaluates the legal sufficiency of the charging document and generally must accept the indictment’s factual allegations as true — i.e., it is confined to the “four corners” of the indictment (and matters properly incorporated or judicially noticeable). It should not resolve disputed facts by considering extrinsic proof such as trading records or other evidence going to the merits.
- The court of appeals reviews that ruling de novo because sufficiency of an indictment presents a question of law (whether the allegations, taken as true, plead each material element), not a discretionary factual determination. United States v. Constantinescu applies these principles (citing Rafoi and Fontenot).

Why the district court is normally limited to the indictment
- Constantinescu reiterates the controlling rule: “the court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated.” (quoting Fontenot). That is the paradigmatic Rule 12(b)(3) posture: the motion challenges the legal adequacy of the pleading, so the judge asks whether the pleading, assumed true, supplies the elements and notice required by the statutes and case law (see the Gaytan/Guzman‑Ocampo formulation the panel cites).
- Practical consequence: the district court should not use trading records or other extrinsic evidence to “test” the sufficiency of the indictment because doing so would convert the Rule 12 legal inquiry into a merits contest. Factual disputes over what really happened are for trial (or for resolution on a properly supported Rule 29/Rule 33 motion or at summary judgment where appropriate).

Limited exceptions
- Although Constantinescu does not catalogue every exception, the governing approach allows the court to consider (a) documents that are expressly incorporated into the indictment or central to the charges and (b) matters of public record or facts subject to judicial notice without converting the motion into a merits hearing. Those narrow categories permit the court to consult certain extrinsic materials when they are properly part of the charging context, but not to resolve contested evidentiary questions via evidentiary proof. (The panel’s reliance on Fontenot and Crow underscores the “take allegations as true” rule.)

Why appellate review is de novo
- Constantinescu cites Rafoi for the proposition that “We review the sufficiency of an indictment de novo.” That follows from the character of the issue: whether the indictment, assumed true, alleges each material element of the offense is a legal question. De novo review is appropriate for legal questions and statutory or constitutional interpretation; it asks anew whether the pleading meets the legal standard (e.g., the elements discussed in Guzman‑Ocampo, Gaytan, and the securities‑fraud elements the panel applies).
- By contrast, abuse‑of‑discretion review applies where the district court exercised judgment over procedural or discretionary matters (sanctions, discovery disputes, evidentiary rulings). A dismissal for failure to state an offense is not primarily a discretionary factual ruling but a legal determination about the pleading’s adequacy, so the court of appeals reviews it de novo.

How that played out in Constantinescu
- The panel reversed because the indictment, taken as true, alleged a fraudulent‑inducement theory (victims were induced to buy securities and part with money/property), which satisfies the elements of § 1348 under governing Supreme Court precedents (Ciminelli distinguished; Kousisis construed). The panel treated the indictment’s allegations as true and resolved the legal question whether those allegations, if true, state an offense — the precise de novo legal inquiry the authorities require.

Bottom line for practice
- On a motion to dismiss an indictment, stick to the indictment’s allegations (and only extrinsic materials that are incorporated or judicially noticeable). If the defense wants to dispute facts in trading records, that is better raised in discovery, at trial, or in a motion that litigates factual issues with an appropriate record (not by asking the court to go beyond the indictment on a Rule 12 sufficiency challenge).
