Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Influencer Hype Meets Criminal Fraud: Speech, Social Media, and the Line Between Puffs and Deception

## Why this case matters

United States v. Constantinescu is the Fifth Circuit’s blunt reminder that social-media trading hype can cross from protected puffery into criminal securities fraud when it is tied to a fraudulent inducement scheme. At bottom, the court held that an indictment alleging that high-profile social-media traders “pumped” securities with false and misleading posts and then “dumped” into followers’ purchases sufficiently pleads a scheme to defraud and the requisite intent under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. That answer is straightforward under the Supreme Court’s recent clarifications rejecting a right-to-control theory (Ciminelli) and confirming the viability of a fraudulent-inducement theory even where victims are not left economically worse off (Kousisis). But the decision also raises practical and doctrinal questions about how ordinary promotional speech in fast-moving online markets will be treated by prosecutors, and where the constitutional and regulatory lines should be drawn.

## The court’s reasoning — concise walkthrough

The Fifth Circuit framed the issue as one of indictment sufficiency. The district court had dismissed the indictment on the ground that the alleged misconduct—depriving followers of valuable economic information—did not target a “traditional property interest” and so could not support fraud under Ciminelli. The Fifth Circuit reversed, identifying two critical points.

First, the indictment did not rest on the right-to-control theory the Supreme Court rejected in Ciminelli. Rather, it pleaded a classic fraudulent-inducement theory: defendants made material misrepresentations to induce followers to buy securities, thereby causing followers to part with money or property. Because fraudulent inducement targets money or property transferred under false pretenses, Ciminelli’s restriction on information-as-property did not doom the indictment.

Second, the court relied on Kousisis to reject the defendants’ contention that intent to harm victims’ economic interests is necessary. Kousisis holds that a fraudster can be guilty when the object of the scheme is to obtain money or property—even if the victim is not left worse off in net terms. Thus, according to the Fifth Circuit, allegations that the defendants recruited followers via false posts to purchase securities, and then sold into that demand to realize $114 million in profits, sufficiently allege both a scheme and the intent to defraud.

Taken together, the court concluded the indictment adequately alleged each material element of securities fraud and remanded for trial.

## Analytical perspective: what the opinion gets right — and what it leaves open

The Fifth Circuit correctly applied the Supreme Court’s recent doctrinal signals. Ciminelli narrowed the universe of cognizable property interests; Kousisis clarified that fraudulent inducement remains a valid pathway to liability and that prosecutors need not prove victims were left with net losses. Reading the two decisions together, the panel’s conclusion—that inducing purchases through deception is classic fraud—fits neatly within current precedent.

But the opinion is also modest: it resolves an indictment-sufficiency question, not the merits of any conviction, and it does not try to map a bright-line rule between permissible promotional speech and criminal deception. That restraint matters. Social-media-driven markets turn on speech that often mixes bullish hyperbole, selectively disclosed positions, and aggressive self-promotion. How courts will separate criminally actionable lies from routine “puffs” remains a fact-intensive question to be resolved at trial (or on summary judgment), not on a facial reading of an indictment.

Two doctrinally important uncertainties follow. First, what degree of specificity must an indictment (or later proof at trial) show about the defendant’s awareness of falsity and specific intent to induce purchases? The panel leaned on allegations that the influencers “post[ed] false and misleading information . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase,” and on the profit motive; but proving scienter in a world of aspirational claims and shifting positions can be hard. Second, the decision does not address how intermediary speech (retweets, algorithmic boosts, or coordinated but not centrally orchestrated hype) will be treated, leaving open how conspiracy and aiding-and-abetting theories will fare as prosecutors pursue sophisticated networks of promoters.

## First Amendment friction: will speech be chilled?

The opinion increases the exposure of prominent online traders and “finfluencers” to criminal prosecution when their posts are part of an allegation that they intended to induce purchases under materially false pretenses. That exposure raises legitimate free-speech concerns.

However, the opinion does not criminalize opinion, advocacy, or harmless exaggeration. The fraudulent-inducement theory targets deceptive misrepresentations of fact—for example, statements falsely claiming an executed buy or invented inside information when the post is designed to cause others to buy. Courts traditionally distinguish protected opinion and puffery from materially deceptive factual assertions. Under existing doctrine, robust advocacy about a security’s prospects remains protected unless it crosses into knowingly false or materially misleading factual claims that are used to obtain money or property.

Still, the line is blurrier on social media where a post can be both a recommendation and a representation about one’s trading position, and where followers often rely on the influencer’s stated “track record.” The risk is real that overbroad or aggressive prosecutions could chill legitimate commentary or disclosure-poor hype. To avoid that chilling effect, prosecutions will need to be careful to allege and prove objective falsity and scienter, not mere sophistication or success-based envy.

## Compliance risks for online traders and finfluencers

Practitioners and market participants should treat Constantinescu as a clear signal that prosecutors view social-media-driven market manipulation as within the reach of federal securities fraud statutes when easily-pleaded indicia of a pump-and-dump exist. Specific compliance lessons:

- Be transparent about positions and timing. Saying “I bought X today” when you did not, or obscuring undisclosed plans to sell while encouraging purchases, is a red flag. Disclosures should be contemporaneous and accurate.
- Avoid affirmative misrepresentations of fact. Opinion and analysis are less risky than demonstrably false statements (e.g., fabricated trades, invented insider information).
- Disclose conflicts and compensation. Paid promotions or undisclosed coordination with other market actors who will buy or sell should be disclosed to avoid allegations of deceptive intent.
- Keep records. Screenshots, trading logs, and contemporaneous notes about why a post was made can help rebut allegations of knowing falsity or fraudulent intent.
- Beware of coordinated activity. Coordinated campaigns to drive demand—especially when followed by sell-offs—invite conspiracy and aiding-and-abetting scrutiny.
- Consult counsel before paid endorsements or coordinated campaigns, especially for securities with low liquidity, where falsely induced purchases can move prices more dramatically.

## Downstream effects and prosecution strategy

Expect prosecutors to lean on the fraudulent-inducement framework in future pump-and-dump cases grounded in social-media activity. The Constantinescu panel emphasizes that prosecutors do not need to prove victims ended up worse off, only that the defendants intended to induce purchases and obtained money or property. That lowers one hurdle for the government—but other evidentiary challenges remain: establishing materiality, falsity, reliance (if required at trial), and scienter.

Defendants will likely focus on showing that statements were opinion or puffery, that followers’ purchases were independent, or that the alleged misstatements were immaterial. They may also press First Amendment defenses and urge heightened scienter standards where expression and advocacy overlap. Defense counsel will push factual distinctions: did the influencer genuinely hold a position and express belief, or fabricate positions and conceal sell orders? The factual texture will matter enormously.

Regulators (SEC) and civil plaintiffs may also be encouraged by the decision. Criminal indictments often spur parallel civil enforcement and private damages claims; an indictment that survives dismissal can embolden follow-on actions, settlements, and regulatory enforcement.

## Bottom line

Constantinescu is a reminder that modern pump-and-dump schemes can be prosecuted under the fraudulent-inducement theory even when the misconduct plays out on Twitter, Instagram, Discord, or TikTok. The Fifth Circuit applied recent Supreme Court principles to draw a sensible boundary: information itself is not property under Ciminelli, but false statements that induce the transfer of money or securities remain classic fraud. That framework protects legitimate advocacy while preserving a tool to police deceptive, profit-driven manipulation of retail investors.

For finfluencers and online traders, the practical takeaway is simple: treat public trading commentary as potentially consequential legal conduct. Accurate disclosures, careful separation of opinion from asserted facts, and transparent handling of compensation and positions are the best prophylactics against criminal and regulatory exposure. For courts and policymakers, the hard work remains: policing deception without chilling speech, and developing fact-sensitive standards that reflect how marketplace communication now occurs in real time and at scale.
