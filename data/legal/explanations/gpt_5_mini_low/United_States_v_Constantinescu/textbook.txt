Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Alleged conduct: coordinated purchase, promotional ‘pump’ via false social‑media posts, and secret ‘dump’ for profit

The indictment alleges a classic “pump‑and‑dump” scheme carried out through coordinated trading and social‑media promotion. According to the charging document, the defendants purchased shares of target securities and then used their social‑media presences to “pump” prices by posting false and misleading statements about those securities. Those posts were calculated to induce followers and other retail investors to buy the promoted stocks, driving up market prices. Once the price was inflated, the defendants allegedly “dumped” their holdings—selling into the artificially elevated market—realizing substantial gains. The government frames these acts as a single fraudulent scheme in which misrepresentations on social media were an integral means of causing victims to part with money by purchasing securities.

## Defendants and platforms; public persona as skilled traders; alleged $114 million in profits

The indictment names eight defendants—Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight (who later pleaded guilty). It emphasizes that each defendant maintained large followings across multiple social‑media platforms and cultivated a public persona as a skilled trader who regularly posted trading activity and investment advice. The government alleges that, by leveraging those followings and reputations, the defendants induced followers to buy target securities. The indictment quantifies the alleged benefit to the defendants at approximately $114 million in profits derived from the scheme.

## Charges: 18 U.S.C. § 1349 (conspiracy) and 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2 (securities fraud and aiding/abetting)

The indictment charges the remaining defendants with conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 and with substantive securities‑fraud offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, together with aiding and abetting liability under 18 U.S.C. § 2 where applicable. Section 1348 criminalizes schemes to defraud in connection with any security and schemes to obtain money or property by means of false or fraudulent pretenses in connection with the purchase or sale of securities. The conspiracy count alleges agreement among the defendants to carry out the pump‑and‑dump scheme; the substantive counts allege the underlying fraudulent acts by which the defendants induced purchases of securities.

## District court ruling: indictment fails to allege deprivation of traditional property; focuses only on loss of valuable economic information

The district court dismissed the indictment. Its dismissal rested on the view that the indictment did not allege deprivation of a “traditional property interest” protected by the federal fraud statutes. The district court treated the government’s theory as effectively alleging the defendants deprived their followers of valuable economic information—i.e., by concealing material facts about the defendants’ trading positions or intentions—rather than alleging that the defendants obtained victims’ money or property. Relying on Supreme Court precedent excluding a “right‑to‑control” theory that treats the right to economic information as property, the district court concluded the indictment failed to state an offense because it charged only a deprivation of information rather than a cognizable property interest.

## Government’s timely appeal

The government appealed the dismissal to the Fifth Circuit. Daniel Knight, one of the original defendants, pleaded guilty before the appeal; the remaining defendants pursued the district court’s dismissal order. The government’s appeal challenged the district court’s legal conclusion that the indictment did not sufficiently allege a scheme and intent to defraud within the meaning of § 1348.

## Standard of review: de novo; taking indictment’s allegations as true; requirements for indictment sufficiency (enumerate elements, fair notice, double‑jeopardy protection)

On appeal, the Fifth Circuit reviewed the sufficiency of the indictment de novo. Under settled circuit law, the appellate court accepts the facts alleged in the indictment as true for purposes of the sufficiency inquiry and asks whether those allegations, so taken, state an offense. An indictment must meet three core requirements to be legally sufficient: it must enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; it must fairly inform the defendant of the charges so the defendant can prepare a defense; and it must provide the defendant with protection against double jeopardy in any future prosecution for the same conduct. In practice, this means the indictment must allege the material elements of the securities fraud and conspiracy statutes—here, a scheme to defraud (including material misrepresentations), the requisite intent to defraud, and the object of obtaining money or property through the scheme—such that the defendants receive fair notice of the conduct at issue and the court can determine whether the charge fits within the fraud statutes’ scope.

# Governing Law: Securities Fraud under § 1348 and Indictment Sufficiency

This chapter explains the legal framework the Fifth Circuit applied in United States v. Constantinescu to evaluate whether the superseding indictment for securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 was legally sufficient. The court’s analysis rests on two linked inquiries: (1) what § 1348 requires (as informed by mail/wire fraud doctrine), and (2) whether the indictment, taken as true, alleged each material element sufficiently to give defendants notice and to bar future prosecutions for the same conduct.

## Text of § 1348: schemes to defraud and schemes to obtain money or property in connection with securities

Section 1348 criminalizes two closely related types of wrongs in the securities context. In plain terms, it reaches (A) schemes to “defraud any person in connection with . . . any security,” and (B) schemes “to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security.” The opinion treats those two formulations as tracking the classic fraud picture: an actionable scheme involves deception (a material misrepresentation or omission) and seeks to obtain money or property in connection with securities transactions. The Fifth Circuit framed the statute’s requirements in terms that closely parallel mail and wire fraud doctrine, which provides interpretive guidance where § 1348’s terms are not otherwise defined by circuit precedent.

## Borrowing from mail and wire fraud: Greenlaw and Evans as interpretive anchors

Because this circuit had relatively little independent securities-fraud precedent, the court relied on prior Fifth Circuit mail-and-wire fraud decisions as interpretive anchors. The opinion cites United States v. Greenlaw and United States v. Evans for the two-part framing used throughout: a fraud under § 1348 requires (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” Those earlier cases supply vocabulary and tests—what counts as a “scheme,” what makes a misrepresentation “material,” and what constitutes the requisite mental state—so the court treated mail/wire fraud doctrine as controlling interpretive guidance, consistent with how other courts have analyzed § 1348.

## Elements: scheme to defraud (material misrepresentation to obtain value) and intent to deceive plus causing harm

The opinion sets out the operative elements with precision drawn from Evans and Greenlaw:

- Scheme to defraud: The indictment must allege a material misrepresentation (or omission) that is intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value—typically money or property. The court emphasizes that the misrepresentation must be material and that the object of the scheme must be to obtain value from the deceived party.

- Intent to defraud: The government must allege that the defendant acted with intent to deceive and with an objective to cause some harm to result from the deceit. The panel borrowed the two-part formulation used in prior decisions—intent to deceive and an intent that deceit produce a harmful result—while recognizing recent Supreme Court refinements (discussed below) about what kinds of harms or transfers satisfy the statute.

The opinion applies those elements to the indictment’s allegations that defendants purchased shares, published false and misleading posts to induce followers to buy, and then sold into the resulting price rise—allegedly obtaining $114 million in proceeds. In the Fifth Circuit’s view, those allegations plausibly describe a scheme in which defendants used material misrepresentations to obtain money or property (followers’ purchase proceeds, or transfers of value in the securities market) and did so with fraudulent intent.

## Supreme Court synthesis: how Ciminelli and Kousisis refine the scope of “fraud” under federal statutes

Because recent Supreme Court decisions clarified the difference between permissible fraud theories and theories that extend the statutes too far, the panel closely considered Ciminelli and Kousisis and applied their teachings to the indictment.

- Ciminelli (wire‑fraud context): The Court rejected the so‑called “right-to-control” theory of fraud—i.e., a theory that treats the victim’s “right to valuable economic information” as a protected property interest. Ciminelli holds that the federal fraud statutes protect traditional property interests and do not reach mere deprivation of valuable information needed to make discretionary economic decisions. The Fifth Circuit read Ciminelli as a limit: fraud cannot be based solely on the loss of information or control over decision-making; there must be a protected property or money interest at stake.

- Kousisis (fraudulent‑inducement theory): Kousisis clarifies that the fraudulent-inducement theory—where a defendant uses material falsehoods to induce another to enter a transaction that hands over money or property—is a valid basis for fraud liability. Crucially, Kousisis rejects the notion that the government must show the defendant intended to make the victim net worse off. It is enough that the defendant intended to obtain the victim’s money or property through deceit, even if the victim ostensibly received something of market value in return. Kousisis also explains that the fraudulent-inducement theory is not a repackaged right-to-control theory because it directly protects money and property, not merely information rights.

Applying those precedents, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the Constantinescu indictment did not rely on a forbidden right-to-control theory. Instead, it charged a classic fraudulent-inducement scheme—social-media posts that induced followers to buy securities (i.e., to part with money or transfer property) on false premises so defendants could sell at inflated prices. Under Kousisis, the government need not allege that victims were left economically worse off; it is enough that defendants intended to and did obtain money or property through the deception.

## Indictment sufficiency: pleading each material element; fair notice; double-jeopardy protection

The court reviewed the indictment’s sufficiency de novo and applied the conventional standards that govern challenges under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 12(b) to factual sufficiency of an indictment:

- Pleading each material element: An indictment must set out the prima facie elements of the offense. The Fifth Circuit relied on cases such as United States v. Gaytan and United States v. Guzman-Ocampo to restate this baseline requirement. For § 1348 offenses, that means the indictment must allege the scheme (including the material misrepresentations) and the defendant’s intent to obtain money or property by fraud.

- Fair notice to defendants: The indictment must fairly inform defendants of the charges so they can prepare a defense. The court treated the indictment’s allegations—the purchases, the false social-media posts, the induced purchases by followers, and the defendants’ secret sales—as sufficiently specific to inform each defendant of the charged conduct.

- Double jeopardy protection: The indictment must provide enough detail to prevent later prosecutorial duplication for the same conduct. Alleging the material elements and the essential facts of the alleged scheme satisfies this requirement per the cited precedents (Fontenot; Crow).

Taken together, the panel concluded that the superseding indictment satisfied these pleading requirements: it alleged a scheme to fraudulently induce followers to buy securities (a deprivation of money/property), alleged defendants acted with intent to deceive and to obtain money (and indeed profited), and thereby fairly informed defendants of the charges while identifying the material elements necessary to bar duplicative prosecutions.

Conclusion tied to the opinion’s holding

Grounded in the mail/wire fraud framework and informed by the Supreme Court’s limiting and clarifying decisions in Ciminelli and Kousisis, the Fifth Circuit held that an indictment alleging a pump-and-dump scheme by social-media influencers adequately pleads a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud under § 1348. It therefore reversed the district court’s dismissal for insufficiency, finding that the indictment did more than allege a mere deprivation of economic information—it alleged fraudulent inducement to part with money or property.

# Issue 1: Does the Indictment Allege a Scheme to Defraud (Traditional Property) or Only Loss of Information?

## Ciminelli’s holding: the ‘right‑to‑control’ theory (loss of valuable economic information) is not a traditional property interest

Understanding the court’s reasoning begins with Ciminelli, the Supreme Court decision that set a limiting principle for federal fraud prosecutions. In Ciminelli the government prosecuted a defendant under the wire fraud statute by advancing a “right‑to‑control” theory: the indictment alleged the defendant deprived victims of valuable economic information—information necessary to exercise discretion over public contracting decisions. The Supreme Court held that the federal fraud statutes protect only “traditional” property interests, and that a right to valuable economic information is not such a traditional property interest. Because the government’s theory rested solely on deprivation of that informational right, the Court concluded the fraud statute could not support a conviction. The upshot of Ciminelli for indictment drafting is clear: an allegation that a defendant merely withheld or distorted information so that a victim lacked full knowledge or control over an asset will not, standing alone, satisfy the “money or property” requirement of the fraud statutes.

## Key distinction: right‑to‑control versus fraudulent‑inducement theories

The important doctrinal lesson from Ciminelli is that not all deceptive conduct falls within the reach of federal fraud statutes; liability depends on which legal theory of “property” the prosecution advances. Two prototypical theories arise in the cases:

- The right‑to‑control theory posits that the victim’s property is the right to make informed, discretionary choices about an asset. Depriving a victim of information about that asset is characterized as depriving the victim of a property interest. Ciminelli rejects this as a stand‑alone basis for federal fraud liability because the right to economic information is not a traditional property interest.

- The fraudulent‑inducement theory, by contrast, frames the defendant’s deception as a means to trick a victim into surrendering money or property through a contract or transaction. Under this approach the protected interest is not an abstract informational right but the actual money or property the victim relinquished as a consequence of the deceit.

That distinction matters because the statutes criminalize schemes “to obtain . . . money or property” by false pretenses. If the deception causes victims to hand over money or property, the fraudulent‑inducement theory ties the government’s allegation to the traditional property interests the statutes protect; if the deception only obscures facts so that victims exercise discretion differently but do not part with money or property, Ciminelli warns that the statute may not reach the conduct.

## Kousisis’s validation of fraudulent‑inducement: using material misstatements to induce transactions that transfer money or property

Kousisis, decided after Ciminelli and relied on by the Fifth Circuit in Constantinescu, explains and confirms the legitimate scope of the fraudulent‑inducement theory. There, the Supreme Court held that a defendant may be guilty of fraud when he uses material misstatements to induce a counterparty into a contract that requires handing over money or property, even if the fraudster provides something of ostensibly equal value in return and even if the victim does not suffer a net pecuniary loss. The Court emphasized that the fraud statutes prohibit schemes to “obtain” money or property by deception; obtaining the money or property is the object of the fraud and suffices even when the victim’s bottom line isn’t ultimately worsened. Critically, Kousisis distinguished fraudulent inducement from the right‑to‑control theory: fraudulent inducement protects the transfer of money or property induced by deceit, whereas the right‑to‑control approach improperly elevates informational control to the status of traditional property.

For indictment sufficiency, Kousisis means that an allegation the defendant intentionally misled victims to induce a purchase or a contractual transfer of funds aligns the charging theory with a traditional property interest—money or property—rather than with the non‑traditional informational interest rejected in Ciminelli.

## Application to the indictment: alleged misrepresentations about positions and price potential intended to induce followers to buy securities (spend money)

Turning to the facts charged in the Constantinescu indictment, the government alleges that the defendants were social‑media personalities who: purchased thinly traded securities, posted false and misleading statements about those securities (including misstatements about their trading positions and the securities’ price potential), used those misrepresentations to induce their followers to buy the securities, and then sold their own holdings at inflated prices (“pump and dump”). The indictment thus charges more than a mere withholding or distortion of information; it alleges an affirmative scheme to cause followers to part with money by purchasing securities on the defendants’ representations.

Under the fraudulent‑inducement framework articulated in Kousisis, those allegations squarely describe a scheme to obtain money or property. The followers’ act of purchasing shares constitutes a transfer of money or property that the indictment alleges was caused by defendants’ material misrepresentations. Unlike the right‑to‑control theory, the government does not frame the followers’ loss as merely the loss of an informational right to make better discretionary decisions; it frames the loss as an actual transfer of wealth—money paid for securities—that the defendants sought to and did obtain through deception.

The Fifth Circuit in Constantinescu accepted this characterization. The court distinguished the facts from Ciminelli because the indictment alleged defendants induced purchases (and thereby obtained money) rather than merely depriving decision‑makers of information necessary to exercise control over assets. The indictment’s language—alleging purposeful misrepresentations intended to induce purchases and allegations that defendants profited millions—directly supports a fraudulent‑inducement theory tied to traditional property (the money followers paid).

## Conclusion on Element 1: indictment alleges deprivation of money/property through fraudulent inducement, not mere loss of information

The court’s analysis leads to a straightforward conclusion: the indictment in Constantinescu does not rely on the right‑to‑control theory disallowed in Ciminelli. Instead, it alleges fraudulent inducement—material misstatements intended to and resulting in followers purchasing securities—thereby causing them to transfer money or property to the marketplace that defendants sought to exploit. Kousisis confirms that such allegations state the “scheme to defraud” element of the securities‑fraud statute because they allege a scheme to obtain money or property by means of deception. Consequently, the indictment sufficiently pleads a scheme to defraud a protected property interest rather than only a deprivation of valuable economic information.

# Issue 2: Intent to Defraud—Must the Government Allege Intended Economic Harm?

## Defendants’ argument: intent to enrich themselves, not to harm followers; followers received market‑value securities
The defendants argued that the indictment should be dismissed because it does not allege an intent to cause economic harm to their followers. Their position rests on two linked propositions: first, the indictment shows they sought to enrich themselves by selling shares at inflated prices, not to injure followers; second, followers who bought the promoted securities received market‑value shares, so there was no necessarily cognizable pecuniary loss. From this perspective, the defendants contend that the fraud statutes require proof that the scheme aimed to diminish a victim’s economic position, and that an absence of intended or actual net loss should defeat the government’s case at the pleading stage.

## Kousisis’s rule: fraud is complete when defendant seeks to obtain victim’s money or property, even absent intended or actual net economic loss
The court rejected that line of argument by applying the Supreme Court’s decision in Kousisis. Kousisis clarifies the fraudulent‑inducement theory: a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he uses a material misstatement to induce a victim to hand over money or property as part of a transaction, even if the fraudster supplies something of equivalent value or even if the victim does not suffer a net pecuniary loss. In short, the critical inquiry is whether the defendant schemed to “obtain” the victim’s money or property; actual or intended net economic harm to the victim is not a necessary element. The Fifth Circuit therefore treats a fraudulent inducement that secures a victim’s money or property as a completed fraud irrespective of whether the victim is left worse off overall.

## Supporting Fifth Circuit precedent: Baker rejects mirror‑image loss‑gain requirement
The opinion anchors its ruling in existing Fifth Circuit authority, particularly United States v. Baker. Baker stands for the proposition that the fraud statutes do not impose a “mirror image” requirement—i.e., they do not demand that the victim’s loss match the defendant’s gain. The Fifth Circuit relies on that principle to show that an indictment need not allege that defendants sought to leave victims economically worse off; it is sufficient to allege an object of the scheme was to obtain the victims’ money or property by deception.

## Application to the indictment: allegations of false posts designed to induce purchases show intent to obtain followers’ money; ‘had money in mind’
Applying Kousisis and Baker to the indictment, the court finds the government sufficiently alleged intent to defraud. The indictment alleges defendants purchased securities, then posted false and misleading information on social media “so that other investors were induced to purchase the security,” and then sold their holdings at inflated prices. Those allegations directly describe an attempt to induce followers to part with money in exchange for securities. Because the indictment alleges that defendants acted with the object of obtaining money through deception, the court concludes defendants “had money in mind”—the requisite intent to obtain victims’ money or property.

## Rejection of harm requirement: receipt of market‑value securities does not negate fraudulent inducement
The court expressly rejects the notion that a victim’s receipt of market‑value securities defeats the fraudulent‑inducement theory. Under Kousisis, even when a fraudster provides something of value in return (for example, securities that are worth the purchase price at the time), the fraud is nonetheless complete if the defendant induced the victim, through materially false statements, to hand over money or property. Thus, the fact that followers received tradable shares does not negate the allegation that defendants intended to and did obtain their money by fraud.

## Persuasive authority: Seventh Circuit’s Smith applying Kousisis in similar context
To reinforce its conclusion, the court cites a recent Seventh Circuit decision, United States v. Smith, which applied Kousisis in a closely analogous fact pattern and upheld indictments premised on fraudulent inducement despite the absence of alleged net loss to victims. The Fifth Circuit views that decision as persuasive support for the proposition that a scheme designed to induce purchases through false statements suffices to allege the intent-to‑defraud element at the indictment stage.

Conclusion
Taken together, Kousisis, Baker, and the indictment’s specific allegations foreclose dismissal on the theory that the government must plead an intent to cause victims net economic harm. The indictment sufficiently alleges that defendants intended to obtain their followers’ money by deception, and the absence of an allegation that followers were left worse off economically does not defeat the securities‑fraud charges at the pleading stage.

# Disposition and Practical Significance

## Holding and disposition
The Fifth Circuit reversed the district court’s dismissal of the superseding indictment and remanded for further proceedings. The court held that, as pleaded, the indictment sufficiently alleges both (1) a “scheme to defraud” under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 and (2) the requisite “intent to defraud.” The panel concluded that the indictment does not rely on the disallowed “right‑to‑control” theory rejected in Ciminelli, but instead alleges a classic fraudulent‑inducement scheme: defendants made material misrepresentations to induce followers to hand over money (by buying securities), and defendants’ object was to obtain money by selling their positions at inflated prices. Because the pleadings, taken as true at this stage, allege deception plus an object of obtaining money or property, the indictment survives a facial sufficiency challenge.

## What survives to trial
Reversal of the dismissal returns the case to the ordinary criminal process: the government must still prove the charged offenses beyond a reasonable doubt at trial. Important elements the government must ultimately establish with evidence are the same elements whose pleading the court found sufficient:

- That defendants made material misrepresentations or omissions in connection with the purchase or sale of securities;
- That those misrepresentations were part of a scheme to defraud (i.e., a plan to induce purchases so defendants could profit);
- That defendants acted with the requisite intent to deceive and to obtain money or property (the opinion treats obtaining money as the object of the fraud, even if the victims do not suffer net pecuniary loss);
- That the alleged acts occurred in connection with the purchase or sale of securities.

The opinion does not adjudicate the factual merits. It treats the indictment’s allegations as true for purposes of sufficiency review; factual disputes, credibility contests, and evidentiary questions remain for the district court and jury.

## Doctrinal takeaways
Several doctrinal points emerge from the court’s reasoning, grounded in Supreme Court precedent cited in the opinion:

- Fraudulent‑inducement theory remains a valid basis for prosecution under § 1348. If a defendant uses materially false statements to induce a victim to hand over money or property (for example, to buy securities), that theory targets a traditional property interest and is consistent with Ciminelli and Kousisis as interpreted here.
- There is no requirement that the defendant intend to leave the victim worse off in net economic terms. Following Kousisis, the Fifth Circuit reiterates that the offense can be complete when the defendant obtains the victim’s money or property by deceit, even if the victim receives something of market value in return or ultimately suffers no net loss.
- The right‑to‑control theory—treating the victim’s informational advantage or control over assets as the protected property interest—remains impermissible after Ciminelli. The panel distinguished that theory from fraudulent inducement and expressly found the indictment did not rely on right‑to‑control.

These doctrinal conclusions align § 1348 analysis with the line of mail/wire fraud cases the court cites, using the fraudulent‑inducement framework to define the protected property interest.

## Implications for social‑media promoters and market‑manipulation prosecutions
The decision signals several practical consequences for prosecutions (and for compliance) in the age of social‑media stock promotion:

- Posts by influencers that knowingly contain material falsehoods or misleading statements and are designed to cause followers to buy securities can form the basis for securities‑fraud indictments under § 1348 when the object is to obtain money or property (e.g., to generate a sell‑off profit).
- The form of the statement (social post, tweet, video) does not immunize the speaker from criminal liability; what matters is whether the alleged misrepresentations were material, connected to a purchase or sale, and intended to induce transactions that benefit the promoter.
- Evidence that promoters coordinated purchases, concealed their own sales, or otherwise structured communications to create artificial demand will be particularly relevant to proving scheme and intent.

For practitioners advising social‑media promoters, the decision underscores the importance of truthful, non‑misleading statements and proper disclosures of positions and conflicts; failing that can pose criminal as well as civil and regulatory risk.

## Limits and what this opinion does not change
The opinion is narrowly framed and should not be read to expand the elements of fraud or lower the government’s burden:

- The court resolved only a pleading sufficiency challenge. It did not find that the government has proved the elements, nor did it relax the beyond‑a‑reasonable‑doubt standard or the need for admissible evidence at trial.
- Materiality and scienter remain essential elements. The panel’s decision rests on the indictment’s allegations that the misstatements were material and made with deceptive intent; the government will still need to prove those elements at trial.
- The opinion does not resurrect right‑to‑control claims or otherwise authorize prosecutions based solely on deprivation of informational advantages. Ciminelli’s bar against treating valuable economic information as a protected property interest remains intact.

In short, United States v. Constantinescu clarifies that properly pleaded fraudulent‑inducement allegations rooted in social‑media market‑manipulation can survive a motion to dismiss under § 1348, but it preserves ordinary limits on the government’s proof obligations and the doctrinal boundary established by Ciminelli.
