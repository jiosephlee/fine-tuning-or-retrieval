Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# The APA, Interagency Roles, and Defunding by Fiat: Why State’s Termination of Resettlement Agreements Faces Headwinds

## Why this matters

The Ninth Circuit’s interim order in Pacito v. Trump—staying district-court injunctions while signaling that plaintiffs are likely to prevail on at least one Administrative Procedure Act claim—puts a spotlight on a recurring question in immigration and administrative law: when the Executive alters refugee admissions or funding, how far does deference to presidential authority extend, and when do statutory duties constrain that discretion? The panel’s short order makes clear that the court views the President’s authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend admissions as highly deferential, but it also identifies a separate, nontrivial line of legal exposure for the government tied to statutory obligations under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 (and related statutes and regulations) that govern domestic reception and placement services for refugees. Practically, the order instructs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements needed to provide reception and placement services for refugees already admitted—an unmistakable signal that abrupt defunding and wholesale termination of agreements may not be an unreviewable exercise of executive power.

## What the court said, in short

The panel granted a stay of the district court’s injunctions pending appeal, concluding that the government is likely to prevail on the merits of plaintiffs’ challenge to the President’s suspension of refugee admissions under § 1182(f). That tracks recent Supreme Court precedent (Trump v. Hawaii) treating § 1182(f) as textually capacious and deserving deference.

But crucially, the court also stated that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA challenge: the termination of cooperative agreements and the suspension of funding for the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) may have violated statutory duties set out in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 (and implementing regulations). Because the government admitted—at least in the district court and on appeal—that it had terminated cooperative agreements and could not show that it had actually secured replacements (despite representing that it was preparing an RFP), the Ninth Circuit directed the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services for refugees who have already been admitted.

Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and partial dissent crystallizes the competing statutory readings: the majority interprets § 1522 as imposing mandatory duties (or at least duties enforceable under the APA in this context), while Judge Lee reads § 1522 as primarily authorizing discretionary programs, not commanding them.

## Where the APA defects likely lie

The opinion points to three related APA-based vulnerabilities in the government’s course of action:

1. Contrary to law. Section 1522’s text and regulatory framework allocate responsibilities for reception and placement services (employment training and placement, English training, cash and medical assistance, and special provisions for unaccompanied minors). The Ninth Circuit flagged that the government’s termination of cooperative agreements might be inconsistent with those statutory prescriptions and with HHS/ORR’s duties to provide certain services “to the extent of available appropriations.” If § 1522 imposes at least some mandatory duties (especially where Congress has appropriated funds for refugee and migration assistance), then terminating agreements that implement those duties without lawful justification risks running “contrary to law” under the APA.

2. Failure to consider statutory responsibilities and related consequences. The administrative-record picture—termination of agreements by State, use of a separate foreign-aid executive order (EO 14169) to halt USRAP-related funding, and an unfulfilled promise of an RFP and replacement contractors—suggests the government did not adequately consider whether and how other agencies (notably HHS/ORR) would discharge their statutory duties once funding and agreements were cut. The APA requires reasoned decisionmaking. If State terminated agreements without accounting for ORR’s statutory obligations under § 1522 (and the practical consequences for refugees already admitted), that omission is a classic APA failing: an agency must address important aspects of a problem and cannot ignore clear statutory constraints on its discretion.

3. Disregard of reliance interests. The abrupt termination of long-standing cooperative agreements disrupted a network of nonprofit resettlement agencies that relied on federal funding and predictable workflows to serve refugees. The district court evidently viewed these reliance interests as material, and the Ninth Circuit implicitly confirmed that abrupt repudiation of such relationships—especially where the government gave no plausible assurance that replacement arrangements were in place—can render an action arbitrary and capricious. Reliance can be particularly weighty where Congress has set up a statutory program implemented through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with nonprofits.

Those three lines—statutory duty, failure to account for interagency and statutory constraints, and disregard of reliance—are the APA needles through which the Ninth Circuit is threading the government’s conduct.

## The interagency wrinkle: State vs. ORR/HHS

Pacito underscores an often-overlooked institutional dynamic: cooperative agreements for reception and placement are historically entered into by State (and sometimes DOS programs) but the statutory duties targeted by the court are housed in HHS (Office of Refugee Resettlement). The government’s strategy—using a separate foreign-aid order (EO 14169) to suspend funding streams and then having State terminate cooperative agreements—raises the question whether the termination improperly usurped or frustrated obligations located in another agency. The Ninth Circuit’s directive that the government reinstate cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted” practically recognizes that interagency actions cannot be taken in silo where statutory responsibilities are shared or coordinated across departments.

This is litigationally significant because the Executive could argue that appropriations and executive orders confer broad discretion to prioritize foreign aid and that § 1522’s duties are conditional (“to the extent of available appropriations”). The court, however, points to concrete congressional appropriations for migration and refugee assistance in FY 2025 (roughly $3.178 billion, even after a rescission) and to statutory reporting and accountability mechanisms in § 1523, suggesting that blanket defunding and termination is not automatically insulated from review, particularly in the absence of a credible transition plan or replacement contracts.

## § 1523 and accountability as a lever for judicial scrutiny

The Ninth Circuit cited § 1523’s reporting requirement—annual detailed reports to Congressional judiciary committees—implicitly as a reminder that Congress envisioned oversight and accountability for refugee programs. Where statutes create reporting and accountability structures, courts are more inclined to treat agency behavior as subject to judicially manageable legal constraints. A program that agencies are required to report on annually is less plausibly characterized as wholly discretionary. That reporting duty could sharpen judicial review in at least two ways: first, by evidencing congressional intent that refugee assistance be sustained and monitored; second, by providing a concrete record of program outputs and reliance interests that courts can use to assess whether an agency’s decisionmaking was reasoned.

## Practical stakes and likely downstream effects

For NGOs and refugees on the ground, the Ninth Circuit’s order is consequential. The court’s immediate instruction to reinstate necessary cooperative agreements for already-admitted refugees preserves essential resettlement services and signals judicial skepticism toward unilateral, across-the-board program shutdowns without transition plans. For the Executive Branch, Pacito should prompt careful interagency coordination and documented reasoning if it intends to wind down or reorganize refugee resettlement operations—especially where existing cooperative agreements implement statutory duties or where Congress has appropriated relevant funds.

Doctrinally, the decision (and the forthcoming full opinion) will likely be read as reinforcing two propositions: (1) the President’s suspension power under § 1182(f) is robust and heavily deferred to, and (2) the Executive cannot deploy that broad immigration authority (or separate foreign-aid vehicles) to nullify statutory programs administered by other agencies without reasoned explanation or attention to statutory obligations and reliance interests. That split—deference on admissions, but limits on defunding programmatic implementation—reflects the functional separation between immigration-control prerogatives and statutory administrative programs.

If the Ninth Circuit ultimately holds that § 1522 imposes enforceable duties as to certain services, that ruling could constrain future Administrations from attempting to effect programmatic change by withdrawing support at the funding or cooperative-agreement level without engaging in the procedural and substantive obligations required by the APA. Even if the court’s final opinion adopts a narrower equilibrium—mandating relief only for already-admitted refugees or only where funding and appropriations clearly exist—the decision will still make it harder to use blunt funding cuts to sidestep statutory responsibilities.

## A final analytic take

Pacito is a reminder that executive power in immigration is not a universal trump card. The President may have broad authority to suspend entries under § 1182(f), and the Ninth Circuit accepts that deference; but where Congress has established a statutory program implemented through cooperative agreements and appropriated funds, the APA provides a check on sudden administrative retrenchment done without reasoned explanation, interagency coordination, or attention to reliance and statutory obligations. The government’s own representations matters: promising an RFP while having no evidence of progress will not inoculate a wholesale termination when ORR/HHS duties and NGO reliance are implicated.

Look for the forthcoming opinion to elaborate the boundaries of § 1522’s obligations and the APA defects the panel flagged, and expect the case to influence how agencies coordinate when policy shifts intersect with statutorily mandated service programs.
