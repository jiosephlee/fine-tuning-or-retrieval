Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

## Hawaii’s Long Shadow: Ninth Circuit Reaffirms Sweeping Presidential Power Under §1182(f) to Halt Refugee Admissions

Why this matters

The Ninth Circuit’s expedited stay in Pacito v. Trump is short on prose but long on consequence. In holding that the President “has undoubtedly fulfilled” the only textual prerequisite of 8 U.S.C. §1182(f)—a finding that entry of a class of aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States”—the panel treated Trump v. Hawaii as controlling and effectively insulated suspension-of-entry decisions from searching judicial review. That posture matters because it confirms that, at least in the Ninth Circuit’s view, the President’s judgment that certain refugees pose a national-interest problem is entitled to extreme deference for the duration of litigation. At the same time, the court’s stay order signals an important carveout: statutory duties under the Refugee Act (8 U.S.C. §1522) can still give rise to judicially enforceable obligations separate from entry decisions. The result is an accommodation of broad executive power over admissions alongside a recognition that statutory programmatic duties may constrain how the government treats refugees once they are admitted.

What the panel did

Pacito came to the Ninth Circuit on an expedited appeal from preliminary injunctions entered by the Western District of Washington. The district court had enjoined key parts of an executive order (EO 14163) suspending refugee admissions under §1182(f) and had also enjoined the State Department’s termination of cooperative agreements that funded resettlement “reception and placement” services. The Ninth Circuit’s stay vacated earlier partial stays and, pending full merits briefing, stayed the district court’s preliminary injunctions in their entirety — with one important exception: the panel directed the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States, to the extent required by 8 U.S.C. §1522.

The court applied the familiar four-factor stay framework from Nken v. Holder, focusing on the two “most critical” factors: likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable harm. On the first factor the panel concluded the government “is likely to prevail” as to plaintiffs’ challenge to the suspension of admissions because §1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and contains a single predicate: the President’s finding that entry of a class of aliens would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Quoting Trump v. Hawaii, the panel said courts may not undertake “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” The remaining Nken factors—irreparable injury, potential harm to other parties, and the public interest—also tipped toward the government given the executive’s broad discretion in immigration and foreign affairs.

But the panel did not sweep away all judicial relief. It found plaintiffs likely to prevail on an APA-based challenge tied to the Refugee Act’s statutory obligations to provide resettlement services—principally 8 U.S.C. §1522—so it required the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted. That remedy was grounded in a statutory-program argument distinct from the question whether the executive could suspend entries under §1182(f).

How the decision cements the Hawaii framework

Pacito reads Trump v. Hawaii as doing much of the heavy lifting. The high-water mark from Hawaii was the Court’s observation that §1182(f) “exudes deference” and vests “ample power” in the President to impose entry restrictions. The Ninth Circuit treats that language as largely dispositive of the threshold statutory question: the statute requires only a presidential finding and courts are not to police the substantive merits of that finding beyond modest review. Practically, that means when the government invokes §1182(f) with national-security, foreign-relations, or other broad “interests of the United States” rationales, federal courts will be extremely reluctant to substitute their own assessments for the President’s judgment—especially at the preliminary-injunction stage.

Two features of Pacito deserve emphasis. First, the court applied Nken’s high bar for a stay and found likelihood of success on the merits satisfied by statutory text and Hawaii’s deference doctrine. That approach effectively collapses the §1182(f) merits inquiry into a near-presumptive win for the government when the executive makes a cognizable finding. Second, the panel’s invocation of national and foreign interests in evaluating the other stay factors underscores that these interests weigh heavily in the balance when the executive frames a suspension as protective of security, diplomacy, or related concerns. In short, Pacito operationalizes Hawaii: the President’s finding under §1182(f) is potent; courts will give it breathing room pending full review.

Where limits remain: statutory programs, APA review, and political branches

Pacito is not an unfettered blank check to the President. The Ninth Circuit’s requirement that the government reinstate cooperative agreements for reception-and-placement services demonstrates two important limits.

First, section 1182(f) operates on the question of “entry.” It does not magically erase other statutory duties Congress has imposed with respect to persons already admitted or to domestic programs. The court read §1522 as creating judicially enforceable programmatic duties—at least sufficiently plausible to warrant relief at the injunction stage—and compelled reinstatement of those agreements for admitted refugees. That does not undermine a broad reading of §1182(f) for admissions, but it does remind us that Congress can cabin executive action by creating statutory entitlements or programmatic frameworks that courts may enforce.

Second, the court’s willingness to entertain APA-based claims signals that not all consequences of executive policy are immunized by Hawaii deference. Hawaii dealt mainly with whether the President’s §1182(f) proclamation was subject to close judicial scrutiny; it did not and could not rewrite other statutory regimes that regulate federal agencies’ conduct. Thus, agencies remain subject to notice-and-comment, reasoned-decisionmaking, and statutory constraints when they act in their own capacity—especially when the action affects domestic programs and beneficiaries.

Practical and doctrinal implications

Several succinct implications follow from Pacito:

- Executive advantage on entry suspensions. Because the Ninth Circuit treats the presidential finding under §1182(f) as the dispositive trigger and borrows Hawaii’s restraint on “searching inquiry,” future administrations invoking §1182(f) will enjoy substantial defensive leverage at the preliminary-relief stage. Plaintiffs suing to enjoin new suspensions face an uphill climb to show likelihood of success absent clear textual or procedural infirmities.

- Litigation strategy shifts to statutory and administrative claims. Plaintiffs who cannot prevail on §1182(f) grounds may pivot to statutory claims tied to the Refugee Act or to APA challenges focused on agency action distinct from the proclamation itself—exactly what succeeded partially in Pacito. Challengers will press arguments that agency terminations or defunding measures violate statutory duties, that the agency failed to follow required procedures, or that the agency’s reasons were arbitrary and capricious.

- Nken’s factors in national-security-inflected immigration cases. Pacito demonstrates how courts operationalize the Nken framework when national-security considerations are invoked: likelihood of success favors the government if the relevant statute contains a simple textual trigger coupled with precedent emphasizing presidential deference, while irreparable harm and the public interest are read through a national-security lens that often favors governmental pause. Practitioners should thus expect preliminary-relief battles to hinge on whether plaintiffs can show non-deferential claims—statutory violations or process defects—that avoid Hawaii’s shadow.

- Congressional oversight and funding levers regain importance. If §1182(f) suspensions are difficult to enjoin, Congress’s role—through appropriations, reporting requirements (e.g., §1523), and programmatic statutes—becomes the real check. Pacito’s acceptance that §1522 can constrain agency conduct underscores the institutional levers Congress can deploy to shape outcomes even when admissions policy is presidentially directed.

Open questions and potential downstream effects

Pacito leaves unresolved contours that will matter in future litigation. The court’s stay was interlocutory and the merits opinion is forthcoming; the panel’s framing, however, telegraphs how it may approach the merits. Important open questions include whether courts will, in a full merits posture, accept any meaningful review beyond the textual finding in §1182(f). Will courts demand some minimal connection between the asserted harm and the class of aliens targeted? Hawaii emphasized deference; Pacito’s brief stay order suggests the Ninth Circuit may be unwilling to entertain anything more than a nominal review at the preliminary stage.

Another area to watch is the scope of the Refugee Act’s statutory duties. Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent crystallizes an alternative textual reading: §1522 as an authorization rather than a command. If a merits opinion adopts a narrower reading, the door to statutory enforcement might close. Conversely, if the panel affirms the district court’s conclusion that §1522 creates enforceable obligations, agencies will face substantial litigation risk when they cut off funding or terminate agreements that operationalize those obligations.

Finally, Pacito may spur strategic administrative design. Administrations seeking to limit admissions while minimizing domestic statutory constraints might try to separate the decision to suspend entries (a pure §1182(f) proclamation) from domestic programmatic changes, handling the latter through careful APA-compliant procedures or leaving in place baseline services for already-admitted individuals to avoid triggering §1522 claims. Conversely, opponents of suspension will invest more heavily in statutory and procedural claims, and in pushing Congress to act.

Conclusion

Pacito confirms that Hawaii’s doctrine remains the controlling framework for §1182(f)-based suspensions in at least the Ninth Circuit: a presidential finding that entry “would be detrimental” is a powerful, often dispositive, threshold at the preliminary stage. But the decision also confirms that presidential authority over admission does not automatically extinguish statutory boundaries imposed by Congress on federal programs. That split—extreme deference on the admission question, coupled with judicial willingness to enforce statutory resettlement duties—will shape the next phase of litigation and congressional response. The forthcoming merits opinion in Pacito will be important, but for now the Ninth Circuit’s stay sends a clear message: Hawaii’s long shadow continues to shelter executive suspension-of-entry decisions, even as statutory-program protections remain a viable route for judicial relief.
