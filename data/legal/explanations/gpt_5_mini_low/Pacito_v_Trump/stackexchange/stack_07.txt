Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### How do appropriations constraints interact with alleged statutory mandates in § 1522?
Question:
The order notes FY 2025 appropriations for migration and refugee assistance and an $800 million rescission. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) conditions some obligations "to the extent of available appropriations." If § 1522 imposes duties, how do courts evaluate whether appropriations are "available" and sufficient to compel performance? Can the executive cite reduced appropriations as a basis to terminate all cooperative agreements, or must it scale services consistent with available funds? Are there cases where courts ordered continued services notwithstanding reduced or uncertain appropriations?
Answer:
Short answer
- Under Pacito, the § 1522 “to the extent of available appropriations” language is a limiting clause courts will treat as a factual condition: whether appropriations are in fact “available” is a question the government must satisfy before it can sidestep statutory duties.  
- If a court concludes § 1522 imposes mandatory duties and appropriations are (or can reasonably be treated as) available, the executive may not simply terminate cooperative agreements across the board; the government must show that funds are unavailable or otherwise insufficient, and courts can order continued performance or scaled services consistent with the statutory mandate.  
- Pacito itself is an example: the panel concluded plaintiffs were likely to succeed on an APA claim that § 1522 requires services and directed reinstatement of cooperative agreements despite the government’s announced funding suspension and a rescission that reduced FY2025 migration/refugee appropriations.

Relevant legal standard and how the opinion applies it
- The statute’s operative constraint—“to the extent of available appropriations”—is a condition precedent to compelled performance, not a free pass to blanket nonperformance. The panel reads § 1522 as imposing duties (at least as to certain refugees admitted before the suspension) but expressly frames those duties as subject to the availability of appropriations. That makes “availability” a factual and legal question for the government to prove when it claims lack of funds as an excuse.  
- The court relied on the statutory text (the “shall” provisions in § 1522 and the specific duties enumerated in subsections (a), (d)(2)(B), and (e)(7)(A)) and on the FY2025 appropriations background (the $3.178 billion appropriation and the $800 million rescission) to assess whether the government had satisfied that limiting clause. The panel found the government had not shown it could lawfully discontinue the reception-and-placement services—indeed, the government admitted uncertainty about replacing cooperative agreements—so the court ordered reinstatement of agreements necessary to carry out § 1522 services for refugees already admitted.

What that means for executive action and scaling of services
- The executive can rely on reduced appropriations only if it can show the appropriations are truly unavailable for the statutory duties: i.e., either Congress withheld or rescinded funds in a way that leaves no funds available for the required services, or the executive can demonstrate a bona fide zero-fund situation. Mere budget reductions or policy-driven reallocations are not necessarily dispositive.  
- Where the statute imposes duties but appropriations are limited, the government’s lawful options are (1) demonstrate no funds are available for the mandated program (so the “to the extent” clause defeats a duty), or (2) provide a defensible, documented plan to scale services consistent with available funds while still complying with the statutory scheme. Absent satisfactory proof, courts may order continuation of services to the extent required by the statute and prior practice.

Pacito as precedent for court-ordered continued services
- Pacito squarely demonstrates that courts will order continued services when (i) they find a statutory duty likely exists and (ii) the government has not carried its burden of showing appropriations dooms performance. The panel (majority) directed reinstatement of the cooperative agreements to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted, despite the government’s termination of agreements and the FY2025 rescission. That relief was based on the court’s conclusion that plaintiffs were likely to succeed on an APA claim and on the government’s admitted uncertainty about providing substitute arrangements.

Counterpoint within the opinion
- The concurrence/dissent by Judge Lee emphasizes the other plausible reading: § 1522 is an authorization rather than an absolute mandate, and the “to the extent of available appropriations” and repeated “is authorized” language suggest executive discretion. That is the precise statutory ambiguity courts will grapple with when assessing whether duties are mandatory or permissive in light of appropriations. Pacito shows both outcomes are legally arguable; the panel majority (for the limited subset of plaintiffs) treated § 1522 as imposing duties subject to appropriations, while the dissent urged a permissive reading.

Practical implications for litigation and agency practice
- For challengers: point to statutory “shall” provisions, prior practice, and the existence of appropriations (even if reduced) and emphasize any government admissions of uncertainty; courts may order continued services pending resolution.  
- For the government: document precisely how appropriations were reduced or rendered unavailable and explain any necessary scaling decisions; bear the burden of proof that funds are unavailable for the statutory duties if it seeks to terminate services.

Bottom line
Pacito illustrates that “to the extent of available appropriations” is not an automatic shield. When the statute is plausibly mandatory and the government cannot show appropriations make compliance impossible, courts can and will require continued provision of services (or at least partial relief) consistent with § 1522. Conversely, where Congress or the record plainly shows there are no appropriated funds available for the statutory duties, courts will treat the limiting clause as dispositive—but the government must make that showing.
