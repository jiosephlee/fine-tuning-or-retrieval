Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Does 8 U.S.C. § 1522 impose mandatory duties or merely authorize refugee resettlement services?
Question:
The majority treats § 1522 as imposing statutory obligations to provide reception and placement services, while Judge Lee reads the section as authorizing (not requiring) programs, emphasizing the title and repeated "is authorized" language in §§ 1522(b)-(e). The majority points to § 1522(a)'s "shall" and HHS obligations for unaccompanied minors (§ 1522(d)(2)(B)) and for projects under § 1522(e)(7)(A). What canons of construction and precedent guide whether the "shall" in § 1522(a) converts the authorizations in (b)-(e) into mandates once appropriations exist? Are there cases interpreting § 1522 specifically on mandate vs. authorization?
Answer:
Short answer
The dispute turns on competing canons of construction and which textual features the court treats as decisive. The majority treats § 1522(a)’s “shall” (qualified by “to the extent of available appropriations”) plus discrete mandatory phrases in subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A) as imposing operational duties once appropriations exist; Judge Lee relies on the section title and the repeated “is authorized” language in subsections (b)–(e) to read the statute as permissive. The opinion does not cite any prior Supreme Court or Ninth Circuit decision that squarely resolves whether § 1522 is a command rather than an authorization; the panel therefore resolves the question by applying the usual textual canons described below.

What canons and principles the court used (and which are most relevant)
- Read the statute as a whole / noscitur a sociis: Judge Lee emphasizes the section title — “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees” — and the repeated statutory phrase that the Director “is authorized” to carry out programs in subsections (b)–(e). Titles and recurring permissive verbs are classic textual signals pointing to discretion rather than mandate; Lee treats them as controlling context for § 1522(a).
- Ordinary-meaning of “shall”: the majority points to § 1522(a)’s use of “shall” as the prototypical signal of a mandatory duty. Courts routinely treat “shall” as mandatory absent clear context to the contrary; the majority relies on that ordinary meaning to read (a) as imposing duties.
- Qualification by appropriations: both sides accept that statutory duties are limited by the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations.” That phrase is a statutory limit, not a nullifier: the majority treats it as making the obligation contingent on funding (i.e., an obligation that becomes operative when appropriations exist); Lee treats the same phrase as further evidence of permissive, discretionary authority (Congress authorizes programs subject to appropriations).
- Contextual hookup clause: Lee reads the opening clause of § 1522(a) (“In providing assistance under this section…”) to mean (a) prescribes how the executive must act if it elects to provide assistance under the authorization in (b)–(e). The majority reads (a) instead as imposing duties that govern the provision of services under the section when the executive is acting (or when appropriations are available).
- Plain-text vs. structure: the disagreement is a paradigmatic plain-text/structural contest — whether the explicit “shall” and particularized duties in (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A) outweigh the structural and labeling indicators of permissiveness in the section’s title and subsection language.
- Chevron/agency deference (implicitly relevant): the opinion does not decide deference issues here, but where statutory text is ambiguous courts sometimes defer to an agency interpretation; neither judgment in the interim order adopts an agency interpretive rule as decisive.

How the opinion applies those canons to §1522
- Majority side (panel order): the court finds the government “is not likely to prevail” on its APA defense because § 1522 “requires the government to provide certain reception and placement services.” It leans on (a)(1)(A)’s “shall … to the extent of available appropriations” and on provisions that use “shall” in (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A) to identify concrete obligations (financial/legal responsibility for unaccompanied minors when necessary; “shall develop and implement” alternative projects for refugees). The presence of FY‑2025 appropriations for migration and refugee assistance is treated as relevant support for the majority’s view that statutory duties are operative.
- Lee’s partial dissent: she emphasizes the section title (“Authorization…”) and the recurring “is authorized” phrasing in (b)–(e). She interprets §1522(a) as setting mandatory conditions that apply only if the government elects to fund programs — i.e., (a) constrains how the executive must act in providing assistance, but does not itself convert permissive authorizations into compulsory spending obligations.

Are there cases interpreting §1522 on mandate vs. authorization?
- The court’s interim order does not cite any prior decision that directly resolves whether § 1522 is a mandatory command rather than an authorization. The opinion frames the question as one of statutory interpretation resolved here by textual analysis rather than by controlling precedent specifically addressing §1522’s mandatory/authorizing character.

Practical takeaways for future argument or briefing
- If you want a court to read § 1522 as mandatory: emphasize the plain meaning of “shall,” point to the specific “shall” duties in (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), and show appropriations have been made (the majority treated the FY‑2025 appropriations as relevant).
- If you want a court to read § 1522 as permissive: stress the section title, the recurring “is authorized” language in (b)–(e), the opening clause tying (a) to “providing assistance under this section,” and the permissive nature of the “to the extent of available appropriations” limitation.
- Because the panel did not identify controlling precedent resolving §1522 itself, litigation will turn on which textual canons a particular court favors and on evidentiary facts about the existence and scope of appropriations and whether the government is actually “providing assistance” under the section.

Bottom line
The Ninth Circuit’s interim order records a split in interpretive emphasis: the panel majority treats §1522’s “shall” and particular mandatory phrases as imposing duties when appropriations exist; Judge Lee treats the statute’s title and repeated “is authorized” phrasing as showing Congress merely authorized, not required, resettlement programs. The opinion does not cite any prior case squarely resolving §1522’s command-versus-authorization question, so future resolution will continue to turn on textual canons (plain meaning of “shall,” titles and recurring permissive language, and the appropriations qualification) and on which interpretive signals a court finds decisive.
