Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### What is the scope of presidential authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions compared to refugee processing and funding decisions?
Question:
Relying on Trump v. Hawaii, the panel suggests the President is likely to prevail on the suspension of refugee entry under § 1182(f). But the funding and service-provision issues seem to be treated separately, potentially under the APA and § 1522. Where is the line between a permissible suspension of "entry" under § 1182(f) and actions that affect domestic resettlement services and contracts post-admission? Are there limits preventing § 1182(f) from being used to indirectly nullify domestic statutory obligations to already-admitted refugees?
Answer:
Short answer
- Under Pacito, § 1182(f) gives the President broad, deferential authority to suspend the “entry” of classes of aliens — a power the Ninth Circuit panel treated much as the Supreme Court did in Trump v. Hawaii. That authority reaches decisions that actually prevent people from coming into the United States.
- But that suspension power does not automatically authorize the Executive to nullify or ignore statutory duties that attach after admission. Pacito treats funding, cooperative agreements, and reception-and-placement obligations under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as analytically distinct from a suspension of entry and subject to ordinary statutory and APA review. The panel therefore stayed the district court’s injunction as to suspending admissions but directed reinstatement of cooperative agreements needed to fulfill § 1522 services for refugees already admitted.

Relevant legal doctrine and how Pacito applies it
1) § 1182(f) — suspension of entry
- Pacito repeats the Trump v. Hawaii framework: Congress vested “ample power” and the statute’s single textual prerequisite is the President’s finding that entry “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Pacito (quoting Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 684–85). The panel concluded the government was “likely to prevail” on the claim that EO 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions falls within § 1182(f) because the Order “does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority” to suspend “entry.” Pacito, Order at 5–6.
- Practical implication: a facially internal immigration measure that halts entry of a class of aliens is squarely within § 1182(f) and receives substantial judicial deference on the merits.

2) Post‑admission programs and § 1522 — separate statutory obligations
- Pacito draws a clear line between (a) the President’s authority to bar entry and (b) statutory duties to provide resettlement services to refugees once they are admitted. The court described § 1522’s provisions (reception and placement, cash and medical assistance, special rules for unaccompanied children) and noted that Congress has historically funded those programs and required reporting. Pacito, Order ¶¶ discussing § 1522.
- The panel declined to treat those post‑admission obligations as automatically displaced by a § 1182(f) entry suspension. It concluded the government was not likely to prevail on all APA challenges and, because of statutory duties and the government’s admitted uncertainty about replacing service providers, ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide the § 1522 services to refugees already in the United States. Pacito, Order at 9–11.

3) How the line is drawn in practice
- The operative distinction in Pacito is functional: § 1182(f) covers decisions that stop or restrict the entry of aliens (i.e., people physically outside the United States seeking admission). Those decisions are highly insulated from judicial second‑guessing.
- Actions that withdraw funding, terminate cooperative agreements, or otherwise interfere with reception-and-placement services for persons already admitted implicate statutory duties (§ 1522) and ordinary administrative-law review (APA). The court treated the government’s termination of cooperative agreements as susceptible to judicial relief because such terminations affect obligations owed to admitted refugees. Pacito, Order at 10–11.
- Put another way: a presidential suspension that prevents a class from entering is different in kind from using that suspension as a means to eliminate executive compliance with Congress’s post‑entry commands.

Limits on using § 1182(f) to nullify post‑admission obligations
- Pacito demonstrates two discrete limits:
  1. Textual/functional limit: § 1182(f) authorizes suspension of entry; it does not by its terms rewrite other statutory programs that apply after admission. The panel emphasized that § 1182(f) authorizes suspending “entry of . . . any class of aliens,” U.S.C. § 1182(f), and treated post‑admission statutory duties under § 1522 as separate legal questions. Pacito, Order at 5–6, 9–11.
  2. Statutory/administrative-law limit: when Congress has prescribed post‑admission duties (even “to the extent of available appropriations”), courts can review executive steps that frustrate those duties under the APA and the relevant statutory text. Pacito analyzed § 1522’s language and funding context and concluded the government was not likely to prevail on at least some APA claims; the panel therefore ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide statutorily required reception-and-placement services. Pacito, Order at 9–11.
- The concurrence/dissent by Judge Lee underscores the point of statutory interpretation dispute (authorization v. mandate). But regardless of that interpretive disagreement, the majority treated § 1522 as creating enforceable expectations the Executive could not simply nullify via an entry suspension. Pacito, Lee, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part (discussing § 1522’s “authorization” language and disagreeing about mandatory effect).

A practical test (derivable from Pacito)
- Ask (1) does the Executive action directly affect who may enter the country? If yes → squarely within § 1182(f) and highly deferential review. Pacito supports this conclusion for entry suspensions.
- If (2) the action withdraws funding, cancels cooperative agreements, or otherwise prevents performance of programs that serve persons already admitted → those steps do not automatically fall within § 1182(f) and are subject to statutory/APA scrutiny; courts can enjoin such steps if they unlawfully undermine statutory obligations (as Pacito did with respect to reinstating cooperative agreements for § 1522 services).

Bottom line
Pacito treats § 1182(f) as a potent but limited instrument: powerful for suspending entry, not a license to erase separate statutory obligations that attach after admission. The panel stayed the district court’s injunction as to suspending admissions (relying on Trump v. Hawaii) but required reinstatement of cooperative agreements and reception-and-placement services under § 1522 for refugees already inside the United States — reflecting the court’s view that post‑admission statutory duties are a distinct legal question subject to normal statutory interpretation and APA constraints.
