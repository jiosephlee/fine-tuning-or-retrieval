Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### On what APA grounds is the government "not likely to prevail" regarding defunding and termination of refugee cooperative agreements?
Question:
The order previews that the government is unlikely to win at least one APA challenge tied to § 1522 obligations, but it does not specify the APA theory. Is the issue arbitrary-and-capricious review under § 706(2)(A), contrary-to-law under § 706(2)(A) because it conflicts with § 1522, or failure to follow required procedures (notice-and-comment) if the terminations functioned as a rule/policy change? How do courts generally classify mass termination of cooperative agreements and funding suspensions for APA purposes—final agency action, procurement discretionary action exempt from APA, or programmatic decisions tied to statutory duties?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel’s interim ruling points most clearly to a contrary‑to‑law APA claim under 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A): the panel concluded that § 1522 imposes statutory obligations to provide reception and placement services and that the State Department’s mass terminations and funding suspension conflict with those duties, so the government “is not likely to prevail” on at least one APA challenge. Other APA theories—arbitrary‑and‑capricious review and procedural‑rulemaking challenges—are possible adjuncts, but the opinion itself rests on a statutory‑conflict theory (i.e., unlawful agency action because it contradicts a statutory mandate) rather than on pure notice‑and‑comment or procurement‑exemption reasoning.

Why that follows from the opinion
- The panel states expressly that “for reasons to be explained in full . . . the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA,” and then points to 8 U.S.C. § 1522’s text and duties. It highlights mandatory language in subsection (a) and specific duties (employment training, English instruction, cash/medical support, responsibility for unaccompanied children) and the statute’s authorization of cooperative agreements to carry out those statutorily described services.
- The district court was directed to reinstate the cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522,” which reflects the court’s view that the terminations are inconsistent with § 1522 and therefore unlawful. That is exactly the posture of a § 706(2)(A) “contrary to law” claim: agency action that conflicts with a statutory command may be set aside.

Relation to arbitrary‑and‑capricious and procedural theories
- Arbitrary‑and‑capricious (also reviewed under § 706(2)(A) as the court described): If the government defunded and terminated agreements without a reasoned explanation, without considering important aspects of the problem (including statutory obligations and available appropriations), or changed a settled program without justification, plaintiffs could alternatively or additionally prevail under an arbitrary‑and‑capricious theory. The opinion suggests the court found the government’s uncertainty about replacing providers relevant (the government told the court it was “actively preparing a request for proposals,” but later said it did not know if any progress had been made), which feeds an APA reasoned‑decisionability claim. But the panel’s cited authority and its direction focus principally on statutory noncompliance.
- Notice‑and‑comment rulemaking: That theory requires showing the terminations/suspension amounted to a rule or substantive policy change subject to the APA’s rulemaking requirements. The opinion does not treat the terminations as classic rulemaking, nor does it rest its interim ruling on a procedural‑notice‑and‑comment deficiency. The panel instead treats the dispute as whether the executive’s action conflicts with § 1522 and therefore is unlawful. So notice‑and‑comment is a plausible ancillary claim only if plaintiffs can show the terminations implemented a general substantive rule rather than discrete contract actions or programmatic, one‑off decisions.

How courts generally classify mass terminations/funding suspensions (as reflected in the opinion)
- Final agency action and reviewability: Courts will treat agency decisions that have direct legal consequences (e.g., ending statutorily mandated services) as final agency action reviewable under the APA unless another statutory bar applies. The Ninth Circuit panel here treated the terminations/funding suspension as subject to review because they implicated statutory duties in § 1522 and produced immediate, concrete effects (termination of cooperative agreements and suspension of services).
- Procurement discretion and § 701(a)(2) limits: Some procurement choices and matters “committed to agency discretion by law” can be unreviewable. But when an agency’s procurement/contracting moves directly contravene a statutory duty (e.g., the statute requires provision of certain services “to the extent of available appropriations” or otherwise creates mandatory conditions), courts are willing to review and to enjoin agency action that defeats those statutory obligations. The panel’s directive to reinstate cooperative agreements needed to supply § 1522 services shows the court viewed these actions as programmatic decisions tied to statutory duties—not nonreviewable procurement discretion.
- Programmatic decisions tied to statutory duties: When mass terminations effectively nullify a Congress‑mandated program or statutory duties, courts treat those decisions as reviewable final agency actions and examine whether they are lawful, as the panel did here.

Bottom line for litigation strategy and doctrinal framing
- The strongest and clearest vehicle suggested by the opinion is a § 706(2)(A) “contrary to law” challenge: show that § 1522 imposes enforceable obligations that the terminations/funding suspension defeat. The panel’s language and its order to reinstate agreements track that theory.
- Secondary or alternative theories that plaintiffs should preserve: arbitrary‑and‑capricious review challenging the reasonableness and record support for the terminations; and, if facts support it, a procedural claim that the government’s actions amounted to a substantive rule change requiring notice‑and‑comment. But the opinion’s emphasis is statutory conflict/reviewability, not a pure procurement‑exemption or pure rulemaking failure.

Caveat from the opinion
- Judge Lee’s partial dissent emphasizes a textual, interpretive dispute about whether § 1522 is genuinely mandatory or chiefly an authorization. That highlights the central contested legal question on the merits: whether § 1522’s text creates judicially enforceable duties. The panel majority’s interim ruling indicates it views § 1522 as imposing sufficient obligations to make the government unlikely to prevail on that APA challenge.
