Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### How can the Ninth Circuit both grant a stay pending appeal and simultaneously order reinstatement of cooperative agreements?
Question:
The panel says it is staying the district court’s preliminary injunctions "in their entirety, except as specified" and then directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. I'm confused procedurally: isn't reinstating agreements affirmative, injunction-like relief? Under Nken, stays preserve the status quo rather than order new actions. What is the doctrinal basis for issuing affirmative directives within a stay order, and how does the court reconcile that with the traditional limits on stays versus injunctions pending appeal?
Answer:
Short answer: The panel treated reinstating the cooperative agreements as restoring the status quo that existed immediately before the government’s February terminations — not as a new, free‑standing injunction substituting the district court’s merits ruling. That restoration was deemed necessary to preserve the status quo for those refugees while the appeal proceeds, so the court incorporated that affirmative direction into its stay order under the same stay/ equitable authority the Supreme Court described in Nken.

Why that is doctrinally permissible (grounded in the opinion)

- Nken’s framework controls the standard for a stay pending appeal — the four factors, with likelihood of success and irreparable harm the most critical. The Ninth Circuit invokes Nken at the start of its analysis and applies those factors. It does not say that Nken forbids any affirmative relief; it uses Nken to justify what relief is necessary to prevent irreparable injury while the appeal runs.

- The panel concluded the government is likely to prevail on the challenge to the President’s suspension of admissions but is not likely to prevail on at least one APA claim grounded in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 (the statutory duties to provide reception and placement services). The government had terminated all cooperative agreements in February 2025 after the district court’s initial oral injunction, and by the panel’s account it had no workable plan in place to replace those providers.

- Given that combination — (a) a serious risk that the government has binding statutory obligations under § 1522 that it is failing to meet, and (b) an existing disruption caused by the government’s affirmative termination of contracts — the panel directed reinstatement of the cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” The panel framed that direction as restoring the services “that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements.” In the panel’s words, the reinstatement is a step “in light of the government’s uncertainty regarding its ability to provide the reception and placement services statutorily mandated under 8 U.S.C. § 1522.”

- In short, the panel saw reinstatement as preservation of the status quo ante (the contractual/resettlement arrangements and services in place for admitted refugees before the government’s terminations) rather than a novel injunction ordering the government to undertake new programs. The court’s equitable power to preserve the status quo pending appeal therefore supports including that restorative direction within the stay order.

Practical distinction the opinion rests on

- Stay (as used here): suspends the district court’s injunctions pending appeal but preserves existing conditions for the limited class that may suffer irreparable harm (admitted refugees who need reception and placement services).
- Affirmative direction (reinstatement): characterized as necessary to prevent irreparable injury and to maintain the prior operative state of affairs produced by the pre‑termination cooperative agreements while the appeal decides whether § 1522 imposes enforceable duties.

Caveat shown in the opinion

- Judge Lee’s partial dissent underscores the tension: he would have stayed the district court’s orders in full because he reads § 1522 as permissive (authorizations, not mandates). That disagreement shows the panel is aware this is not a routine stay but a targeted measure premised on the panel’s preliminary view that some statutory duties likely survive the suspension.

Bottom line

The panel did not treat a stay as a prohibition on any affirmative relief; it used its stay/ equitable authority to order the restoration of a pre‑existing operational state (the cooperative agreements and customary services) because doing so was necessary to prevent irreparable harm to refugees and to preserve the factual and legal posture the appeal must resolve. The opinion explicitly ties the reinstatement to preserving the reception-and‑placement services under § 1522 and to the Nken stay factors.
