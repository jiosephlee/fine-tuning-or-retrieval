Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 3: # Application of the Stay Factors

This section explains how the Ninth Circuit applied the four-factor stay test from Nken v. Holder to two distinct aspects of the dispute: (1) the President’s suspension of refugee entry under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (Executive Order 14163), and (2) the executive branch’s termination of resettlement funding and cooperative agreements that provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States (claims arising under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and the APA). The panel treated these as analytically separable for the stay analysis and reached different conclusions about the government’s likelihood of prevailing on each claim. The court’s reasoning shows how deference in immigration and foreign-affairs contexts narrows judicial review of admission suspensions, while statutory duties that govern domestic post-admission programs may be subject to ordinary statutory and administrative-law scrutiny.

## Likelihood of success — entry suspension under § 1182(f)

The panel concluded the government is likely to succeed on the merits of plaintiffs’ challenge to Executive Order 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions. That conclusion rests on the Supreme Court’s framework in Trump v. Hawaii and the plain text of § 1182(f). Section 1182(f) authorizes the President, whenever he “finds” that entry of any aliens or any class of aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” to suspend entry or impose restrictions. The Ninth Circuit emphasized three points from Hawaii:

- The statutory language is broad and facially unbounded: it permits suspension of “the entry of . . . any class of aliens,” a grant of authority that the Supreme Court characterized as “exuding deference” to the President.
- Judicial inquiry into the President’s national-security or foreign-affairs justifications is limited; courts should not conduct an intrusive probing of the persuasiveness of the President’s findings.
- Where the President has made the requisite “finding,” the text of § 1182(f) supplies the operative prerequisite and signals deference.

Applying those principles, the panel held that the President plainly made the required finding in EO 14163 and that § 1182(f)’s text supplies no judicially enforceable limit that the Executive Order exceeded. Because Nken’s first factor—likelihood of success on the merits—is the “most critical,” the court treated this as a strong reason to grant a stay as to the entry-suspension claim. The panel therefore stayed the district court’s injunction insofar as it barred enforcement of the EO’s suspension of refugee admissions.

## Likelihood of success — statutory/APA challenge to termination of services under § 1522

The court reached the opposite conclusion about the government’s likelihood of success on at least one of plaintiffs’ APA/statutory claims arising from 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which governs reception and placement services for refugees. The panel concluded the government is not likely to prevail on a claim that the executive branch may lawfully terminate cooperative agreements and funding that provide statutorily prescribed services to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.

The court’s reasoning focused on the text and structure of § 1522 and related implementing regulations. Section 1522 enumerates specific programmatic responsibilities—employment training and placement, English-language instruction, cash and medical assistance, and special responsibilities for unaccompanied children—and contemplates provision of those services “to the extent of available appropriations.” The statute authorizes use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies to deliver reception and placement services. The panel saw these statutory provisions as creating substantive obligations the government must honor for admitted refugees, not merely permissive policy options the Executive may discard without regard to statutory constraints. Given the government had terminated cooperative agreements and could not demonstrate progress toward securing alternative arrangements, the court concluded plaintiffs had a strong chance of showing the terminations were unlawful under the APA (for example, arbitrary and capricious or contrary to statute).

Because the government was unlikely to win on the APA/statutory claim concerning provision of domestic resettlement services to admitted refugees, the panel limited the scope of the stay. While it stayed the district court’s preliminary injunctions directed at the EO’s entry suspension, it ordered the government to reinstate cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States” and to provide the usual and customary services previously afforded under those agreements. That remedy reflects the panel’s view that even when the Executive has broad authority to control admissions, it may not unilaterally and immediately abandon statutorily mandated domestic services for people already admitted.

## Irreparable harm and the public interest

Nken’s second and fourth factors—irreparable injury and public interest—were analyzed in tandem and tipped differently depending on the disputed action.

- For the entry suspension, the panel credited the government’s asserted national-security and foreign-affairs harms. Because refugee admissions implicate foreign policy, national security, and the President’s plenary immigration authority, the court gave weight to the harm the government would suffer if an injunction were allowed to block the policy during appeal. The panel invoked Hawaii’s recognition of presidential discretion in immigration matters and concluded that the public interest and potential irreparable injury favored the government on the suspension issue.

- For the termination of resettlement services, the balance was different. The harms the plaintiffs identified were immediate, domestic, and concrete: refugees already admitted who rely on reception and placement services could lose statutory benefits necessary for economic self-sufficiency, health, or care (including particular statutory protections for unaccompanied minors). Those harms are the kind of irreparable injury that supports preliminary relief when they flow from actions the court believes may be legally invalid. The public interest also favored maintaining statutory protections for vulnerable individuals and ensuring compliance with congressional directives governing domestic refugee assistance.

Thus, while national and foreign policy considerations supported staying relief that would impede the EO, immediate domestic harms from cessation of services counseled against staying the district court’s orders that compelled reinstatement of cooperative agreements to serve admitted refugees.

## Balance of equities — preserving presidential discretion while preventing unlawful cessation of services

The fourth Nken factor—balance of equities—required the court to weigh competing harms to the parties. The panel sought to respect presidential discretion in admission policy while preventing what it viewed as a likely unlawful abdication of statutory duties concerning admitted refugees.

- On one side, keeping the district court’s injunction in effect as to the EO would constrain the President’s broad statutory authority under § 1182(f) and could undermine foreign-policy or national-security judgments; the equities thus favored the government regarding admissions.

- On the other side, allowing the government to maintain its termination of cooperative agreements without immediate restoration would uproot the delivery of services to refugees already lawfully in the United States and would likely impede statutory objectives (economic self-sufficiency, medical and cash support, special care for children). The equities therefore favored plaintiffs with respect to domestic resettlement services.

The remedy the panel imposed reflects that weighing: a stay of the district court’s injunctions as to the EO’s suspension of entries, but an order requiring reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide § 1522 reception and placement services to admitted refugees. That approach attempts to thread the needle between deferring to executive authority over admissions and enforcing statutory protections that bind the executive once refugees have entered the country.

## Dissenting perspective (Judge Lee)

Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent underscores the tension the panel navigated. Lee agreed that the government likely would prevail on the validity of the admission suspension under § 1182(f) and would have stayed the district court’s injunctions in full. He read § 1522 as an authorization rather than a mandatory command—pointing to the statute’s title (“Authorization for programs”), repeated use of “is authorized,” and qualifications such as “to the extent of available appropriations” as evidence that Congress granted permissive authority, not a binding duty to maintain specific programs or cooperative agreements. From Lee’s perspective, the court should not compel the Executive to continue programs the statute merely authorizes.

The majority disagreed on that statutory interpretation and accordingly crafted a stay that preserves presidential discretion over admissions while requiring continued provision of services the panel viewed as mandated for refugees already admitted.

## Takeaway for reading the opinion

The panel’s application of Nken illustrates two useful habits for reading opinions that address separable issues:

- Identify the distinct legal regimes at play (here, plenary admission authority under § 1182(f) and domestic statutory duties under § 1522) and treat the stay factors separately for each, because deference and harms vary with institutional context.

- Track how textual interpretation of statutes affects each Nken prong: a broad delegation of authority (as the court read § 1182(f)) reduces the government’s burden on likelihood of success and public-interest harms, while statutory provisions that create specific programmatic duties (as the court read § 1522) increase the risk that the government cannot show likelihood of success and that the balance of equities favors maintaining relief for affected individuals.

The Ninth Circuit’s interim order embodies those distinctions: deference and a stay for the admission suspension; enforcement of statutory reception-and-placement obligations for admitted refugees.
