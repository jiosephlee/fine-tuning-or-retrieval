Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 4: # Interim Disposition and Practical Directives

This chapter explains the Ninth Circuit’s interim order in Pacito v. Trump: what prior stays the panel vacated, which district-court injunctions the panel suspended pending appeal, the narrow operational exception it carved out, and the immediate actions the court directed the government to take. It also explains the legal and practical underpinnings the panel relied on to justify those directives and notes the procedural posture going forward.

## What stays the panel vacated and why that matters

The panel expressly vacated two prior Ninth Circuit orders: the administrative stay it had issued on July 18, 2025, and an earlier partial stay issued March 25, 2025 (as later amended April 21 and May 9, 2025). Vacatur of those orders means the court withdrew those earlier temporary adjustments to the district court’s injunctions and replaced them with the new interim stay described in the panel order. Practically, vacating the prior stays removes any inconsistent or overlapping conditions those prior stays imposed and substitutes a single, clarified interim disposition while the merits are decided.

Why that matters procedurally: the March 25 order had already carved out a narrow exception allowing certain injunctive relief to remain in effect for refugees conditionally approved before January 20, 2025. By vacating that order and its amendments, the panel reasserted control and then reissued a consolidated stay with specific, differently framed exceptions. Vacatur is an ordinary device to avoid confusion and ensure the court’s current interim ruling controls.

## The new stay: what is stayed and what is not

The panel issued a stay pending appeal of the district court’s two preliminary injunctions (the February 28, 2025 written injunction defining scope of the initial February 25 ruling, and the March 24, 2025 injunction ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements). The stay is broad: it pauses enforcement of those district-court orders “in their entirety” except for the limited exception the panel set out.

Functionally, the stay means that, until the Ninth Circuit issues its final opinion in this appeal (or further order), the government is not bound by the district court’s injunctions except to the extent the panel’s exception requires action. A stay pending appeal is an interlocutory tool: it preserves the status quo while the appellate court reviews the district court’s preliminary relief, and it reflects the panel’s assessment—based on the four Nken factors—that the government has shown a likelihood of success on at least some claims and that other factors (irreparable harm, public interest, balance of harms) favor a stay.

## The directed exception: reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees

Although the stay suspends the district court’s injunctions generally, the panel ordered a focused exception. The government is required to reinstate those cooperative agreements that are necessary to provide the “reception and placement” services described in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States. The panel characterized these services as the “usual and customary services” historically provided under prior cooperative agreements.

Key points about the exception’s scope and legal basis:

- Basis in the statute: § 1522 establishes a congressional scheme for domestic refugee resettlement services—often provided through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with nonprofit organizations—and describes specific categories of services the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement must consider “to the extent of available appropriations.” The panel concluded that on the present record the government has not shown it is likely to prevail, under the Administrative Procedure Act challenge, in denying these statutorily described reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted.

- Which refugees are covered: the ordered reinstatement applies to refugees already admitted to the United States. The order does not require reinstatement of cooperative agreements for processing refugees who remain abroad or for admitting refugees going forward while the moratorium under the Executive Order remains stayed by the panel’s deference-based reasoning.

- Nature of services required: the court identified core services that must be made available, invoking § 1522’s text and historical practice. These include employment training and placement (aimed at achieving economic self-sufficiency), English-language training, cash assistance administered in a way designed not to disincentivize self-sufficiency, and case management/support services that have typically been part of reception-and-placement cooperative agreements. The court also referenced the § 1522(e)(7)(A) text concerning interim support and medical services for refugees in their first thirty-six months, which informs the practical contours of required medical and cash assistance programs.

- Special provision for unaccompanied minors: in light of § 1522(d)(2)(B), the panel directed that the Director assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied minor refugees “if necessary” and make necessary decisions to provide for their immediate care. The panel’s instruction is situation-specific—triggered by the presence and needs of such children—and tracks the statute’s language authorizing the Director to take immediate action in interim periods.

- “Usual and customary” qualifier: the court’s command is not open-ended; it is tethered to the historic content of reception-and-placement cooperative agreements (the services “usually and customarily” provided). This allows some operational judgment (e.g., the exact design of programs) while requiring reinstatement of the basic service structure the statute contemplates and that existed before termination of the cooperative agreements.

## Why the panel ordered reinstatement now: concern about the government’s representations

A practical driver of the court’s targeted directive was the government’s own representations about its contingency plans. The government had informed the district court in March it was “actively preparing a request for proposals” (RFP) to identify new resettlement agencies to provide reception-and-placement services. Yet at oral argument before the Ninth Circuit, government counsel acknowledged uncertainty about whether any progress had been made toward securing new providers. Given that uncertainty, and the statutory obligations in § 1522 that contemplate services for refugees admitted to the United States, the panel concluded that the risk of immediate harm to admitted refugees and to unaccompanied children weighed in favor of requiring reinstatement of the cooperative agreements necessary to deliver those services.

In short: the court viewed the government’s lack of demonstrated progress toward a transition to new providers as insufficient to excuse an immediate resumption of the prior cooperative-agreement-based service delivery for already-admitted refugees.

## Denial of the July 17 emergency motion as moot

The panel denied as moot the government’s July 17, 2025 emergency motion that sought clarification, enforcement, or amendment of the previously issued stay. Because the panel replaced the earlier administrative stay and issued the present consolidated stay with its specific operational exception, the earlier emergency motion no longer presented an active dispute requiring separate relief. The ruling thus resolves the earlier emergency filing by supersession.

## Procedural posture going forward: merits opinion to follow; stay remains

The panel explicitly stated that an opinion on the merits will issue “in due course.” The current order is interim: it stays the district court’s injunctions pending final resolution of the appeal, but it also imposes the narrow operational obligation to reinstate cooperative agreements to serve refugees already admitted. The stay will remain in effect until the Ninth Circuit issues its full merits opinion or orders otherwise. Parties should therefore treat the reinstatement directive as an immediate, enforceable obligation even while the broader dispute about the Executive Order and the district court’s preliminary injunctions awaits final appellate resolution.

## Practical implications for the parties and for practitioners

- For the government: immediate steps must be taken to reinstate the specific cooperative agreements (or their functional equivalents) necessary to deliver the usual reception-and-placement services to refugees already in the United States. If the government intends to rely on a different contractual structure (e.g., newly issued RFPs and new agencies), it will need to show either (1) that those alternatives are already in place and provide materially equivalent services without interruption, or (2) obtain further relief from the Ninth Circuit. The panel’s concern about the lack of demonstrated progress means the government should document any concrete transition steps promptly.

- For resettlement organizations: organizations whose cooperative agreements were terminated and who historically provided reception-and-placement services should be prepared to resume work under reinstated agreements for admitted refugees. The “usual and customary” standard will likely be applied operationally—organizations should expect to deliver core services identified in § 1522 (employment training/placement, English training, cash and medical assistance design, case management), and to engage with the court or magistrate-appointed overseer if disputes arise about sufficiency or scope.

- For the district court and enforcement: the district court’s prior enforcement order (the July 14 framework appointment of a magistrate) had been administratively stayed; with the new, more limited requirement from the Ninth Circuit, the district court’s enforcement role may be limited to ensuring compliance with the appellate panel’s specific reinstatement directive, and the Ninth Circuit signaled it will supply a full merits opinion later.

In sum, the Ninth Circuit’s interim order replaces earlier, piecemeal stays with a consolidated stay pending appeal, but it requires the government to reinstate the cooperative-agreement-based infrastructure necessary to deliver statutorily described reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States. The panel tied that requirement to the statutory language of § 1522 and to the government’s failure to demonstrate an operative alternative, and it left the larger merits questions—principally the validity of the Executive Order’s admissions suspension and the scope of § 1522—for the merits opinion to follow.
