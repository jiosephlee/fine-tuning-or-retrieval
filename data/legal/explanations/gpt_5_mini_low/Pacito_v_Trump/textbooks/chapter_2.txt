Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 2: # Governing Legal Framework

This chapter describes the legal standards and statutory provisions the Ninth Circuit relied on in its interim stay order in Pacito v. Trump. The Court’s order applied the familiar stay factors governing stays pending appeal, described the President’s authority to suspend entry of aliens under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (and how Trump v. Hawaii frames judicial review of that authority), and identified the statutory structure created by the Refugee Act—particularly 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and related provisions—relevant to challenges to the Executive’s termination of cooperative agreements and suspension of refugee-program funding. It also explains the role of appropriations and the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) in policing agency action in this context.

## Stay pending appeal: Nken v. Holder factors

A stay pending appeal is an equitable remedy; the Supreme Court’s Nken v. Holder framework sets the governing four-factor test. A movant must show (1) a strong likelihood of success on the merits, (2) that it would suffer irreparable harm absent a stay, (3) that other parties would not be substantially harmed by the stay, and (4) that the public interest favors granting the stay. Nken emphasizes that the first two factors—likelihood of success and irreparable injury—are the most critical. A court may deny or grant a stay if the balance of these considerations favors preservation of the status quo pending appellate review.

In Pacito the Ninth Circuit applied those same factors. The panel concluded that the government had demonstrated it was likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ statutory and constitutional challenges to the President’s suspension of refugee admissions—principally because § 1182(f) grants the President broad power and courts owe substantial deference in national-security and immigration-entry decisions (see next section). The panel also treated the remaining factors (irreparable injury, injury to others, and the public interest) as tipping toward the government in light of the national and foreign-policy interests implicated by a refugee moratorium. Because the first two Nken factors are most important, the panel’s view that the government was likely to succeed was decisive in granting a stay of the district court’s injunctions (with specific, limited exceptions discussed below).

## Presidential authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and Trump v. Hawaii

8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) authorizes the President, after making a finding that “the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” to “suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens” or to impose restrictions on entry “for such period as he shall deem necessary.” The statute is broadly worded and confers plenary-looking authority over admission decisions.

In Trump v. Hawaii (2018), the Supreme Court construed § 1182(f) as providing the President with wide latitude and instructed that courts should not engage in a searching review of the President’s justifications when the suspension of entry is premised on national-security and foreign-relations concerns. The Court described the statute as one that “exudes deference” to the President and said that the “sole prerequisite” is the President’s finding that entry would be detrimental to U.S. interests. The Pacito panel relied on Trump v. Hawaii to justify strong deference to the Executive’s determination that refugee admissions should be suspended and to conclude that the government was likely to prevail on challenges to the suspension under § 1182(f). The panel stressed that courts are not to reweigh competing foreign-policy judgments but should accept the President’s finding unless the executive exceeds textual limits of § 1182(f)—which the panel found the order did not do.

That deference, however, applies to the suspension of entry itself, not necessarily to other statutory duties Congress has imposed on agencies after refugees are admitted. The panel’s stay thus distinguished between (a) the Executive’s authority to halt admissions under § 1182(f), and (b) statutory obligations under the Refugee Act that may constrain how the government treats refugees already admitted or conditionally approved before the moratorium.

## Refugee Act provisions on post-admission services (8 U.S.C. § 1522 and related provisions; 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq.)

The Refugee Act and its implementing regulations establish a statutory scheme for resettlement services after refugees are admitted to the United States. Several provisions are central in Pacito:

- 8 U.S.C. § 1522(a)(1)(A). This subsection contains provisions the district court (and the Ninth Circuit panel for part of its reasoning) read as imposing duties on the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR). It directs the Director, “to the extent of available appropriations,” to make available sufficient resources for employment training, English-language training, cash assistance structured not to discourage self-sufficiency, and gender-equal access to training. The “to the extent of available appropriations” phrase injects a link between statutory requirements and funding.

- 8 U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). This provision permits (and authorizes) the government to provide resettlement services through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies. Historically, the federal government has used cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations to deliver the “reception and placement” services referenced throughout the statute.

- 8 U.S.C. § 1522(d)(2)(B). This subsection addresses obligations concerning unaccompanied refugee minors, authorizing the Director to assume legal and financial responsibility during an interim period and to make “necessary decisions” for the child’s immediate care. The subsection uses both “shall” language and qualifiers—“if necessary”—which the concurrence in Pacito emphasized in arguing that the statutory language is permissive and context-dependent.

- 8 U.S.C. § 1522(e)(7)(A). This paragraph directs the Secretary to “develop and implement alternative projects” for recently arrived refugees (those in the U.S. less than 36 months) to provide interim support, medical services, support services, and case management “as needed.” The “as needed” qualification again highlights that the statutes contemplate a degree of administrative judgment about the scope and timing of services.

- 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. These regulations implement the statutes and describe the federal funding streams and programmatic standards for refugee services and the administration of those funds through grantees and cooperative agreements. They define the mechanics by which reception-and-placement services are commonly provided.

Taken together, these provisions create a statutory framework in which Congress contemplated federal involvement in post-admission resettlement services and authorized the use of cooperative agreements, grants, and contracts to deliver those services. The Ninth Circuit panel concluded that certain language in § 1522—when read alongside the implementing statutory scheme and prior practice of funding cooperative agreements—supports the view that the government has legal obligations to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted. The panel therefore ordered the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide statutory reception and placement services to refugees who have been admitted to the United States, while leaving for a later opinion the full merits analysis under the APA.

Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and dissent takes a contrary interpretive position: she reads § 1522 chiefly as an authorization of programs (per the provision’s title and repeated “is authorized” language in the statute), arguing that the statute does not, on its face, mandate that the Executive run these programs irrespective of executive policy choices. That disagreement reflects a statutory-interpretation dispute the Ninth Circuit said it would resolve later in a full opinion.

## Funding, appropriations context, and § 1523 reporting

Statutory responsibilities under § 1522 are tied to appropriations. Many of § 1522’s directives are expressed “to the extent of available appropriations,” so whether and how the government delivers services depends in part on Congress’s funding decisions. The Ninth Circuit’s interim order referenced FY 2025 appropriations: Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance in FY 2025 (as enacted in Pub. L. No. 119‑4, adopting amounts from earlier legislation), and an $800 million reduction was later effected through the Rescissions Act of 2025 (Pub. L. No. 119‑28). Those appropriations and rescissions shape the practical availability of funds to support the programs Congress envisioned.

Section 1523 complements § 1522 by requiring accountability: the Secretary must provide a detailed report on activities under the refugee subchapter to congressional judiciary committees annually. That reporting requirement supports congressional oversight of how appropriations are spent and how programs are run—context the district court and the panel considered relevant when evaluating whether the executive’s suspension of funding and termination of cooperative agreements comported with statutory constraints.

## APA oversight of agency action: challenges to suspension of funding and termination of cooperative agreements

The Administrative Procedure Act governs judicial review of final agency actions that are “arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law,” or that exceed statutory authority. In Pacito the plaintiffs challenged the Executive’s suspension of USRAP and the Department of State’s termination of cooperative agreements under the APA (and under the Refugee Act). The Ninth Circuit panel signaled that the government’s suspension of refugee admissions under § 1182(f) is likely insulated from APA-style scrutiny to the extent the action is a presidential foreign-affairs and admission decision subject to Trump v. Hawaii’s deferential standard. But the panel carved out a separate APA question concerning actions that affect refugees after admission—particularly the termination of cooperative agreements and the suspension of resettlement funding that supports services statutorily described in § 1522.

The panel concluded that the government was not likely to prevail on at least one APA-based claim: the contention that the Executive’s blanket termination of cooperative agreements and funding suspension frustrated statutory duties to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted. The Court therefore directed reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees admitted to the United States, while staying injunctions that would otherwise compel admissions. The panel left a fuller APA merits analysis for its forthcoming opinion, recognizing that APA review can reach agency actions implementing statutory programs—even where other aspects of the government’s conduct (suspending admissions under § 1182(f)) attract heightened deference.

Conclusion

Pacito illuminates how different legal regimes intersect when a presidential order touches on immigration admission policy and separately statutorily mandated post-admission services. Under Nken, courts evaluate stays through a four-factor test that prioritizes likelihood of success and irreparable harm. Trump v. Hawaii establishes strong deference to the President under § 1182(f) for suspensions of entry, but that deference does not automatically preclude APA or statutory review of the Executive’s implementation choices affecting refugees already in the United States—particularly when Congress has created a statutory program (§ 1522) and tied duties to appropriations and administrative mechanisms (cooperative agreements, reports under § 1523, and implementing regulations). The Ninth Circuit’s interim order reflects this division: it stayed parts of the district court’s injunctions that would interfere with the President’s power to suspend admissions while directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to effectuate statutorily described reception-and-placement services for refugees already admitted pending further appellate review.
