Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Executive Order 14163 (Jan. 20, 2025): suspension of refugee entry under 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f), 1185(a)
On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” The Order invoked 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (and § 1185(a)) to find that “entry into the United States of refugees under the [USRAP] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States” and directed that refugee entry under USRAP be suspended pending further findings. The Ninth Circuit’s interim order emphasizes that § 1182(f) grants broad presidential authority to suspend the entry of a class of aliens when the President makes such a finding; the panel quotes Trump v. Hawaii for the proposition that the statute “exudes deference” to the President and vests ample power to impose entry restrictions. The executive action at issue is therefore a facial suspension of admissions under the statutory authority delegated to the President to regulate entry when he finds detriment to national interests.

## Executive Order 14169 and State Department’s suspension of USRAP funding
Separate from the January 20 refugee-suspension order, the government also took action affecting funding for the refugee program. On January 24, 2025, the State Department suspended all funding for the United States Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP), citing another executive order, No. 14169, concerning foreign aid. This funding suspension included money used for domestic reception and placement services for refugees once admitted. The combination of a suspension of admissions (EO 14163) and suspension of funding/support (via EO 14169 and State Department action) is central to the plaintiffs’ challenge because it affects both prospective arrivals and the services historically provided to refugees already in the United States or conditionally approved for admission.

## Parties: refugees (admitted and approved but abroad) and three resettlement organizations with State Department cooperative agreements
The plaintiffs are a mixed group: individual refugees who either were already admitted to the United States before the suspension or had received approval for resettlement but remained abroad, and three nongovernmental organizations that had cooperative agreements with the State Department to perform refugee processing overseas and to provide reception and placement services domestically. The organizational plaintiffs represent the class of nongovernmental resettlement partners that historically contracted or cooperatively worked with the government to carry out statutory resettlement services. That mix of plaintiffs frames both the statutory and equitable arguments: individuals claim disruption of admission and post-arrival services, while organizations claim injury from termination of cooperative agreements and loss of funding.

## Claims: Refugee Act violations (Refugee Act of 1980) and APA challenges to funding suspensions and contract terminations
The complaint filed in the Western District of Washington alleges two principal types of claims. First, the plaintiffs contend that Executive Order 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions violates the Refugee Act of 1980 (codified within the Immigration and Nationality Act), which the Supreme Court has described as a “general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees.” That statutory challenge targets the legality of the President’s decision to suspend refugee admissions. Second, the plaintiffs bring Administrative Procedure Act (APA) challenges to the executive branch’s suspension of USRAP funding and the State Department’s termination of cooperative agreements—claims that focus on whether the funding and contract actions were arbitrary, capricious, or otherwise unlawful under the APA and whether statutory requirements governing resettlement services were honored.

## District court preliminary injunctions (Feb. 28 and Mar. 24, 2025): enjoining EO provisions and funding suspensions; ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements
In late February and March 2025 the district court issued preliminary injunctive relief in favor of the plaintiffs. On February 25 (oral) and then in a written order on February 28, 2025, the district court enjoined implementation or enforcement of several provisions of Executive Order No. 14163—specifically §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4—and also enjoined the suspension of USRAP funding, defining the scope of its preliminary relief to include funding suspensions. After the State Department terminated cooperative agreements on February 26, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction on March 24, 2025 ordering the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements terminated after the February 25 ruling. These two district court orders together sought to preserve both the admission-related status quo for certain refugees and the funding and institutional infrastructure necessary to provide reception and placement services.

## Government appeals and Ninth Circuit initial stay orders (Mar. 25 with carveout; Apr. 21 and May 9 clarifications)
The government appealed the district court’s orders and moved for a stay pending appeal. On March 25, 2025, a Ninth Circuit motions panel granted a partial stay: it stayed the district court’s preliminary injunctions except for a limited carveout protecting individuals who had been conditionally approved for refugee status by U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025. In granting the partial stay, the panel emphasized the special deference 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) affords to the President’s findings about entry restrictions, citing Trump v. Hawaii. The panel then issued additional orders on April 21 and May 9 clarifying the scope of the carveout and the stay. Those clarifications refined which parties remained covered by the district court injunctions while the broader injunctions were held in abeyance pending fuller consideration.

## District court’s July 14 enforcement framework and magistrate appointment; government’s July 17 emergency motion; Ninth Circuit administrative stay on July 18
After the Ninth Circuit’s partial stay, the district court took steps to operationalize the limited relief that remained in effect. On July 14, 2025, it issued an order establishing a framework to enforce the carveout and appointed a magistrate judge to oversee implementation. The government responded with an emergency motion on July 17 seeking enforcement, clarification, or amendment of the Ninth Circuit’s stay decision. The Ninth Circuit then entered an administrative stay on July 18, 2025 of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order while it considered the government’s emergency filing and the broader appeal. That administrative stay temporarily halted the district court’s enforcement framework pending appellate resolution.

## Current order: vacates prior stays, denies emergency motion as moot, and issues a new stay pending appeal with a limited exception
On September 12, 2025 the Ninth Circuit issued the interim panel order after expedited argument. The court vacated both the March 25 stay (as amended) and the July 18 administrative stay. It denied the government’s July 17 emergency motion as moot. Most significantly for posture, the panel issued a new stay pending appeal: it stayed the district court’s February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions in their entirety, subject to a key exception. The court explained its reasoning under the familiar four-factor stay test (likelihood of success on the merits; irreparable injury; harm to other parties; and public interest), stressing that the government is likely to prevail on the challenge to the President’s authority to suspend refugee admissions under § 1182(f). At the same time, the panel signaled that the government is unlikely to prevail on certain APA-based claims concerning statutory duties to provide reception and placement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. Consequently, the Ninth Circuit directed that cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services under § 1522 be reinstated for refugees who have already been admitted to the United States, and it required that those services include the usual and customary services previously provided under the terminated agreements. The panel therefore fashioned a limited, practical exception to the stay to preserve statutorily mandated or required post-admission services while the broader issues (including the validity of the suspension of admissions) remain pending on appeal. The court announced that a full opinion explaining its reasoning will follow, but the interim order sets the operative procedural posture: the district court injunctions are stayed pending appeal except that the government must reinstate necessary cooperative agreements and provide reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted.
