Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### How can the Ninth Circuit both grant a stay pending appeal and simultaneously order reinstatement of cooperative agreements?
Question:
The panel says it is staying the district court’s preliminary injunctions "in their entirety, except as specified" and then directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. I'm confused procedurally: isn't reinstating agreements affirmative, injunction-like relief? Under Nken, stays preserve the status quo rather than order new actions. What is the doctrinal basis for issuing affirmative directives within a stay order, and how does the court reconcile that with the traditional limits on stays versus injunctions pending appeal?
Answer:
Short answer: The panel treated reinstating the cooperative agreements as restoring the status quo that existed immediately before the government’s February terminations — not as a new, free‑standing injunction substituting the district court’s merits ruling. That restoration was deemed necessary to preserve the status quo for those refugees while the appeal proceeds, so the court incorporated that affirmative direction into its stay order under the same stay/ equitable authority the Supreme Court described in Nken.

Why that is doctrinally permissible (grounded in the opinion)

- Nken’s framework controls the standard for a stay pending appeal — the four factors, with likelihood of success and irreparable harm the most critical. The Ninth Circuit invokes Nken at the start of its analysis and applies those factors. It does not say that Nken forbids any affirmative relief; it uses Nken to justify what relief is necessary to prevent irreparable injury while the appeal runs.

- The panel concluded the government is likely to prevail on the challenge to the President’s suspension of admissions but is not likely to prevail on at least one APA claim grounded in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 (the statutory duties to provide reception and placement services). The government had terminated all cooperative agreements in February 2025 after the district court’s initial oral injunction, and by the panel’s account it had no workable plan in place to replace those providers.

- Given that combination — (a) a serious risk that the government has binding statutory obligations under § 1522 that it is failing to meet, and (b) an existing disruption caused by the government’s affirmative termination of contracts — the panel directed reinstatement of the cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” The panel framed that direction as restoring the services “that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements.” In the panel’s words, the reinstatement is a step “in light of the government’s uncertainty regarding its ability to provide the reception and placement services statutorily mandated under 8 U.S.C. § 1522.”

- In short, the panel saw reinstatement as preservation of the status quo ante (the contractual/resettlement arrangements and services in place for admitted refugees before the government’s terminations) rather than a novel injunction ordering the government to undertake new programs. The court’s equitable power to preserve the status quo pending appeal therefore supports including that restorative direction within the stay order.

Practical distinction the opinion rests on

- Stay (as used here): suspends the district court’s injunctions pending appeal but preserves existing conditions for the limited class that may suffer irreparable harm (admitted refugees who need reception and placement services).
- Affirmative direction (reinstatement): characterized as necessary to prevent irreparable injury and to maintain the prior operative state of affairs produced by the pre‑termination cooperative agreements while the appeal decides whether § 1522 imposes enforceable duties.

Caveat shown in the opinion

- Judge Lee’s partial dissent underscores the tension: he would have stayed the district court’s orders in full because he reads § 1522 as permissive (authorizations, not mandates). That disagreement shows the panel is aware this is not a routine stay but a targeted measure premised on the panel’s preliminary view that some statutory duties likely survive the suspension.

Bottom line

The panel did not treat a stay as a prohibition on any affirmative relief; it used its stay/ equitable authority to order the restoration of a pre‑existing operational state (the cooperative agreements and customary services) because doing so was necessary to prevent irreparable harm to refugees and to preserve the factual and legal posture the appeal must resolve. The opinion explicitly ties the reinstatement to preserving the reception-and‑placement services under § 1522 and to the Nken stay factors.

### Does 8 U.S.C. § 1522 impose mandatory duties or merely authorize refugee resettlement services?
Question:
The majority treats § 1522 as imposing statutory obligations to provide reception and placement services, while Judge Lee reads the section as authorizing (not requiring) programs, emphasizing the title and repeated "is authorized" language in §§ 1522(b)-(e). The majority points to § 1522(a)'s "shall" and HHS obligations for unaccompanied minors (§ 1522(d)(2)(B)) and for projects under § 1522(e)(7)(A). What canons of construction and precedent guide whether the "shall" in § 1522(a) converts the authorizations in (b)-(e) into mandates once appropriations exist? Are there cases interpreting § 1522 specifically on mandate vs. authorization?
Answer:
Short answer
The dispute turns on competing canons of construction and which textual features the court treats as decisive. The majority treats § 1522(a)’s “shall” (qualified by “to the extent of available appropriations”) plus discrete mandatory phrases in subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A) as imposing operational duties once appropriations exist; Judge Lee relies on the section title and the repeated “is authorized” language in subsections (b)–(e) to read the statute as permissive. The opinion does not cite any prior Supreme Court or Ninth Circuit decision that squarely resolves whether § 1522 is a command rather than an authorization; the panel therefore resolves the question by applying the usual textual canons described below.

What canons and principles the court used (and which are most relevant)
- Read the statute as a whole / noscitur a sociis: Judge Lee emphasizes the section title — “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees” — and the repeated statutory phrase that the Director “is authorized” to carry out programs in subsections (b)–(e). Titles and recurring permissive verbs are classic textual signals pointing to discretion rather than mandate; Lee treats them as controlling context for § 1522(a).
- Ordinary-meaning of “shall”: the majority points to § 1522(a)’s use of “shall” as the prototypical signal of a mandatory duty. Courts routinely treat “shall” as mandatory absent clear context to the contrary; the majority relies on that ordinary meaning to read (a) as imposing duties.
- Qualification by appropriations: both sides accept that statutory duties are limited by the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations.” That phrase is a statutory limit, not a nullifier: the majority treats it as making the obligation contingent on funding (i.e., an obligation that becomes operative when appropriations exist); Lee treats the same phrase as further evidence of permissive, discretionary authority (Congress authorizes programs subject to appropriations).
- Contextual hookup clause: Lee reads the opening clause of § 1522(a) (“In providing assistance under this section…”) to mean (a) prescribes how the executive must act if it elects to provide assistance under the authorization in (b)–(e). The majority reads (a) instead as imposing duties that govern the provision of services under the section when the executive is acting (or when appropriations are available).
- Plain-text vs. structure: the disagreement is a paradigmatic plain-text/structural contest — whether the explicit “shall” and particularized duties in (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A) outweigh the structural and labeling indicators of permissiveness in the section’s title and subsection language.
- Chevron/agency deference (implicitly relevant): the opinion does not decide deference issues here, but where statutory text is ambiguous courts sometimes defer to an agency interpretation; neither judgment in the interim order adopts an agency interpretive rule as decisive.

How the opinion applies those canons to §1522
- Majority side (panel order): the court finds the government “is not likely to prevail” on its APA defense because § 1522 “requires the government to provide certain reception and placement services.” It leans on (a)(1)(A)’s “shall … to the extent of available appropriations” and on provisions that use “shall” in (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A) to identify concrete obligations (financial/legal responsibility for unaccompanied minors when necessary; “shall develop and implement” alternative projects for refugees). The presence of FY‑2025 appropriations for migration and refugee assistance is treated as relevant support for the majority’s view that statutory duties are operative.
- Lee’s partial dissent: she emphasizes the section title (“Authorization…”) and the recurring “is authorized” phrasing in (b)–(e). She interprets §1522(a) as setting mandatory conditions that apply only if the government elects to fund programs — i.e., (a) constrains how the executive must act in providing assistance, but does not itself convert permissive authorizations into compulsory spending obligations.

Are there cases interpreting §1522 on mandate vs. authorization?
- The court’s interim order does not cite any prior decision that directly resolves whether § 1522 is a mandatory command rather than an authorization. The opinion frames the question as one of statutory interpretation resolved here by textual analysis rather than by controlling precedent specifically addressing §1522’s mandatory/authorizing character.

Practical takeaways for future argument or briefing
- If you want a court to read § 1522 as mandatory: emphasize the plain meaning of “shall,” point to the specific “shall” duties in (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), and show appropriations have been made (the majority treated the FY‑2025 appropriations as relevant).
- If you want a court to read § 1522 as permissive: stress the section title, the recurring “is authorized” language in (b)–(e), the opening clause tying (a) to “providing assistance under this section,” and the permissive nature of the “to the extent of available appropriations” limitation.
- Because the panel did not identify controlling precedent resolving §1522 itself, litigation will turn on which textual canons a particular court favors and on evidentiary facts about the existence and scope of appropriations and whether the government is actually “providing assistance” under the section.

Bottom line
The Ninth Circuit’s interim order records a split in interpretive emphasis: the panel majority treats §1522’s “shall” and particular mandatory phrases as imposing duties when appropriations exist; Judge Lee treats the statute’s title and repeated “is authorized” phrasing as showing Congress merely authorized, not required, resettlement programs. The opinion does not cite any prior case squarely resolving §1522’s command-versus-authorization question, so future resolution will continue to turn on textual canons (plain meaning of “shall,” titles and recurring permissive language, and the appropriations qualification) and on which interpretive signals a court finds decisive.

### What is the scope of presidential authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions compared to refugee processing and funding decisions?
Question:
Relying on Trump v. Hawaii, the panel suggests the President is likely to prevail on the suspension of refugee entry under § 1182(f). But the funding and service-provision issues seem to be treated separately, potentially under the APA and § 1522. Where is the line between a permissible suspension of "entry" under § 1182(f) and actions that affect domestic resettlement services and contracts post-admission? Are there limits preventing § 1182(f) from being used to indirectly nullify domestic statutory obligations to already-admitted refugees?
Answer:
Short answer
- Under Pacito, § 1182(f) gives the President broad, deferential authority to suspend the “entry” of classes of aliens — a power the Ninth Circuit panel treated much as the Supreme Court did in Trump v. Hawaii. That authority reaches decisions that actually prevent people from coming into the United States.
- But that suspension power does not automatically authorize the Executive to nullify or ignore statutory duties that attach after admission. Pacito treats funding, cooperative agreements, and reception-and-placement obligations under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as analytically distinct from a suspension of entry and subject to ordinary statutory and APA review. The panel therefore stayed the district court’s injunction as to suspending admissions but directed reinstatement of cooperative agreements needed to fulfill § 1522 services for refugees already admitted.

Relevant legal doctrine and how Pacito applies it
1) § 1182(f) — suspension of entry
- Pacito repeats the Trump v. Hawaii framework: Congress vested “ample power” and the statute’s single textual prerequisite is the President’s finding that entry “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Pacito (quoting Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 684–85). The panel concluded the government was “likely to prevail” on the claim that EO 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions falls within § 1182(f) because the Order “does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority” to suspend “entry.” Pacito, Order at 5–6.
- Practical implication: a facially internal immigration measure that halts entry of a class of aliens is squarely within § 1182(f) and receives substantial judicial deference on the merits.

2) Post‑admission programs and § 1522 — separate statutory obligations
- Pacito draws a clear line between (a) the President’s authority to bar entry and (b) statutory duties to provide resettlement services to refugees once they are admitted. The court described § 1522’s provisions (reception and placement, cash and medical assistance, special rules for unaccompanied children) and noted that Congress has historically funded those programs and required reporting. Pacito, Order ¶¶ discussing § 1522.
- The panel declined to treat those post‑admission obligations as automatically displaced by a § 1182(f) entry suspension. It concluded the government was not likely to prevail on all APA challenges and, because of statutory duties and the government’s admitted uncertainty about replacing service providers, ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide the § 1522 services to refugees already in the United States. Pacito, Order at 9–11.

3) How the line is drawn in practice
- The operative distinction in Pacito is functional: § 1182(f) covers decisions that stop or restrict the entry of aliens (i.e., people physically outside the United States seeking admission). Those decisions are highly insulated from judicial second‑guessing.
- Actions that withdraw funding, terminate cooperative agreements, or otherwise interfere with reception-and-placement services for persons already admitted implicate statutory duties (§ 1522) and ordinary administrative-law review (APA). The court treated the government’s termination of cooperative agreements as susceptible to judicial relief because such terminations affect obligations owed to admitted refugees. Pacito, Order at 10–11.
- Put another way: a presidential suspension that prevents a class from entering is different in kind from using that suspension as a means to eliminate executive compliance with Congress’s post‑entry commands.

Limits on using § 1182(f) to nullify post‑admission obligations
- Pacito demonstrates two discrete limits:
  1. Textual/functional limit: § 1182(f) authorizes suspension of entry; it does not by its terms rewrite other statutory programs that apply after admission. The panel emphasized that § 1182(f) authorizes suspending “entry of . . . any class of aliens,” U.S.C. § 1182(f), and treated post‑admission statutory duties under § 1522 as separate legal questions. Pacito, Order at 5–6, 9–11.
  2. Statutory/administrative-law limit: when Congress has prescribed post‑admission duties (even “to the extent of available appropriations”), courts can review executive steps that frustrate those duties under the APA and the relevant statutory text. Pacito analyzed § 1522’s language and funding context and concluded the government was not likely to prevail on at least some APA claims; the panel therefore ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide statutorily required reception-and-placement services. Pacito, Order at 9–11.
- The concurrence/dissent by Judge Lee underscores the point of statutory interpretation dispute (authorization v. mandate). But regardless of that interpretive disagreement, the majority treated § 1522 as creating enforceable expectations the Executive could not simply nullify via an entry suspension. Pacito, Lee, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part (discussing § 1522’s “authorization” language and disagreeing about mandatory effect).

A practical test (derivable from Pacito)
- Ask (1) does the Executive action directly affect who may enter the country? If yes → squarely within § 1182(f) and highly deferential review. Pacito supports this conclusion for entry suspensions.
- If (2) the action withdraws funding, cancels cooperative agreements, or otherwise prevents performance of programs that serve persons already admitted → those steps do not automatically fall within § 1182(f) and are subject to statutory/APA scrutiny; courts can enjoin such steps if they unlawfully undermine statutory obligations (as Pacito did with respect to reinstating cooperative agreements for § 1522 services).

Bottom line
Pacito treats § 1182(f) as a potent but limited instrument: powerful for suspending entry, not a license to erase separate statutory obligations that attach after admission. The panel stayed the district court’s injunction as to suspending admissions (relying on Trump v. Hawaii) but required reinstatement of cooperative agreements and reception-and-placement services under § 1522 for refugees already inside the United States — reflecting the court’s view that post‑admission statutory duties are a distinct legal question subject to normal statutory interpretation and APA constraints.

### On what APA grounds is the government "not likely to prevail" regarding defunding and termination of refugee cooperative agreements?
Question:
The order previews that the government is unlikely to win at least one APA challenge tied to § 1522 obligations, but it does not specify the APA theory. Is the issue arbitrary-and-capricious review under § 706(2)(A), contrary-to-law under § 706(2)(A) because it conflicts with § 1522, or failure to follow required procedures (notice-and-comment) if the terminations functioned as a rule/policy change? How do courts generally classify mass termination of cooperative agreements and funding suspensions for APA purposes—final agency action, procurement discretionary action exempt from APA, or programmatic decisions tied to statutory duties?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel’s interim ruling points most clearly to a contrary‑to‑law APA claim under 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A): the panel concluded that § 1522 imposes statutory obligations to provide reception and placement services and that the State Department’s mass terminations and funding suspension conflict with those duties, so the government “is not likely to prevail” on at least one APA challenge. Other APA theories—arbitrary‑and‑capricious review and procedural‑rulemaking challenges—are possible adjuncts, but the opinion itself rests on a statutory‑conflict theory (i.e., unlawful agency action because it contradicts a statutory mandate) rather than on pure notice‑and‑comment or procurement‑exemption reasoning.

Why that follows from the opinion
- The panel states expressly that “for reasons to be explained in full . . . the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs’ challenges under the APA,” and then points to 8 U.S.C. § 1522’s text and duties. It highlights mandatory language in subsection (a) and specific duties (employment training, English instruction, cash/medical support, responsibility for unaccompanied children) and the statute’s authorization of cooperative agreements to carry out those statutorily described services.
- The district court was directed to reinstate the cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522,” which reflects the court’s view that the terminations are inconsistent with § 1522 and therefore unlawful. That is exactly the posture of a § 706(2)(A) “contrary to law” claim: agency action that conflicts with a statutory command may be set aside.

Relation to arbitrary‑and‑capricious and procedural theories
- Arbitrary‑and‑capricious (also reviewed under § 706(2)(A) as the court described): If the government defunded and terminated agreements without a reasoned explanation, without considering important aspects of the problem (including statutory obligations and available appropriations), or changed a settled program without justification, plaintiffs could alternatively or additionally prevail under an arbitrary‑and‑capricious theory. The opinion suggests the court found the government’s uncertainty about replacing providers relevant (the government told the court it was “actively preparing a request for proposals,” but later said it did not know if any progress had been made), which feeds an APA reasoned‑decisionability claim. But the panel’s cited authority and its direction focus principally on statutory noncompliance.
- Notice‑and‑comment rulemaking: That theory requires showing the terminations/suspension amounted to a rule or substantive policy change subject to the APA’s rulemaking requirements. The opinion does not treat the terminations as classic rulemaking, nor does it rest its interim ruling on a procedural‑notice‑and‑comment deficiency. The panel instead treats the dispute as whether the executive’s action conflicts with § 1522 and therefore is unlawful. So notice‑and‑comment is a plausible ancillary claim only if plaintiffs can show the terminations implemented a general substantive rule rather than discrete contract actions or programmatic, one‑off decisions.

How courts generally classify mass terminations/funding suspensions (as reflected in the opinion)
- Final agency action and reviewability: Courts will treat agency decisions that have direct legal consequences (e.g., ending statutorily mandated services) as final agency action reviewable under the APA unless another statutory bar applies. The Ninth Circuit panel here treated the terminations/funding suspension as subject to review because they implicated statutory duties in § 1522 and produced immediate, concrete effects (termination of cooperative agreements and suspension of services).
- Procurement discretion and § 701(a)(2) limits: Some procurement choices and matters “committed to agency discretion by law” can be unreviewable. But when an agency’s procurement/contracting moves directly contravene a statutory duty (e.g., the statute requires provision of certain services “to the extent of available appropriations” or otherwise creates mandatory conditions), courts are willing to review and to enjoin agency action that defeats those statutory obligations. The panel’s directive to reinstate cooperative agreements needed to supply § 1522 services shows the court viewed these actions as programmatic decisions tied to statutory duties—not nonreviewable procurement discretion.
- Programmatic decisions tied to statutory duties: When mass terminations effectively nullify a Congress‑mandated program or statutory duties, courts treat those decisions as reviewable final agency actions and examine whether they are lawful, as the panel did here.

Bottom line for litigation strategy and doctrinal framing
- The strongest and clearest vehicle suggested by the opinion is a § 706(2)(A) “contrary to law” challenge: show that § 1522 imposes enforceable obligations that the terminations/funding suspension defeat. The panel’s language and its order to reinstate agreements track that theory.
- Secondary or alternative theories that plaintiffs should preserve: arbitrary‑and‑capricious review challenging the reasonableness and record support for the terminations; and, if facts support it, a procedural claim that the government’s actions amounted to a substantive rule change requiring notice‑and‑comment. But the opinion’s emphasis is statutory conflict/reviewability, not a pure procurement‑exemption or pure rulemaking failure.

Caveat from the opinion
- Judge Lee’s partial dissent emphasizes a textual, interpretive dispute about whether § 1522 is genuinely mandatory or chiefly an authorization. That highlights the central contested legal question on the merits: whether § 1522’s text creates judicially enforceable duties. The panel majority’s interim ruling indicates it views § 1522 as imposing sufficient obligations to make the government unlikely to prevail on that APA challenge.

### What is the significance of the earlier "limited carveout" for conditionally approved refugees pre–January 20, 2025, and why was it vacated now?
Question:
The motions panel initially denied a stay "to the extent" it applied to individuals conditionally approved before Jan. 20, 2025, then clarified that carveout twice. The present panel vacates those prior stays and issues a new stay with a different carveout focused on § 1522 services for already-admitted refugees. What legal or factual developments justify abandoning the prior conditional-approval carveout? Was the earlier carveout premised on reliance interests or specific statutory rights of conditionally approved applicants, and why does the new order no longer protect that group?
Answer:
Short answer
- The earlier “limited carveout” for people conditionally approved before Jan. 20, 2025 was an interim, narrow accommodation by the motions panel when it first granted a partial stay. It left in place the district court’s injunction only as to those conditionally approved applicants pending fuller consideration.  
- The present panel vacated that interim carveout because, after full briefing and oral argument, it concluded the legal picture required a different, narrower protection: not for conditionally approved applicants outside the United States, but for refugees who have already been admitted to the United States and thus are covered by statutory resettlement-service obligations under 8 U.S.C. § 1522.  
- The opinion does not rest the change on a finding of reliance interests for conditionally approved applicants. Instead, it reflects (1) a reassessment of the merits under the stay factors and (2) the panel’s view that the government is likely to prevail on the President’s authority under § 1182(f) with respect to suspending admissions, while the government is not likely to prevail on the plaintiffs’ APA claim that the administration unlawfully cut off statutorily required reception-and-placement services for refugees already admitted.

Why the carveout was abandoned — doctrinal and factual grounding in the opinion
1. The March–May carveout was an interim, motions-panel decision
- The March 25 order (as amended) granted a stay pending appeal but expressly “denied the motion for a stay … to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction … applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status … before January 20, 2025.” The panel later issued clarifications calling that a “limited carveout.” Those were preliminary, motions-panel rulings while briefing and oral argument were pending (Order of March 25, 2025; Orders of Apr. 21 and May 9, 2025, summarized in the opinion).

2. The full panel’s later assessment changed the scope based on the merits analysis
- After full briefing and expedited argument, the panel re-evaluated the stay factors (Nken) and the merits. The panel concluded the government is “likely to prevail” on plaintiffs’ challenge to the suspension of admissions under § 1182(f) — stressing the strong deference shown in Hawaii and that “the President has undoubtedly fulfilled” the § 1182(f) prerequisite. That conclusion supports a stay of the injunctions as to suspension of admissions. See Opinion, Discussion (applying Hawaii and § 1182(f)).
- At the same time, the panel found the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA claim: plaintiffs’ challenge tied to 8 U.S.C. § 1522 (statutory resettlement/receiving services). The court explains § 1522 requires certain reception-and-placement services “to refugees after their admission into the United States,” and notes statutory language and appropriations history. For that reason the panel directed reinstatement of cooperative agreements needed to provide § 1522 services to refugees “who have been admitted to the United States.” See Opinion, Discussion (§ 1522 analysis and the order to reinstate cooperative agreements).

3. The earlier carveout did not rest on a statutory right of conditional approvals or explicit reliance findings in this opinion
- The opinion does not say the initial carveout rested on a firm statutory right or on judicial findings of reliance by conditionally approved applicants. It records that the motions panel initially excluded conditionally approved applicants from the stay but does not attribute that exclusion to a settled legal entitlement or to reliance interests. Rather, the opinion treats the March–May actions as interim orders that the court now vacates in light of full consideration. See Background (describing the prior stay and its “limited carveout”) and Discussion (vacating prior stays and issuing the new stay).

4. Practical/legal reason for narrowing the carveout to already-admitted refugees
- Legally, the distinction the court enforces in the new order is admission versus conditional approval while abroad. The court’s view: § 1182(f) supplies broad presidential authority to suspend “entry” (and the panel is likely to find the suspension lawful as to those not yet admitted), but § 1522 imposes statutory duties relating to reception and placement for refugees once they are in the United States. Thus the panel preserves the district court’s injunction only to the extent it requires the government to provide those statutorily-mandated services to admitted refugees. See Discussion (contrasting § 1182(f) analysis with § 1522 obligations and ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees).

Conclusion
- The prior carveout was an interim, motions-panel accommodation for conditionally approved applicants while the case was being fully briefed and calendared. It was not adopted as the governing legal rule in this opinion. After full briefing and argument the panel concluded the proper protective posture is narrower: the stay should preserve the district court’s injunction only insofar as it enforces statutory reception-and-placement obligations under § 1522 for refugees already in the United States, while staying the injunction in all other respects (including as to people still outside the United States who had only been conditionally approved). The opinion does not ground that shift in reliance interests for conditionally approved applicants; it grounds the shift in the panel’s reassessment of the merits under § 1182(f) and § 1522 and the Nken stay factors.

### Do refugees abroad and resettlement agencies have standing to challenge the entry suspension and funding terminations?
Question:
The plaintiffs include refugees outside the United States approved for resettlement and domestic agencies whose cooperative agreements were terminated. For the noncitizen plaintiffs outside the U.S., what injury and zone-of-interests analyses apply after Trump v. Hawaii and related standing precedents? For the agencies, is loss of cooperative agreements sufficient Article III injury? Is their interest in program administration within the zone of interests of § 1522 or the APA, despite procurement-related discretion and sovereign immunity considerations?
Answer:
Short answer
- Noncitizen refugees abroad who had been approved for resettlement before January 20, 2025, can have a concrete injury (denial or delay of entry and related benefits) and thus standing; the court’s stay orders treat that group as a “limited carveout,” consistent with the approach in this opinion and Trump v. Hawaii’s deference to presidential entry suspensions. Whether their claims fall within the “zone of interests” depends on whether their legal complaint rests on statutory rights created by the Refugee Act (and implementing statutes) rather than a generalized grievance about immigration policy; the panel’s discussion treats Refugee Act statutory protections as directly relevant.  
- The domestic resettlement agencies suffered a concrete Article III injury when the State Department terminated their cooperative agreements (loss of funding and contractual expectancies), and the Ninth Circuit’s order directing reinstatement of necessary agreements shows the panel viewed those interests as cognizable. The court also concluded plaintiffs’ APA challenge to the funding terminations under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 is likely to succeed, which reflects the court’s view that the organizations’ interests fall within the zone of interests of § 1522 and the APA — although Judge Lee’s partial dissent frames a permissible counterargument that § 1522 is authorizing rather than mandatory.

What the opinion says about noncitizens abroad (standing and zone of interests)
- Concrete injury: The panel repeatedly distinguishes a group of “individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by USCIS before January 20, 2025,” and excepted them from the stay. That treatment reflects the court’s view that a plaintiff who already has been approved for resettlement has a sufficiently concrete and particularized interest in being admitted that a district court order could protect. In short: an approved refugee who is prevented from entering suffers the kind of injury that Article III requires (denial or delay of admission and the attendant statutory benefits).  
- Causation and redressability: The executive order and the funding suspension are the direct governmental acts preventing entry and funding; an injunction restoring the program or enjoining enforcement of the EO would redress those injuries. That is precisely why the district court’s injunction was carved out for conditionally approved refugees and why the panel’s interim orders focus on that group.  
- Zone of interests: The opinion anchors plaintiffs’ claims in the Refugee Act and related INA provisions. The panel treats the Refugee Act as providing statutory protections and programmatic obligations (see discussion citing Negusie and 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f), 1522). If the refugee’s claim is founded on statutory entitlements or procedural protections created by the Refugee Act or implementing regulations, their interests are aligned with the statute’s purposes and therefore fall within the zone of interests the statute protects. By contrast, a refugee trying to assert only a generalized objection to the President’s immigration judgment (i.e., a mere policy disagreement) would have a harder time clearing the zone-of-interests inquiry in light of Trump v. Hawaii’s emphasis that courts owe deference to the President’s immigration determinations.

What the opinion says about resettlement agencies (Article III injury, zone of interests, APA, sovereign immunity, procurement discretion)
- Article III injury: The panel records that the State Department “terminated all cooperative agreements” and that the government conceded it was preparing RFPs only after termination. The court ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522,” treating loss of agreements and funding as a concrete financial and operational injury sufficient for Article III purposes. Thus, loss of cooperative agreements is treated here as a cognizable injury.  
- Zone of interests under §1522 and the APA: The panel explains § 1522’s statutory scheme, emphasizes that resettlement services “may be provided through the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with ‘public or private nonprofit agencies’” and relies on § 1522 to find that the government has statutory obligations with respect to reception and placement services. The court concluded the government is “not likely to prevail” on at least one APA challenge and directed reinstatement of agreements necessary to deliver § 1522 services. That treatment signals that the agencies’ interests (participation as cooperative agreement recipients and performance of resettlement services) fall within the zone of interests of § 1522 and support an APA claim against agency action terminating funding.  
- Procurement discretion and sovereign immunity: The opinion recognizes that Congress authorized contracts and cooperative agreements and that the executive exercises procurement discretion; Judge Lee’s partial dissent presses a conventional counterpoint — that § 1522 is “authorization” language and therefore permissive, not mandatory. The majority, however, treats the statutory text and programmatic context (including appropriations and reporting requirements) as sufficient to ground a claim that the government has responsibilities that agency action cannot arbitrarily abrogate. The panel’s preliminary holdings therefore suggest sovereign-immunity and ordinary procurement discretion are not dispositive against an APA challenge where the statute creates substantive programmatic expectations and the government’s action frustrates them. The panel’s direction to reinstate necessary agreements demonstrates the court’s view that § 1522 creates enforceable limits on executive action in this operational context.

Practical takeaways for litigation and analysis
- For noncitizen plaintiffs abroad: focus on showing a concrete, individualized entitlement (e.g., USCIS conditional approval or other concrete reliance and statutory expectations under the Refugee Act). Anchor relief to statutory rights and programmatic entitlements, not merely to a policy disagreement with the President’s immigration determination, because Trump v. Hawaii limits courtroom review of broad entry suspensions.  
- For agency plaintiffs: emphasize the concrete financial and operational harms from termination of cooperative agreements, and frame claims under § 1522 and the APA to show that the terminations are agency actions that exceed or frustrate statutory programmatic duties. Anticipate the government’s counterarguments (procurement discretion, permissive “authorization” language, sovereign immunity) and respond by showing statutory text, appropriations context, and program administration expectations that the court here viewed as sufficient to support relief.

Bottom line
Pacito treats pre-approved refugees abroad as having cognizable injuries for purposes of injunctive relief (the district court’s carveout), and it treats termination of cooperative agreements as a cognizable Article III injury to resettlement organizations. The Ninth Circuit sees § 1522 and related statutes as supplying a statutory baseline that can place both refugees and cooperating agencies within the zone of interests protected by the Refugee Act and the APA, although the concurrence/dissent signals a plausible counterargument that some of § 1522’s language is permissive and that deference to executive discretion in immigration remains a powerful defense.

### How do appropriations constraints interact with alleged statutory mandates in § 1522?
Question:
The order notes FY 2025 appropriations for migration and refugee assistance and an $800 million rescission. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) conditions some obligations "to the extent of available appropriations." If § 1522 imposes duties, how do courts evaluate whether appropriations are "available" and sufficient to compel performance? Can the executive cite reduced appropriations as a basis to terminate all cooperative agreements, or must it scale services consistent with available funds? Are there cases where courts ordered continued services notwithstanding reduced or uncertain appropriations?
Answer:
Short answer
- Under Pacito, the § 1522 “to the extent of available appropriations” language is a limiting clause courts will treat as a factual condition: whether appropriations are in fact “available” is a question the government must satisfy before it can sidestep statutory duties.  
- If a court concludes § 1522 imposes mandatory duties and appropriations are (or can reasonably be treated as) available, the executive may not simply terminate cooperative agreements across the board; the government must show that funds are unavailable or otherwise insufficient, and courts can order continued performance or scaled services consistent with the statutory mandate.  
- Pacito itself is an example: the panel concluded plaintiffs were likely to succeed on an APA claim that § 1522 requires services and directed reinstatement of cooperative agreements despite the government’s announced funding suspension and a rescission that reduced FY2025 migration/refugee appropriations.

Relevant legal standard and how the opinion applies it
- The statute’s operative constraint—“to the extent of available appropriations”—is a condition precedent to compelled performance, not a free pass to blanket nonperformance. The panel reads § 1522 as imposing duties (at least as to certain refugees admitted before the suspension) but expressly frames those duties as subject to the availability of appropriations. That makes “availability” a factual and legal question for the government to prove when it claims lack of funds as an excuse.  
- The court relied on the statutory text (the “shall” provisions in § 1522 and the specific duties enumerated in subsections (a), (d)(2)(B), and (e)(7)(A)) and on the FY2025 appropriations background (the $3.178 billion appropriation and the $800 million rescission) to assess whether the government had satisfied that limiting clause. The panel found the government had not shown it could lawfully discontinue the reception-and-placement services—indeed, the government admitted uncertainty about replacing cooperative agreements—so the court ordered reinstatement of agreements necessary to carry out § 1522 services for refugees already admitted.

What that means for executive action and scaling of services
- The executive can rely on reduced appropriations only if it can show the appropriations are truly unavailable for the statutory duties: i.e., either Congress withheld or rescinded funds in a way that leaves no funds available for the required services, or the executive can demonstrate a bona fide zero-fund situation. Mere budget reductions or policy-driven reallocations are not necessarily dispositive.  
- Where the statute imposes duties but appropriations are limited, the government’s lawful options are (1) demonstrate no funds are available for the mandated program (so the “to the extent” clause defeats a duty), or (2) provide a defensible, documented plan to scale services consistent with available funds while still complying with the statutory scheme. Absent satisfactory proof, courts may order continuation of services to the extent required by the statute and prior practice.

Pacito as precedent for court-ordered continued services
- Pacito squarely demonstrates that courts will order continued services when (i) they find a statutory duty likely exists and (ii) the government has not carried its burden of showing appropriations dooms performance. The panel (majority) directed reinstatement of the cooperative agreements to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted, despite the government’s termination of agreements and the FY2025 rescission. That relief was based on the court’s conclusion that plaintiffs were likely to succeed on an APA claim and on the government’s admitted uncertainty about providing substitute arrangements.

Counterpoint within the opinion
- The concurrence/dissent by Judge Lee emphasizes the other plausible reading: § 1522 is an authorization rather than an absolute mandate, and the “to the extent of available appropriations” and repeated “is authorized” language suggest executive discretion. That is the precise statutory ambiguity courts will grapple with when assessing whether duties are mandatory or permissive in light of appropriations. Pacito shows both outcomes are legally arguable; the panel majority (for the limited subset of plaintiffs) treated § 1522 as imposing duties subject to appropriations, while the dissent urged a permissive reading.

Practical implications for litigation and agency practice
- For challengers: point to statutory “shall” provisions, prior practice, and the existence of appropriations (even if reduced) and emphasize any government admissions of uncertainty; courts may order continued services pending resolution.  
- For the government: document precisely how appropriations were reduced or rendered unavailable and explain any necessary scaling decisions; bear the burden of proof that funds are unavailable for the statutory duties if it seeks to terminate services.

Bottom line
Pacito illustrates that “to the extent of available appropriations” is not an automatic shield. When the statute is plausibly mandatory and the government cannot show appropriations make compliance impossible, courts can and will require continued provision of services (or at least partial relief) consistent with § 1522. Conversely, where Congress or the record plainly shows there are no appropriated funds available for the statutory duties, courts will treat the limiting clause as dispositive—but the government must make that showing.
