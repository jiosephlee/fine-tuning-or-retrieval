Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

## Hawaii’s Long Shadow: Ninth Circuit Reaffirms Sweeping Presidential Power Under §1182(f) to Halt Refugee Admissions

Why this matters

The Ninth Circuit’s expedited stay in Pacito v. Trump is short on prose but long on consequence. In holding that the President “has undoubtedly fulfilled” the only textual prerequisite of 8 U.S.C. §1182(f)—a finding that entry of a class of aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States”—the panel treated Trump v. Hawaii as controlling and effectively insulated suspension-of-entry decisions from searching judicial review. That posture matters because it confirms that, at least in the Ninth Circuit’s view, the President’s judgment that certain refugees pose a national-interest problem is entitled to extreme deference for the duration of litigation. At the same time, the court’s stay order signals an important carveout: statutory duties under the Refugee Act (8 U.S.C. §1522) can still give rise to judicially enforceable obligations separate from entry decisions. The result is an accommodation of broad executive power over admissions alongside a recognition that statutory programmatic duties may constrain how the government treats refugees once they are admitted.

What the panel did

Pacito came to the Ninth Circuit on an expedited appeal from preliminary injunctions entered by the Western District of Washington. The district court had enjoined key parts of an executive order (EO 14163) suspending refugee admissions under §1182(f) and had also enjoined the State Department’s termination of cooperative agreements that funded resettlement “reception and placement” services. The Ninth Circuit’s stay vacated earlier partial stays and, pending full merits briefing, stayed the district court’s preliminary injunctions in their entirety — with one important exception: the panel directed the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States, to the extent required by 8 U.S.C. §1522.

The court applied the familiar four-factor stay framework from Nken v. Holder, focusing on the two “most critical” factors: likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable harm. On the first factor the panel concluded the government “is likely to prevail” as to plaintiffs’ challenge to the suspension of admissions because §1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and contains a single predicate: the President’s finding that entry of a class of aliens would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Quoting Trump v. Hawaii, the panel said courts may not undertake “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” The remaining Nken factors—irreparable injury, potential harm to other parties, and the public interest—also tipped toward the government given the executive’s broad discretion in immigration and foreign affairs.

But the panel did not sweep away all judicial relief. It found plaintiffs likely to prevail on an APA-based challenge tied to the Refugee Act’s statutory obligations to provide resettlement services—principally 8 U.S.C. §1522—so it required the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted. That remedy was grounded in a statutory-program argument distinct from the question whether the executive could suspend entries under §1182(f).

How the decision cements the Hawaii framework

Pacito reads Trump v. Hawaii as doing much of the heavy lifting. The high-water mark from Hawaii was the Court’s observation that §1182(f) “exudes deference” and vests “ample power” in the President to impose entry restrictions. The Ninth Circuit treats that language as largely dispositive of the threshold statutory question: the statute requires only a presidential finding and courts are not to police the substantive merits of that finding beyond modest review. Practically, that means when the government invokes §1182(f) with national-security, foreign-relations, or other broad “interests of the United States” rationales, federal courts will be extremely reluctant to substitute their own assessments for the President’s judgment—especially at the preliminary-injunction stage.

Two features of Pacito deserve emphasis. First, the court applied Nken’s high bar for a stay and found likelihood of success on the merits satisfied by statutory text and Hawaii’s deference doctrine. That approach effectively collapses the §1182(f) merits inquiry into a near-presumptive win for the government when the executive makes a cognizable finding. Second, the panel’s invocation of national and foreign interests in evaluating the other stay factors underscores that these interests weigh heavily in the balance when the executive frames a suspension as protective of security, diplomacy, or related concerns. In short, Pacito operationalizes Hawaii: the President’s finding under §1182(f) is potent; courts will give it breathing room pending full review.

Where limits remain: statutory programs, APA review, and political branches

Pacito is not an unfettered blank check to the President. The Ninth Circuit’s requirement that the government reinstate cooperative agreements for reception-and-placement services demonstrates two important limits.

First, section 1182(f) operates on the question of “entry.” It does not magically erase other statutory duties Congress has imposed with respect to persons already admitted or to domestic programs. The court read §1522 as creating judicially enforceable programmatic duties—at least sufficiently plausible to warrant relief at the injunction stage—and compelled reinstatement of those agreements for admitted refugees. That does not undermine a broad reading of §1182(f) for admissions, but it does remind us that Congress can cabin executive action by creating statutory entitlements or programmatic frameworks that courts may enforce.

Second, the court’s willingness to entertain APA-based claims signals that not all consequences of executive policy are immunized by Hawaii deference. Hawaii dealt mainly with whether the President’s §1182(f) proclamation was subject to close judicial scrutiny; it did not and could not rewrite other statutory regimes that regulate federal agencies’ conduct. Thus, agencies remain subject to notice-and-comment, reasoned-decisionmaking, and statutory constraints when they act in their own capacity—especially when the action affects domestic programs and beneficiaries.

Practical and doctrinal implications

Several succinct implications follow from Pacito:

- Executive advantage on entry suspensions. Because the Ninth Circuit treats the presidential finding under §1182(f) as the dispositive trigger and borrows Hawaii’s restraint on “searching inquiry,” future administrations invoking §1182(f) will enjoy substantial defensive leverage at the preliminary-relief stage. Plaintiffs suing to enjoin new suspensions face an uphill climb to show likelihood of success absent clear textual or procedural infirmities.

- Litigation strategy shifts to statutory and administrative claims. Plaintiffs who cannot prevail on §1182(f) grounds may pivot to statutory claims tied to the Refugee Act or to APA challenges focused on agency action distinct from the proclamation itself—exactly what succeeded partially in Pacito. Challengers will press arguments that agency terminations or defunding measures violate statutory duties, that the agency failed to follow required procedures, or that the agency’s reasons were arbitrary and capricious.

- Nken’s factors in national-security-inflected immigration cases. Pacito demonstrates how courts operationalize the Nken framework when national-security considerations are invoked: likelihood of success favors the government if the relevant statute contains a simple textual trigger coupled with precedent emphasizing presidential deference, while irreparable harm and the public interest are read through a national-security lens that often favors governmental pause. Practitioners should thus expect preliminary-relief battles to hinge on whether plaintiffs can show non-deferential claims—statutory violations or process defects—that avoid Hawaii’s shadow.

- Congressional oversight and funding levers regain importance. If §1182(f) suspensions are difficult to enjoin, Congress’s role—through appropriations, reporting requirements (e.g., §1523), and programmatic statutes—becomes the real check. Pacito’s acceptance that §1522 can constrain agency conduct underscores the institutional levers Congress can deploy to shape outcomes even when admissions policy is presidentially directed.

Open questions and potential downstream effects

Pacito leaves unresolved contours that will matter in future litigation. The court’s stay was interlocutory and the merits opinion is forthcoming; the panel’s framing, however, telegraphs how it may approach the merits. Important open questions include whether courts will, in a full merits posture, accept any meaningful review beyond the textual finding in §1182(f). Will courts demand some minimal connection between the asserted harm and the class of aliens targeted? Hawaii emphasized deference; Pacito’s brief stay order suggests the Ninth Circuit may be unwilling to entertain anything more than a nominal review at the preliminary stage.

Another area to watch is the scope of the Refugee Act’s statutory duties. Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent crystallizes an alternative textual reading: §1522 as an authorization rather than a command. If a merits opinion adopts a narrower reading, the door to statutory enforcement might close. Conversely, if the panel affirms the district court’s conclusion that §1522 creates enforceable obligations, agencies will face substantial litigation risk when they cut off funding or terminate agreements that operationalize those obligations.

Finally, Pacito may spur strategic administrative design. Administrations seeking to limit admissions while minimizing domestic statutory constraints might try to separate the decision to suspend entries (a pure §1182(f) proclamation) from domestic programmatic changes, handling the latter through careful APA-compliant procedures or leaving in place baseline services for already-admitted individuals to avoid triggering §1522 claims. Conversely, opponents of suspension will invest more heavily in statutory and procedural claims, and in pushing Congress to act.

Conclusion

Pacito confirms that Hawaii’s doctrine remains the controlling framework for §1182(f)-based suspensions in at least the Ninth Circuit: a presidential finding that entry “would be detrimental” is a powerful, often dispositive, threshold at the preliminary stage. But the decision also confirms that presidential authority over admission does not automatically extinguish statutory boundaries imposed by Congress on federal programs. That split—extreme deference on the admission question, coupled with judicial willingness to enforce statutory resettlement duties—will shape the next phase of litigation and congressional response. The forthcoming merits opinion in Pacito will be important, but for now the Ninth Circuit’s stay sends a clear message: Hawaii’s long shadow continues to shelter executive suspension-of-entry decisions, even as statutory-program protections remain a viable route for judicial relief.



# Are Refugee Services Mandatory? The Ninth Circuit’s Split Reading of §1522 and Post-Chevron Statutory Interpretation

## Why this case matters
The Ninth Circuit’s interim order in Pacito v. Trump puts a spotlight on a deceptively technical statutory question with serious human consequences: when Congress enacted 8 U.S.C. §1522, did it create a mandatory duty for the federal government to provide post‑admission “reception and placement” services to refugees, or did it merely authorize the executive to provide such services when it chooses and funds permit? The panel’s order stays a district court injunction (pending a full opinion) but also directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements providing resettlement services for refugees already admitted. Judge Lee’s partial dissent frames §1522 as permissive. That split crystallizes competing interpretive approaches to statutes that mix words like “shall,” “authorized,” and “to the extent of available appropriations,” and it raises broader questions about how courts will read statutes in a post‑Chevron environment where agency deference is attenuated or unavailable.

## The court’s holdings and reasoning (as stated)
Although the Ninth Circuit’s detailed merits opinion is forthcoming, the panel’s interim order articulates two principal points. First, following the Supreme Court’s immigration‑deference precedent (Trump v. Hawaii), the panel is inclined to treat the President’s suspension of refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. §1182(f) as within his broad authority—so the government is likely to prevail on the claim that the admission suspension itself is lawful. Second, and crucial here, the panel says the government is unlikely to prevail on at least one Administrative Procedure Act challenge: §1522 imposes obligations to provide, post‑admission, certain reception and placement services, and because the government has terminated cooperative agreements, the district court’s injunction requiring reinstatement of agreements necessary to provide those services should be preserved for admitted refugees. Judge Lee parts ways on that statutory point, arguing that §1522 is an authorization, not a mandate—its title and repeated use of “authorized” indicate permissive congressional permission tethered to appropriations, and the “shall” language in §1522(a) functions as a condition on how aid must be administered if provided, not an independent spending command.

## The heart of the textual disagreement
The disagreement is focused and doctrinally familiar: how to reconcile (1) subsections of §1522 that repeatedly say the Director or Secretary “is authorized” to carry out refugee programs, (2) §1522(a), which uses mandatory diction—“shall . . . to the extent of available appropriations”—and (3) the statute’s title, “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” The majority reads the “shall” provisions (and the statutory architecture requiring certain kinds of reception and placement services and responsibilities for unaccompanied minors) as imposing at least some substantive duties that the executive cannot simply abandon. Judge Lee reads the text and structure the other way: the statute confers discretion to create and fund programs, and the “shall” clauses qualify how funds, if appropriated, are to be administered rather than creating a freestanding obligation independent of appropriations and agency prioritization.

Both readings draw on sound canons. The majority emphasizes the operative requirements scattered through §1522—duties to make resources available for job training, to assume responsibility for unaccompanied children when necessary, and to develop interim support projects—and links them to statutory accountability provisions (§1523 reporting) and to the reality that Congress has historically appropriated substantial funds for migration and refugee assistance. Lee emphasizes titles and repeated “authorized” phrasing to support a permissive reading and stresses the critical qualification “to the extent of available appropriations,” which often signals that Congress left funding decisions to appropriations politics and executive priorities.

## The interpretive stakes in a post‑Chevron world
Pacito’s statutory fight is emblematic of a larger shift in lower‑court statutory interpretation. Where courts once might have deferred to an agency’s reasonable construction under Chevron, the contemporary landscape—where judicial deference to agencies is weakened—pushes judges to resolve textual ambiguity themselves. That produces two consequences visible in Pacito.

First, textual features that previously might have been resolved by looking to agency practice and expertise are now litigated front and center: the relative weight of titles, permissive words like “authorized,” the placement of “shall” clauses, and the “to the extent of available appropriations” caveat. Judges’ methodological instincts—textualism, purposivism, structuralist readings—thus matter more. The Ninth Circuit’s split here reflects that diversity: the majority treats statutory duties as enforceable even against a politically accountable executive action, while Judge Lee reads the same provisions as framing conditions for discretionary programs.

Second, the absence of agency deference makes appropriations‑law constraints more salient. When courts identify a statutory duty to perform certain services, they must confront that such duties are often explicitly conditioned on appropriations. If §1522 is read as imposing obligations “to the extent of available appropriations,” then two follow‑on questions arise: (1) whether Congress has already appropriated funds sufficient to satisfy the duty for the relevant period and (2) whether courts can order the executive to spend funds or reallocate them among programs. The majority’s interim directive—reinstating cooperative agreements—suggests the panel believes there is at least some appropriated money or that agency termination of agreements cannot defeat the statutory duty to provide services for admitted refugees. Lee’s approach signals judicial caution about second‑guessing executive funding priorities and about courts trying to micromanage appropriations decisions.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects
If the Ninth Circuit ultimately holds §1522 mandatory, several significant consequences follow. First, the ruling would strengthen statutory claims as a tool for civil‑society and individual plaintiffs challenging executive rollback of social‑welfare or resettlement programs. Plaintiffs would have a clearer path under the APA to obtain injunctive relief restoring benefits for protected groups after the government curtails programs. Second, agencies would face increased litigation risk when they defund or terminate programs tied to statutory texts that mix “shall” and “authorized.” Administrations seeking to realign priorities would need to pay greater attention to statutory text and anticipate possible court orders to continue services for existing beneficiaries.

Conversely, if Judge Lee’s permissive reading were to prevail—or if courts generally adopt more permissive readings in similar contexts—executive flexibility would increase. That has both pro‑ and anti‑administrative implications: it empowers elected officials to shift programmatic focus and budgeting without always triggering judicial mandates, but it also risks leaving vulnerable populations with fewer enforceable statutory protections.

A more procedural but no less important downstream effect is the potential for circuit splits. Refugee resettlement and immigration intersect with many statutes in different ways across circuits. If other circuits adopt Lee‑style permissive readings while some adopt the majority’s mandatory approach, the Supreme Court may have to resolve the disagreement—particularly because the matter implicates immigration enforcement, national sovereignty, and foreign‑relations considerations that courts treat with special solicitude.

Finally, the case presses on separation‑of‑powers lines. Courts that find and enforce substantive spending or programmatic duties against executive judgment raise delicate institutional questions: to what extent should courts compel the executive to execute programs when Congress conditions duties on appropriations and when program implementation requires administrative expertise? Conversely, refusing to enforce statutory duties may allow the executive to nullify statutory protections through non‑action, shifting the political burden back to Congress and appropriators.

## Bottom line
Pacito is a test case in contemporary statutory interpretation and administrative law. The Ninth Circuit’s interim order displays both the political sensitivity of immigration and refugee policy and the doctrinal puzzles courts now face when obligations are expressed in statutes with mixed signals. The majority’s view that §1522 creates enforceable post‑admission service obligations, and Judge Lee’s countervailing view that §1522 is permissive, are both defensible from well‑worn interpretive moves. What will determine the ultimate outcome—and broader doctrinal fallout—are (1) how the panel’s forthcoming full opinion resolves textual plus contextual signals, (2) whether the Supreme Court’s own posture on agency deference and appropriations constrains or empowers judicial enforcement of such statutory duties, and (3) whether other circuits take divergent paths. For advocates, agencies, and courts, Pacito will be a must‑read when it issues in full: it may reshape how we think about statutory commands, presidential discretion, and the judiciary’s role in policing the line between authorization and obligation.



## A Stay That Orders Action: The Panel’s Unusual Remedial Crafting and What It Signals About Equitable Powers on Appeal

Why this matters — in brief
The Ninth Circuit’s expedited order in Pacito v. Trump is noteworthy not because it resolves the merits (it expressly reserves that for a forthcoming opinion) but because it pairs a stay of the district court’s injunctions with a targeted affirmative command: the panel stays the injunctions barring the President’s suspension of refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), yet simultaneously directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements needed to provide statutorily mandated reception-and-placement services for refugees already admitted. That hybrid remedy—staying a lower-court injunction while carving out and enforcing statutory duties—raises immediate doctrinal questions about appellate equitable power, the interplay between immigration deference and statutory commandeering, and how courts weigh irreparable harms against presidential discretion in national-security-adjacent policy.

What the panel did and the legal touchstones it invoked
The panel applied the familiar Nken stay framework (likelihood of success on the merits; irreparable harm; harm to other parties; public interest), emphasizing that the “most critical” factors are the government’s showing on the merits and irreparable injury. It concluded that the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of the refugee moratorium under § 1182(f), leaning heavily on Trump v. Hawaii’s language that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” and imposes only the prerequisite that the President “find[]” that entry would be detrimental. On the other hand, the panel signaled that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA-based challenge: the district court’s injunction compelling reinstatement of cooperative agreements to provide reception-and-placement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. Accordingly, while staying the district court’s orders “in their entirety,” the panel expressly directed the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide § 1522 services to refugees already admitted to the United States.

Two immediate doctrinal threads run through the order. First, the panel accepts broad presidential authority to suspend refugee admissions under § 1182(f) and applies substantial deference. Second, the panel treats § 1522—at least as to refugees already in the country—as imposing statutory obligations that can be enforced even where the government asserts discretionary policymaking tied to foreign affairs and immigration.

Why the remedial posture is unusual
Appellate stays commonly preserve the status quo while an appeal proceeds; they may carve out limited categories (for example, leaving relief for already-admitted individuals in place). What stands out here is that the Ninth Circuit did more than preserve the status quo for a class of individuals: it ordered affirmative relief—the reinstatement of cooperative agreements—to ensure statutory reception-and-placement services are provided. Two features make the panel’s move striking.

First, it is a positive, not merely prohibitory, injunction from an appellate court pending appeal. Courts of appeals have equitable power, but they more often enjoin or stay lower-court orders in negative terms (i.e., do not enforce X). Ordering the executive to take concrete legislative-administration measures while a stay remains in effect is an unusual hybrid: it limits the district court’s structural remedy (by staying the admissions injunction) while enforcing statutory obligations that the panel views as non-discretionary as to beneficiaries already admitted.

Second, the order threads an unusual substantive needle: it gives the President leeway to suspend future admissions under § 1182(f) (a broad national-security/immigration discretion) while simultaneously insisting that statutory domestic resettlement duties under § 1522 be honored for those already inside the country. That splitting of relief signals that deference in the immigration context does not automatically erode statutory constraints on welfare-like obligations for people physically present in the United States.

Appellate equitable authority and Nken: is this a comfortable fit?
The panel grounded its relief in the Nken factors, and courts have long used those factors flexibly to shape stays and carveouts. But imposing affirmative obligations raises questions about the limits of appellate equitable power pending appeal. Two points are worth emphasizing.

First, appellate courts have equitable authority to fashion interim relief when necessary to preserve rights and prevent irreparable harm—particularly where statutory rights are implicated and where the status quo would cause obvious, tangible injury (here, the alleged disruption of reception-and-placement services for refugees). The panel’s explicit finding that the government was uncertain whether it could deliver those services supports an equity-based compulsion to prevent irreparable harm to a vulnerable class.

Second, though equitable authority exists, it is subject to separation-of-powers sensitivities. Compelling an executive agency to enter or reinstate cooperative agreements touches on resource allocation, contracting, and international coordination—areas where courts traditionally tread carefully. The panel sought to confine its affirmative order: it directs reinstatement only “to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States” and limits those services to “the usual and customary services” under the prior agreements. That tailoring can be seen as an attempt to respect executive prerogatives while enforcing an apparently statutory baseline, but it also raises hard questions about how specific and intrusive a court may be while a case is on appeal.

Separation-of-powers concerns — competing readings of § 1522
Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent spotlights the principal counter-argument: § 1522 is primarily an authorization, not a mandate. Lee reasons that the statute’s title (“Authorization for programs…”) and repeated “is authorized” language suggest permissive congressional intent, and that mandatory elements in § 1522(a) are conditions that attach only if the executive elects to provide funding. From that perspective, a court ordering the reinstatement of cooperative agreements would be commandeering executive discretionary choices about program design and funding—an unacceptable intrusion pending appeal.

The panel’s approach assumes a different textual and functional reading: at least as to refugees already admitted, § 1522 imposes enforceable obligations—conditions and duties the government cannot escape by a blanket suspension of funding or termination of cooperative agreements. The panel’s reference to statutory specifics (e.g., the Director’s responsibilities, the statutory appropriation level, reporting obligations) buttresses the view that Congress intended an ongoing statutory program for domestic resettlement with minimum commitments that survive administrative reorganization.

Both readings have plausible textual hooks. The panel implicitly resolves the ambiguity in favor of enforceability for admitted refugees; Lee warns about judicial micromanagement of discretionary programs. How courts ultimately resolve that tension will shape the boundary between judicially enforceable statutory backstops and executive discretion in asylum/refugee policy.

How the panel balanced harms
The order signals two balancing moves worth noting. First, the panel gave the government the principal policy victory on admissions—recognizing the high hurdle plaintiffs face when challenging a presidential § 1182(f) determination. That reduces (for now) the risk that nationwide moratoria will be checked on a simple statutory-deference theory.

Second, the panel recognized that allowing the government to simultaneously terminate on-the-ground services for refugees already admitted could cause concrete, irreparable harms that a stay should not countenance. By requiring reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees, the court attempted to calibrate relief so that the government’s broader policy goals could proceed without inflicting immediate harm on people who already had lawful presence and statutory protections.

Downstream implications — what to watch for
Several practical and doctrinal downstream effects follow from this hybrid remedy.

- For litigants: The order suggests appellate courts may be willing to craft nuanced interim orders—staying parts of district court relief while enforcing statutory minima—rather than choosing an all-or-nothing posture. Litigants should prepare to argue not just global stay factors but also how narrow carveouts should be fashioned to prevent irreparable harms.

- For agencies and the Executive Branch: Courts may scrutinize abrupt programmatic shutdowns that leave statutorily protected beneficiaries without services. If agencies terminate cooperative agreements wholesale while asserting broad policy authority, they risk judicially imposed stopgaps, even on appeal. That practical risk may push agencies to use phased transitions or to preserve minimal service levels while policy reviews proceed.

- For separation-of-powers doctrine: The case will likely test the limits of judicially compelled affirmative action against the executive. If the forthcoming opinion sustains the panel’s inference that § 1522 creates enforceable duties for admitted refugees, future disputes over program suspensions where Congress left ambiguous funding language could become regularized judicial events. Conversely, a narrower final opinion could reassert Lee’s restraint and limit courts’ willingness to order positive relief.

- For shadow-docket comparisons: The order resembles recent “carveout” practice in emergency or shadow-docket rulings where courts preserve relief for narrow classes (e.g., particular plaintiffs or categories of persons) while granting broader deference to government policy. Pacito follows that pattern but goes further by prescribing affirmative administrative action—raising the stakes in debates about transparency, reasoning, and the use of expedited appellate intervention.

Bottom line
Pacito’s interim order is a reminder that appellate courts retain robust equitable tools and that they may deploy them, even on expedited review, to protect statutory entitlements for people physically present in the United States while deferring to executive policymaking on future admissions. The panel’s hybrid remedy—simultaneously deferring to presidential authority under § 1182(f) and demanding compliance with § 1522 for admitted refugees—illuminates fault lines over statutory interpretation, separation of powers, and the practical limits of administrative discretion. The forthcoming precedential opinion will be the real test: it will have to reconcile those tensions and set clearer boundaries for when courts may prescribe affirmative remedies against the executive while appeals proceed. Until then, Pacito offers a cautionary lesson to administrations and litigants alike: programmatic shutdowns that leave statutory beneficiaries unserved may be fertile ground for judicially imposed stopgaps, even on an expedited appellate docket.



# The APA, Interagency Roles, and Defunding by Fiat: Why State’s Termination of Resettlement Agreements Faces Headwinds

## Why this matters

The Ninth Circuit’s interim order in Pacito v. Trump—staying district-court injunctions while signaling that plaintiffs are likely to prevail on at least one Administrative Procedure Act claim—puts a spotlight on a recurring question in immigration and administrative law: when the Executive alters refugee admissions or funding, how far does deference to presidential authority extend, and when do statutory duties constrain that discretion? The panel’s short order makes clear that the court views the President’s authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend admissions as highly deferential, but it also identifies a separate, nontrivial line of legal exposure for the government tied to statutory obligations under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 (and related statutes and regulations) that govern domestic reception and placement services for refugees. Practically, the order instructs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements needed to provide reception and placement services for refugees already admitted—an unmistakable signal that abrupt defunding and wholesale termination of agreements may not be an unreviewable exercise of executive power.

## What the court said, in short

The panel granted a stay of the district court’s injunctions pending appeal, concluding that the government is likely to prevail on the merits of plaintiffs’ challenge to the President’s suspension of refugee admissions under § 1182(f). That tracks recent Supreme Court precedent (Trump v. Hawaii) treating § 1182(f) as textually capacious and deserving deference.

But crucially, the court also stated that the government is not likely to prevail on at least one APA challenge: the termination of cooperative agreements and the suspension of funding for the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) may have violated statutory duties set out in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 (and implementing regulations). Because the government admitted—at least in the district court and on appeal—that it had terminated cooperative agreements and could not show that it had actually secured replacements (despite representing that it was preparing an RFP), the Ninth Circuit directed the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services for refugees who have already been admitted.

Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and partial dissent crystallizes the competing statutory readings: the majority interprets § 1522 as imposing mandatory duties (or at least duties enforceable under the APA in this context), while Judge Lee reads § 1522 as primarily authorizing discretionary programs, not commanding them.

## Where the APA defects likely lie

The opinion points to three related APA-based vulnerabilities in the government’s course of action:

1. Contrary to law. Section 1522’s text and regulatory framework allocate responsibilities for reception and placement services (employment training and placement, English training, cash and medical assistance, and special provisions for unaccompanied minors). The Ninth Circuit flagged that the government’s termination of cooperative agreements might be inconsistent with those statutory prescriptions and with HHS/ORR’s duties to provide certain services “to the extent of available appropriations.” If § 1522 imposes at least some mandatory duties (especially where Congress has appropriated funds for refugee and migration assistance), then terminating agreements that implement those duties without lawful justification risks running “contrary to law” under the APA.

2. Failure to consider statutory responsibilities and related consequences. The administrative-record picture—termination of agreements by State, use of a separate foreign-aid executive order (EO 14169) to halt USRAP-related funding, and an unfulfilled promise of an RFP and replacement contractors—suggests the government did not adequately consider whether and how other agencies (notably HHS/ORR) would discharge their statutory duties once funding and agreements were cut. The APA requires reasoned decisionmaking. If State terminated agreements without accounting for ORR’s statutory obligations under § 1522 (and the practical consequences for refugees already admitted), that omission is a classic APA failing: an agency must address important aspects of a problem and cannot ignore clear statutory constraints on its discretion.

3. Disregard of reliance interests. The abrupt termination of long-standing cooperative agreements disrupted a network of nonprofit resettlement agencies that relied on federal funding and predictable workflows to serve refugees. The district court evidently viewed these reliance interests as material, and the Ninth Circuit implicitly confirmed that abrupt repudiation of such relationships—especially where the government gave no plausible assurance that replacement arrangements were in place—can render an action arbitrary and capricious. Reliance can be particularly weighty where Congress has set up a statutory program implemented through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with nonprofits.

Those three lines—statutory duty, failure to account for interagency and statutory constraints, and disregard of reliance—are the APA needles through which the Ninth Circuit is threading the government’s conduct.

## The interagency wrinkle: State vs. ORR/HHS

Pacito underscores an often-overlooked institutional dynamic: cooperative agreements for reception and placement are historically entered into by State (and sometimes DOS programs) but the statutory duties targeted by the court are housed in HHS (Office of Refugee Resettlement). The government’s strategy—using a separate foreign-aid order (EO 14169) to suspend funding streams and then having State terminate cooperative agreements—raises the question whether the termination improperly usurped or frustrated obligations located in another agency. The Ninth Circuit’s directive that the government reinstate cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted” practically recognizes that interagency actions cannot be taken in silo where statutory responsibilities are shared or coordinated across departments.

This is litigationally significant because the Executive could argue that appropriations and executive orders confer broad discretion to prioritize foreign aid and that § 1522’s duties are conditional (“to the extent of available appropriations”). The court, however, points to concrete congressional appropriations for migration and refugee assistance in FY 2025 (roughly $3.178 billion, even after a rescission) and to statutory reporting and accountability mechanisms in § 1523, suggesting that blanket defunding and termination is not automatically insulated from review, particularly in the absence of a credible transition plan or replacement contracts.

## § 1523 and accountability as a lever for judicial scrutiny

The Ninth Circuit cited § 1523’s reporting requirement—annual detailed reports to Congressional judiciary committees—implicitly as a reminder that Congress envisioned oversight and accountability for refugee programs. Where statutes create reporting and accountability structures, courts are more inclined to treat agency behavior as subject to judicially manageable legal constraints. A program that agencies are required to report on annually is less plausibly characterized as wholly discretionary. That reporting duty could sharpen judicial review in at least two ways: first, by evidencing congressional intent that refugee assistance be sustained and monitored; second, by providing a concrete record of program outputs and reliance interests that courts can use to assess whether an agency’s decisionmaking was reasoned.

## Practical stakes and likely downstream effects

For NGOs and refugees on the ground, the Ninth Circuit’s order is consequential. The court’s immediate instruction to reinstate necessary cooperative agreements for already-admitted refugees preserves essential resettlement services and signals judicial skepticism toward unilateral, across-the-board program shutdowns without transition plans. For the Executive Branch, Pacito should prompt careful interagency coordination and documented reasoning if it intends to wind down or reorganize refugee resettlement operations—especially where existing cooperative agreements implement statutory duties or where Congress has appropriated relevant funds.

Doctrinally, the decision (and the forthcoming full opinion) will likely be read as reinforcing two propositions: (1) the President’s suspension power under § 1182(f) is robust and heavily deferred to, and (2) the Executive cannot deploy that broad immigration authority (or separate foreign-aid vehicles) to nullify statutory programs administered by other agencies without reasoned explanation or attention to statutory obligations and reliance interests. That split—deference on admissions, but limits on defunding programmatic implementation—reflects the functional separation between immigration-control prerogatives and statutory administrative programs.

If the Ninth Circuit ultimately holds that § 1522 imposes enforceable duties as to certain services, that ruling could constrain future Administrations from attempting to effect programmatic change by withdrawing support at the funding or cooperative-agreement level without engaging in the procedural and substantive obligations required by the APA. Even if the court’s final opinion adopts a narrower equilibrium—mandating relief only for already-admitted refugees or only where funding and appropriations clearly exist—the decision will still make it harder to use blunt funding cuts to sidestep statutory responsibilities.

## A final analytic take

Pacito is a reminder that executive power in immigration is not a universal trump card. The President may have broad authority to suspend entries under § 1182(f), and the Ninth Circuit accepts that deference; but where Congress has established a statutory program implemented through cooperative agreements and appropriated funds, the APA provides a check on sudden administrative retrenchment done without reasoned explanation, interagency coordination, or attention to reliance and statutory obligations. The government’s own representations matters: promising an RFP while having no evidence of progress will not inoculate a wholesale termination when ORR/HHS duties and NGO reliance are implicated.

Look for the forthcoming opinion to elaborate the boundaries of § 1522’s obligations and the APA defects the panel flagged, and expect the case to influence how agencies coordinate when policy shifts intersect with statutorily mandated service programs.
