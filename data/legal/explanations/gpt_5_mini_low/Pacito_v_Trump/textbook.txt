Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Executive Order 14163 (Jan. 20, 2025): suspension of refugee entry under 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f), 1185(a)
On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” The Order invoked 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (and § 1185(a)) to find that “entry into the United States of refugees under the [USRAP] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States” and directed that refugee entry under USRAP be suspended pending further findings. The Ninth Circuit’s interim order emphasizes that § 1182(f) grants broad presidential authority to suspend the entry of a class of aliens when the President makes such a finding; the panel quotes Trump v. Hawaii for the proposition that the statute “exudes deference” to the President and vests ample power to impose entry restrictions. The executive action at issue is therefore a facial suspension of admissions under the statutory authority delegated to the President to regulate entry when he finds detriment to national interests.

## Executive Order 14169 and State Department’s suspension of USRAP funding
Separate from the January 20 refugee-suspension order, the government also took action affecting funding for the refugee program. On January 24, 2025, the State Department suspended all funding for the United States Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP), citing another executive order, No. 14169, concerning foreign aid. This funding suspension included money used for domestic reception and placement services for refugees once admitted. The combination of a suspension of admissions (EO 14163) and suspension of funding/support (via EO 14169 and State Department action) is central to the plaintiffs’ challenge because it affects both prospective arrivals and the services historically provided to refugees already in the United States or conditionally approved for admission.

## Parties: refugees (admitted and approved but abroad) and three resettlement organizations with State Department cooperative agreements
The plaintiffs are a mixed group: individual refugees who either were already admitted to the United States before the suspension or had received approval for resettlement but remained abroad, and three nongovernmental organizations that had cooperative agreements with the State Department to perform refugee processing overseas and to provide reception and placement services domestically. The organizational plaintiffs represent the class of nongovernmental resettlement partners that historically contracted or cooperatively worked with the government to carry out statutory resettlement services. That mix of plaintiffs frames both the statutory and equitable arguments: individuals claim disruption of admission and post-arrival services, while organizations claim injury from termination of cooperative agreements and loss of funding.

## Claims: Refugee Act violations (Refugee Act of 1980) and APA challenges to funding suspensions and contract terminations
The complaint filed in the Western District of Washington alleges two principal types of claims. First, the plaintiffs contend that Executive Order 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions violates the Refugee Act of 1980 (codified within the Immigration and Nationality Act), which the Supreme Court has described as a “general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees.” That statutory challenge targets the legality of the President’s decision to suspend refugee admissions. Second, the plaintiffs bring Administrative Procedure Act (APA) challenges to the executive branch’s suspension of USRAP funding and the State Department’s termination of cooperative agreements—claims that focus on whether the funding and contract actions were arbitrary, capricious, or otherwise unlawful under the APA and whether statutory requirements governing resettlement services were honored.

## District court preliminary injunctions (Feb. 28 and Mar. 24, 2025): enjoining EO provisions and funding suspensions; ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements
In late February and March 2025 the district court issued preliminary injunctive relief in favor of the plaintiffs. On February 25 (oral) and then in a written order on February 28, 2025, the district court enjoined implementation or enforcement of several provisions of Executive Order No. 14163—specifically §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4—and also enjoined the suspension of USRAP funding, defining the scope of its preliminary relief to include funding suspensions. After the State Department terminated cooperative agreements on February 26, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction on March 24, 2025 ordering the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements terminated after the February 25 ruling. These two district court orders together sought to preserve both the admission-related status quo for certain refugees and the funding and institutional infrastructure necessary to provide reception and placement services.

## Government appeals and Ninth Circuit initial stay orders (Mar. 25 with carveout; Apr. 21 and May 9 clarifications)
The government appealed the district court’s orders and moved for a stay pending appeal. On March 25, 2025, a Ninth Circuit motions panel granted a partial stay: it stayed the district court’s preliminary injunctions except for a limited carveout protecting individuals who had been conditionally approved for refugee status by U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025. In granting the partial stay, the panel emphasized the special deference 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) affords to the President’s findings about entry restrictions, citing Trump v. Hawaii. The panel then issued additional orders on April 21 and May 9 clarifying the scope of the carveout and the stay. Those clarifications refined which parties remained covered by the district court injunctions while the broader injunctions were held in abeyance pending fuller consideration.

## District court’s July 14 enforcement framework and magistrate appointment; government’s July 17 emergency motion; Ninth Circuit administrative stay on July 18
After the Ninth Circuit’s partial stay, the district court took steps to operationalize the limited relief that remained in effect. On July 14, 2025, it issued an order establishing a framework to enforce the carveout and appointed a magistrate judge to oversee implementation. The government responded with an emergency motion on July 17 seeking enforcement, clarification, or amendment of the Ninth Circuit’s stay decision. The Ninth Circuit then entered an administrative stay on July 18, 2025 of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order while it considered the government’s emergency filing and the broader appeal. That administrative stay temporarily halted the district court’s enforcement framework pending appellate resolution.

## Current order: vacates prior stays, denies emergency motion as moot, and issues a new stay pending appeal with a limited exception
On September 12, 2025 the Ninth Circuit issued the interim panel order after expedited argument. The court vacated both the March 25 stay (as amended) and the July 18 administrative stay. It denied the government’s July 17 emergency motion as moot. Most significantly for posture, the panel issued a new stay pending appeal: it stayed the district court’s February 28 and March 24 preliminary injunctions in their entirety, subject to a key exception. The court explained its reasoning under the familiar four-factor stay test (likelihood of success on the merits; irreparable injury; harm to other parties; and public interest), stressing that the government is likely to prevail on the challenge to the President’s authority to suspend refugee admissions under § 1182(f). At the same time, the panel signaled that the government is unlikely to prevail on certain APA-based claims concerning statutory duties to provide reception and placement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. Consequently, the Ninth Circuit directed that cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services under § 1522 be reinstated for refugees who have already been admitted to the United States, and it required that those services include the usual and customary services previously provided under the terminated agreements. The panel therefore fashioned a limited, practical exception to the stay to preserve statutorily mandated or required post-admission services while the broader issues (including the validity of the suspension of admissions) remain pending on appeal. The court announced that a full opinion explaining its reasoning will follow, but the interim order sets the operative procedural posture: the district court injunctions are stayed pending appeal except that the government must reinstate necessary cooperative agreements and provide reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted.

# Governing Legal Framework

This chapter describes the legal standards and statutory provisions the Ninth Circuit relied on in its interim stay order in Pacito v. Trump. The Court’s order applied the familiar stay factors governing stays pending appeal, described the President’s authority to suspend entry of aliens under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (and how Trump v. Hawaii frames judicial review of that authority), and identified the statutory structure created by the Refugee Act—particularly 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and related provisions—relevant to challenges to the Executive’s termination of cooperative agreements and suspension of refugee-program funding. It also explains the role of appropriations and the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) in policing agency action in this context.

## Stay pending appeal: Nken v. Holder factors

A stay pending appeal is an equitable remedy; the Supreme Court’s Nken v. Holder framework sets the governing four-factor test. A movant must show (1) a strong likelihood of success on the merits, (2) that it would suffer irreparable harm absent a stay, (3) that other parties would not be substantially harmed by the stay, and (4) that the public interest favors granting the stay. Nken emphasizes that the first two factors—likelihood of success and irreparable injury—are the most critical. A court may deny or grant a stay if the balance of these considerations favors preservation of the status quo pending appellate review.

In Pacito the Ninth Circuit applied those same factors. The panel concluded that the government had demonstrated it was likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ statutory and constitutional challenges to the President’s suspension of refugee admissions—principally because § 1182(f) grants the President broad power and courts owe substantial deference in national-security and immigration-entry decisions (see next section). The panel also treated the remaining factors (irreparable injury, injury to others, and the public interest) as tipping toward the government in light of the national and foreign-policy interests implicated by a refugee moratorium. Because the first two Nken factors are most important, the panel’s view that the government was likely to succeed was decisive in granting a stay of the district court’s injunctions (with specific, limited exceptions discussed below).

## Presidential authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and Trump v. Hawaii

8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) authorizes the President, after making a finding that “the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” to “suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens” or to impose restrictions on entry “for such period as he shall deem necessary.” The statute is broadly worded and confers plenary-looking authority over admission decisions.

In Trump v. Hawaii (2018), the Supreme Court construed § 1182(f) as providing the President with wide latitude and instructed that courts should not engage in a searching review of the President’s justifications when the suspension of entry is premised on national-security and foreign-relations concerns. The Court described the statute as one that “exudes deference” to the President and said that the “sole prerequisite” is the President’s finding that entry would be detrimental to U.S. interests. The Pacito panel relied on Trump v. Hawaii to justify strong deference to the Executive’s determination that refugee admissions should be suspended and to conclude that the government was likely to prevail on challenges to the suspension under § 1182(f). The panel stressed that courts are not to reweigh competing foreign-policy judgments but should accept the President’s finding unless the executive exceeds textual limits of § 1182(f)—which the panel found the order did not do.

That deference, however, applies to the suspension of entry itself, not necessarily to other statutory duties Congress has imposed on agencies after refugees are admitted. The panel’s stay thus distinguished between (a) the Executive’s authority to halt admissions under § 1182(f), and (b) statutory obligations under the Refugee Act that may constrain how the government treats refugees already admitted or conditionally approved before the moratorium.

## Refugee Act provisions on post-admission services (8 U.S.C. § 1522 and related provisions; 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq.)

The Refugee Act and its implementing regulations establish a statutory scheme for resettlement services after refugees are admitted to the United States. Several provisions are central in Pacito:

- 8 U.S.C. § 1522(a)(1)(A). This subsection contains provisions the district court (and the Ninth Circuit panel for part of its reasoning) read as imposing duties on the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR). It directs the Director, “to the extent of available appropriations,” to make available sufficient resources for employment training, English-language training, cash assistance structured not to discourage self-sufficiency, and gender-equal access to training. The “to the extent of available appropriations” phrase injects a link between statutory requirements and funding.

- 8 U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). This provision permits (and authorizes) the government to provide resettlement services through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies. Historically, the federal government has used cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations to deliver the “reception and placement” services referenced throughout the statute.

- 8 U.S.C. § 1522(d)(2)(B). This subsection addresses obligations concerning unaccompanied refugee minors, authorizing the Director to assume legal and financial responsibility during an interim period and to make “necessary decisions” for the child’s immediate care. The subsection uses both “shall” language and qualifiers—“if necessary”—which the concurrence in Pacito emphasized in arguing that the statutory language is permissive and context-dependent.

- 8 U.S.C. § 1522(e)(7)(A). This paragraph directs the Secretary to “develop and implement alternative projects” for recently arrived refugees (those in the U.S. less than 36 months) to provide interim support, medical services, support services, and case management “as needed.” The “as needed” qualification again highlights that the statutes contemplate a degree of administrative judgment about the scope and timing of services.

- 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. These regulations implement the statutes and describe the federal funding streams and programmatic standards for refugee services and the administration of those funds through grantees and cooperative agreements. They define the mechanics by which reception-and-placement services are commonly provided.

Taken together, these provisions create a statutory framework in which Congress contemplated federal involvement in post-admission resettlement services and authorized the use of cooperative agreements, grants, and contracts to deliver those services. The Ninth Circuit panel concluded that certain language in § 1522—when read alongside the implementing statutory scheme and prior practice of funding cooperative agreements—supports the view that the government has legal obligations to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted. The panel therefore ordered the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide statutory reception and placement services to refugees who have been admitted to the United States, while leaving for a later opinion the full merits analysis under the APA.

Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and dissent takes a contrary interpretive position: she reads § 1522 chiefly as an authorization of programs (per the provision’s title and repeated “is authorized” language in the statute), arguing that the statute does not, on its face, mandate that the Executive run these programs irrespective of executive policy choices. That disagreement reflects a statutory-interpretation dispute the Ninth Circuit said it would resolve later in a full opinion.

## Funding, appropriations context, and § 1523 reporting

Statutory responsibilities under § 1522 are tied to appropriations. Many of § 1522’s directives are expressed “to the extent of available appropriations,” so whether and how the government delivers services depends in part on Congress’s funding decisions. The Ninth Circuit’s interim order referenced FY 2025 appropriations: Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance in FY 2025 (as enacted in Pub. L. No. 119‑4, adopting amounts from earlier legislation), and an $800 million reduction was later effected through the Rescissions Act of 2025 (Pub. L. No. 119‑28). Those appropriations and rescissions shape the practical availability of funds to support the programs Congress envisioned.

Section 1523 complements § 1522 by requiring accountability: the Secretary must provide a detailed report on activities under the refugee subchapter to congressional judiciary committees annually. That reporting requirement supports congressional oversight of how appropriations are spent and how programs are run—context the district court and the panel considered relevant when evaluating whether the executive’s suspension of funding and termination of cooperative agreements comported with statutory constraints.

## APA oversight of agency action: challenges to suspension of funding and termination of cooperative agreements

The Administrative Procedure Act governs judicial review of final agency actions that are “arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law,” or that exceed statutory authority. In Pacito the plaintiffs challenged the Executive’s suspension of USRAP and the Department of State’s termination of cooperative agreements under the APA (and under the Refugee Act). The Ninth Circuit panel signaled that the government’s suspension of refugee admissions under § 1182(f) is likely insulated from APA-style scrutiny to the extent the action is a presidential foreign-affairs and admission decision subject to Trump v. Hawaii’s deferential standard. But the panel carved out a separate APA question concerning actions that affect refugees after admission—particularly the termination of cooperative agreements and the suspension of resettlement funding that supports services statutorily described in § 1522.

The panel concluded that the government was not likely to prevail on at least one APA-based claim: the contention that the Executive’s blanket termination of cooperative agreements and funding suspension frustrated statutory duties to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted. The Court therefore directed reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees admitted to the United States, while staying injunctions that would otherwise compel admissions. The panel left a fuller APA merits analysis for its forthcoming opinion, recognizing that APA review can reach agency actions implementing statutory programs—even where other aspects of the government’s conduct (suspending admissions under § 1182(f)) attract heightened deference.

Conclusion

Pacito illuminates how different legal regimes intersect when a presidential order touches on immigration admission policy and separately statutorily mandated post-admission services. Under Nken, courts evaluate stays through a four-factor test that prioritizes likelihood of success and irreparable harm. Trump v. Hawaii establishes strong deference to the President under § 1182(f) for suspensions of entry, but that deference does not automatically preclude APA or statutory review of the Executive’s implementation choices affecting refugees already in the United States—particularly when Congress has created a statutory program (§ 1522) and tied duties to appropriations and administrative mechanisms (cooperative agreements, reports under § 1523, and implementing regulations). The Ninth Circuit’s interim order reflects this division: it stayed parts of the district court’s injunctions that would interfere with the President’s power to suspend admissions while directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to effectuate statutorily described reception-and-placement services for refugees already admitted pending further appellate review.

# Application of the Stay Factors

This section explains how the Ninth Circuit applied the four-factor stay test from Nken v. Holder to two distinct aspects of the dispute: (1) the President’s suspension of refugee entry under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (Executive Order 14163), and (2) the executive branch’s termination of resettlement funding and cooperative agreements that provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States (claims arising under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and the APA). The panel treated these as analytically separable for the stay analysis and reached different conclusions about the government’s likelihood of prevailing on each claim. The court’s reasoning shows how deference in immigration and foreign-affairs contexts narrows judicial review of admission suspensions, while statutory duties that govern domestic post-admission programs may be subject to ordinary statutory and administrative-law scrutiny.

## Likelihood of success — entry suspension under § 1182(f)

The panel concluded the government is likely to succeed on the merits of plaintiffs’ challenge to Executive Order 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions. That conclusion rests on the Supreme Court’s framework in Trump v. Hawaii and the plain text of § 1182(f). Section 1182(f) authorizes the President, whenever he “finds” that entry of any aliens or any class of aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” to suspend entry or impose restrictions. The Ninth Circuit emphasized three points from Hawaii:

- The statutory language is broad and facially unbounded: it permits suspension of “the entry of . . . any class of aliens,” a grant of authority that the Supreme Court characterized as “exuding deference” to the President.
- Judicial inquiry into the President’s national-security or foreign-affairs justifications is limited; courts should not conduct an intrusive probing of the persuasiveness of the President’s findings.
- Where the President has made the requisite “finding,” the text of § 1182(f) supplies the operative prerequisite and signals deference.

Applying those principles, the panel held that the President plainly made the required finding in EO 14163 and that § 1182(f)’s text supplies no judicially enforceable limit that the Executive Order exceeded. Because Nken’s first factor—likelihood of success on the merits—is the “most critical,” the court treated this as a strong reason to grant a stay as to the entry-suspension claim. The panel therefore stayed the district court’s injunction insofar as it barred enforcement of the EO’s suspension of refugee admissions.

## Likelihood of success — statutory/APA challenge to termination of services under § 1522

The court reached the opposite conclusion about the government’s likelihood of success on at least one of plaintiffs’ APA/statutory claims arising from 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which governs reception and placement services for refugees. The panel concluded the government is not likely to prevail on a claim that the executive branch may lawfully terminate cooperative agreements and funding that provide statutorily prescribed services to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.

The court’s reasoning focused on the text and structure of § 1522 and related implementing regulations. Section 1522 enumerates specific programmatic responsibilities—employment training and placement, English-language instruction, cash and medical assistance, and special responsibilities for unaccompanied children—and contemplates provision of those services “to the extent of available appropriations.” The statute authorizes use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies to deliver reception and placement services. The panel saw these statutory provisions as creating substantive obligations the government must honor for admitted refugees, not merely permissive policy options the Executive may discard without regard to statutory constraints. Given the government had terminated cooperative agreements and could not demonstrate progress toward securing alternative arrangements, the court concluded plaintiffs had a strong chance of showing the terminations were unlawful under the APA (for example, arbitrary and capricious or contrary to statute).

Because the government was unlikely to win on the APA/statutory claim concerning provision of domestic resettlement services to admitted refugees, the panel limited the scope of the stay. While it stayed the district court’s preliminary injunctions directed at the EO’s entry suspension, it ordered the government to reinstate cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States” and to provide the usual and customary services previously afforded under those agreements. That remedy reflects the panel’s view that even when the Executive has broad authority to control admissions, it may not unilaterally and immediately abandon statutorily mandated domestic services for people already admitted.

## Irreparable harm and the public interest

Nken’s second and fourth factors—irreparable injury and public interest—were analyzed in tandem and tipped differently depending on the disputed action.

- For the entry suspension, the panel credited the government’s asserted national-security and foreign-affairs harms. Because refugee admissions implicate foreign policy, national security, and the President’s plenary immigration authority, the court gave weight to the harm the government would suffer if an injunction were allowed to block the policy during appeal. The panel invoked Hawaii’s recognition of presidential discretion in immigration matters and concluded that the public interest and potential irreparable injury favored the government on the suspension issue.

- For the termination of resettlement services, the balance was different. The harms the plaintiffs identified were immediate, domestic, and concrete: refugees already admitted who rely on reception and placement services could lose statutory benefits necessary for economic self-sufficiency, health, or care (including particular statutory protections for unaccompanied minors). Those harms are the kind of irreparable injury that supports preliminary relief when they flow from actions the court believes may be legally invalid. The public interest also favored maintaining statutory protections for vulnerable individuals and ensuring compliance with congressional directives governing domestic refugee assistance.

Thus, while national and foreign policy considerations supported staying relief that would impede the EO, immediate domestic harms from cessation of services counseled against staying the district court’s orders that compelled reinstatement of cooperative agreements to serve admitted refugees.

## Balance of equities — preserving presidential discretion while preventing unlawful cessation of services

The fourth Nken factor—balance of equities—required the court to weigh competing harms to the parties. The panel sought to respect presidential discretion in admission policy while preventing what it viewed as a likely unlawful abdication of statutory duties concerning admitted refugees.

- On one side, keeping the district court’s injunction in effect as to the EO would constrain the President’s broad statutory authority under § 1182(f) and could undermine foreign-policy or national-security judgments; the equities thus favored the government regarding admissions.

- On the other side, allowing the government to maintain its termination of cooperative agreements without immediate restoration would uproot the delivery of services to refugees already lawfully in the United States and would likely impede statutory objectives (economic self-sufficiency, medical and cash support, special care for children). The equities therefore favored plaintiffs with respect to domestic resettlement services.

The remedy the panel imposed reflects that weighing: a stay of the district court’s injunctions as to the EO’s suspension of entries, but an order requiring reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide § 1522 reception and placement services to admitted refugees. That approach attempts to thread the needle between deferring to executive authority over admissions and enforcing statutory protections that bind the executive once refugees have entered the country.

## Dissenting perspective (Judge Lee)

Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent underscores the tension the panel navigated. Lee agreed that the government likely would prevail on the validity of the admission suspension under § 1182(f) and would have stayed the district court’s injunctions in full. He read § 1522 as an authorization rather than a mandatory command—pointing to the statute’s title (“Authorization for programs”), repeated use of “is authorized,” and qualifications such as “to the extent of available appropriations” as evidence that Congress granted permissive authority, not a binding duty to maintain specific programs or cooperative agreements. From Lee’s perspective, the court should not compel the Executive to continue programs the statute merely authorizes.

The majority disagreed on that statutory interpretation and accordingly crafted a stay that preserves presidential discretion over admissions while requiring continued provision of services the panel viewed as mandated for refugees already admitted.

## Takeaway for reading the opinion

The panel’s application of Nken illustrates two useful habits for reading opinions that address separable issues:

- Identify the distinct legal regimes at play (here, plenary admission authority under § 1182(f) and domestic statutory duties under § 1522) and treat the stay factors separately for each, because deference and harms vary with institutional context.

- Track how textual interpretation of statutes affects each Nken prong: a broad delegation of authority (as the court read § 1182(f)) reduces the government’s burden on likelihood of success and public-interest harms, while statutory provisions that create specific programmatic duties (as the court read § 1522) increase the risk that the government cannot show likelihood of success and that the balance of equities favors maintaining relief for affected individuals.

The Ninth Circuit’s interim order embodies those distinctions: deference and a stay for the admission suspension; enforcement of statutory reception-and-placement obligations for admitted refugees.

# Interim Disposition and Practical Directives

This chapter explains the Ninth Circuit’s interim order in Pacito v. Trump: what prior stays the panel vacated, which district-court injunctions the panel suspended pending appeal, the narrow operational exception it carved out, and the immediate actions the court directed the government to take. It also explains the legal and practical underpinnings the panel relied on to justify those directives and notes the procedural posture going forward.

## What stays the panel vacated and why that matters

The panel expressly vacated two prior Ninth Circuit orders: the administrative stay it had issued on July 18, 2025, and an earlier partial stay issued March 25, 2025 (as later amended April 21 and May 9, 2025). Vacatur of those orders means the court withdrew those earlier temporary adjustments to the district court’s injunctions and replaced them with the new interim stay described in the panel order. Practically, vacating the prior stays removes any inconsistent or overlapping conditions those prior stays imposed and substitutes a single, clarified interim disposition while the merits are decided.

Why that matters procedurally: the March 25 order had already carved out a narrow exception allowing certain injunctive relief to remain in effect for refugees conditionally approved before January 20, 2025. By vacating that order and its amendments, the panel reasserted control and then reissued a consolidated stay with specific, differently framed exceptions. Vacatur is an ordinary device to avoid confusion and ensure the court’s current interim ruling controls.

## The new stay: what is stayed and what is not

The panel issued a stay pending appeal of the district court’s two preliminary injunctions (the February 28, 2025 written injunction defining scope of the initial February 25 ruling, and the March 24, 2025 injunction ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements). The stay is broad: it pauses enforcement of those district-court orders “in their entirety” except for the limited exception the panel set out.

Functionally, the stay means that, until the Ninth Circuit issues its final opinion in this appeal (or further order), the government is not bound by the district court’s injunctions except to the extent the panel’s exception requires action. A stay pending appeal is an interlocutory tool: it preserves the status quo while the appellate court reviews the district court’s preliminary relief, and it reflects the panel’s assessment—based on the four Nken factors—that the government has shown a likelihood of success on at least some claims and that other factors (irreparable harm, public interest, balance of harms) favor a stay.

## The directed exception: reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees

Although the stay suspends the district court’s injunctions generally, the panel ordered a focused exception. The government is required to reinstate those cooperative agreements that are necessary to provide the “reception and placement” services described in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States. The panel characterized these services as the “usual and customary services” historically provided under prior cooperative agreements.

Key points about the exception’s scope and legal basis:

- Basis in the statute: § 1522 establishes a congressional scheme for domestic refugee resettlement services—often provided through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with nonprofit organizations—and describes specific categories of services the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement must consider “to the extent of available appropriations.” The panel concluded that on the present record the government has not shown it is likely to prevail, under the Administrative Procedure Act challenge, in denying these statutorily described reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted.

- Which refugees are covered: the ordered reinstatement applies to refugees already admitted to the United States. The order does not require reinstatement of cooperative agreements for processing refugees who remain abroad or for admitting refugees going forward while the moratorium under the Executive Order remains stayed by the panel’s deference-based reasoning.

- Nature of services required: the court identified core services that must be made available, invoking § 1522’s text and historical practice. These include employment training and placement (aimed at achieving economic self-sufficiency), English-language training, cash assistance administered in a way designed not to disincentivize self-sufficiency, and case management/support services that have typically been part of reception-and-placement cooperative agreements. The court also referenced the § 1522(e)(7)(A) text concerning interim support and medical services for refugees in their first thirty-six months, which informs the practical contours of required medical and cash assistance programs.

- Special provision for unaccompanied minors: in light of § 1522(d)(2)(B), the panel directed that the Director assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied minor refugees “if necessary” and make necessary decisions to provide for their immediate care. The panel’s instruction is situation-specific—triggered by the presence and needs of such children—and tracks the statute’s language authorizing the Director to take immediate action in interim periods.

- “Usual and customary” qualifier: the court’s command is not open-ended; it is tethered to the historic content of reception-and-placement cooperative agreements (the services “usually and customarily” provided). This allows some operational judgment (e.g., the exact design of programs) while requiring reinstatement of the basic service structure the statute contemplates and that existed before termination of the cooperative agreements.

## Why the panel ordered reinstatement now: concern about the government’s representations

A practical driver of the court’s targeted directive was the government’s own representations about its contingency plans. The government had informed the district court in March it was “actively preparing a request for proposals” (RFP) to identify new resettlement agencies to provide reception-and-placement services. Yet at oral argument before the Ninth Circuit, government counsel acknowledged uncertainty about whether any progress had been made toward securing new providers. Given that uncertainty, and the statutory obligations in § 1522 that contemplate services for refugees admitted to the United States, the panel concluded that the risk of immediate harm to admitted refugees and to unaccompanied children weighed in favor of requiring reinstatement of the cooperative agreements necessary to deliver those services.

In short: the court viewed the government’s lack of demonstrated progress toward a transition to new providers as insufficient to excuse an immediate resumption of the prior cooperative-agreement-based service delivery for already-admitted refugees.

## Denial of the July 17 emergency motion as moot

The panel denied as moot the government’s July 17, 2025 emergency motion that sought clarification, enforcement, or amendment of the previously issued stay. Because the panel replaced the earlier administrative stay and issued the present consolidated stay with its specific operational exception, the earlier emergency motion no longer presented an active dispute requiring separate relief. The ruling thus resolves the earlier emergency filing by supersession.

## Procedural posture going forward: merits opinion to follow; stay remains

The panel explicitly stated that an opinion on the merits will issue “in due course.” The current order is interim: it stays the district court’s injunctions pending final resolution of the appeal, but it also imposes the narrow operational obligation to reinstate cooperative agreements to serve refugees already admitted. The stay will remain in effect until the Ninth Circuit issues its full merits opinion or orders otherwise. Parties should therefore treat the reinstatement directive as an immediate, enforceable obligation even while the broader dispute about the Executive Order and the district court’s preliminary injunctions awaits final appellate resolution.

## Practical implications for the parties and for practitioners

- For the government: immediate steps must be taken to reinstate the specific cooperative agreements (or their functional equivalents) necessary to deliver the usual reception-and-placement services to refugees already in the United States. If the government intends to rely on a different contractual structure (e.g., newly issued RFPs and new agencies), it will need to show either (1) that those alternatives are already in place and provide materially equivalent services without interruption, or (2) obtain further relief from the Ninth Circuit. The panel’s concern about the lack of demonstrated progress means the government should document any concrete transition steps promptly.

- For resettlement organizations: organizations whose cooperative agreements were terminated and who historically provided reception-and-placement services should be prepared to resume work under reinstated agreements for admitted refugees. The “usual and customary” standard will likely be applied operationally—organizations should expect to deliver core services identified in § 1522 (employment training/placement, English training, cash and medical assistance design, case management), and to engage with the court or magistrate-appointed overseer if disputes arise about sufficiency or scope.

- For the district court and enforcement: the district court’s prior enforcement order (the July 14 framework appointment of a magistrate) had been administratively stayed; with the new, more limited requirement from the Ninth Circuit, the district court’s enforcement role may be limited to ensuring compliance with the appellate panel’s specific reinstatement directive, and the Ninth Circuit signaled it will supply a full merits opinion later.

In sum, the Ninth Circuit’s interim order replaces earlier, piecemeal stays with a consolidated stay pending appeal, but it requires the government to reinstate the cooperative-agreement-based infrastructure necessary to deliver statutorily described reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States. The panel tied that requirement to the statutory language of § 1522 and to the government’s failure to demonstrate an operative alternative, and it left the larger merits questions—principally the validity of the Executive Order’s admissions suspension and the scope of § 1522—for the merits opinion to follow.

# Separate Opinion and Doctrinal Dispute Over § 1522

This chapter explains Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and partial dissent in Pacito v. Trump, focusing on the doctrinal split over the meaning of 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The split frames the crucial legal question that the Ninth Circuit will resolve on the merits: does § 1522 create a statutory duty that compels the executive to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted, or does it merely authorize discretionary programs that the executive may choose to fund or suspend? Understanding the parties’ competing interpretive anchors and the consequences for Administrative Procedure Act (APA) review is essential to reading the court’s forthcoming merits opinion.

## Agreement: broad § 1182(f) authority and the stay of the entry injunction

Judge Lee concurs with the panel’s conclusion that the President enjoys broad, deferential authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend the entry of aliens—a finding the majority relied on to stay the portion of the district court’s injunction that sought to block the Executive Order’s suspension of refugee admissions. Lee accepts the Trump v. Hawaii framework: § 1182(f) vests the President with expansive power to restrict entry and courts should not conduct a searching inquiry into the President’s national-security or foreign-affairs justifications for an admissions moratorium. Thus the narrow issue where Lee departs from the majority is not the admissions moratorium but whether the federal government has a legal obligation to continue providing statutorily described resettlement services to refugees who were admitted before that moratorium took effect.

## Core disagreement: mandate versus authorization in § 1522

The disagreement turns on statutory interpretation of § 1522. The majority reads § 1522 as creating mandatory duties—legal obligations on executive-branch agencies—to provide reception and placement services to refugees admitted to the United States. Based on that reading, the majority concluded the government is unlikely to prevail on the APA challenge to its termination of cooperative agreements and defunding of resettlement services, and therefore ordered reinstatement of agreements sufficient to provide the services described in § 1522 to admitted refugees.

Judge Lee reads the same statutory text differently. He contends § 1522 is best read as permissive—an authorization for the executive to establish programs providing services, but not a command that such programs must be run or funded. From Lee’s perspective, if § 1522 is authorized rather than mandatory, the executive’s termination of cooperative agreements and suspension of funding falls within agency discretion and is not properly enjoined by a court as contrary to a statutory mandate.

## Textual cues emphasized by the dissent

Lee marshals a set of interpretive signals to support the authorization reading:

- The statute’s title: Section 1522 is captioned “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” Lee treats that label as evidence that Congress intended permissive, not mandatory, action. He cites the ordinary meaning of “authorization” to show Congress was granting permission rather than imposing a duty.

- Repeated “is authorized” phrasing: Subsections that enumerate categories of services and funding (the discrete program provisions in §§ 1522(b)–(e)) use language such as “is authorized” or “the Director is authorized,” which Lee reads as further confirmation that Congress established options for the executive rather than commands.

- Conditional language in specific provisions: Lee focuses on subsections that qualify obligations with terms like “if necessary” (in § 1522(d)(2)(B) regarding unaccompanied minor refugees) and “as needed” (in § 1522(e)(7)(A) regarding alternative projects for cash and medical assistance). He treats those qualifiers as the kind of discretionary phrasing inconsistent with an unconditional statutory mandate.

- The structure of § 1522(a): Lee reads subsection (a) as setting “conditions and considerations” that apply when the executive elects to provide assistance under the authorization provisions, rather than transforming those conditions into an affirmative duty to provide assistance regardless of executive choice.

Taken together, these textual features lead Lee to conclude the statute confers discretion—“permission to do something”—and thus does not support a judicially enforceable command to continue the prior cooperative agreements.

## Majority’s contrary indicators

Although the opinion here is an order rather than the full merits opinion, the majority identifies textual and contextual reasons supporting a mandatory reading:

- Mandatory “shall” in § 1522(a)(1)(A): The majority points to language in subsection (a) that uses “shall” and requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, “to the extent of available appropriations,” to make available sufficient resources for various services (employment training, English-language training, cash assistance design, women’s access). The majority reads that “shall” as imposing a duty to provide those services when appropriations are available.

- Statutory and regulatory structure: The majority treats § 1522 as part of a coherent statutory scheme that contemplates concrete, recurring obligations to refugees after admission (including reporting and accountability provisions in § 1523 and regulatory schemes in 45 C.F.R. § 400.200 et seq.). This structure supports a reading that Congress required certain post-admission services and accountability, rather than merely authorizing ad hoc programs.

- Appropriations context: The majority emphasizes that Congress appropriated funds for migration and refugee assistance (citing specific FY 2025 appropriations and rescissions), which, in their view, supports the inference that Congress expected the statutory duties to be carried out when appropriations were available. The “to the extent of available appropriations” qualifier is read not as a free pass to disregard the statute, but as a limiting clause: the duties apply insofar as funding exists.

The majority therefore concludes—at least preliminarily for stay purposes—that plaintiffs have a likelihood of success on the APA claim that the government unlawfully terminated statutory resettlement functions.

## Implications for APA review

The doctrinal stakes are procedural and remedial as well as interpretive. If § 1522 creates a mandatory statutory duty, then the government’s termination of cooperative agreements and suspension of funding can be challenged under the APA as agency action that is “not in accordance with law” and therefore arbitrary and capricious or otherwise unlawful. A court could order reinstatement of agreements or other relief necessary to effectuate the statutory mandate.

If, by contrast, § 1522 is a permissive authorization, the government’s decision to defund programs and terminate cooperative agreements is an exercise of executive discretion not compelled by statute. Under that view, the APA ordinarily will not permit courts to substitute their judgment for an agency’s discretionary policy choice unless the action is arbitrary, capricious, or otherwise unlawful on non–statutory grounds. In short, the outcome of the interpretive dispute determines whether the plaintiffs’ APA claim can succeed as a facial or as-applied claim that the government violated a statutory duty.

Judge Lee emphasizes that—if § 1522 is merely authorizing—there is no statutory violation to vindicate. Thus, in his view, the district court exceeded its role by enjoining the executive’s policy choices regarding funding and program administration; a stay of the injunctions in full would be warranted.

## Significance for the court’s forthcoming merits opinion

The merits opinion the Ninth Circuit promises will need to do several things, and the Lee concurrence/dissent highlights what will matter most:

- Define the contours of any statutory duty: The court must decide whether the “shall” and the statutory structure create an enforceable obligation, and if so, identify its scope—who is covered (admitted refugees, conditionally approved-but-outside applicants, unaccompanied minors), what services are required, and to what temporal extent.

- Reconcile textual cues: The majority will need to explain why “authorization” and “is authorized” language do not defeat a mandatory reading (or why they are compatible with a limited mandatory duty), or why subsection (a)’s “shall” and appropriations context prevail over permissive diction in other subsections.

- Determine the proper remedy under the APA: If the court finds a statutory duty, it must decide what relief is appropriate—reinstatement of cooperative agreements, specific programmatic relief, injunctive relief limited to covered categories of persons, or another remedy tailored to balancing statutory commands and executive discretion.

- Address the intersection with foreign affairs and national security deference: Even if § 1522 imposes duties, the court may still need to show deference to executive assessments regarding practicability and national-security implications of continuing services. The court’s approach to balancing statutory mandates against the President’s broad authority under § 1182(f) will be central.

For students reading the court’s future opinion, Judge Lee’s separate opinion serves as a roadmap of the textual and structural arguments the majority will have to engage. The interpretive battle over whether § 1522 authorizes or mandates refugee services is not just academic: it determines whether courts may compel the executive to restore programs and funding when the executive asserts competing foreign-policy or resource-allocation prerogatives.

## Conclusion

Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and partial dissent isolates the pivotal statutory question that will determine the case’s outcome on the APA claims: is § 1522 an authorization that leaves programmatic choices to the executive, or a mandate that courts can enforce? The majority’s preliminary stay ruling reflects a view that § 1522 likely creates enforceable obligations for admitted refugees when appropriations exist; Lee insists the statutory text and structure more naturally support executive discretion. The forthcoming merits opinion must resolve that interpretive dispute, explain how to read the statute as a whole, and then specify the legal standards and remedies appropriate where statutory duties intersect with the President’s broad immigration and foreign-affairs authority.
