Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

This case arises from a dispute about the legal effect of cancellations for automobile insurance policies that were financed through a third-party premium finance company. Understanding the dispute requires following three sets of actors (the insureds and claimant, the insurer, and the premium financier), the financing mechanism that gave the financier authority to cancel, the sequence of events leading to cancellation and an accident, the legal claims asserted, and the narrow procedural history that brought the parties to the Fifth Circuit on cross-motions for summary judgment.

Parties. The plaintiffs in the consolidated action were Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, each a former GoAuto insured, and Nicholas Jenkins, a third‑party claimant injured in an accident with another GoAuto insured (that insured is not a party to this appeal). The defendants were GoAuto Insurance Company (the insurer) and American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”), the company that financed the plaintiffs’ GoAuto premiums.

Premium‑finance structure and power of attorney. The financing arrangement between APAC and each insured is central. Under these agreements APAC paid GoAuto the policy premium up front; the insureds then owed installments to APAC. Critically, each premium finance agreement included a power-of-attorney provision authorizing APAC to cancel the insured’s GoAuto policy if the insured defaulted on the financing and failed to cure within the statutory cure period. The Louisiana statute at issue conditions an insurer’s ability to act on a premium financer’s cancellation request on a specified form of notice and certification from the premium finance company. The parties’ dispute focuses on whether APAC’s and GoAuto’s practices satisfied those statutory requirements.

Events leading to cancellation and the accident. In each plaintiff’s case the insured failed to make timely installment payments to APAC. APAC sent the insureds a written “Ten Day Notice of Cancellation” warning that nonpayment would result in cancellation if payment was not received within ten days. When payment was not made by the deadline, APAC’s automated processes generated an e‑mail to GoAuto containing a copy of the Ten Day Notice and a written request and certification for cancellation on APAC letterhead. Upon receipt of that e‑mail, GoAuto’s computer system automatically canceled the policy. Separately, at an early morning hour before a human reviewed paper records, GoAuto’s systems executed the cancellation once the e‑mail/web‑service request was received. Later the same morning an APAC employee stamped a printed copy of the certification, and since 2017 that printed, stamped document was handed to a GoAuto employee who marked it “received” and filed it in a shared cabinet. These timing and signature details are the facts that drove the statutory‑interpretation dispute.

Claims asserted. The plaintiffs sued GoAuto and APAC in Louisiana state court, asserting that APAC’s and GoAuto’s cancellation procedures did not effectuate lawful cancellations under the Louisiana statute governing cancellations by premium finance companies. The plaintiffs alleged that the cancellations therefore were ineffective; they also asserted related claims of misrepresentation and breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing arising from the allegedly ineffective cancellations. The legal core of the case, however, became whether the specific procedures APAC and GoAuto used complied with the statutory requirements for notice and certification.

Procedural path and posture on appeal. Williams and Jenkins initially filed a putative class action in state court; Wright was added in an amended complaint. GoAuto removed the case to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties narrowed the dispute to statutory compliance and filed cross‑motions for summary judgment confined to whether GoAuto’s cancellation procedures met Louisiana law. The district court concluded that GoAuto’s procedures complied with the statute and granted GoAuto’s summary judgment motion. That ruling resulted in dismissal of all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. The plaintiffs appealed only the district court’s statutory‑compliance rulings and the denial of their partial summary judgment; the Fifth Circuit’s review therefore is limited to the legal question whether the facts as found by the district court show compliance with the Louisiana cancellation statute.

In short, this appeal turns on whether APAC’s automated e‑mail/request and the later-stamped paper record satisfied the statutory requirements that a premium finance company “certify” certain facts and that the insurer have “receipt” of the cancellation request before cancelling the policy. The procedural posture—cross‑motions for summary judgment on statutory compliance with dismissal after the district court found compliance—frames the Court of Appeals’ task: interpret the statute, apply it to the undisputed procedures, and decide whether the district court erred.

# Louisiana Framework for Cancelling Financed Insurance Policies

This chapter explains the statutory framework and the Louisiana courts’ demand for “strict adherence” that govern cancellations of insurance policies when the premiums are financed by a third‑party finance company. The Williams v. GoAuto Insurance opinion turns entirely on whether GoAuto/APAC’s procedures met this framework, so understanding the statute’s text, the four required certification elements, the statutory trigger for insurer action, and how Louisiana courts interpret and apply these requirements is essential to reading the opinion.

## Statutory source: La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)

The controlling provision is La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3). It authorizes a premium finance company — the entity that paid the insurer the premium and then collects installments from the insured — to request cancellation of the underlying insurance policy when the finance agreement is in default. The statute specifies both the form of the request and the content that must accompany it; those prescribed procedures supply the legal baseline. The Williams opinion treats the statute as setting mandatory requirements that condition the effectiveness of a cancellation requested through the power of attorney in the finance agreement.

## How cancellation is requested

Under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a), the finance company effects a cancellation request by sending to the insurer (by depositing in the mail, using a private carrier, or via electronic mail) two things: (1) a copy of the notice of cancellation that was sent to the insured, and (2) a statement “certifying” certain matters about the finance agreement and the notices sent. The statute thus contemplates a two‑part transmission: the insured’s cancellation notice itself plus a separate certification statement from the finance company. The statute explicitly allows modern methods (including electronic mail), so the form of delivery is not limited to paper mail.

In Williams the Fifth Circuit focuses on whether the company’s chosen method—an automated e‑mail from APAC to a GoAuto web service that triggered immediate automated cancellation—satisfied the statutory requirement that the insurer receive the notice and certification.

## The four certification elements (§ 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv))

The certification that must accompany the notice is not merely pro forma. The statute lists four specific items the premium finance company must certify:

1. That the premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney authorizing cancellation as provided by the statute. This establishes that the finance company has authority to act on the insured’s behalf.

2. That the premium finance agreement is in default and that the default has not been timely cured. This explains the substantive basis for cancellation.

3. That, upon default, a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured in the manner required by the statute, and that the certificate specifies the date the finance company sent that notice to the insured. This fixes the timeline and shows the insured received the statutorily required warning and cure opportunity.

4. That copies of the notice of cancellation were sent to all persons shown by the finance agreement to have an interest in any loss that may occur under the policy, with the names and addresses of any governmental agencies, lienholders, or other third parties to whom the finance company sent notice. This protects third‑party interests and ensures those with a stake in coverage had notice.

These four items together are designed to document authority, substantiate default, and identify who was warned and when — all prerequisites the statute makes part of an effective cancellation request.

## Trigger for insurer action: “receipt” of the notice and statement (§ 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i))

The statute then sets the operative trigger for insurer conduct. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) provides that “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” Put simply, the insurer’s authority to cancel pursuant to the finance company’s power of attorney arises when the insurer receives both the notice to the insured and the accompanying certification from the finance company.

Williams interprets “receipt” in light of ordinary definitions as the act of taking possession or coming into possession of something sent. The opinion emphasizes that modern electronic communications and automated systems can constitute receipt: an emailed certification that is received by the insurer’s systems and that triggers an automatic cancellation meets the statutory receipt requirement so long as the insurer has come into possession, custody, or delivery of the notice and certification.

## Judicial gloss: strict adherence required; defects render cancellation ineffective (Delatte; Eaglin; Britten)

Louisiana courts have repeatedly characterized the statutory requirements not as optional formalities but as mandatory conditions that must be strictly followed. The Williams opinion cites three representative precedents:

- Delatte v. Lemotte: Louisiana courts require strict adherence to the statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel a policy.

- Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co.: Any defect in the statutorily prescribed process can result in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.

- Britten v. Reavis: The court articulated why strictness matters, explaining that the statute serves two central functions (discussed below) and so must be followed precisely.

The takeaway in Williams is that the statutory checklist — the notice plus the certification of the four elements and actual receipt by the insurer — is not expendable. A cancellation that fails any required element risks being voided by the courts.

## Policy purposes of strict adherence: fixing time/date and protecting cure periods (Britten)

Williams underscores the policy rationales the Louisiana intermediate appellate courts have attributed to strict enforcement of the statute. Britten explains two primary purposes:

1. Fix the exact time and date of cancellation. The statute’s requirement that the certification specify the date the finance company sent the insured’s cancellation notice is aimed at creating a clear, verifiable record so parties and courts can determine precisely when coverage ceased.

2. Guarantee a minimum period during which the insured may cure the default. The nine‑ or ten‑day cure window (as implemented by many finance companies) and the documentation of mailing ensure the insured is given a statutorily protected chance to avoid cancellation. Requiring strict compliance prevents premature or unrecorded cancellations that would deprive insureds of the chance to reinstate coverage.

Those twin functions explain why Louisiana courts treat procedural defects seriously: they preserve notice, timing certainty, and the cure opportunity on which the statute insists.

## How this framework sets the baseline for Williams v. GoAuto

In Williams the Fifth Circuit measures GoAuto/APAC’s automated process against the statute and the Louisiana caselaw demanding strict adherence. The court frames questions in two parts that flow directly from the statutory baseline: (1) did APAC send to GoAuto, and did GoAuto receive, a copy of the insured’s notice plus a statement certifying the four statutory items; and (2) did the method of transmission and the timing of any human signature or filing defeat the statutory “receipt” requirement or otherwise introduce a defect that would render the cancellations ineffective under Louisiana precedent?

Because the statute specifies the content and receipt requirements and Louisiana courts treat any material defect as fatal to a cancellation, the appellate inquiry focuses narrowly on whether APAC’s certification and delivery process actually satisfied each statutory element in substance. Williams ultimately concludes that electronic delivery to GoAuto’s systems and the content of APAC’s certification met the statute’s requirements under the modern reading the opinion adopts, thereby satisfying the strict‑adherence baseline without adding extra, signature‑specific formalities not required by the statute or decided Louisiana authority.

# The Challenged Procedures and the Legal Questions Presented

This part of the opinion sets out the concrete mechanics by which APAC and GoAuto handled financed-policy cancellations and frames the two narrow statutory interpretive disputes the plaintiffs pressed on appeal. The court’s analysis ties the factual workflow to the literal text of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(b) and the governing requirement that courts demand “strict adherence” when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel a policy. Understanding the timeline and automated steps is essential because the plaintiffs’ claims rest entirely on whether, under Louisiana law, (1) the finance company’s “certifying” must be a signed, individually identified statement completed before a cancellation becomes effective, and (2) the insurer must take physical possession by a human before it can be deemed to have “receipt” of the cancellation request.

## APAC workflow: the 10‑day notice, automated e‑mail, and automatic cancellation

The operative chain of events begins with APAC’s ten‑day cure notice to the insured: when a policyholder misses a payment, APAC sends a notice warning that the policy will be cancelled if payment is not received within ten days. If payment is not made by midnight on that tenth day, APAC’s systems automatically generate and send an email to GoAuto containing (a) a copy of the ten‑day notice of cancellation and (b) a written request for cancellation that includes a four‑part certification statement tracking the statutory elements (valid power of attorney, uncured default, notice sent to insured with date, and copies sent to interested third parties). This e‑mail is sent in the very early morning hours and is received by a GoAuto computer service.

GoAuto’s information technology is configured so that the moment the APAC e‑mail/web service call is received, GoAuto’s computer system cancels the policy automatically. No human intervenes before that automatic cancellation occurs. Thus, at the precise time the policy is terminated in GoAuto’s system, the only thing transmitted and received is the electronic notice and the certification text produced by APAC’s automated process.

## Post‑cancellation documentation: signature stamping and physical filing

Later that same morning—after the GoAuto system has already effectuated the cancellation—human steps occur to create a paper record. An APAC employee prints the cancellation documents and affixes a signature stamp to them; prior to 2017, those printed-and‑stamped documents were taken to a filing cabinet in a shared room (belonging to GoAuto Management Services), and since 2017 the signed documents are handed to a GoAuto employee who stamps them “received” and places them in a shared cabinet. In short, the signed and/or “received” physical paperwork is created and placed in shared custody only after the electronic cancellation already has taken effect.

These temporal facts are the core events the court examines to resolve the statutory questions: certification language exists and is sent by APAC before cancellation, but any human signature and physical “receipt” by a GoAuto employee occur only after the policy has been cancelled by GoAuto’s automated system.

## Issue 1 — “Certifying”: does the statute require a pre‑cancellation human signature or named preparer?

The statute requires the premium finance company to send “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” enumerated items. The plaintiffs argued that “certifying” necessarily implies a signature or an identifiable individual who actually signed the certification before the insurer may cancel the policy. Their position is that without a signed, employee‑identified certification in hand prior to cancellation, the cancellation process is defective under Louisiana’s strict‑compliance rule.

The panel rejected that argument. The opinion notes first that the statute does not expressly require a signature or identification of an employee; it speaks of the insurance premium finance company certifying. The Fifth Circuit surveyed Louisiana appellate decisions and found no clear precedent holding a signature is required in every case. One appellate decision had criticized a notice for lacking a signature among other defects, but it did not hold that absent a signature the cancellation must fail if the statutory elements otherwise appear. Given that silence in Louisiana law and the statutory phrasing that attributes the act of certifying to the company itself, the court concluded that Louisiana does not impose a per se requirement that an individual sign the certification before cancellation. The opinion emphasizes that courts must adhere strictly to statutory requirements but should not add requirements the legislature did not write. Here, a machine‑generated statement from the finance company that recites the statutorily required facts satisfied the “certifying” language even though the physical signature stamp was applied later.

In sum, under the court’s reading of § 9:3550(G)(3)(a), the presence of the statutorily required certification language transmitted by the finance company is what matters; a contemporaneous human signature or named preparer is not a textual prerequisite for a valid certification and thus not necessary to permit cancellation.

## Issue 2 — “Receipt”: must GoAuto have physical, human possession before cancellation?

The statute conditions the insurer’s authority to treat the policy as cancelled on “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” The plaintiffs argued “receipt” requires the insurer to take physical possession—i.e., a human at GoAuto must receive the printed, signed request—before the insurer can cancel. Because no GoAuto human had taken physical custody until after the electronic system already cancelled the policy, the plaintiffs said the statutory receipt requirement was not met and the cancellation was ineffective.

The court rejected that reading as well. It began by noting the statute does not define “receipt,” and observed that contemporary usage—reflected in Black’s Law Dictionary and common meaning—contemplates receipt as the act of taking delivery, coming into possession, or otherwise getting something from an outside source. The court accepted that a modern insurer may receive a notice electronically: the APAC e‑mail/web‑service call entered GoAuto’s systems in the early morning, and GoAuto’s listener/web service processed that incoming request and immediately executed the cancellation. The court treated GoAuto’s computer system as the insurer’s agent/instrumentality that can take possession, custody, or control of incoming communications. Because the insurer’s system had, practically and legally, “received” the APAC notice via electronic transmission before or at the time the cancellation was enacted, the statutory receipt requirement was satisfied. The fact that a printed, stamped copy and a human “received” stamp followed later did not defeat receipt that had already occurred through the insurer’s electronic systems.

The court’s reasoning is shaped by the statute’s modern context: the legislature’s words must be read against a reality in which companies routinely exchange enforceable notices electronically and rely on automated processes to act on those notices. Under the court’s interpretation, receipt need not mean contemporaneous physical possession by a named human; it is satisfied when the insurer’s systems take in the transmission and act upon it.

## Secondary district court rationale: “private carrier” theory (not reached)

The district court had adopted an alternative rationale: because APAC physically delivered printed notices to a shared filing room, an APAC employee fit the statutory phrase “private carrier,” and GoAuto therefore had constructive receipt. The Fifth Circuit declined to rely on or analyze this “private carrier” characterization because it resolved the case on the electronic‑receipt and certification issues. The panel expressly stated it would not reach the district court’s imaginative definition of “private carrier.” Thus, the “private carrier” theory is a secondary rationale that the appellate decision leaves undecided.

## Standard framing: strict statutory adherence, summary judgment posture, and the plaintiffs’ procedural position

The opinion repeatedly anchors itself to two procedural and interpretive points that govern how the court reviews the facts and law. First, Louisiana case law demands strict adherence to § 9:3550’s procedures when a finance company cancels an insurance policy by power of attorney; any defect can render a cancellation ineffective. That strictness, however, does not license courts to read additional requirements into the statute—statutory text controls. Second, this dispute was resolved on cross‑motions for summary judgment that focused solely on statutory compliance; the factual record about the automated processes and the later stamping was undisputed, so the question was legal: whether those undisputed procedures satisfied the statutory text. The plaintiffs also sought partial summary judgment on the statutory interpretation, but because the court concluded GoAuto’s procedures complied with the statute, it affirmed the district court’s denial of the plaintiffs’ motion and the grant of GoAuto’s motion.

Taken together, the workflow facts plus the court’s textualist approach produce a narrow holding: an electronically transmitted certification that contains the statute’s required statements suffices for “certifying,” and an insurer’s electronic system that receives a transmitted notice satisfies the statute’s “receipt” requirement, so long as those steps occur before or at the time the insurer cancels the policy. The opinion leaves open related questions—such as whether some other form of defect in a transmission could defeat receipt or certification—but on the record before it the Fifth Circuit concluded the APAC/GoAuto procedures complied with § 9:3550.

# Court’s Analysis: Certification Without Signature and Electronic Receipt

The Fifth Circuit’s opinion in Williams v. GoAuto Insurance turns on two interrelated questions of statutory interpretation: whether Louisiana’s premium-finance cancellation statute requires a signature on the finance company’s certification, and what it means for the insurer to have “receipt” of the finance company’s cancellation request. The court applied the statute’s text, surveyed Louisiana appellate authority, and then assessed GoAuto’s computerized cancellation process against that legal framework. This chapter explains the court’s reasoning in manageable steps so a student can see how statutory language, precedent, and modern information systems were brought together to resolve the dispute.

## No signature requirement in § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)

The statute authorizes a premium finance company to effect cancellation by “sending to the insurer . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” specified facts about the financing agreement, default, and notice to interested parties. The statute repeatedly uses “the insurance premium finance company” as the actor who must “certify.” Crucially, the statutory text is silent about any required formality for that certification—there is no express mention of a signature, a named signer, or an attestation executed by an individual.

The Fifth Circuit began from that textual silence. Because the statute speaks of the corporate actor certifying rather than of a particular employee signing, the court concluded there is no textual basis to import a signature requirement. The court emphasized a basic interpretive principle it applied here: courts should not add procedural requirements to a statute that the legislature did not include. Put differently, “strict adherence” to the statute means enforcing the statutory steps, but not creating extra ones—such as a mandatory handwritten or named signature—when the statute itself does not demand them.

## How Louisiana appellate cases treat certification and signature

The court then placed this textual analysis alongside Louisiana appellate decisions addressing the statute. Two recurring formulations in the appellate cases were (1) that the premium finance company must send “a statement certifying compliance” and (2) that cancellation may occur “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Those paraphrases—found in KMJ Services and Hodges—refer to a required “statement certifying,” but they do not indicate that the statement must be personally signed or identify an individual signer.

One appellate opinion, Benitez, did flag the absence of a signature in criticizing a defective notice—observing that the notice neither identified nor was signed by an individual and did not “purport to ‘certify’” the statements. But Benitez did not hold that the absence of a signature alone invalidated the cancellation; rather, the lack of signature was one of several infirmities noted in evaluating the notice’s overall adequacy. The Fifth Circuit read Benitez as not dispositive on the signature question and found nothing in the other appellate decisions that required importing a signature mandate.

From this survey the court drew a limited but important practical rule: Louisiana precedent requires strict compliance with the statute’s listed steps, but where the statute omits a signature requirement and the appellate cases do not compel one, courts should not read a signature into the statutory certification step.

## Holding on who may “certify”

The court’s conclusion followed directly: the statute contemplates the finance company itself doing the certifying. That corporate act can be accomplished without identifying or producing a named individual’s signature on the mailed (or sent) statement. The court emphasized that allowing a signatureless corporate certification is consistent with the statutory language and with the purpose of the statute (fixing cancellation timing and providing a cure period), and that it avoids judicially grafting an extra formalism onto the statutory scheme.

Thus, in this case APAC’s unsigned but explicitly “certifying” notice satisfied the statutory requirement for a certification by the premium finance company, at least insofar as a signature was concerned.

## Defining “receipt”: absence of statutory meaning and use of dictionary definitions

A separate but related issue was the statute’s requirement that cancellation be effective “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” The plaintiffs argued that “receipt” requires the insurer to take physical possession of the printed, signed certification before canceling the policy. The statute does not define “receipt,” and the Louisiana Supreme Court had not interpreted that term in this context.

To resolve the term, the Fifth Circuit relied on standard dictionary definitions in Black’s Law Dictionary. “Receipt” was treated as the act of receiving—especially by taking possession—and “receive” as coming into possession or getting something from an outside source. Using these ordinary meanings, the court concluded the statute demands that the insurer take possession, custody, or delivery of the finance company’s notice and statement before it may treat cancellation as requested. The key inquiry thus became whether GoAuto had taken possession or custody of APAC’s cancellation request at the time the policy was cancelled.

## Application to electronic systems: web services and automated cancellation

Modern company processes, the court noted, often substitute machine-to-machine communication for manual handoffs. The undisputed facts showed that APAC’s computer system automatically sent an e‑mail/ electronic request to GoAuto’s systems when an insured failed to cure a ten‑day notice. GoAuto’s web service functioned as an automated listener: the APAC process “calls” the web service, the web service receives the call with the cancellation information, and the service executes the cancellation. The GoAuto system therefore received APAC’s request electronically and immediately acted on it by cancelling the policy. That machine-to-machine exchange occurred before any human employee printed documents, affixed a stamp, or filed a paper copy.

Applying the ordinary meaning of “receipt,” the court held that GoAuto did “receive” the cancellation request when its computer system took custody of the electronic notice and invoked the cancellation operation. In other words, electronic transmission into a system that takes possession and acts on the request qualifies as receipt under the statute.

## No human involvement required pre-cancellation

The plaintiffs emphasized that the printed documents did not bear a signature until later that morning, when an APAC employee stamped the printed pages, and that physical filing occurred even later. The Fifth Circuit treated these later human actions as immaterial to the statutory question. Once the insurer’s system had taken custody of the electronic notice and effectuated cancellation, later signature stamping and filing did not retroactively create or defeat the statutory receipt. The court’s practical point was that the statute’s objectives—fixing the date of cancellation and preserving a cure period—are satisfied when the insurer’s system receives the finance company’s certified statement electronically and cancels in reliance on that receipt.

Thus, the absence of a pre‑cancellation human signature or physical transfer of paper did not invalidate the cancellation because the insurer had already taken possession through its automated systems.

## Declining the district court’s “private carrier” rationale as unnecessary

The district court had offered an alternative reason to uphold the cancellations: it treated the APAC employee who delivered the printed notices as a “private carrier,” which—if accepted—would make the delivery sufficient to count as receipt. The Fifth Circuit characterized that analysis as imaginative and unnecessary. Because it concluded the e‑mailed submission to GoAuto’s web service satisfied the statute’s receipt requirement, the court explicitly declined to reach the district court’s private‑carrier framing. This preserves the appellate holding narrowly: the cancellations were valid because the insurer received the certified notice electronically, not because of any contested characterization of on‑the‑ground couriers or private‑carrier status.

## Practical takeaways for reading the opinion

There are several lessons here about statutory interpretation and how courts accommodate technological change. First, when a statute prescribes a corporate actor’s act (here, the finance company “certifying”), courts will not lightly impose additional formalities absent textual or precedential support. Second, where a statute uses ordinary words like “receipt” without definition, courts often turn to ordinary or dictionary meanings to determine whether modern practices fit the statutory framework. Third, automated communications that transfer custody or possession to an insurer’s system can satisfy statutory receipt requirements unless the statute or controlling precedent requires a different form of delivery.

For students, this opinion is a clear example of (1) starting from the statute’s text, (2) checking controlling state precedent for specific glosses on statutory terms, (3) using ordinary meaning where the statute is silent, and (4) applying those meanings to concrete, contemporary business processes rather than insisting on outdated formalities.

# Disposition, Certification to State Court, and Practical Implications

This final section of the opinion resolves the case, rejects the plaintiffs’ request to certify unsettled statutory questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, and explains the practical consequences for insurers and premium finance companies. The court’s analysis ties the disposition to its statutory interpretation: because GoAuto’s cancellation process satisfied Louisiana’s premium-finance cancellation statute, the district court’s grant of summary judgment for GoAuto was correct and all related claims were properly dismissed with prejudice.

## Certification to the Louisiana Supreme Court: why the court declined

The plaintiffs asked the Fifth Circuit to certify unresolved questions of Louisiana law—principally whether the statute requires a signature on the premium finance company’s certification and what constitutes the insurer’s “receipt” of the cancellation request—to the Louisiana Supreme Court. The Fifth Circuit declined. The opinion rests on two points anchored in federal appellate practice as reflected in Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n: (1) the court will not certify merely because a state supreme court has not yet addressed a term used in a statute; novelty alone is insufficient, and (2) certification should be reserved for truly compelling circumstances to avoid overusing the procedure and thereby jeopardizing its availability as a limited resource.

In short, although some state intermediate appellate decisions had skirted the signature and receipt questions without definitive ruling, that absence of state-high-court authority did not justify certification. The Fifth Circuit concluded it could and would interpret the statute itself and applicable precedent. Because it found GoAuto’s procedures complied with the statute as written, there was no compelling need to seek the Louisiana Supreme Court’s guidance.

## Disposition: affirming summary judgment and denying plaintiffs’ motion

The court affirmed the district court’s judgment in favor of GoAuto. Central to that affirmation were two statutory determinations: first, Louisiana’s § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) does not require a signed, individually identified certification from a named employee; and second, an insurer’s “receipt” of a cancellation request may be satisfied by electronic transmission and automatic processing where the insurer’s systems take possession of the notice.

Because GoAuto’s procedures met those requirements—APAC’s communications expressly “certif[ied]” the four elements set out by the statute, and GoAuto’s automated systems received and acted on the emailed requests—GoAuto complied with the statutory cancellation scheme. The district court therefore correctly denied the plaintiffs’ motion for partial summary judgment and properly entered summary judgment for GoAuto. The Fifth Circuit’s affirmation disposed of the appeal by upholding dismissal with prejudice.

## Effect on ancillary claims (misrepresentation, breach of good faith, etc.)

The plaintiffs framed claims beyond statutory noncompliance: misrepresentation and breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. Those ancillary claims were premised on the contention that the cancellations had been ineffective—if cancellation was invalid, then conduct surrounding the cancellation could support misrepresentation or bad-faith theories. By holding the cancellations effective under § 9:3550, the court left no statutory defect on which to base those claims against GoAuto. The consequence is straightforward in this opinion: because the cancellations were valid, the related tort and contract-based claims against GoAuto failed and remain dismissed with prejudice. The opinion therefore illustrates how resolution of a threshold statutory compliance issue can moot or doom downstream claims.

## Key practical takeaways for practitioners

This opinion provides several pragmatic rules of thumb for insurers, premium finance companies, and litigators operating within Louisiana’s statutory framework:

- Signature and named preparer: The court refused to import a requirement that an individual employee sign or be identified on the certification sent to the insurer. The statute requires the premium finance company itself to “certify” the enumerated facts; a company-generated statement that expressly contains the four statutory certifications sufficed. Practitioners should therefore understand that an individual signature is not necessarily required under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a), at least in the Fifth Circuit’s view of Louisiana law.

- Electronic transmission and automated receipt: Electronic delivery of the notice and a system that automatically receives and processes that notice can constitute the insurer’s “receipt.” The court accepted that a GoAuto web service that is “listening” and acts upon APAC’s emailed request means the insurer took possession of the cancellation request before or contemporaneous with cancellation. Modern automated workflows are therefore capable of satisfying the statutory “receipt” requirement.

- Preserve evidence of the four statutory certifications and timing: Although neither a signature nor human receipt is necessary, the opinion underscores the practical importance of retaining the records that demonstrate the four statutory elements and the timing of notice and cancellation. The finance company’s statement must show (i) a valid power of attorney, (ii) an uncured default, (iii) that the required notice to the insured was sent with the date specified, and (iv) copies/recipients as required. Because Louisiana courts demand strict adherence to the statute’s text, having clear, contemporaneous electronic or printed records will be decisive in litigation.

- Time-stamping and system logs: Given that automated processes can effect cancellation, maintaining server logs, email timestamps, and system transaction records that show exactly when an insurer’s system received and acted on a cancellation request is prudent. Those records provide the factual foundation for proving “receipt” and the date of cancellation if challenged.

## Boundary of “strict adherence”: courts enforce the statute’s text but will not add requirements

A recurring theme in the opinion is the court’s endorsement of judicial enforcement of statutory text without judicial supplementation. The Fifth Circuit reiterated that Louisiana courts require strict adherence to the statute’s procedural prerequisites because the statute fixes the date of cancellation and guarantees a cure period. But strict adherence does not mean courts may invent additional requirements—such as a handwritten signature or identification of the specific employee who prepared a notice—when the statute does not demand them. The court thus balanced deference to the statute’s strictness (to protect insureds’ rights) with fidelity to the statute’s actual language (to avoid judicially importing extra-formalities).

For practitioners, this means two things. First, follow the statute’s four certification elements precisely; courts will enforce those requirements stringently. Second, don’t assume courts will impose additional formalities not found in the statutory text—especially where business processes involve automated or electronic transmission mechanisms that can reliably demonstrate compliance.

Conclusion

The Fifth Circuit affirmed because GoAuto’s automation and APAC’s company-level certification satisfied the statutory conditions for cancellation; there was no need to certify unsettled questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court; and the successful statutory cancellations disposed of all related claims against GoAuto. The opinion thus provides practical guidance: electronic workflows and company certifications can meet § 9:3550’s demands, but careful recordkeeping of the four statutory certifications and precise timing remains essential to withstand litigation.
