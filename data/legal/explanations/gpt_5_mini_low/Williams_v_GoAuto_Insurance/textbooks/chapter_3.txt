Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

Chapter 3: # The Challenged Procedures and the Legal Questions Presented

This part of the opinion sets out the concrete mechanics by which APAC and GoAuto handled financed-policy cancellations and frames the two narrow statutory interpretive disputes the plaintiffs pressed on appeal. The court’s analysis ties the factual workflow to the literal text of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(b) and the governing requirement that courts demand “strict adherence” when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel a policy. Understanding the timeline and automated steps is essential because the plaintiffs’ claims rest entirely on whether, under Louisiana law, (1) the finance company’s “certifying” must be a signed, individually identified statement completed before a cancellation becomes effective, and (2) the insurer must take physical possession by a human before it can be deemed to have “receipt” of the cancellation request.

## APAC workflow: the 10‑day notice, automated e‑mail, and automatic cancellation

The operative chain of events begins with APAC’s ten‑day cure notice to the insured: when a policyholder misses a payment, APAC sends a notice warning that the policy will be cancelled if payment is not received within ten days. If payment is not made by midnight on that tenth day, APAC’s systems automatically generate and send an email to GoAuto containing (a) a copy of the ten‑day notice of cancellation and (b) a written request for cancellation that includes a four‑part certification statement tracking the statutory elements (valid power of attorney, uncured default, notice sent to insured with date, and copies sent to interested third parties). This e‑mail is sent in the very early morning hours and is received by a GoAuto computer service.

GoAuto’s information technology is configured so that the moment the APAC e‑mail/web service call is received, GoAuto’s computer system cancels the policy automatically. No human intervenes before that automatic cancellation occurs. Thus, at the precise time the policy is terminated in GoAuto’s system, the only thing transmitted and received is the electronic notice and the certification text produced by APAC’s automated process.

## Post‑cancellation documentation: signature stamping and physical filing

Later that same morning—after the GoAuto system has already effectuated the cancellation—human steps occur to create a paper record. An APAC employee prints the cancellation documents and affixes a signature stamp to them; prior to 2017, those printed-and‑stamped documents were taken to a filing cabinet in a shared room (belonging to GoAuto Management Services), and since 2017 the signed documents are handed to a GoAuto employee who stamps them “received” and places them in a shared cabinet. In short, the signed and/or “received” physical paperwork is created and placed in shared custody only after the electronic cancellation already has taken effect.

These temporal facts are the core events the court examines to resolve the statutory questions: certification language exists and is sent by APAC before cancellation, but any human signature and physical “receipt” by a GoAuto employee occur only after the policy has been cancelled by GoAuto’s automated system.

## Issue 1 — “Certifying”: does the statute require a pre‑cancellation human signature or named preparer?

The statute requires the premium finance company to send “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” enumerated items. The plaintiffs argued that “certifying” necessarily implies a signature or an identifiable individual who actually signed the certification before the insurer may cancel the policy. Their position is that without a signed, employee‑identified certification in hand prior to cancellation, the cancellation process is defective under Louisiana’s strict‑compliance rule.

The panel rejected that argument. The opinion notes first that the statute does not expressly require a signature or identification of an employee; it speaks of the insurance premium finance company certifying. The Fifth Circuit surveyed Louisiana appellate decisions and found no clear precedent holding a signature is required in every case. One appellate decision had criticized a notice for lacking a signature among other defects, but it did not hold that absent a signature the cancellation must fail if the statutory elements otherwise appear. Given that silence in Louisiana law and the statutory phrasing that attributes the act of certifying to the company itself, the court concluded that Louisiana does not impose a per se requirement that an individual sign the certification before cancellation. The opinion emphasizes that courts must adhere strictly to statutory requirements but should not add requirements the legislature did not write. Here, a machine‑generated statement from the finance company that recites the statutorily required facts satisfied the “certifying” language even though the physical signature stamp was applied later.

In sum, under the court’s reading of § 9:3550(G)(3)(a), the presence of the statutorily required certification language transmitted by the finance company is what matters; a contemporaneous human signature or named preparer is not a textual prerequisite for a valid certification and thus not necessary to permit cancellation.

## Issue 2 — “Receipt”: must GoAuto have physical, human possession before cancellation?

The statute conditions the insurer’s authority to treat the policy as cancelled on “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” The plaintiffs argued “receipt” requires the insurer to take physical possession—i.e., a human at GoAuto must receive the printed, signed request—before the insurer can cancel. Because no GoAuto human had taken physical custody until after the electronic system already cancelled the policy, the plaintiffs said the statutory receipt requirement was not met and the cancellation was ineffective.

The court rejected that reading as well. It began by noting the statute does not define “receipt,” and observed that contemporary usage—reflected in Black’s Law Dictionary and common meaning—contemplates receipt as the act of taking delivery, coming into possession, or otherwise getting something from an outside source. The court accepted that a modern insurer may receive a notice electronically: the APAC e‑mail/web‑service call entered GoAuto’s systems in the early morning, and GoAuto’s listener/web service processed that incoming request and immediately executed the cancellation. The court treated GoAuto’s computer system as the insurer’s agent/instrumentality that can take possession, custody, or control of incoming communications. Because the insurer’s system had, practically and legally, “received” the APAC notice via electronic transmission before or at the time the cancellation was enacted, the statutory receipt requirement was satisfied. The fact that a printed, stamped copy and a human “received” stamp followed later did not defeat receipt that had already occurred through the insurer’s electronic systems.

The court’s reasoning is shaped by the statute’s modern context: the legislature’s words must be read against a reality in which companies routinely exchange enforceable notices electronically and rely on automated processes to act on those notices. Under the court’s interpretation, receipt need not mean contemporaneous physical possession by a named human; it is satisfied when the insurer’s systems take in the transmission and act upon it.

## Secondary district court rationale: “private carrier” theory (not reached)

The district court had adopted an alternative rationale: because APAC physically delivered printed notices to a shared filing room, an APAC employee fit the statutory phrase “private carrier,” and GoAuto therefore had constructive receipt. The Fifth Circuit declined to rely on or analyze this “private carrier” characterization because it resolved the case on the electronic‑receipt and certification issues. The panel expressly stated it would not reach the district court’s imaginative definition of “private carrier.” Thus, the “private carrier” theory is a secondary rationale that the appellate decision leaves undecided.

## Standard framing: strict statutory adherence, summary judgment posture, and the plaintiffs’ procedural position

The opinion repeatedly anchors itself to two procedural and interpretive points that govern how the court reviews the facts and law. First, Louisiana case law demands strict adherence to § 9:3550’s procedures when a finance company cancels an insurance policy by power of attorney; any defect can render a cancellation ineffective. That strictness, however, does not license courts to read additional requirements into the statute—statutory text controls. Second, this dispute was resolved on cross‑motions for summary judgment that focused solely on statutory compliance; the factual record about the automated processes and the later stamping was undisputed, so the question was legal: whether those undisputed procedures satisfied the statutory text. The plaintiffs also sought partial summary judgment on the statutory interpretation, but because the court concluded GoAuto’s procedures complied with the statute, it affirmed the district court’s denial of the plaintiffs’ motion and the grant of GoAuto’s motion.

Taken together, the workflow facts plus the court’s textualist approach produce a narrow holding: an electronically transmitted certification that contains the statute’s required statements suffices for “certifying,” and an insurer’s electronic system that receives a transmitted notice satisfies the statute’s “receipt” requirement, so long as those steps occur before or at the time the insurer cancels the policy. The opinion leaves open related questions—such as whether some other form of defect in a transmission could defeat receipt or certification—but on the record before it the Fifth Circuit concluded the APAC/GoAuto procedures complied with § 9:3550.
