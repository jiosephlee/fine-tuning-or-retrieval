Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural Posture

This case arises from a dispute about the legal effect of cancellations for automobile insurance policies that were financed through a third-party premium finance company. Understanding the dispute requires following three sets of actors (the insureds and claimant, the insurer, and the premium financier), the financing mechanism that gave the financier authority to cancel, the sequence of events leading to cancellation and an accident, the legal claims asserted, and the narrow procedural history that brought the parties to the Fifth Circuit on cross-motions for summary judgment.

Parties. The plaintiffs in the consolidated action were Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, each a former GoAuto insured, and Nicholas Jenkins, a third‑party claimant injured in an accident with another GoAuto insured (that insured is not a party to this appeal). The defendants were GoAuto Insurance Company (the insurer) and American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”), the company that financed the plaintiffs’ GoAuto premiums.

Premium‑finance structure and power of attorney. The financing arrangement between APAC and each insured is central. Under these agreements APAC paid GoAuto the policy premium up front; the insureds then owed installments to APAC. Critically, each premium finance agreement included a power-of-attorney provision authorizing APAC to cancel the insured’s GoAuto policy if the insured defaulted on the financing and failed to cure within the statutory cure period. The Louisiana statute at issue conditions an insurer’s ability to act on a premium financer’s cancellation request on a specified form of notice and certification from the premium finance company. The parties’ dispute focuses on whether APAC’s and GoAuto’s practices satisfied those statutory requirements.

Events leading to cancellation and the accident. In each plaintiff’s case the insured failed to make timely installment payments to APAC. APAC sent the insureds a written “Ten Day Notice of Cancellation” warning that nonpayment would result in cancellation if payment was not received within ten days. When payment was not made by the deadline, APAC’s automated processes generated an e‑mail to GoAuto containing a copy of the Ten Day Notice and a written request and certification for cancellation on APAC letterhead. Upon receipt of that e‑mail, GoAuto’s computer system automatically canceled the policy. Separately, at an early morning hour before a human reviewed paper records, GoAuto’s systems executed the cancellation once the e‑mail/web‑service request was received. Later the same morning an APAC employee stamped a printed copy of the certification, and since 2017 that printed, stamped document was handed to a GoAuto employee who marked it “received” and filed it in a shared cabinet. These timing and signature details are the facts that drove the statutory‑interpretation dispute.

Claims asserted. The plaintiffs sued GoAuto and APAC in Louisiana state court, asserting that APAC’s and GoAuto’s cancellation procedures did not effectuate lawful cancellations under the Louisiana statute governing cancellations by premium finance companies. The plaintiffs alleged that the cancellations therefore were ineffective; they also asserted related claims of misrepresentation and breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing arising from the allegedly ineffective cancellations. The legal core of the case, however, became whether the specific procedures APAC and GoAuto used complied with the statutory requirements for notice and certification.

Procedural path and posture on appeal. Williams and Jenkins initially filed a putative class action in state court; Wright was added in an amended complaint. GoAuto removed the case to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties narrowed the dispute to statutory compliance and filed cross‑motions for summary judgment confined to whether GoAuto’s cancellation procedures met Louisiana law. The district court concluded that GoAuto’s procedures complied with the statute and granted GoAuto’s summary judgment motion. That ruling resulted in dismissal of all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. The plaintiffs appealed only the district court’s statutory‑compliance rulings and the denial of their partial summary judgment; the Fifth Circuit’s review therefore is limited to the legal question whether the facts as found by the district court show compliance with the Louisiana cancellation statute.

In short, this appeal turns on whether APAC’s automated e‑mail/request and the later-stamped paper record satisfied the statutory requirements that a premium finance company “certify” certain facts and that the insurer have “receipt” of the cancellation request before cancelling the policy. The procedural posture—cross‑motions for summary judgment on statutory compliance with dismissal after the district court found compliance—frames the Court of Appeals’ task: interpret the statute, apply it to the undisputed procedures, and decide whether the district court erred.
