Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

## What Does It Mean to “Certify” Without a Signature? The Fifth Circuit’s Textual Turn in Williams v. GoAuto

Why this matters: Louisiana’s statute governing cancellations initiated by premium finance companies has long been read under a strict-compliance rubric: if the finance company fails to follow the statutory steps exactly, the cancellation can be ineffective, with real consequences for insurers, insureds, and third-party claimants. In Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, the Fifth Circuit confronted a modern procedural reality—automated notices, email transmission, and post-hoc signature stamps—and made a key textual choice about what “certifying” means. The panel’s decision rejects the plaintiffs’ argument that the statute requires a contemporaneous, handwritten (or at least individual) signature on the finance company’s certification before a cancellation becomes effective. That ruling aligns statutory language with electronic-age practices and narrows the doctrinal bite of some prior appellate dicta that flagged unsigned notices as problematic.

The court’s bottom line and the path to it: The Fifth Circuit affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to GoAuto, holding that APAC’s electronic notice and accompanying entity-level certification satisfied La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The panel’s reasoning unfolded in two linked steps. First, it treated the statutory term “certifying” as attributable to the insurance premium finance company itself rather than as an implicit command that a particular individual must sign the certification prior to cancellation. Because the statute directs that “the insurance premium finance company” effect cancellation “by sending . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” specified facts, the court saw no textual hook that converts “certifying” into a contemporaneous, signed declaration by a named person. Second, the court held that “receipt” by the insurer need not mean physical possession of a printed, signed document; an automated email and a web-service call that triggers GoAuto’s cancellation process sufficed as the insurer taking “possession, delivery, or custody” of the cancellation request.

How the court handled precedent, and where Benitez fits: The opinion squarely distinguishes the court of appeal decision in Benitez v. Elsayed, which had criticized an unsigned notice among other shortcomings. The Fifth Circuit observed that Benitez stopped short of holding that a lack of signature alone rendered a cancellation ineffective. Here, by contrast, the notice did include a certification statement that tracked the statute’s checklist, even though it did not identify or bear the autograph of a specific employee. The panel relied on that distinction: an entity can “certify” by making a clear, company-level statement of the required facts, and Louisiana precedent has not compelled courts to graft an individual-signature requirement onto the text. Put differently, Benitez’s infirmity was a constellation of faults—not signaturelessness alone—and Williams confirms that an unsigned but substantively compliant corporate certification is not per se fatal.

Electronic processes and the modern receipt question: The Fifth Circuit also embraced a commonsense response to the “receipt” issue. The plaintiffs argued the statute’s use of “receipt” requires the insurer to take actual physical possession of the cancellation request before cancelling. The court declined to adopt a rigid, paper-era view. It invoked Black’s Law Dictionary definitions and accepted that “receipt” may be satisfied when an insurer’s systems take custody of an electronic transmission in the ordinary course—that is, when the insurer’s automated listener (web service) receives the cancellation call and acts on it. Because GoAuto’s systems automatically cancelled policies when APAC’s emailed request called the web service, the panel concluded GoAuto had “receipt” within the meaning of the statute. The court expressly reversed course on the district court’s alternative reasoning that “private carrier” principles saved the cancellation; it did not reach that novel theory because the email/web-service route independently satisfied the statute.

A textualist tilt with practical consequences: The decision reflects a classic textualist move: defer to the statute’s language without adding unstated formalities (a named, handwritten signature) or clinging to anachronistic understandings of receipt. That approach reduces judicial second-guessing about which corporate formalities must accompany a premium finance cancellation, so long as the statutory content is plainly communicated to the insurer and the insurer actually receives it (even electronically). For insurers and premium finance companies, Williams offers some breathing room to rely on modern communications and automated workflows. For plaintiffs and insureds, it narrows one avenue for attacking cancellations as ineffective.

Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

1) The strict-compliance doctrine survives, but with updated content. Louisiana courts have insisted on strict adherence to § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) for a long time. Williams doesn’t undermine that principle; rather, it refines what strict adherence requires in the digital age. The statute’s checklist remains the governing baseline, but practitioners should understand that compliance can be accomplished by entity-level statements transmitted electronically—so long as the elements are present and the insurer actually receives them.

2) The decision reduces formalism as a collateral attack vector. Plaintiffs who previously sought to invalidate cancellations on the ground of a missing autograph or a clerk’s unsigned form now face a tougher uphill fight, particularly in federal courts within the Fifth Circuit. The ruling suggests courts there will ask whether the substance of the statutorily required certification appears and whether the insurer received the notice, not whether a particular individual affixed his or her signature before the automated cancellation executed.

3) Records-management, authentication, and evidentiary questions remain live. Although Williams blesses unsigned, corporate certifications in principle, practical disputes will arise over authentication and proof. The panel accepted GoAuto’s explanation of the email/web-service interaction and the contemporaneous computer event that effectuated cancellation. In closer cases—where logs are missing, timestamps are ambiguous, or the certification language diverges from the statutory checklist—fact questions about whether the insurer truly “received” the required statement will persist. Moreover, state courts in Louisiana are not bound by a federal court’s construction, and the Louisiana Supreme Court could interpret “certifying” or “receipt” differently if presented with a clean vehicle.

4) ESIGN, UETA, and the broader statutory ecosystem. Although the opinion does not explicitly rely on electronic-signature statutes such as ESIGN or UETA, its reasoning is entirely compatible with them. Those laws were designed to give electronic communications parity with paper, and Williams implicitly treats emails and automated electronic transmissions as legitimate vehicles for effecting statutorily required notices. Practitioners should anticipate that courts increasingly will treat automated transmissions and system-generated attestations as effective “deliveries” unless the statute’s text or policy signals otherwise.

Practical drafting and operational takeaways

- Mirror the statute’s checklist verbatim in the certification statement. Williams’ safe harbor hinged on the certification plainly tracking § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv). If the words are there, courts are less likely to hold the notice defective on a technicality.

- Retain contemporaneous system logs and metadata. The Fifth Circuit credited evidence about how APAC’s systems called GoAuto’s web service and how GoAuto’s listener acted. Time-stamped logs showing transmission, receipt, and system action will be invaluable proof if the cancellation’s timing or “receipt” becomes contested.

- Consider named agents and durable electronic attestations for extra insurance. Even though Williams rejects a categorical signature requirement, including a named employee or an electronic signature block (or an audit trail showing a specific operator’s authorization) can blunt arguments that the certification is amorphous or unauthenticated—useful in jurisdictions less receptive than the Fifth Circuit.

- Coordinate internal workflows and shared-file handling. The plaintiffs emphasized that APAC employees handled later stamping and filing. Clear, documented handoffs and an auditable chain of custody for cancellations reduce arguments that the insurer never truly took custody of the notice.

- Prepare rebuttal narratives for litigants: plaintiffs will still allege that automated processes produced defective cancellations where the certification is materially inaccurate, the notice deviates from the statutory text, or the insurer’s systems did not actually receive the transmission. Compliance is substantive as well as procedural.

Concluding assessment: Williams v. GoAuto is a pragmatic, text-focused opinion that updates Louisiana’s strict-compliance cancellation doctrine for an electronic age without overturning it. By treating “certifying” as an act the company may perform—and “receipt” as satisfied by electronic possession—the Fifth Circuit aligns statutory interpretation with contemporary business practices. The decision narrows one technical attack on cancellations but leaves intact the statute’s substantive safeguards: companies must still communicate the precise statutory elements, and insurers must in fact receive them. For practitioners, the ruling is a reminder that meticulous statutory mirroring, robust electronic records, and deliberate operational controls will determine whether a finance-company cancellation stands or falls.
