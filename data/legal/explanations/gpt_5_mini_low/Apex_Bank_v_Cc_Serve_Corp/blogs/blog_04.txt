Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

## TTAB Litigation in a Crowded Financial Marketplace: Building the Right Record After Apex v. CC Serve

Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a practical reminder that likelihood-of-confusion battles at the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board turn on careful record-building and consistent legal framing across the DuPont factors. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding that Apex’s planned ASPIRE BANK marks and CC Serve’s long‑registered ASPIRE mark cover highly similar services, but it vacated and remanded the Board’s treatment of two interrelated DuPont factors—six (third‑party use) and one (overall commercial impression)—because the Board narrowed the universe of third‑party marks it considered in a way inconsistent with its own finding on services. The decision is short, pragmatic, and directly relevant to brand owners and challengers in the financial-services sphere, where generic and descriptive terms (Aspire, Aspire‑formative marks) and overlapping service channels (banking, lending, payments, card programs) are common.

What the court did and why

The Federal Circuit applied familiar doctrine. It accepted the Board’s factual finding—supported by substantial evidence—that CC Serve’s “credit card services” and Apex’s identified “banking and financing services” are highly similar and partially legally identical. Having reached that conclusion under DuPont factor two, the court held that the Board erred when it tightened the scope of DuPont factor six to consider only third‑party marks used explicitly for “credit card services,” excluding third‑party ASPIR E–formative uses across other banking and financing services. Because factor six (crowding/consumer education) bears directly on the commercial strength of the prior mark, the court vacated the Board’s factor‑six analysis and—because commercial impression under factor one depends on that strength—also vacated the Board’s factor‑one analysis and remanded.

That sequence is straightforward but revealing: once the Board determined the services were highly similar, it could not then constrict the relevant marketplace for evaluating third‑party use without creating an inconsistent analytical posture. The court’s remedy was not to decide who wins; it directed the Board to consider third‑party ASPIR E uses across the full range of services the Board had already found similar, and to re‑assess commercial impression accordingly.

Analytical perspective

Two doctrinal points follow. First, DuPont is an integrated inquiry; findings on one factor can and should inform the scope of others. The Federal Circuit did not invent a new rule, but it underscored that the Board must be coherent: a broad finding of relatedness requires treating third‑party marks used on any similar services as relevant to crowding/weakness and consumer sophistication questions. Second, evidence of program‑manager or co‑branded structures (as with CC Serve’s role managing bank‑issued ASPIRE cards) will not automatically immunize a mark from being “related” to other financial services. The Board’s original conclusion that the services were highly similar defeated any narrow framing that tried to cabin relevant third‑party uses to a single subcategory.

Practical implications for brand owners and challengers

For applicants and registrants in finance and payments, the decision highlights how to shape a winning record—or defeat one.

For challengers defending older marks, start by establishing the correct universe of comparison. If you argue services are broad and encompassing, accept the logical consequence: the broader the services you claim are related, the broader the set of third‑party uses the Board may consider. That can cut both ways—broad relatedness strengthens confusion arguments but also exposes the mark to more evidence of crowding and consumer sophistication.

For applicants (or opposers challenging registrations), do not assume that a contractual or program‑manager role insulates the prior registrant’s mark from relatedness. If the registrant’s mark is used in connection with credit card issuance and account servicing, show why consumers perceive a distinct source for your banking or deposit services despite program structures—survey evidence, consumer testimony, or examples of branding practices that clearly separate the services can be decisive.

On third‑party evidence and the crowded field

Apex teaches that the Board must consider third‑party marks across all services already found similar. Practically, that means collecting and presenting a full spectrum of third‑party registrations and uses that link the contested term to any banking, lending, payments, card‑program, or account‑servicing context—not just to the hyper‑specific functional label that the opponent’s registration uses. Quantify and contextualize third‑party use. Mere counts of registrations are less persuasive than targeted evidence showing consumer exposure and market practices that teach consumers to distinguish marks (co‑branding, brand architecture, how consumers encounter services in the real world).

The decision also clarifies the role of consumer education. Courts treat crowding evidence not only as a proxy for descriptive weakness but as an indication that consumers in a crowded field will be more adept at distinguishing similarly worded marks. To persuade the Board that factor six favors narrow scope, produce evidence that consumers commonly encounter many similar marks across the relevant service types and thus have been “educated” to differentiate them—real‑world examples, marketing channels, and survey work can help.

Strategic considerations for intent‑to‑use filings and prosecution

Apex filed intent‑to‑use applications for ASPIRE BANK. The case underscores the risks when seeking registration of common or evocative terms in a field populated with prior uses. During prosecution, applicants should anticipate a robust third‑party record and take steps to shape the ID and record: specify services carefully, be ready to argue how the proposed services differ in channels of trade and consumer classes, and when possible, submit evidence showing distinctive branding or service features that reduce the likelihood of source confusion.

On the evidentiary side, don’t over‑rely on the ID Manual or dictionary definitions alone. The Board drew on dictionary definitions and the deletion of a Trademark ID Manual entry to define services; courts will accept such definitions, but real marketplace evidence—how consumers actually encounter and use services—is often dispositive in DuPont analysis. Present clear factual record on how services are marketed, who purchases them, and how the parties’ marks are used in the market.

Positioning factor 6 so it informs factor 1

The Federal Circuit’s remand illuminates a tactical lesson: litigators should present the third‑party use and crowding evidence in a way that naturally feeds into the overall commercial impression analysis. That requires two related moves: first, build the broad record of third‑party uses across all similar service categories; second, explicitly link that crowding evidence to the mark’s commercial strength and to the perception of the mark in the marketplace. Do not treat factor six as a compartmentalized sidebar; show the Board how crowding alters the commercial impression and, accordingly, the scope of protection the prior mark merits.

Bottom line

Apex v. CC Serve is a pragmatic, record‑focused decision. It does not change the law, but it insists on internal coherence: once the Board finds broad service relatedness, it must evaluate third‑party crowding across that same breadth and then re‑examine commercial impression. For practitioners in financial services—where overlapping offerings and shared terminology are the norm—the case is a clear call to marshal comprehensive third‑party evidence, tailor service identifications and market evidence carefully, and connect the dots between crowding, mark strength, and commercial impression in a way the Board cannot ignore.
