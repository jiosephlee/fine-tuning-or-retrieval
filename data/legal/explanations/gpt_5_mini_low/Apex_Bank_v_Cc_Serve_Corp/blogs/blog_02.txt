Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# What Counts as “Credit Card Services” When the ID Manual Entry Is Gone? Legacy Identifications, Fintech Structures, and Factor 2

Why this case matters: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a useful clinic on how tribunals should treat legacy identifications and industry structure when assessing relatedness under the second DuPont factor. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s conclusion that “credit card services” and a bank’s broadly worded “banking and financing services” are highly similar—even “legally identical, in part”—despite the Trademark ID Manual no longer listing “credit card services.” That holding signals that (1) old registration language can retain force when properly interpreted, (2) parsimonious parsing of industry roles (program manager vs. chartered bank) will not always inoculate a party from a finding of relatedness, and (3) applicants should be deliberate in how they describe services in intent‑to‑use filings (and challengers should marshal evidence about market realities).

What the court did: The Federal Circuit reviewed the Board’s DuPont analysis de novo as to law and for substantial evidence as to fact. It affirmed the Board’s second‑factor finding that Apex’s proposed “banking and financing services” (for an internet bank branded ASPIRE BANK) are highly similar to CC Serve’s longstanding registration for ASPIRE in connection with “credit card services.” The Board had read “credit card services” broadly to encompass issuing credit cards, processing transactions, account management (including monitoring and alerts), authorization, and counseling regarding card debt. The Board also relied on third‑party registrations and dictionary definitions showing overlap between “banking,” “finance,” and the extension of credit via card products. Apex’s argument that CC Serve only functions as a program manager for issuing banks did not persuade: the Board’s factfinding that the services overlap was supported by substantial evidence, and the Federal Circuit declined to upset that factual conclusion.

Key pieces of reasoning to keep in mind
- Legacy identification language can be construed broadly. The Board started with the text of CC Serve’s 1998 registration, filled in the meaning of “credit card services” with dictionary and documentary evidence, and treated that legacy ID as still operative despite changes in the ID Manual after the registration issued. The Federal Circuit affirmed that approach where the Board tied its construction to record evidence of what the services entail.
- Functional reality matters. The Board paid attention to how card programs are delivered in the market—issuance, processing, account servicing, authorization, and monitoring—that overlap conceptually with core banking and financing services. The fact that CC Serve is not itself a chartered bank but partners with banks did not defeat similarity when the functions could reasonably be perceived by consumers as part of the same family of financial services.
- Third‑party registrations and definitions can bridge the gap. The Board’s reliance on third‑party registrations that bundle card and banking/finance services reinforced the conclusion that consumers might view the services as emanating from a single source under one mark.

A critical gloss on the court’s approach
The Federal Circuit’s posture is properly deferential on mixed fact–law questions: it will not disturb the Board’s factual findings if supported by substantial evidence. That standard explains much of the outcome here. But the opinion also illustrates a few doctrinal lines that practitioners should note.

First, the decision reaffirms that the DuPont relatedness inquiry is elastic and driven by market realities, not rigid labels. Trademark identifications are important, but they are not dispositive in a vacuum; tribunals will look beyond four corners of the registration to industry practice and consumer perception. For registrants seeking broad protection, the upside is evident: a well‑drafted legacy registration with a broadly understood term may continue to sweep in newer forms of financial services. For applicants seeking registration in the crowded fintech space, the downside is that simply using a different corporate structure (program manager vs. issuing bank) will not necessarily create distance in the consumer’s eye.

Second, the opinion implicitly endorses a pragmatic use of dictionary definitions and third‑party registrations to flesh out old identifications when the ID Manual has moved on. The Board did not treat the deletion of the ID Manual entry as fatal; instead, it used contemporaneous definitional and marketplace evidence to read the legacy ID. That is unsurprising, but it’s a reminder that the ID Manual is a tool, not a final oracle. Courts and the TTAB will keep leaning on other objective sources when construing services—especially where technological or business‑model change has outpaced the Manual.

Third, this decision highlights a drafting and prosecution imperative: specificity (or intentional breadth). Applicants who want to avoid being shoehorned into a competitor’s prior registration should narrowly tailor their identifications to exclude services that could be viewed as overlapping (for example, carving out “credit card issuance and processing” if they do not intend to provide those functions). Conversely, registrants who wish to maximize protection should ensure that their registrations describe services in a manner that captures real‑world functions associated with their mark.

Practical implications for fintechs, banks, and program managers
- For fintech program managers who do not hold charters: your back‑office or white‑label role may still create an overlap in the eyes of consumers. Even if a fintech is contractually a non‑bank servicer, TTAB/CFCA review will treat the functional attributes—cards issued in partnership with banks, account management, transaction processing—as relevant to relatedness.
- For banks offering internet banking or white‑label brands: beware legacy registrations in related corners of the financial services market. A preexisting mark claiming “credit card services” may be read to cover broader “banking and financing” consumer impressions, so clearance searches and oppositions should explicitly address functional overlap.
- For trademark counsel preparing ITU applications: be strategic about scope. If you need room for growth, broader language might be fine, but expect pushback if the mark will sit near long‑standing registrations in adjacent financial services. If avoidance is the goal, consider expressly disclaiming or excluding categories that could trigger a DuPont conflict.
- For litigators and trademark examiners: gather functional evidence. Dictionary definitions, third‑party registrations that bundle services, and documentary proof of how services are presented to consumers are effective ways to support a finding of relatedness when the business model is mosaic rather than vertically integrated.

Limitations and open questions
Apex Bank also won partial relief: the Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s treatment of the sixth DuPont factor (number and nature of similar marks) because the Board had limited its survey to third‑party marks expressly tied to “credit card services,” while the Board had already found the parties’ services to be highly similar to broader banking/finance services. That is a useful equilibrium point: once the Board establishes a broad scope of relatedness under factor two, it must apply the same scope when assessing competitive crowding under factor six. Practically, that means a challenger who succeeds on a broad reading of services should be ready for a broader canvass of third‑party marks too—good news for defendants hoping to show weakness of a plaintiff’s mark, and a caution for plaintiffs relying on narrow surveys of third‑party uses.

Conclusion
Apex Bank v. CC Serve reinforces a simple but consequential principle in modern trademark disputes over financial services: word‑for‑word labels and corporate technicalities matter less than the services’ real‑world functions and consumer perceptions. Legacy identifications survive deletion from the ID Manual only if tribunals can anchor their meanings in evidence; and where the functions overlap—issuance, processing, authorization, and account servicing—banks and fintechs may find their marks treated as operating in the same trademark space. For practitioners, the lesson is tactical drafting and evidentiary preparation: define clearly, prove the market structure, and when you assert or attack a mark, control the narrative about what the services actually do and how consumers understand them.
