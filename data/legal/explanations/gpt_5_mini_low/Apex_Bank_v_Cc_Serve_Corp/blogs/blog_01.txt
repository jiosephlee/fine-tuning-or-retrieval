Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

## You Can’t Shrink the Marketplace Mid‑Analysis: The Federal Circuit Demands Consistent Scope Across DuPont Factors

Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25, 2025) is a tidy but important reminder that the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board cannot adopt one conception of the relevant marketplace for one DuPont factor and then narrow it when doing a different factor analysis. The Federal Circuit reversed the Board’s tendency to “toggle” scopes: after the Board found Apex’s and CC Serve’s services to be “highly similar, in fact, partially legally identical” under the second DuPont factor, it nevertheless limited its sixth‑factor inquiry about third‑party use to a smaller subset of services—credit card services—excluding marks that used “Aspire” in connection with other banking and financing services. The appeals court rejected that bifurcation, vacating the Board’s sixth‑factor and first‑factor analyses and remanding for reconsideration with a consistent scope.

What the court did

The dispute is straightforward on the facts. CC Serve owns a longstanding registration for the standard character mark ASPIRE covering credit card services; Apex, a retail bank, applied to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services (it does not issue credit cards but plans an internet‑banking brand). The Board found the services highly similar and sustained an opposition based on likelihood of confusion. On appeal, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding that the services were highly similar (the second DuPont factor), but it found legal error in the Board’s treatment of the sixth DuPont factor.

The sixth DuPont prong asks whether a mark is relatively weak because of the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods or services. That analysis often depends heavily on the universe of third‑party marks the Board considers. Here, Apex produced evidence of many third‑party uses of ASPIR(E) across the financial space. The Board, however, limited the “relevant public” to consumers of credit card services and considered only third‑party uses tied explicitly to credit card services, concluding that the evidence was not voluminous enough to show weakness.

The Federal Circuit’s core holding is simple: once the Board has determined—based on the second DuPont factor—that the parties’ services are highly similar (or partly legally identical), it cannot constrict the field when assessing third‑party use under the sixth factor to a narrower subset of services inconsistent with that earlier finding. The court relied on settled precedent that similarity for DuPont purposes does not require identity of goods or services (Olde Tyme Foods; Juice Generation) and emphasized that the Board should retain the same scope in evaluating other relevant factors. Because the sixth‑factor error could also affect the first DuPont factor (similarity of the marks in their entireties and the resulting commercial impression), the court vacated both and remanded.

Why the mismatch is more than semantics

On its face, the Board’s maneuver might look like a technical cataloging decision: “We found services similar but we’ll look narrowly at the most closely overlapping slice for third‑party use.” The Federal Circuit treats that as more substantive. DuPont is a multifactor inquiry: many of the factors interact. A factual finding that two services are highly similar is not an isolated breadcrumb; it defines the scope of who the relevant consumers are, what marketplace confusion might look like, and what third‑party uses are meaningfully probative of marketplace crowding and consumer sophistication.

If the Board is allowed to narrow scope midstream, it can undercut a robust sixth‑factor analysis by excluding evidence that bears on commercial strength. In practice that creates a risk of manipulating outcomes: adopt a broad view when the Board wants to emphasize overlap (to help an opposer on likelihood), and then adopt a narrow view when it wants to diminish evidence of crowding (to deny weakness and preserve a normal scope of protection). The Federal Circuit rejects that selective framing.

How this builds on earlier precedent

The opinion tracks comfortably with prior Federal Circuit decisions. Olde Tyme Foods and Juice Generation emphasize that similarity does not demand literal identity; marks used on related goods or services can, and often do, inform the crowding inquiry. Jack Wolfskin, by contrast, demonstrates the flip side: when third‑party evidence is truly voluminous across the relevant marketplace, it can meaningfully narrow a mark’s protection. Apex forces the Board to apply those lines consistently: if the Board’s second‑factor finding sweeps in a category of services as similar, the Board must consider third‑party uses across that same category when evaluating the sixth factor.

Practical implications for practitioners

Plaintiffs (opposers/examiners) and defendants (applicants) should take away straightforward, practice‑focused lessons.

- For applicants defending against an opposition: assemble as broad a third‑party record as the services overlap allows. If the Board or PTO has already indicated the services are related or overlapping, submit third‑party registrations and evidence across the entire overlapping service field (e.g., “credit cards,” “banking,” and “financing”), not just the most restrictive label. That broader record will pressure the Board to recognize crowding and potential weakness.

- For opposers trying to prove likelihood of confusion: be prepared for the Board to face scrutiny if it both finds services similar and then narrows the third‑party universe. If you argue that services overlap, explain why third‑party evidence outside a narrow label is irrelevant or genuinely not probative of crowding (for instance, differences in consumer class, distribution channels, or functional distinctions). Don’t assume the Board can safely ignore broader evidence once it has made a similarity finding.

- For both sides presenting evidence: frame your exhibits with consistent marketplace definitions. Provide declarations or industry evidence regarding the relevant consumer cohorts, channels of trade, and how services are marketed together. That helps the Board anchor its scope and reduces opportunities for inconsistent treatment.

Downstream doctrinal effects

Apex sharpens the requirement that DuPont factor analyses be coherent and factually consistent. The opinion does not alter the flexible nature of DuPont—boards and courts still choose which factors are “relevant and of record”—but it insists that factfindings used to delimit the market carry through to later factors that depend on that market definition. Expect appellants to raise (and the Federal Circuit to scrutinize) any decisions where the Board’s market definition varies factor‑by‑factor without articulation.

Two likely follow‑on developments: first, the Board will be more careful to define the “relevant public” and the market early in its opinions and then use that definition consistently across factors, expressly explaining any departures. Second, litigants will push for broader third‑party lists where goods and services overlap and will contest exclusions more aggressively. We may also see a modest uptick in remands where the Board’s factor analyses employ inconsistent frames.

Conclusion

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a useful corrective: DuPont is not a menu where the Board can pick different marketplaces to suit different factors. Once the Board embraces a factual finding about similarity, that finding should inform other inquiries that hinge on the contours of the marketplace. The decision favors principled coherence over opportunistic framing and gives practitioners a clear path: if services overlap, assemble and press third‑party evidence across the whole overlapping field, and respond swiftly when the Board’s analyses stray from a consistent market definition.
