Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Strength Bleeds Into Similarity: Why Vacating Factor 6 Forced a Do‑Over on Factor 1

Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a useful reminder that trademark law doesn’t analyze DuPont factors in isolation. The Federal Circuit’s decision to affirm the Board on the services-similarity question (DuPont factor 2) but to vacate its treatment of the crowded-field evidence (factor 6) — and, critically, to remand factor 1 (similarity of the marks) as well — underscores a simple but often overlooked point: commercial strength and marketplace context are integral to how similar two marks will be perceived by consumers. Brand owners and challengers alike should heed the court’s message: evidence that a mark like ASPIRE is used broadly in a category can compress the scope of protection and materially alter the overall commercial impression of allegedly conflicting marks.

What the panel did and why

At the heart of the dispute was Apex’s attempt to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services against CC Serve’s earlier registration for the plain word ASPIRE covering credit card services. The Board found the parties’ services highly similar — even “legally identical, in part” — and so concluded confusion was likely. Apex appealed. The Federal Circuit affirmed that finding (factor 2) on substantial-evidence grounds, but it found two discrete errors in the Board’s treatment of other DuPont factors.

First, the Board’s factor 6 analysis — which asks whether the field is crowded with similar marks — improperly limited the universe of third-party uses to marks tied expressly to “credit card services,” excluding numerous third‑party uses of ASPIRE in other banking and financing contexts. Yet the Board itself had concluded the parties’ services were highly similar. The court held that, once services are found similar or overlapping, the Board must cast the net for third‑party marks across that same scope. Put differently, you cannot say the services are broad and overlapping for factor 2, then constrict the relevant market for factor 6.

Second, because factor 6 bears on a mark’s commercial strength (crowded fields tend to weaken a term’s source-identifying power), the court reasoned that a re-evaluation of the field could change the commercial impression consumers take from the marks. And commercial impression is the analytic core of factor 1 (similarity in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression). For that reason, vacating factor 6 compelled a do‑over on factor 1. The court remanded so the Board could reexamine the number and nature of third‑party ASPIRE uses across the full field of banking and financing services and then reassess the marks’ overall similarity in light of any revised view of ASPIRE’s strength.

Doctrinal implications: strength and similarity are woven together

The Federal Circuit’s remand reflects two intertwined doctrinal points that practitioners should internalize.

First, factor 6 is not an isolated tally of others using a word; it’s probative on the mark’s commercial strength — how source‑identifying the mark actually is in the marketplace. A term shown to be widely used in a category bears less distinctiveness in consumers’ eyes, which naturally narrows the scope of protection. That narrowed scope changes how much weight should be given to resemblance under factor 1. A weak, descriptive or widely used term will tolerate greater variation before confusion is likely; a strong, arbitrary or fanciful one will not.

Second, the opinion emphasizes consistency across factors. Once the Board takes a position about the breadth or identity of the relevant services (as it did here for factor 2), it should apply that same scope when considering third‑party uses for factor 6. The court refused to let a party have it both ways: broad, overlapping services to support confusion, but a narrow third‑party universe to deny evidence of generic or common usage. This is a commonsense application of the DuPont framework, but it is one that Boards and lawyers sometimes mistake in practice.

Practical lessons for advocates on remand

For challengers (owners of earlier marks): if you claim your mark is strong and entitled to broad protection, be prepared to defend that assertion with robust evidence that the term is unusual, fanciful, or commercially strong across the entire class of services the Board has found overlapping. Relying solely on registrations or uses narrowly focused on a subset of services is risky when the Board has already characterized the services more broadly.

For applicants (those seeking registration): if the challenged mark appears common in the field, assemble and present comprehensive, well-documented third‑party use evidence spanning all similar services the Board finds relevant. The Federal Circuit made clear that excluding uses because they are not word-for-word within a narrower subcategory (e.g., credit cards only) is error when the parties’ services overlap more broadly. On remand, that evidence could materially undercut a likelihood-of-confusion finding by showing ASPIRE is a crowded sign in banking and financing as a whole.

Specific forms of evidence to emphasize on remand include: contemporaneous commercial uses (websites, product names, program names), registrations covering a range of banking/finance services (not just credit cards), advertising materials showing how the word is used in market, and consumer-facing materials that illustrate how the public encounters ASPIRE‑formative names across different financial services. Demonstrating consumer sophistication or differentiation cues across contexts (how consumers actually distinguish brands in banking/finance) can also help calibrate both strength and commercial impression.

Broader downstream effects

Apex Bank v. CC Serve is likely to be cited as a tidy example of the interdependence of DuPont factors. Trademark examiners, TTAB judges, and litigators should expect courts to scrutinize any inconsistent scope choices across factors — for instance, finding wide service overlap to support confusion while simultaneously narrowing the field of third‑party uses to avoid finding weakness. The decision also reinscribes a practical reality: crowded fields dilute protection. In crowded categories like banking and finance — where many players favor aspirational, descriptive, or laudatory terms — it will be harder for any single user to claim exclusive rights absent strong evidence of distinctiveness or substantially different trade channels or consumer perceptions.

Conclusion

The Federal Circuit didn’t create new law in Apex Bank, but it did apply existing DuPont principles in a way that sharpens the lesson for practitioners: strength and similarity are not siloed inquiries. Evidence that ASPIRE is ubiquitous across banking and financing services can narrow the term’s protective reach and change how close ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK feel to consumers. On remand, the fate of Apex’s registrations will turn on whether the Board — now considering the full field of relevant third‑party uses — finds ASPIRE sufficiently weak that the addition of “BANK” and the design elements avoid likely confusion. Whatever the outcome, the decision is a practical roadmap: if you want a reliable resolution on likelihood of confusion, build your record to cover the full competitive field the Board has already acknowledged.
