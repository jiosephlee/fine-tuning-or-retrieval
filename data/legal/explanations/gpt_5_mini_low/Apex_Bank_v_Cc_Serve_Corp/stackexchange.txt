Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Why does the Federal Circuit review DuPont factor findings for “substantial evidence” but the ultimate likelihood-of-confusion question de novo?
Question:
The opinion says each DuPont factor is a fact question reviewed for substantial evidence, but likelihood of confusion is a legal question reviewed de novo. I'm struggling to understand where the line is drawn. For example, when the Board decides the services are “highly similar,” is that a factual finding insulated by substantial evidence review, while the weight that similarity gets in the overall confusion analysis is legal? How does this framework affect what arguments can win on appeal from the TTAB?
Answer:
Short answer
The Federal Circuit treats the DuPont factors as fact questions (reviewed for substantial evidence) because they are the underlying factual findings about the marks, goods/services, marketplace, etc. But the ultimate determination—“is likelihood of confusion established?”—is a legal conclusion that the court makes by applying the law to those factual findings and legal standards, so it is reviewed de novo. Apex Bank illustrates both points: the court affirmed the Board’s factual finding that the services were “highly similar” (substantial evidence), but it reversed/vacated parts of the Board’s legal analysis where the Board applied an incorrect scope/standard under the sixth factor (legal error) and remanded for reconsideration of related legal conclusions.

Why the Court draws the line this way
- DuPont factors = facts. As the opinion quotes, “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence” (citing Bose and M2 Software). Those findings—what the services actually are, what third-party use shows, how consumers perceive the goods, etc.—are factual determinations of the Board.
- Likelihood = law. The court treats “likelihood of confusion” as a legal question that “is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors” (M2 Software language quoted in the opinion). That makes the ultimate balance and legal conclusion a matter for de novo review.

How that plays out in practice (useful boundaries)
- If you challenge a factual finding (e.g., the Board’s finding that services are “highly similar”), you must show the finding lacks substantial evidence—i.e., no reasonable mind could accept the record the way the Board did. In Apex the court found substantial evidence supported the Board’s second-factor finding and affirmed it.
- If you challenge the Board’s legal application of the facts (e.g., it applied the wrong legal scope, ignored a necessary legal principle, or made inconsistent legal inferences from its facts), you argue legal error subject to de novo review. In Apex the Board limited the universe of third-party “Aspire” uses to credit-card services only, despite having found the services highly similar; the Federal Circuit held that narrowing was a legal error and vacated the sixth-factor analysis (and, because that error could affect commercial impression, also remanded the first factor).
- Mixed issues: sometimes a factual finding controls how broad a legal inquiry must be. If the Board’s facts require a certain legal scope (as here—the Board found services highly similar, so it should have considered similar marks beyond only credit-card uses), failing to follow that is a legal mistake even if the factual finding itself survives substantial-evidence review.

What arguments are most likely to succeed on appeal
- Attack factual insufficiency (substantial-evidence): show the record does not support the Board’s factual finding (contradictory evidence, gaps in the record, or improper reliance on extraneous materials).
- Attack legal error in applying the DuPont framework: show the Board applied the wrong legal standard, defined the universe too narrowly or inconsistently with its own findings, failed to consider a relevant factor, or misapplied precedent. Apex succeeded on this ground for the sixth factor.
- Preserve the record: because fact findings are hard to overturn under substantial-evidence review, build a robust factual record at the Board (evidence, testimony, third-party use, market surveys); and, separately, develop clear legal argumentation about how the Board should apply those facts to the DuPont framework.

Bottom line
Treat the Board’s DuPont determinations as two layers: (1) factual findings (substantial-evidence review) about marks, goods, consumers, and third-party use; and (2) the legal judgment—balancing those factual findings into the ultimate likelihood-of-confusion conclusion—which the Federal Circuit reviews de novo. Apex Bank is a good model: factual finding of high similarity affirmed, but legal error in how that fact was used under the sixth factor led to vacatur and remand.

### How should the sixth DuPont factor’s “similar goods” be defined when the second factor already found services to be highly similar or partially legally identical?
Question:
The court faults the Board for narrowing the sixth factor to only credit card services, given it had already found Apex’s ‘banking and financing services’ highly similar and partially legally identical to CC Serve’s ‘credit card services’ under the second factor. Do courts require the scope of ‘similar goods/services’ for third-party-use evidence (factor six) to match the scope used for relatedness (factor two)? Are there limits to how broadly you can cast ‘similar goods’ under factor six, and how do Olde Tyme Foods, Juice Generation, and Jack Wolfskin guide that line-drawing?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — when the Board has already found the parties’ services to be highly similar (or partially legally identical) under DuPont factor two, the Board must ordinarily use that same scope when deciding which third‑party “similar marks” are relevant under DuPont factor six. The Federal Circuit in Apex Bank v. CC Serve held the Board’s narrowing to only “credit card services” was legal error: factor six requires consideration of marks used on similar (not identical) goods/services, and the Board had already found the broader category (banking and financing services) “highly similar.” Olde Tyme Foods and Juice Generation support that similar (not identical) uses are probative; Jack Wolfskin shows that the quantity/nature of the third‑party uses matters (you need a sufficient showing of crowding/use to prove weakness). But the Board may narrow the universe if the record supports a different consumer group or meaningful distinctions — it must explain that factual basis.

Relevant doctrine and the court’s reasoning
- DuPont factor two asks whether the goods/services are related; they need not be identical — only related or marketed in a way that could cause source confusion. The Board here found Apex’s “banking and financing services” highly similar (partially legally identical) to CC Serve’s “credit card services.” Apex Bank, J.A. 27–28; opinion § III.A (affirming factor two).
- DuPont factor six examines “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” Third‑party use can show a mark is commercially weak and entitled to narrower protection. Apex Bank, § III.B (citing DuPont; Palm Bay; Juice Generation; Jack Wolfskin).
- The court held the Board erred by defining the relevant third‑party universe more narrowly for factor six than it had for factor two. Once the Board found the services highly similar, there was no reason to impose a stricter similarity test for factor six. Apex Bank, § III.B (“When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar … the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors.”).

How Olde Tyme Foods, Juice Generation, and Jack Wolfskin guide the line‑drawing
- Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200 (Fed. Cir. 1992): confirms factor six does not require identical goods — evidence of similarity across related goods can be probative. Apex Bank relies on Olde Tyme to say the Board may look beyond literally identical identifications. Apex Bank, § III.B (citing Olde Tyme).
- Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334 (Fed. Cir. 2015): treats a substantial number of third‑party marks across related goods as evidence the field is “crowded” and consumers are accustomed to distinguishing similar marks. Apex Bank cites Juice Generation to show the relevance of related‑goods evidence and that a “considerable” number of third‑party uses can show weakness. Apex Bank, § III.B (citing Juice Generation).
- Jack Wolfskin Ausrüstung Fur Draussen GmbH v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363 (Fed. Cir. 2015): emphasizes that the court looks to the quantity and nature of third‑party registrations/uses — “voluminous” or “ubiquitous” evidence is persuasive, while a handful of isolated uses is not. The Apex court cites Jack Wolfskin for the proposition that the degree of third‑party use (not merely its existence) determines whether a mark’s strength is reduced. Apex Bank, § III.B (citing Jack Wolfskin).

Practical limits and what the Board must do on remand
- Consistency: If the Board’s factor‑two finding covers a class of services (here, banking & financing), it should normally include third‑party uses across that class when evaluating factor six. It cannot pick a narrower subset (credit cards only) without factual justification.
- Permissible narrowing: The Board may legitimately focus on a narrower universe when the record supports a different “relevant public” or meaningful functional/market distinctions (e.g., different distribution channels, different consumer sophistication, or services marketed under vastly different trade channels). But that narrowing must be supported and explained with evidence — it cannot be imposed as a legal rule.
- Qualitative and quantitative assessment: Even when broader third‑party uses are considered, the Board must evaluate the number, geographic extent, and nature of those uses. Is the use widespread/longstanding? Are the marks used in a way that would educate consumers to distinguish? Jack Wolfskin and Juice Generation show that isolated or thin uses will not prove weakness, but a considerable showing can.
- Relevance to consumer perception: The Board should tie its factor‑six analysis back to the consumers identified in factor two: do consumers of banking/financing services encounter and distinguish the third‑party “Aspire” uses? The Apex court vacated because the Board failed to align these considerations. Apex Bank, § III.B.

Bottom line for the Board (and practitioners)
- On remand the Board must (1) use the same scope of services underlying its factor‑two finding (banking & financing) when deciding which third‑party “Aspire” uses are relevant under factor six, unless the record gives a solid, explained reason to do otherwise; (2) consider all third‑party uses that fall within that scope; and (3) weigh the quantity and nature of those uses (how “considerable” or “ubiquitous” they are) before deciding whether CC Serve’s mark is commercially weak. See Apex Bank § III.B; Olde Tyme; Juice Generation; Jack Wolfskin.

Short example of application to these facts
- Board should consider the 42 Aspire uses Apex says exist within the broader banking/finance space, not only the 9 limited to credit‑card labels, and then decide whether that broader showing demonstrates a crowded field sufficient to narrow CC Serve’s protection under Jack Wolfskin/Juice Generation standards.

This is essentially what the Federal Circuit directed in Apex Bank v. CC Serve.

### What evidentiary showing is needed under factor six to prove a mark is commercially weak—are registrations enough, or is marketplace use required?
Question:
The Board counted nine Aspire-formative marks for credit card services and said that wasn’t ‘considerable’ compared to other cases with 14 or 26 examples. On remand, if Apex can include 42 additional Aspire uses in broader banking/finance, how does the Board weigh registrations versus actual use? Does the Federal Circuit require proof of marketplace use to show consumer conditioning, or can a critical mass of third-party registrations alone demonstrate conceptual weakness?
Answer:
Short answer
The Federal Circuit in Apex says the Board must consider all third‑party marks used on “similar” goods or services (as defined by its finding on the second DuPont factor). It did not adopt a bright‑line rule that registrations alone either suffice or are categorically insufficient. Rather, third‑party registrations and third‑party uses are both relevant evidence of a mark’s relative weakness; the Board must weigh the number and nature of those marks (and any evidence of marketplace use/consumer conditioning) in light of the relevant marketplace.

What the opinion actually holds and why it matters
- The sixth DuPont factor looks to “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” The opinion (citing Palm Bay, Juice Generation, Jack Wolfskin) reiterates that third‑party use/registration evidence is relevant to show a mark is relatively weak and entitled to a narrower scope of protection. Apex, slip op. at Sections III.B (pp. 6–8).  
- The court’s error was not that the Board relied on registrations, but that the Board artificially limited the universe of third‑party marks to only those labeled “credit card services” even though the Board had already found the parties’ services to be highly similar (partially legally identical) to broader banking/finance services. Because the Board itself found high similarity under DuPont factor two, it had to use the same scope when assessing factor six. Apex, slip op. at 7–8.  
- The opinion observes the Board counted nine Aspire marks for credit‑card services and compared that to other cases (14, 26 marks) but found nine not “considerable.” The court did not endorse a numerical cutoff; it vacated and remanded for a proper scope analysis and reevaluation of the weight to be given the third‑party evidence. Apex, slip op. at 7 (discussing the Board’s reliance on nine examples and comparing prior cases).

What you should take from it for the remand
- Registrations and asserted third‑party uses are both admissible and probative; the Board may consider both. The opinion treats the submitted exhibits (which included third‑party uses and registrations) as relevant evidence. Apex, slip op. at 6–7.  
- But the Board must apply a consistent scope of “similar goods/services”: because it already found the parties’ services highly similar, it must consider third‑party Aspire marks used in the broader banking/finance space (the 42 additional Aspire uses Apex seeks to introduce) when reassessing factor six. Apex, slip op. at 7–8.  
- The strength of the evidence will turn on quality and quantity: how many third‑party marks exist within the properly defined market, whether they show actual marketplace use (not merely filed registrations), and whether that use indicates consumer “conditioning” to distinguish among similar marks. The opinion cites cases where voluminous registration/use evidence and “notable examples” supported weakness findings, but it does not require proof of consumer conditioning in every case; rather, the Board must weigh the evidence as part of the factor analysis. Apex, slip op. at 6–7 (discussing Jack Wolfskin and Juice Generation comparisons).

Practical implication for Apex on remand
- Present the full set of third‑party evidence within the scope the Board must use (banking/finance as well as credit cards).  
- Emphasize actual marketplace use and any evidence of consumer recognition/distinction (websites, advertising, product offerings, length and geographic spread of use), because such evidence tends to be more persuasive of consumer conditioning than registrations alone. The opinion signals that both registrations and use were considered in prior cases, and that “voluminous evidence” of use/registration has been persuasive (Jack Wolfskin, Juice Generation), but it leaves fact‑dependent weight for the Board to decide on remand. Apex, slip op. at 6–7.

Bottom line
Registrations can be counted, but they are not automatically dispositive. The Board must consider both registrations and real‑world use within the properly defined market (here, broader banking/finance) and weigh whether the quantity and nature of those marks demonstrate commercial weakness/consumer conditioning sufficient to narrow CC Serve’s mark. The Federal Circuit remanded so the Board can make that fact‑intensive assessment using the correct scope.

### Was it proper for the Board to treat ‘credit card services’ and ‘banking and financing services’ as legally identical in part, especially given the deletion of ‘credit card services’ from the ID Manual?
Question:
The Board defined the legacy ‘credit card services’ identification to include issuing cards, processing, authorization, account management, etc., and then used dictionary definitions of ‘banking’ and ‘finance’ to find partial legal identity with Apex’s services. Is this consistent with TTAB precedent on construing older IDs when the ID Manual has changed? What sources can the Board rely on to interpret an existing registration’s scope, and how much overlap is necessary to say services are ‘legally identical, in part’?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — insofar as the Board interpreted the older identification of “credit card services” to include card issuance, processing, authorization, account management, etc., and then relied on ordinary meaning (dictionary definitions) and evidence of third‑party registrations/uses to show that “banking” and “finance” encompass those activities, that approach is consistent with the controlling principles the Federal Circuit applied in this case. The court affirmed the Board’s second‑DuPont holding that the services are “highly similar” and “legally identical, in part,” because the Board (1) construed the legacy ID, (2) showed overlap between that construction and Apex’s listed “banking and financing services” using dictionary definitions, and (3) supported the finding with third‑party registrations and use. The court treated that finding as supported by substantial evidence, while remanding only because the Board then misapplied a narrower scope when considering the sixth and first DuPont factors.

What legal doctrine the Board applied
- Construction of an existing identification: When an older registration’s ID (here, “credit card services”) no longer appears in the ID Manual, the Board must determine what the registration’s own identification means. The Board may look to the language of the registration itself and other evidence of ordinary meaning. In this opinion the Board expressly defined “credit card services” to include issuing cards, processing payments, authorization, account management, monitoring/alerts, credit counseling, and the like. The court accepted that construction as the starting point for comparing services.
- Relatedness vs. identity: The DuPont/likelihood‑of‑confusion framework does not require literal identity of services. The services need only be related such that consumers could reasonably believe they come from the same source. The Board found, and the court affirmed, that some of the activities encompassed by “credit card services” overlap with activities encompassed by “banking and financing services,” so the services are “legally identical, in part.”
- Substantial‑evidence review: The court reviewed the Board’s factual findings for substantial evidence. It concluded the Board’s conclusion that the services are highly similar was supported by substantial evidence and acceptable legal reasoning. The court did not disturb that part of the Board’s analysis.

What sources the Board can rely on (as shown in the opinion)
- The registration’s own identification (the wording of the registered services). The Board here started by construing the legacy ID itself.
- Dictionary definitions or other extrinsic evidence of ordinary meaning. The Board used dictionary definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” to show that those terms can include extending credit and issuing credit instruments.
- Third‑party registrations and evidence of third‑party use. The Board relied on registrations that covered both credit card services and banking/finance services to show relatedness and that the services may emanate from a single source under one mark.
The opinion explicitly cites all three sources in its second‑DuPont analysis (J.A. 24–35) and affirms the Board’s reliance on them as adequate support for finding overlap.

How much overlap is necessary to call services “legally identical, in part”
- The touchstone is whether there is an actual overlap in the services described such that an ordinary consumer could believe the services emanate from the same source. Literal identity of all services is not required. Partial legal identity exists when at least some of the listed activities in the registrations are the same or one description necessarily encompasses activities in the other description (here: issuance/extension of credit via card → falls within “banking/finance”).
- Practically, the Board must identify the overlapping activity(ies) and show (with its construction, definitions, and evidence) that those activities are shared. The court upheld the Board’s finding because the Board defined “credit card services” to include card issuance/credit functions and showed that “banking/finance” as commonly understood also covers extending credit and issuance of credit instruments. That factual showing satisfied the requirement for partial legal identity under DuPont.
- The level of overlap required is ultimately a fact question: enough overlap to create a reasonable likelihood of source confusion for the relevant consumers. The court’s review is limited to whether substantial evidence supports that factual conclusion.

Practical implications from the opinion
- If you are defending or attacking an older registration whose ID no longer appears in the current ID Manual, be prepared to (a) show what the registration’s own identification reasonably describes, and (b) show how ordinary meanings and marketplace evidence connect that description to the challenger’s listed goods/services.
- Third‑party registrations and use are appropriate to show the practical marketplace overlap, but the Board must be consistent about scope across DuPont factors. The court faulted the Board only for narrowing the universe of third‑party marks under factor six despite having found broad similarity under factor two (hence the vacatur/remand on factors six and one).

Conclusion
The Board’s method—construing the legacy ID, using dictionary definitions to show overlapping meanings, and supporting the overlap with third‑party registrations/use—was legally proper and is what the Federal Circuit affirmed for the second DuPont factor in this case. The key is a supported factual showing that at least some of the services actually described in the older registration are encompassed by the applicant’s descriptions so that consumers could confuse the sources.

### How does commercial strength under factor six affect the first DuPont factor’s ‘commercial impression’ analysis of the marks themselves?
Question:
The court vacated the first factor because revisiting factor six might change the mark’s commercial strength and thus the overall commercial impression. I thought the first factor focuses on appearance, sound, and connotation of the marks. In practice, how does the perceived strength/weakness of the cited mark change the first-factor analysis? Is strength normally treated under factor six only, or does it permeate factor one as part of ‘commercial impression’?
Answer:
Short answer
Factor six (third‑party use/registrations) normally is where the Board assesses commercial strength (showing whether a mark is relatively weak and entitled to a narrow scope of protection). But strength is not hermetically sealed off from factor one. The “commercial impression” inquiry under DuPont factor one includes how the public perceives the mark, and evidence that the mark is weak or crowded under factor six can (and routinely does) affect how a court or the Board evaluates the overall commercial impression and the weight to give similarities between marks. That is exactly why the Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s factor‑one ruling here: reconsidering factor six might change the mark’s commercial strength and thus the overall commercial impression. (Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., slip op. § III.C.)

Why and how the two factors interact (doctrinally)
- Factor one asks whether the marks are similar in appearance, sound, connotation, and overall commercial impression. It is an overall, perceptual comparison focused on source confusion. (DuPont; In re I.AM.Symbolic cited in the opinion, § III.C.)
- Factor six tests the field for third‑party use of similar marks on similar goods/services and is the primary way to show a mark is commercially weak (crowded field). The opinion cites the standard that third‑party use is “relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” (Apex § III.B; citing Palm Bay, Juice Generation, Jack Wolfskin.)
- Those two inquiries overlap in practice because “commercial impression” is not just a morphological test of letters and design; it includes how consumers actually perceive the mark in the marketplace. If factor six shows the term is widely used in the relevant market, consumers are more likely to distinguish similar marks and attribute less source‑identifying force to the cited mark. That changes the practical import of similarities identified under factor one.

What the opinion says (grounded points)
- The court expressly vacated factor one because “reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression.” (Apex § III.C.)
- The court also explains factor six’s role in showing weakness: “Evidence of third‑party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” (Apex § III.B.)
- The Board’s error was narrowing the universe of third‑party marks contrary to its own finding that the services were highly similar; because the sixth‑factor analysis was flawed, the Board could not be sure its factor‑one commercial‑impression conclusion was correct. Hence the remand.

Practical application for a practitioner or student
- When you do a DuPont analysis, evaluate factor one on its own terms (appearance, sound, connotation, overall impression), but do not stop there. If there is evidence under factor six that the term is common in the relevant market, explicitly state how that affects the commercial impression: e.g., “Although the marks share X, third‑party use has diluted the cited mark’s source‑identifying power, so the shared element is less likely to cause confusion.”
- If factor six shows a crowded field, courts will give the cited mark a narrower scope of protection; small similarities matter less. Conversely, if factor six shows few or no third‑party users, the cited mark retains normal or broad protection and even modest similarities can weigh heavily under factor one.
- Be careful to use the same relevant market/scope across factors. The Federal Circuit faulted the Board for using a narrower comparison under factor six than it used under factor two; that inconsistency can force a remand because it directly bears on commercial strength and thus on factor one.

Bottom line
Strength is primarily analyzed under factor six, but it legitimately “permeates” factor one because the public’s commercial impression of a mark depends on how strong or crowded that mark is in the marketplace. The Apex opinion illustrates that if factor six is reexamined and the mark’s strength changes, the Board must revisit factor one because the commercial impression (and the weight of similarities) may change.

### Does CC Serve’s role as a program manager partnering with issuing banks matter for the relatedness of services under factor two?
Question:
Apex argued CC Serve doesn’t ‘provide banking services’ but partners with banks to manage ASPIRE credit card programs. The court called that unavailing. In likelihood-of-confusion analysis, does it matter that the opposer is not itself a bank if it offers card-related services closely tied to banking? How do tribunals treat distinctions between issuers, servicers, and program managers when the identifications of services—and consumer perception of source—overlap?
Answer:
Short answer: No — CC Serve’s label as a “program manager” or partner to issuing banks does not insulate its services from being deemed related to a bank’s “banking and financing services” under DuPont factor two if the actual identifications and marketplace facts show close overlap or that consumers could perceive a common source. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s conclusion that the services were “highly similar” even though CC Serve works with a bank to issue ASPIRE cards.

Why (doctrinally and as the opinion applies it)
- Factor two asks whether the parties’ goods/services are related in the sense that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs., cited at slip op. § III.A. The services need not be identical.
- The court relied on the parties’ identifications and record evidence. It accepted the Board’s definition of “credit card services” to include issuing cards, processing transactions, managing accounts, etc., and concluded those activities overlap with the ordinary meanings of “banking” and “finance” (which encompass extending credit and issuing cards). Slip op. § III.A, J.A.25–28.
- Apex’s characterization of CC Serve as merely “partnering with banks to manage credit card programs” was “unavailing” because the Board had examined the actual descriptions and marketplace evidence and reasonably found a high degree of similarity. Slip op. § III.A.

How tribunals treat issuer/servicer/program-manager distinctions in practice (per the opinion)
- Tribunals look to the identifications of services and to how the services are actually presented and used in the marketplace, not merely to internal corporate labels. Here the Board considered the services CC Serve actually provides (e.g., issuance-related activity, account servicing) and found overlap with Apex’s banking services. Slip op. § III.A.
- When the identifications and evidence show functional overlap (issuance, account management, transaction processing), tribunals will treat those services as related for likelihood-of-confusion purposes even if an issuing bank formally holds the loan/obligation. The court affirmed that such overlap can make services “legally identical, in part.” Slip op. § III.A, J.A.27–28.
- Relatedness analysis is driven by consumer perception and the potential for source confusion. The Board relied on dictionary definitions and third‑party registrations to show the services are of a type that “may emanate from a single source under one mark.” Slip op. § III.A, J.A.33–34.

Practical takeaways for argument
- Don’t rely solely on contractual/structural labels (issuer vs. servicer vs. program manager). Emphasize (or counter) the actual activities identified in the application/registration and evidence of how consumers encounter the services.
- If you want to narrow relatedness, show record evidence that consumers distinguish the entities (e.g., clear branding, disclosures, separate marketing channels) or that the functional activities do not overlap with the applicant’s identified services.
- Conversely, if the record shows CC Serve’s role includes issuing-branded cards, account servicing, authorization/processing, etc., expect tribunals to treat those services as related to banking/finance for DuPont factor two, as the Federal Circuit affirmed here.

Finally, note that the court’s rejection of Apex’s label-based argument was limited to the record and identifications before it: because the Board found the services highly similar, the court affirmed factor two but remanded other factors (1 and 6) for reconsideration in light of that finding. Slip op. §§ III.A–C.

### On remand, is the Board limited to reconsidering only factors one and six, and can it take new evidence or must it decide on the existing record?
Question:
The Federal Circuit affirmed the second factor but vacated and remanded as to the sixth and first factors. Procedurally, is the TTAB confined to the current record when reevaluating third-party use and commercial impression, or can it reopen the record for additional evidence? Also, is the Board precluded from revisiting the second factor’s relatedness findings, or can new sixth-factor analysis indirectly affect how much weight it gives other factors?
Answer:
Short answer
- The Federal Circuit limited its substantive remand to DuPont factors six and one (vacating those findings and affirming factor two). The Board must reassess the sixth-factor evidence (and then reexamine the first-factor commercial impression) in light of its earlier, affirmed finding that the parties’ services are highly similar. See opinion at III.B–C (vacating sixth and first; affirming second).
- The opinion does not rigidly confine the Board to the existing evidentiary record. On remand the Board may take whatever procedural steps are appropriate (including, if it chooses, reopening the record to admit additional evidence relevant to factors six and one), provided any factfinding is supported by substantial evidence and consistent with the Court’s directives.
- The Board is not free to relitigate or disturb the Court‑affirmed findings on factor two absent proper basis (e.g., new, admissible evidence). But the Court expressly recognized that a revised assessment of factor six (and resulting change in perceived commercial strength) could affect the first-factor analysis and thus the overall balancing—so the Board may (and must) consider how its new sixth-factor factual findings alter the weight accorded to factor one and the ultimate likelihood-of-confusion determination.

Explanation and textual support

1) Scope of the remand (which DuPont factors)
- The Federal Circuit explicitly affirmed the Board’s finding on the second DuPont factor as supported by substantial evidence: “We affirm the Board’s finding as to the second DuPont factor.” (Opinion § III.A.)
- The court vacated the Board’s sixth-factor finding and remanded for reconsideration because the Board had narrowed the universe of third‑party marks improperly given its own finding that the services are highly similar: “We thus vacate the Board’s finding with respect to the sixth DuPont factor and remand for reconsideration of the appropriate scope of third‑party marks eligible for consideration in view of the Board’s factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.” (Opinion § III.B.)
- Because reconsideration of the sixth factor “may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression,” the court also vacated and remanded the first factor. (Opinion § III.C.)

2) Whether the Board can take new evidence on remand
- The panel did not say the record is closed or bar new evidence. It directed the Board to reconsider the scope of third‑party marks “in view of the Board’s factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar,” which is a merits direction, not an evidentiary constraint. (III.B.)
- Agencies typically have discretion on remand to supplement the record as needed to fulfill a court’s mandate; here, the Court’s instruction to reconsider which third‑party uses are “eligible for consideration” implies the Board should examine the broader universe of marks the Court said it should have considered. Practically, that may require admitting additional third‑party use evidence (e.g., registrations or use examples covering banking and financing services beyond credit‑card‑only entries) so the Board can determine whether the mark is weak or entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.
- Any such additional fact‑finding must still meet the governing standard: findings must be supported by substantial evidence and be consistent with the Court’s legal rulings.

3) Whether the Board may revisit the affirmed second‑factor findings
- The Court affirmed the Board’s second‑factor determination as legally correct and supported by substantial evidence. That affirmation means the Board should treat that finding as controlling on remand unless there is a valid procedural basis to reopen and receive new evidence that could change the factual record supporting factor two. The opinion itself does not authorize re‑weighing or re‑adjudicating the second factor in the absence of new, admissible evidence; it affirms the Board’s prior finding (III.A).
- However, the Court signaled that reassessment of factor six (and consequential changes to perceived commercial strength) can affect how the Board views factor one and the overall balancing. That is, while the underlying factual finding about service similarity was affirmed, the weight accorded to other factors (and the ultimate likelihood‑of‑confusion conclusion) can legitimately shift after the Board reconsiders factor six. The Court tied this together: “reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression. Therefore, we remand for reconsideration of the first factor as well.” (III.C.)

Practical consequences for the Board on remand
- Reassess factor six using the broader universe of third‑party Aspire‑formative uses related to banking/finance (not limited to credit‑card‑only uses), consistent with its affirmed finding that the services are highly similar. (III.B.)
- If the Board admits or considers additional evidence on factor six (permissible), it must explain how that evidence affects the commercial strength of CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark and whether that changes the first-factor commercial impression and the overall DuPont balance. (III.C.)
- Do not relitigate the second‑factor factual finding unless the Board concludes (with appropriate procedural steps and new evidence) that the substantial‑evidence basis for that finding has been undermined—something the Court did not direct and would be beyond the scope of this remand absent new record evidence.

Bottom line: The remand is focused on redoing the sixth‑factor analysis (and, consequentially, the first factor) consistent with the affirmed finding on services similarity. The Board may, as necessary, receive additional evidence to make those reassessments, but it should not upset the Court‑affirmed second‑factor finding without a proper new evidentiary basis.
