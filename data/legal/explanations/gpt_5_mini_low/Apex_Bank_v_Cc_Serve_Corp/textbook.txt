Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

This case arises from a trademark opposition in which CC Serve Corporation sought to block Apex Bank’s attempts to register ASPIRE BANK-formative marks for banking services. Understanding the dispute requires keeping straight the two parties, the relevant marks and services, the sequence of filings and challenges at the Trademark Office, the Board’s decision under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, and the limited issue presented to the Federal Circuit on appeal.

## Parties and marks

CC Serve is a company that provides credit card services. Its federally registered mark is the standard-character word mark ASPIRE, registered in 1998 with an effective priority date of October 17, 1996, and used in connection with credit card services. CC Serve’s business model is important to the dispute: it does not itself issue banks’ credit cards as a retail bank would, but rather partners with banks—joining with a bank that issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and accounts to consumers while CC Serve and affiliates service the accounts and provide related credit-card functions.

Apex Bank is a Tennessee-chartered retail bank with a modest brick-and-mortar footprint (18 branches) offering deposit accounts, loans, mortgages, and similar retail banking services. Apex does not currently offer credit cards. In August 2019 Apex filed intent-to-use trademark applications to register ASPIRE BANK (both word and design forms) for “banking and financing services.” Apex’s stated plan was to operate an internet bank under the ASPIRE BANK brand.

## CC Serve’s registration and priority

CC Serve’s ASPIRE registration issued in 1998 and carries an effective priority date of October 17, 1996. That registered priority and the scope of CC Serve’s services under the registration frame the Section 2(d) dispute: CC Serve is the prior registrant asserting that Apex’s later-filed ASPIRE BANK applications would likely cause confusion with CC Serve’s earlier ASPIRE mark on related services.

## USPTO prosecution, CC Serve’s letter of protest, and publication

During prosecution of Apex’s intent-to-use applications, CC Serve submitted a letter of protest to the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office expressing concern that Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK marks were confusingly similar to CC Serve’s ASPIRE registration. Despite that protest, the examining attorney allowed Apex’s applications to proceed and approved the marks for publication. The applications published for opposition on December 17, 2019. Publication is the point at which interested parties may file oppositions at the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB).

## TTAB opposition under Section 2(d) and the Board’s decision

CC Serve filed an opposition in February 2020 before the TTAB, alleging a likelihood of confusion under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), between CC Serve’s registered ASPIRE mark (for credit card services) and Apex’s ASPIRE BANK applications (for banking and financing services). The Board evaluated the DuPont factors (the established multi-factor likelihood-of-confusion framework) and concluded that confusion was likely. In particular, the Board found substantial similarity between the parties’ services—treating portions of the services as legally identical or highly similar—and that, on balance, the DuPont analysis supported sustaining the opposition and refusing registration of Apex’s marks.

The Board also considered third-party uses of ASPIRE-formative marks and the scope of the relevant consumer group, and it made determinations about the commercial strength of CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark. Those assessments informed the Board’s rulings on specific DuPont factors and the ultimate conclusion that Apex’s ASPIRE BANK marks should not register because they were likely to cause confusion with CC Serve’s ASPIRE registration.

## Appeal to the Federal Circuit and appellate jurisdiction

Apex appealed the TTAB’s decision to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, which has jurisdiction over appeals from final decisions of the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B). The Federal Circuit reviews the Board’s legal conclusions de novo and its factual findings for substantial evidence. On appeal Apex challenged the Board’s analysis of several DuPont factors (primarily the first, second, and sixth factors). The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding that the parties’ services are highly similar (the second DuPont factor) but concluded the Board erred in how it narrowed the scope of third-party ASPIRE-formative marks considered under the sixth DuPont factor and, because that error could affect the commercial impression analysis under the first DuPont factor, vacated and remanded the Board’s findings on the first and sixth factors while affirming in part and remanding in part. The Federal Circuit retained the ordinary standard of review—de novo for legal questions and substantial evidence for factual findings—when reaching its disposition.

# Governing Law and Standards of Review

This chapter explains the statutory and doctrinal framework the Federal Circuit applied in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., No. 2023-2143, and the standards the court uses when reviewing Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB or Board) determinations. The opinion turns on Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, the familiar multi-factor DuPont likelihood‑of‑confusion test, and the Federal Circuit’s rules for the allocation of review between law and fact—each of which is summarized here and tied to how the court treated those legal categories in this appeal.

## Lanham Act § 2(d): when registration can be refused

Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act provides the statutory hook for opposing or refusing federal trademark registration when an applicant’s mark is likely to cause confusion with a previously used or registered mark. The board must refuse registration when “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods,” a formulation the Federal Circuit cites from the CCPA-era DuPont decision. In practice, a Section 2(d) action is forward-looking: the TTAB determines whether the public is likely to confuse the source of goods or services offered under the applicant’s mark and the registrant’s mark. Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. illustrates this application: CC Serve invoked § 2(d) to oppose Apex’s intent-to-use filings for ASPIRE BANK marks on credit and banking services, and the Board sustained the opposition on the ground that consumer confusion was likely.

## The DuPont factors: a multi-factor, fact-intensive inquiry

The Board’s likelihood-of-confusion analysis is governed by the DuPont framework. DuPont sets out a non‑exhaustive list of factors—classically thirteen—that are relevant to whether confusion is likely. The DuPont inquiry is inherently fact-intensive: no single factor is dispositive, and the Board need only address the factors that are relevant and supported by the record. The Federal Circuit in Apex Bank reiterates these principles: likelihood of confusion is a legal question grounded in factual findings under the DuPont factors, and the Board may decline to consider every DuPont factor so long as it addresses those that matter on the record.

Two procedural points flow from that structure and are important on appeal. First, the Board’s analysis must be coherent: factual findings under individual DuPont factors should be consistent with one another and with the record. Second, because the DuPont factors overlap in purpose, a factual finding under one factor (for example, about the relatedness of goods or services) constrains what is relevant under other factors (for example, what third‑party marks count as competitors for assessing commercial strength). Apex Bank highlights both points: the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar under the second DuPont factor, but it vacated and remanded the Board’s handling of the sixth and first factors because the Board narrowed the universe of third‑party marks in a way inconsistent with its earlier finding of service relatedness.

The opinion also reminds readers that each DuPont factor presents a question of fact (reviewed for substantial evidence), and the ultimate likelihood‑of‑confusion determination is a legal conclusion based on those factual findings.

## First DuPont factor: similarity of the marks (appearance, sound, connotation, commercial impression)

The first DuPont factor focuses on the marks themselves—whether they are similar in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression when considered in their entireties. The inquiry is not a side‑by‑side mechanical comparison of isolated elements; rather it asks whether the overall commercial impression the marks convey is sufficiently similar to cause source confusion among consumers. The Federal Circuit cites its precedents emphasizing commercial impression as the controlling consideration in the first-factor analysis.

In Apex Bank, the Board’s first-factor analysis was vacated and remanded—not because the court established a rule about how to compare words and designs generally, but because the Board’s assessment of commercial impression could be affected by a corrected evaluation of the sixth factor (commercial strength). The court explained that commercial impression and the strength or weakness of a mark are interconnected: a mark’s relative commercial strength can change how similar two marks will appear to consumers in the marketplace. Therefore, when the Board must reconsider the sixth factor’s scope, it must also reassess the first factor in light of any new conclusions about the mark’s strength and how consumers perceive it.

## Second DuPont factor: similarity or relatedness of the goods/services and marketing circumstances

The second DuPont factor looks to the similarity or relatedness of the goods and services and the circumstances under which they are marketed. The services need not be identical; it is enough that they are related so that consumers might reasonably believe they come from the same source. The Board determines the ordinary commercial meaning of the identified goods or services and compares those to see whether overlap exists in function, channels of trade, customers, or marketing.

In this case the Board examined Apex’s identification of “banking and financing services” and CC Serve’s “credit card services.” The Board interpreted “credit card services” broadly to include issuing cards, account management, processing, authorization, credit monitoring, and related counseling. The Board further found that the terms “banking” and “finance” can encompass issuance of credit and credit-card‑type functions, and it relied on third‑party registrations demonstrating that credit-card services and banking services sometimes flow from a single source. The Federal Circuit found substantial evidence supporting the Board’s conclusion that the parties’ services are highly similar (and partially legally identical), and it affirmed that conclusion. That finding was pivotal because it set the proper scope for assessing certain other DuPont factors—an issue the court confronted when reviewing the sixth factor.

## Sixth DuPont factor: number and nature of similar marks in use (commercial strength/weakness)

The sixth DuPont factor examines the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods or services. The primary evidentiary role of this factor is to assess a mark’s commercial (and sometimes conceptual) strength or weakness. If a field is crowded with third‑party marks that are similar, a court may conclude the mark at issue is weak and entitled to a narrower scope of protection, because consumers in that crowded field are considered more likely to distinguish between similar marks.

A key principle the Federal Circuit emphasizes—and applied in Apex Bank—is that the sixth factor requires consideration of similar marks used on similar, not necessarily identical, goods or services. Thus, when the Board has already found that two services are highly similar under the second factor, it must not artificially narrow the class of third‑party marks considered under the sixth factor to marks tied only to a subset of services. In Apex Bank, the Board restricted its sixth-factor review to third‑party uses of “Aspire” in connection with credit-card services alone and disregarded uses tied to broader banking and financing services. The Federal Circuit determined that restriction was legally erroneous because it ignored the Board’s own finding that credit card services and banking/financing services are closely related. Accordingly, the court vacated the sixth-factor analysis and remanded for consideration of third‑party marks across the broader set of similar services the Board had found.

This linkage—treating the universe of third‑party marks consistently with the established scope of similarity between the parties’ services—is the practical lesson the opinion draws for assessing commercial strength.

## Appellate standards: de novo review of law; substantial evidence review of facts

Finally, Apex Bank reinforces the Federal Circuit’s standard allocations on appeal from TTAB decisions. Legal conclusions are reviewed de novo; factual findings—including DuPont-factor findings—are reviewed for substantial evidence. Substantial evidence means such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support the conclusion. Where factual findings are supported by substantial evidence, the appellate court will not disturb them; where the Board’s legal analysis or the logical coherence of its fact-finding is defective—such as using inconsistent scopes for evaluating relatedness versus third‑party mark crowding—the court will correct the legal error by vacating and remanding the affected findings.

In this appeal, the Federal Circuit applied those standards concretely: it affirmed the Board’s factual finding under the second DuPont factor because substantial evidence supported it, but treated the Board’s narrower selection of third‑party marks under the sixth factor as a legal error inconsistent with the second-factor finding and remanded that analysis (and the related first-factor assessment) for reconsideration under the correct scope.

Taken together, these principles explain how courts should structure a DuPont analysis and how appellate courts will review the TTAB’s mixed questions of law and fact.

# Affirmed: The Second DuPont Factor (Relatedness of Services)

This chapter explains why the Federal Circuit affirmed the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s determination that the second DuPont factor—the relatedness of the parties’ services—weighed heavily in favor of CC Serve. That factor asks whether the services offered under the competing marks are similar enough that consumers could believe they come from the same source. The court’s decision rests on how the Board defined the services at issue, the evidentiary record (dictionary definitions and third‑party registrations), and why Apex’s factual arguments did not overcome the Board’s findings. The discussion below follows the opinion’s factual and legal reasoning and highlights the doctrinal points a student needs to understand how the second DuPont factor was resolved.

## Legal standard and the function of the second DuPont factor

Under DuPont, the second factor evaluates the similarity of the goods or services. The test is not strict identity; it is sufficient if the services are related in such a way, or marketed in such circumstances, that consumers may assume a common source. Courts look to the identifications of services in the involved registrations/applications, evidence about how those services operate in the marketplace, dictionary meanings, and third‑party use or registration to determine relatedness. The Board’s factual findings about relatedness are reviewed for substantial evidence; legal conclusions are reviewed de novo.

## How the Board defined “credit card services” after the ID Manual change

A key procedural point in the Board’s analysis was that the Trademark ID Manual entry for “credit card services” had been deleted after CC Serve’s registration issued. When an identification term changes or is deleted from the ID Manual, the Board must determine what the original, identified services encompass by looking to the record. Here, the Board parsed the scope of CC Serve’s identified “credit card services” to include not only issuance of credit cards but also related activities that typically accompany credit card programs: counseling about card debt, transaction processing and authorization, monitoring and alerts, account management, and access to credit scores. The Board grounded this scope determination in the record (the application language, industry practice, and descriptive evidence submitted), and the Federal Circuit treated that factual determination as supported by substantial evidence.

Understanding this step is important: the breadth the Board ascribed to “credit card services” is what allowed it to compare CC Serve’s services to Apex’s broader “banking and financing services.” If the Board had adopted a narrow definition (e.g., only issuance of a physical card), the relatedness analysis might have been different. The Board’s classification of credit card services as encompassing issuance plus a suite of account‑management and processing services set the stage for finding substantial overlap with banking services.

## Dictionary evidence showing overlap between “banking/finance” and credit card activity

The Board relied on dictionary definitions of “bank,” “banking,” and “finance” that include extending credit or providing funds—activities that plainly intersect with credit card issuance and account management. The Board reasoned that these definitional overlaps show that credit card services can fall within the ordinary scope of banking and financing services. That is a recognized method for demonstrating relatedness: if ordinary dictionary or industry definitions of one party’s services encompass the other party’s services, similarity is established at least in part.

The Federal Circuit accepted that reasoning as a proper factual and legal basis: where the common meanings of “banking” and “finance” include extending credit and related account functions, services involving issuance and management of credit cards naturally overlap with “banking and financing services.” Thus the dictionary evidence supported the Board’s factual finding that the services are similar.

## Finding of partial legal identity: why issuance/management fits within banking/financing services

Applying the Board’s service definitions and the dictionary evidence, the Board concluded that the parties’ services were “legally identical, in part.” That phrase means that some of the identified services fall within the same categorical description—here, credit card issuance and account management are activities a bank or financial institution commonly provides. The court treated that as a factual finding supported by the record: because the applicants’ identifications encompassed overlapping activities, the services were highly similar enough to support a finding of potential confusion.

It is important to distinguish legal identity in part from complete identity: the Board did not say Apex and CC Serve provide identical portfolios of services in all respects. Rather, the Board found a significant, legally relevant overlap—enough to weigh the second DuPont factor heavily in favor of the prior registrant.

## Third‑party registrations showing combined credit card and banking/finance offerings

The Board also considered third‑party trademark registrations that covered both credit card services and broader banking/financing services. Such registrations are admissible and relevant because they show how marks are actually used in commerce and can demonstrate that different but related services can come from a single source under one mark. The Board used those third‑party registrations to show that it is common for a single provider (or a single brand) to cover both credit card programs and broader banking or financial services—supporting the conclusion that consumers could associate the services as emanating from the same source.

The Federal Circuit viewed the Board’s reliance on these third‑party registrations as consistent with settled practice: when registrations show that credit card and banking services are offered together under the same marks, they corroborate a finding of high relatedness.

## Apex’s counterarguments and why they failed

Apex raised several factual and conceptual objections. First, Apex argued the services are not competitive because Apex is a bank (or plans an internet bank) and CC Serve is a program manager that partners with banks—CC Serve does not itself hold a banking charter nor issue credit cards as a bank. Apex also emphasized that CC Serve’s role is servicing and managing card programs for issuing banks, not offering traditional retail banking services, and therefore the marketplace roles differ.

The Board considered these points but discounted them for two reasons the court accepted. One, the DuPont second factor focuses on the relationship between the services as identified, not merely on the corporate form or label of each party. Even if CC Serve operates by partnering with banks, the services it provides—issuing or enabling issuance of credit cards, processing transactions, account management—overlap materially with banking/financing services. Two, the record contained evidence (definitions and registrations) showing that the services are marketed and presented in ways that can cause consumer confusion over source; corporate structure or a claim of non‑competition does not negate that similarity where the services themselves overlap.

The Federal Circuit therefore concluded Apex had not shown that the Board’s factual findings were unsupported by substantial evidence. Apex’s arguments attacked the Board’s credibility determinations and factual inferences but did not overcome the evidentiary showing before the Board.

## Federal Circuit holding on the second DuPont factor

Summarizing the court’s treatment: the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s second DuPont factor finding. The Board reasonably defined “credit card services” broadly in light of the deleted ID Manual entry, relied on dictionary definitions that equated banking/finance with extending credit (including via cards), and supported its conclusion with third‑party registrations showing combined credit card and banking/finance offerings. On that record, substantial evidence supported the Board’s determination that the parties’ services are highly similar—indeed legally identical in part—and that this factor weighed heavily in favor of CC Serve.

For students, the takeaway is that the second DuPont factor centers on the services as actually identified and described in the registrations/applications and the relevant marketplace understanding. Evidence such as definitions and third‑party registrations that show services are commonly provided together can be decisive, and a registrant’s corporate label or contractual role (e.g., program manager vs. bank) will not defeat a finding of relatedness if the services themselves overlap and the record supports consumer confusion.

# Vacated: The Sixth DuPont Factor (Third-Party Use) and Its Ripple Effect on the First Factor

This chapter explains why the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s handling of the sixth DuPont factor in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. was legally flawed, why that error required vacating part of the Board’s decision, and how the sixth factor’s reassessment necessarily affects the Board’s analysis of the first DuPont factor. The explanation stays close to the Court of Appeals’ reasoning: it treats the sixth factor as a tool for measuring a mark’s relative strength through evidence of third-party use on similar goods and services, shows where the Board narrowed its inquiry improperly, and traces the logical consequence that a revised strength finding may change the marks’ overall commercial impressions.

## The Proper Scope of the Sixth DuPont Factor

The sixth DuPont factor asks courts to consider “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” If many third parties use similar marks on similar goods or services, the owner’s mark is relatively weaker and receives narrower protection. The objective is not to require identical goods or services for those third-party uses to be relevant; rather, the third-party marks must be used on goods or services that are similar enough to the parties’ identified offerings that consumer exposure to those uses would reduce the likelihood of confusion between the contested marks.

In other words, the sixth factor assesses the marketplace “crowdedness” around a formative term and whether consumers have been trained to distinguish among similar marks. Conceding that similarity of goods is sufficient (not identity) is critical: third-party uses on services that are similar to, related to, or within the same commercial sphere as the contested services are probative of weakness and are properly considered.

## What the Board Did: An Improper Narrowing

In this case, the Board first found (in its second-factor analysis) that Apex’s banking and financing services and CC Serve’s credit card services are highly similar and even partially legally identical. That finding establishes that consumers and the marketplace see these services as closely related.

Despite that factual finding, the Board, when analyzing the sixth factor, restricted the set of third-party marks it examined to only those using “Aspire” in connection with credit card services. It excluded Aspire-formative marks used in other banking or financing services and focused the relevant public narrowly on “consumers of credit card services.” The Board then concluded that only a small number of Aspire uses for credit card services existed and that the evidence did not show the kind of “voluminous” third-party use that would demonstrate weakness.

This narrowing is the error the Federal Circuit identified. Once the Board concluded the services are highly similar, it could not legally impose a stricter or narrower similarity requirement when deciding whether third-party uses on related banking and financing services should be counted. Limiting the universe of third-party marks to a subset of closely related services contradicts the earlier finding of service similarity and undermines the sixth factor’s purpose.

## The Federal Circuit’s Directive: Maintain Consistent Scope Across Factors

The Federal Circuit held that the Board must keep the same scope of similarity across its DuPont analysis. Practically, that means:

- If the Board finds the parties’ services are highly similar (or partially legally identical), then third-party uses on services within that same spectrum of banking and financing—beyond just credit card services—are relevant under the sixth factor.
- The Board should not require stricter identity for third-party uses than it applied when comparing the parties’ own services under the second factor.

The Court vacated the Board’s sixth-factor finding and remanded for reconsideration using a properly defined universe of third-party uses consistent with the Board’s own factual findings about service similarity.

## How Broader Third-Party Use Affects Commercial Strength

Third-party uses on similar services inform whether a mark is conceptually or commercially weak. If many different entities use Aspire-formed marks across banking and financing services (credit cards, deposit accounts, loan services, online banking, etc.), that would indicate the term is relatively commonplace in the relevant marketplace. The practical effects are twofold:

- Conceptual weakness: frequent third-party adoption suggests the term is not uniquely distinctive and is more descriptive or merely suggestive in the financial context.
- Commercial weakness: wide use signals that consumers are accustomed to distinguishing among multiple Aspire-formative marks, reducing the likelihood that a new Aspire-formative mark will be perceived as coming from the same source.

Either finding narrows the scope of protection accorded to CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark and reduces the weight that similarity in the marks themselves should carry when evaluating likelihood of confusion.

## Why the First DuPont Factor Must Be Revisited

The first DuPont factor examines the marks’ similarity in appearance, sound, connotation, and overall commercial impression. That assessment is not purely formal; it depends materially on the mark’s commercial context and strength. If a term is weak (because of extensive third-party use on similar services), small differences between marks matter more, and the same degree of resemblance is less likely to create consumer confusion. Conversely, if the term is strong and unique, even modest similarities may cause confusion.

Because the Board’s sixth-factor analysis improperly truncated the universe of third-party uses, its conclusion about ASPIRE’s strength (that it was entitled to a “normal” scope of protection) may be unsound. The Federal Circuit therefore vacated the Board’s first-factor analysis as well, directing that the Board reassess the marks’ appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression after it properly evaluates third-party use.

## Practical Remand Instructions from the Court

On remand, the Board should proceed in two linked steps consistent with the opinion:

1. Reassess the sixth DuPont factor by considering third-party Aspire-formative marks used on services that are similar to Apex’s and CC Serve’s services as the Board earlier defined them (i.e., banking and financing services broadly, not only credit card services). The Board should explain why each third-party use is within the relevant scope and evaluate whether the number and nature of those uses show that ASPIRE is conceptually or commercially weak in this marketplace.

2. Reevaluate the first DuPont factor in light of any revised strength finding from step one. If broader third-party use demonstrates weakness, the Board must recalibrate how it weighs similarities in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression between ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK (design). The Board should explicitly connect its conclusions about commercial strength to its assessment of the marks’ overall impressions and the ultimate likelihood-of-confusion conclusion.

By tying the two steps together, the Board will avoid the inconsistency that produced the Federal Circuit’s vacatur—i.e., finding services highly similar but then artificially narrowing the relevant crowd of third-party marks when determining mark strength.

## Takeaway for Reading Trademark Opinions

When a tribunal finds goods or services to be similar or related, you must check that the tribunal applies the same scope consistently across other DuPont factors that rely on similarity (especially the sixth factor). A finding that services are related makes third-party uses on those related services probative of mark strength; excluding those uses without a reasoned distinction is reversible error. Also, remember that strength (sixth factor evidence) and the first-factor commercial impression are interdependent: a change in one can and often must produce a reassessment of the other. The Federal Circuit’s remand enforces that analytical consistency.

# Disposition and Key Takeaways

The Federal Circuit affirmed-in-part, vacated-in-part, and remanded the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s decision in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. The court affirmed the Board’s finding on the second DuPont factor—that Apex’s banking and financing services are highly similar to CC Serve’s credit card services. But the court vacated the Board’s analysis of the sixth DuPont factor (third-party use evidence) and the first DuPont factor (similarity of the marks in their entirety) and remanded for further proceedings consistent with its opinion. The court awarded costs to Apex (the appellant).

## Consistency principle across DuPont factors

A central lesson from the opinion is procedural consistency: once the Board makes a factual finding about the scope or similarity of the parties’ goods or services for one DuPont factor, it should retain that scope in evaluating related factors unless there is a record basis to change it. Here, the Board found—after examining identifications and dictionary meanings—that the parties’ services are highly similar and partially legally identical. The Board nevertheless narrowed the universe of third-party marks considered under the sixth factor to only those labeled expressly for “credit card services,” excluding marks used for other banking and financing services. The Federal Circuit held that was error. Because the Board had already determined the services to be highly similar, it could not impose a stricter or different definition of “similar” when assessing third-party use and mark strength. In short: factual findings about goods/services that are relied on for one DuPont factor should govern the relevant scope for other factors that depend on similarity, unless the Board explains a rational basis for departing from that scope.

## Third-party use evidence: similar goods/services suffice

The opinion reinforces that third-party use and registration evidence under the sixth DuPont factor need not be confined to marks used on textually identical goods or services. The controlling question is whether the third-party marks are used on goods or services similar enough to the parties’ offerings to show marketplace crowding and, therefore, weakness of the senior mark. The Board’s error was treating “credit card services” as the only relevant universe when it had already concluded that “banking and financing services” overlap substantially with credit card services. The Federal Circuit reminded the Board (consistent with its prior precedents) that evidence of similar—not necessarily identical—uses is probative of whether consumers are educated to distinguish between similar marks and whether a mark is commercially weak or entitled only to a narrow scope of protection.

## Interdependence of factors: commercial strength influences commercial impression

The court also emphasized the interdependence of the DuPont factors. The sixth factor (number and nature of similar marks in use) bears directly on the commercial strength of the senior mark. Commercial strength and marketplace recognition inform the “appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression” inquiry under the first factor. Because the Board’s narrow treatment of third-party evidence could materially undercut its assessment of CC Serve’s commercial strength, the Federal Circuit concluded the Board must reexamine the first-factor analysis after properly reassessing the sixth factor. That reasoning demonstrates how an erroneous or incomplete evaluation under one factor can change the outcome under another—so the Board must reassess related factors in light of any corrected findings.

## Practice pointers for litigants and practitioners

1. Build a full record of third-party use across similar services. When your services are alleged to be similar to the opponent’s, collect evidence of third-party registrations and real-world uses that cover the full range of similar goods/services (not only identically worded identifications). Be prepared to show use and context (web pages, marketing, service descriptions) that tie third-party marks to services similar to those at issue.

2. Maintain consistent scope definitions across factors. Use the same factual definitions of the parties’ goods or services when arguing different DuPont factors unless you can justify a change with new or different record evidence. Inconsistent definitions invite reversal for procedural error.

3. Use dictionaries and registration evidence carefully. The Board relied on dictionary meanings and Trademark ID Manual entries to define services. Those tools are useful to show overlap, but the court will expect coherence between those definitions and subsequent analytical choices (for example, which third-party marks are considered).

4. Emphasize commercial strength context. If the opponent’s mark is relatively weak in the marketplace because many similar marks are in use across related services, highlight that both with registrations and evidence of consumer exposure. Conversely, if you argue for a broad scope of protection, explain why third-party uses are not relevant or are distinguishable despite surface similarity.

5. Demonstrate consumer perception where appropriate. Because DuPont asks whether consumers would be likely to believe the marks emanate from the same source, evidence showing how consumers perceive services (surveys, market channels, typical purchasing conditions) can tie together the similarity of services, the relevance of third-party uses, and the ultimate commercial impression of the marks.

In sum, Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. stresses that DuPont factor analyses must be coherent and mutually consistent. Once a tribunal adopts a factual baseline about the similarity of services, it must apply that baseline when evaluating related evidence—especially third-party use—because such evidence affects mark strength and thus the overall likelihood-of-confusion inquiry.
