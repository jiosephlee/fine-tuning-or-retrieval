Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Why does the Federal Circuit review DuPont factor findings for “substantial evidence” but the ultimate likelihood-of-confusion question de novo?
Question:
The opinion says each DuPont factor is a fact question reviewed for substantial evidence, but likelihood of confusion is a legal question reviewed de novo. I'm struggling to understand where the line is drawn. For example, when the Board decides the services are “highly similar,” is that a factual finding insulated by substantial evidence review, while the weight that similarity gets in the overall confusion analysis is legal? How does this framework affect what arguments can win on appeal from the TTAB?
Answer:
Short answer
The Federal Circuit treats the DuPont factors as fact questions (reviewed for substantial evidence) because they are the underlying factual findings about the marks, goods/services, marketplace, etc. But the ultimate determination—“is likelihood of confusion established?”—is a legal conclusion that the court makes by applying the law to those factual findings and legal standards, so it is reviewed de novo. Apex Bank illustrates both points: the court affirmed the Board’s factual finding that the services were “highly similar” (substantial evidence), but it reversed/vacated parts of the Board’s legal analysis where the Board applied an incorrect scope/standard under the sixth factor (legal error) and remanded for reconsideration of related legal conclusions.

Why the Court draws the line this way
- DuPont factors = facts. As the opinion quotes, “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence” (citing Bose and M2 Software). Those findings—what the services actually are, what third-party use shows, how consumers perceive the goods, etc.—are factual determinations of the Board.
- Likelihood = law. The court treats “likelihood of confusion” as a legal question that “is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors” (M2 Software language quoted in the opinion). That makes the ultimate balance and legal conclusion a matter for de novo review.

How that plays out in practice (useful boundaries)
- If you challenge a factual finding (e.g., the Board’s finding that services are “highly similar”), you must show the finding lacks substantial evidence—i.e., no reasonable mind could accept the record the way the Board did. In Apex the court found substantial evidence supported the Board’s second-factor finding and affirmed it.
- If you challenge the Board’s legal application of the facts (e.g., it applied the wrong legal scope, ignored a necessary legal principle, or made inconsistent legal inferences from its facts), you argue legal error subject to de novo review. In Apex the Board limited the universe of third-party “Aspire” uses to credit-card services only, despite having found the services highly similar; the Federal Circuit held that narrowing was a legal error and vacated the sixth-factor analysis (and, because that error could affect commercial impression, also remanded the first factor).
- Mixed issues: sometimes a factual finding controls how broad a legal inquiry must be. If the Board’s facts require a certain legal scope (as here—the Board found services highly similar, so it should have considered similar marks beyond only credit-card uses), failing to follow that is a legal mistake even if the factual finding itself survives substantial-evidence review.

What arguments are most likely to succeed on appeal
- Attack factual insufficiency (substantial-evidence): show the record does not support the Board’s factual finding (contradictory evidence, gaps in the record, or improper reliance on extraneous materials).
- Attack legal error in applying the DuPont framework: show the Board applied the wrong legal standard, defined the universe too narrowly or inconsistently with its own findings, failed to consider a relevant factor, or misapplied precedent. Apex succeeded on this ground for the sixth factor.
- Preserve the record: because fact findings are hard to overturn under substantial-evidence review, build a robust factual record at the Board (evidence, testimony, third-party use, market surveys); and, separately, develop clear legal argumentation about how the Board should apply those facts to the DuPont framework.

Bottom line
Treat the Board’s DuPont determinations as two layers: (1) factual findings (substantial-evidence review) about marks, goods, consumers, and third-party use; and (2) the legal judgment—balancing those factual findings into the ultimate likelihood-of-confusion conclusion—which the Federal Circuit reviews de novo. Apex Bank is a good model: factual finding of high similarity affirmed, but legal error in how that fact was used under the sixth factor led to vacatur and remand.
