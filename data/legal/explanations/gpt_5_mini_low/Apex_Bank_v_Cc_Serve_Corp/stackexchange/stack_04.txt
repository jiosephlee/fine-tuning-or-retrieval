Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Was it proper for the Board to treat ‘credit card services’ and ‘banking and financing services’ as legally identical in part, especially given the deletion of ‘credit card services’ from the ID Manual?
Question:
The Board defined the legacy ‘credit card services’ identification to include issuing cards, processing, authorization, account management, etc., and then used dictionary definitions of ‘banking’ and ‘finance’ to find partial legal identity with Apex’s services. Is this consistent with TTAB precedent on construing older IDs when the ID Manual has changed? What sources can the Board rely on to interpret an existing registration’s scope, and how much overlap is necessary to say services are ‘legally identical, in part’?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — insofar as the Board interpreted the older identification of “credit card services” to include card issuance, processing, authorization, account management, etc., and then relied on ordinary meaning (dictionary definitions) and evidence of third‑party registrations/uses to show that “banking” and “finance” encompass those activities, that approach is consistent with the controlling principles the Federal Circuit applied in this case. The court affirmed the Board’s second‑DuPont holding that the services are “highly similar” and “legally identical, in part,” because the Board (1) construed the legacy ID, (2) showed overlap between that construction and Apex’s listed “banking and financing services” using dictionary definitions, and (3) supported the finding with third‑party registrations and use. The court treated that finding as supported by substantial evidence, while remanding only because the Board then misapplied a narrower scope when considering the sixth and first DuPont factors.

What legal doctrine the Board applied
- Construction of an existing identification: When an older registration’s ID (here, “credit card services”) no longer appears in the ID Manual, the Board must determine what the registration’s own identification means. The Board may look to the language of the registration itself and other evidence of ordinary meaning. In this opinion the Board expressly defined “credit card services” to include issuing cards, processing payments, authorization, account management, monitoring/alerts, credit counseling, and the like. The court accepted that construction as the starting point for comparing services.
- Relatedness vs. identity: The DuPont/likelihood‑of‑confusion framework does not require literal identity of services. The services need only be related such that consumers could reasonably believe they come from the same source. The Board found, and the court affirmed, that some of the activities encompassed by “credit card services” overlap with activities encompassed by “banking and financing services,” so the services are “legally identical, in part.”
- Substantial‑evidence review: The court reviewed the Board’s factual findings for substantial evidence. It concluded the Board’s conclusion that the services are highly similar was supported by substantial evidence and acceptable legal reasoning. The court did not disturb that part of the Board’s analysis.

What sources the Board can rely on (as shown in the opinion)
- The registration’s own identification (the wording of the registered services). The Board here started by construing the legacy ID itself.
- Dictionary definitions or other extrinsic evidence of ordinary meaning. The Board used dictionary definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” to show that those terms can include extending credit and issuing credit instruments.
- Third‑party registrations and evidence of third‑party use. The Board relied on registrations that covered both credit card services and banking/finance services to show relatedness and that the services may emanate from a single source under one mark.
The opinion explicitly cites all three sources in its second‑DuPont analysis (J.A. 24–35) and affirms the Board’s reliance on them as adequate support for finding overlap.

How much overlap is necessary to call services “legally identical, in part”
- The touchstone is whether there is an actual overlap in the services described such that an ordinary consumer could believe the services emanate from the same source. Literal identity of all services is not required. Partial legal identity exists when at least some of the listed activities in the registrations are the same or one description necessarily encompasses activities in the other description (here: issuance/extension of credit via card → falls within “banking/finance”).
- Practically, the Board must identify the overlapping activity(ies) and show (with its construction, definitions, and evidence) that those activities are shared. The court upheld the Board’s finding because the Board defined “credit card services” to include card issuance/credit functions and showed that “banking/finance” as commonly understood also covers extending credit and issuance of credit instruments. That factual showing satisfied the requirement for partial legal identity under DuPont.
- The level of overlap required is ultimately a fact question: enough overlap to create a reasonable likelihood of source confusion for the relevant consumers. The court’s review is limited to whether substantial evidence supports that factual conclusion.

Practical implications from the opinion
- If you are defending or attacking an older registration whose ID no longer appears in the current ID Manual, be prepared to (a) show what the registration’s own identification reasonably describes, and (b) show how ordinary meanings and marketplace evidence connect that description to the challenger’s listed goods/services.
- Third‑party registrations and use are appropriate to show the practical marketplace overlap, but the Board must be consistent about scope across DuPont factors. The court faulted the Board only for narrowing the universe of third‑party marks under factor six despite having found broad similarity under factor two (hence the vacatur/remand on factors six and one).

Conclusion
The Board’s method—construing the legacy ID, using dictionary definitions to show overlapping meanings, and supporting the overlap with third‑party registrations/use—was legally proper and is what the Federal Circuit affirmed for the second DuPont factor in this case. The key is a supported factual showing that at least some of the services actually described in the older registration are encompassed by the applicant’s descriptions so that consumers could confuse the sources.
