Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### On remand, is the Board limited to reconsidering only factors one and six, and can it take new evidence or must it decide on the existing record?
Question:
The Federal Circuit affirmed the second factor but vacated and remanded as to the sixth and first factors. Procedurally, is the TTAB confined to the current record when reevaluating third-party use and commercial impression, or can it reopen the record for additional evidence? Also, is the Board precluded from revisiting the second factor’s relatedness findings, or can new sixth-factor analysis indirectly affect how much weight it gives other factors?
Answer:
Short answer
- The Federal Circuit limited its substantive remand to DuPont factors six and one (vacating those findings and affirming factor two). The Board must reassess the sixth-factor evidence (and then reexamine the first-factor commercial impression) in light of its earlier, affirmed finding that the parties’ services are highly similar. See opinion at III.B–C (vacating sixth and first; affirming second).
- The opinion does not rigidly confine the Board to the existing evidentiary record. On remand the Board may take whatever procedural steps are appropriate (including, if it chooses, reopening the record to admit additional evidence relevant to factors six and one), provided any factfinding is supported by substantial evidence and consistent with the Court’s directives.
- The Board is not free to relitigate or disturb the Court‑affirmed findings on factor two absent proper basis (e.g., new, admissible evidence). But the Court expressly recognized that a revised assessment of factor six (and resulting change in perceived commercial strength) could affect the first-factor analysis and thus the overall balancing—so the Board may (and must) consider how its new sixth-factor factual findings alter the weight accorded to factor one and the ultimate likelihood-of-confusion determination.

Explanation and textual support

1) Scope of the remand (which DuPont factors)
- The Federal Circuit explicitly affirmed the Board’s finding on the second DuPont factor as supported by substantial evidence: “We affirm the Board’s finding as to the second DuPont factor.” (Opinion § III.A.)
- The court vacated the Board’s sixth-factor finding and remanded for reconsideration because the Board had narrowed the universe of third‑party marks improperly given its own finding that the services are highly similar: “We thus vacate the Board’s finding with respect to the sixth DuPont factor and remand for reconsideration of the appropriate scope of third‑party marks eligible for consideration in view of the Board’s factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.” (Opinion § III.B.)
- Because reconsideration of the sixth factor “may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression,” the court also vacated and remanded the first factor. (Opinion § III.C.)

2) Whether the Board can take new evidence on remand
- The panel did not say the record is closed or bar new evidence. It directed the Board to reconsider the scope of third‑party marks “in view of the Board’s factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar,” which is a merits direction, not an evidentiary constraint. (III.B.)
- Agencies typically have discretion on remand to supplement the record as needed to fulfill a court’s mandate; here, the Court’s instruction to reconsider which third‑party uses are “eligible for consideration” implies the Board should examine the broader universe of marks the Court said it should have considered. Practically, that may require admitting additional third‑party use evidence (e.g., registrations or use examples covering banking and financing services beyond credit‑card‑only entries) so the Board can determine whether the mark is weak or entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.
- Any such additional fact‑finding must still meet the governing standard: findings must be supported by substantial evidence and be consistent with the Court’s legal rulings.

3) Whether the Board may revisit the affirmed second‑factor findings
- The Court affirmed the Board’s second‑factor determination as legally correct and supported by substantial evidence. That affirmation means the Board should treat that finding as controlling on remand unless there is a valid procedural basis to reopen and receive new evidence that could change the factual record supporting factor two. The opinion itself does not authorize re‑weighing or re‑adjudicating the second factor in the absence of new, admissible evidence; it affirms the Board’s prior finding (III.A).
- However, the Court signaled that reassessment of factor six (and consequential changes to perceived commercial strength) can affect how the Board views factor one and the overall balancing. That is, while the underlying factual finding about service similarity was affirmed, the weight accorded to other factors (and the ultimate likelihood‑of‑confusion conclusion) can legitimately shift after the Board reconsiders factor six. The Court tied this together: “reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression. Therefore, we remand for reconsideration of the first factor as well.” (III.C.)

Practical consequences for the Board on remand
- Reassess factor six using the broader universe of third‑party Aspire‑formative uses related to banking/finance (not limited to credit‑card‑only uses), consistent with its affirmed finding that the services are highly similar. (III.B.)
- If the Board admits or considers additional evidence on factor six (permissible), it must explain how that evidence affects the commercial strength of CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark and whether that changes the first-factor commercial impression and the overall DuPont balance. (III.C.)
- Do not relitigate the second‑factor factual finding unless the Board concludes (with appropriate procedural steps and new evidence) that the substantial‑evidence basis for that finding has been undermined—something the Court did not direct and would be beyond the scope of this remand absent new record evidence.

Bottom line: The remand is focused on redoing the sixth‑factor analysis (and, consequentially, the first factor) consistent with the affirmed finding on services similarity. The Board may, as necessary, receive additional evidence to make those reassessments, but it should not upset the Court‑affirmed second‑factor finding without a proper new evidentiary basis.
