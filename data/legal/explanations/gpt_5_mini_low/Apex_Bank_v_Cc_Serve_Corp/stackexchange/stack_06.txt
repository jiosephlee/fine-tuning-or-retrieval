Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Does CC Serve’s role as a program manager partnering with issuing banks matter for the relatedness of services under factor two?
Question:
Apex argued CC Serve doesn’t ‘provide banking services’ but partners with banks to manage ASPIRE credit card programs. The court called that unavailing. In likelihood-of-confusion analysis, does it matter that the opposer is not itself a bank if it offers card-related services closely tied to banking? How do tribunals treat distinctions between issuers, servicers, and program managers when the identifications of services—and consumer perception of source—overlap?
Answer:
Short answer: No — CC Serve’s label as a “program manager” or partner to issuing banks does not insulate its services from being deemed related to a bank’s “banking and financing services” under DuPont factor two if the actual identifications and marketplace facts show close overlap or that consumers could perceive a common source. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s conclusion that the services were “highly similar” even though CC Serve works with a bank to issue ASPIRE cards.

Why (doctrinally and as the opinion applies it)
- Factor two asks whether the parties’ goods/services are related in the sense that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs., cited at slip op. § III.A. The services need not be identical.
- The court relied on the parties’ identifications and record evidence. It accepted the Board’s definition of “credit card services” to include issuing cards, processing transactions, managing accounts, etc., and concluded those activities overlap with the ordinary meanings of “banking” and “finance” (which encompass extending credit and issuing cards). Slip op. § III.A, J.A.25–28.
- Apex’s characterization of CC Serve as merely “partnering with banks to manage credit card programs” was “unavailing” because the Board had examined the actual descriptions and marketplace evidence and reasonably found a high degree of similarity. Slip op. § III.A.

How tribunals treat issuer/servicer/program-manager distinctions in practice (per the opinion)
- Tribunals look to the identifications of services and to how the services are actually presented and used in the marketplace, not merely to internal corporate labels. Here the Board considered the services CC Serve actually provides (e.g., issuance-related activity, account servicing) and found overlap with Apex’s banking services. Slip op. § III.A.
- When the identifications and evidence show functional overlap (issuance, account management, transaction processing), tribunals will treat those services as related for likelihood-of-confusion purposes even if an issuing bank formally holds the loan/obligation. The court affirmed that such overlap can make services “legally identical, in part.” Slip op. § III.A, J.A.27–28.
- Relatedness analysis is driven by consumer perception and the potential for source confusion. The Board relied on dictionary definitions and third‑party registrations to show the services are of a type that “may emanate from a single source under one mark.” Slip op. § III.A, J.A.33–34.

Practical takeaways for argument
- Don’t rely solely on contractual/structural labels (issuer vs. servicer vs. program manager). Emphasize (or counter) the actual activities identified in the application/registration and evidence of how consumers encounter the services.
- If you want to narrow relatedness, show record evidence that consumers distinguish the entities (e.g., clear branding, disclosures, separate marketing channels) or that the functional activities do not overlap with the applicant’s identified services.
- Conversely, if the record shows CC Serve’s role includes issuing-branded cards, account servicing, authorization/processing, etc., expect tribunals to treat those services as related to banking/finance for DuPont factor two, as the Federal Circuit affirmed here.

Finally, note that the court’s rejection of Apex’s label-based argument was limited to the record and identifications before it: because the Board found the services highly similar, the court affirmed factor two but remanded other factors (1 and 6) for reconsideration in light of that finding. Slip op. §§ III.A–C.
