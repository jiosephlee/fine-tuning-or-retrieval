Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

## You Can’t Shrink the Marketplace Mid‑Analysis: The Federal Circuit Demands Consistent Scope Across DuPont Factors

Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25, 2025) is a tidy but important reminder that the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board cannot adopt one conception of the relevant marketplace for one DuPont factor and then narrow it when doing a different factor analysis. The Federal Circuit reversed the Board’s tendency to “toggle” scopes: after the Board found Apex’s and CC Serve’s services to be “highly similar, in fact, partially legally identical” under the second DuPont factor, it nevertheless limited its sixth‑factor inquiry about third‑party use to a smaller subset of services—credit card services—excluding marks that used “Aspire” in connection with other banking and financing services. The appeals court rejected that bifurcation, vacating the Board’s sixth‑factor and first‑factor analyses and remanding for reconsideration with a consistent scope.

What the court did

The dispute is straightforward on the facts. CC Serve owns a longstanding registration for the standard character mark ASPIRE covering credit card services; Apex, a retail bank, applied to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services (it does not issue credit cards but plans an internet‑banking brand). The Board found the services highly similar and sustained an opposition based on likelihood of confusion. On appeal, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding that the services were highly similar (the second DuPont factor), but it found legal error in the Board’s treatment of the sixth DuPont factor.

The sixth DuPont prong asks whether a mark is relatively weak because of the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods or services. That analysis often depends heavily on the universe of third‑party marks the Board considers. Here, Apex produced evidence of many third‑party uses of ASPIR(E) across the financial space. The Board, however, limited the “relevant public” to consumers of credit card services and considered only third‑party uses tied explicitly to credit card services, concluding that the evidence was not voluminous enough to show weakness.

The Federal Circuit’s core holding is simple: once the Board has determined—based on the second DuPont factor—that the parties’ services are highly similar (or partly legally identical), it cannot constrict the field when assessing third‑party use under the sixth factor to a narrower subset of services inconsistent with that earlier finding. The court relied on settled precedent that similarity for DuPont purposes does not require identity of goods or services (Olde Tyme Foods; Juice Generation) and emphasized that the Board should retain the same scope in evaluating other relevant factors. Because the sixth‑factor error could also affect the first DuPont factor (similarity of the marks in their entireties and the resulting commercial impression), the court vacated both and remanded.

Why the mismatch is more than semantics

On its face, the Board’s maneuver might look like a technical cataloging decision: “We found services similar but we’ll look narrowly at the most closely overlapping slice for third‑party use.” The Federal Circuit treats that as more substantive. DuPont is a multifactor inquiry: many of the factors interact. A factual finding that two services are highly similar is not an isolated breadcrumb; it defines the scope of who the relevant consumers are, what marketplace confusion might look like, and what third‑party uses are meaningfully probative of marketplace crowding and consumer sophistication.

If the Board is allowed to narrow scope midstream, it can undercut a robust sixth‑factor analysis by excluding evidence that bears on commercial strength. In practice that creates a risk of manipulating outcomes: adopt a broad view when the Board wants to emphasize overlap (to help an opposer on likelihood), and then adopt a narrow view when it wants to diminish evidence of crowding (to deny weakness and preserve a normal scope of protection). The Federal Circuit rejects that selective framing.

How this builds on earlier precedent

The opinion tracks comfortably with prior Federal Circuit decisions. Olde Tyme Foods and Juice Generation emphasize that similarity does not demand literal identity; marks used on related goods or services can, and often do, inform the crowding inquiry. Jack Wolfskin, by contrast, demonstrates the flip side: when third‑party evidence is truly voluminous across the relevant marketplace, it can meaningfully narrow a mark’s protection. Apex forces the Board to apply those lines consistently: if the Board’s second‑factor finding sweeps in a category of services as similar, the Board must consider third‑party uses across that same category when evaluating the sixth factor.

Practical implications for practitioners

Plaintiffs (opposers/examiners) and defendants (applicants) should take away straightforward, practice‑focused lessons.

- For applicants defending against an opposition: assemble as broad a third‑party record as the services overlap allows. If the Board or PTO has already indicated the services are related or overlapping, submit third‑party registrations and evidence across the entire overlapping service field (e.g., “credit cards,” “banking,” and “financing”), not just the most restrictive label. That broader record will pressure the Board to recognize crowding and potential weakness.

- For opposers trying to prove likelihood of confusion: be prepared for the Board to face scrutiny if it both finds services similar and then narrows the third‑party universe. If you argue that services overlap, explain why third‑party evidence outside a narrow label is irrelevant or genuinely not probative of crowding (for instance, differences in consumer class, distribution channels, or functional distinctions). Don’t assume the Board can safely ignore broader evidence once it has made a similarity finding.

- For both sides presenting evidence: frame your exhibits with consistent marketplace definitions. Provide declarations or industry evidence regarding the relevant consumer cohorts, channels of trade, and how services are marketed together. That helps the Board anchor its scope and reduces opportunities for inconsistent treatment.

Downstream doctrinal effects

Apex sharpens the requirement that DuPont factor analyses be coherent and factually consistent. The opinion does not alter the flexible nature of DuPont—boards and courts still choose which factors are “relevant and of record”—but it insists that factfindings used to delimit the market carry through to later factors that depend on that market definition. Expect appellants to raise (and the Federal Circuit to scrutinize) any decisions where the Board’s market definition varies factor‑by‑factor without articulation.

Two likely follow‑on developments: first, the Board will be more careful to define the “relevant public” and the market early in its opinions and then use that definition consistently across factors, expressly explaining any departures. Second, litigants will push for broader third‑party lists where goods and services overlap and will contest exclusions more aggressively. We may also see a modest uptick in remands where the Board’s factor analyses employ inconsistent frames.

Conclusion

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a useful corrective: DuPont is not a menu where the Board can pick different marketplaces to suit different factors. Once the Board embraces a factual finding about similarity, that finding should inform other inquiries that hinge on the contours of the marketplace. The decision favors principled coherence over opportunistic framing and gives practitioners a clear path: if services overlap, assemble and press third‑party evidence across the whole overlapping field, and respond swiftly when the Board’s analyses stray from a consistent market definition.



# What Counts as “Credit Card Services” When the ID Manual Entry Is Gone? Legacy Identifications, Fintech Structures, and Factor 2

Why this case matters: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a useful clinic on how tribunals should treat legacy identifications and industry structure when assessing relatedness under the second DuPont factor. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s conclusion that “credit card services” and a bank’s broadly worded “banking and financing services” are highly similar—even “legally identical, in part”—despite the Trademark ID Manual no longer listing “credit card services.” That holding signals that (1) old registration language can retain force when properly interpreted, (2) parsimonious parsing of industry roles (program manager vs. chartered bank) will not always inoculate a party from a finding of relatedness, and (3) applicants should be deliberate in how they describe services in intent‑to‑use filings (and challengers should marshal evidence about market realities).

What the court did: The Federal Circuit reviewed the Board’s DuPont analysis de novo as to law and for substantial evidence as to fact. It affirmed the Board’s second‑factor finding that Apex’s proposed “banking and financing services” (for an internet bank branded ASPIRE BANK) are highly similar to CC Serve’s longstanding registration for ASPIRE in connection with “credit card services.” The Board had read “credit card services” broadly to encompass issuing credit cards, processing transactions, account management (including monitoring and alerts), authorization, and counseling regarding card debt. The Board also relied on third‑party registrations and dictionary definitions showing overlap between “banking,” “finance,” and the extension of credit via card products. Apex’s argument that CC Serve only functions as a program manager for issuing banks did not persuade: the Board’s factfinding that the services overlap was supported by substantial evidence, and the Federal Circuit declined to upset that factual conclusion.

Key pieces of reasoning to keep in mind
- Legacy identification language can be construed broadly. The Board started with the text of CC Serve’s 1998 registration, filled in the meaning of “credit card services” with dictionary and documentary evidence, and treated that legacy ID as still operative despite changes in the ID Manual after the registration issued. The Federal Circuit affirmed that approach where the Board tied its construction to record evidence of what the services entail.
- Functional reality matters. The Board paid attention to how card programs are delivered in the market—issuance, processing, account servicing, authorization, and monitoring—that overlap conceptually with core banking and financing services. The fact that CC Serve is not itself a chartered bank but partners with banks did not defeat similarity when the functions could reasonably be perceived by consumers as part of the same family of financial services.
- Third‑party registrations and definitions can bridge the gap. The Board’s reliance on third‑party registrations that bundle card and banking/finance services reinforced the conclusion that consumers might view the services as emanating from a single source under one mark.

A critical gloss on the court’s approach
The Federal Circuit’s posture is properly deferential on mixed fact–law questions: it will not disturb the Board’s factual findings if supported by substantial evidence. That standard explains much of the outcome here. But the opinion also illustrates a few doctrinal lines that practitioners should note.

First, the decision reaffirms that the DuPont relatedness inquiry is elastic and driven by market realities, not rigid labels. Trademark identifications are important, but they are not dispositive in a vacuum; tribunals will look beyond four corners of the registration to industry practice and consumer perception. For registrants seeking broad protection, the upside is evident: a well‑drafted legacy registration with a broadly understood term may continue to sweep in newer forms of financial services. For applicants seeking registration in the crowded fintech space, the downside is that simply using a different corporate structure (program manager vs. issuing bank) will not necessarily create distance in the consumer’s eye.

Second, the opinion implicitly endorses a pragmatic use of dictionary definitions and third‑party registrations to flesh out old identifications when the ID Manual has moved on. The Board did not treat the deletion of the ID Manual entry as fatal; instead, it used contemporaneous definitional and marketplace evidence to read the legacy ID. That is unsurprising, but it’s a reminder that the ID Manual is a tool, not a final oracle. Courts and the TTAB will keep leaning on other objective sources when construing services—especially where technological or business‑model change has outpaced the Manual.

Third, this decision highlights a drafting and prosecution imperative: specificity (or intentional breadth). Applicants who want to avoid being shoehorned into a competitor’s prior registration should narrowly tailor their identifications to exclude services that could be viewed as overlapping (for example, carving out “credit card issuance and processing” if they do not intend to provide those functions). Conversely, registrants who wish to maximize protection should ensure that their registrations describe services in a manner that captures real‑world functions associated with their mark.

Practical implications for fintechs, banks, and program managers
- For fintech program managers who do not hold charters: your back‑office or white‑label role may still create an overlap in the eyes of consumers. Even if a fintech is contractually a non‑bank servicer, TTAB/CFCA review will treat the functional attributes—cards issued in partnership with banks, account management, transaction processing—as relevant to relatedness.
- For banks offering internet banking or white‑label brands: beware legacy registrations in related corners of the financial services market. A preexisting mark claiming “credit card services” may be read to cover broader “banking and financing” consumer impressions, so clearance searches and oppositions should explicitly address functional overlap.
- For trademark counsel preparing ITU applications: be strategic about scope. If you need room for growth, broader language might be fine, but expect pushback if the mark will sit near long‑standing registrations in adjacent financial services. If avoidance is the goal, consider expressly disclaiming or excluding categories that could trigger a DuPont conflict.
- For litigators and trademark examiners: gather functional evidence. Dictionary definitions, third‑party registrations that bundle services, and documentary proof of how services are presented to consumers are effective ways to support a finding of relatedness when the business model is mosaic rather than vertically integrated.

Limitations and open questions
Apex Bank also won partial relief: the Federal Circuit vacated the Board’s treatment of the sixth DuPont factor (number and nature of similar marks) because the Board had limited its survey to third‑party marks expressly tied to “credit card services,” while the Board had already found the parties’ services to be highly similar to broader banking/finance services. That is a useful equilibrium point: once the Board establishes a broad scope of relatedness under factor two, it must apply the same scope when assessing competitive crowding under factor six. Practically, that means a challenger who succeeds on a broad reading of services should be ready for a broader canvass of third‑party marks too—good news for defendants hoping to show weakness of a plaintiff’s mark, and a caution for plaintiffs relying on narrow surveys of third‑party uses.

Conclusion
Apex Bank v. CC Serve reinforces a simple but consequential principle in modern trademark disputes over financial services: word‑for‑word labels and corporate technicalities matter less than the services’ real‑world functions and consumer perceptions. Legacy identifications survive deletion from the ID Manual only if tribunals can anchor their meanings in evidence; and where the functions overlap—issuance, processing, authorization, and account servicing—banks and fintechs may find their marks treated as operating in the same trademark space. For practitioners, the lesson is tactical drafting and evidentiary preparation: define clearly, prove the market structure, and when you assert or attack a mark, control the narrative about what the services actually do and how consumers understand them.



# Strength Bleeds Into Similarity: Why Vacating Factor 6 Forced a Do‑Over on Factor 1

Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a useful reminder that trademark law doesn’t analyze DuPont factors in isolation. The Federal Circuit’s decision to affirm the Board on the services-similarity question (DuPont factor 2) but to vacate its treatment of the crowded-field evidence (factor 6) — and, critically, to remand factor 1 (similarity of the marks) as well — underscores a simple but often overlooked point: commercial strength and marketplace context are integral to how similar two marks will be perceived by consumers. Brand owners and challengers alike should heed the court’s message: evidence that a mark like ASPIRE is used broadly in a category can compress the scope of protection and materially alter the overall commercial impression of allegedly conflicting marks.

What the panel did and why

At the heart of the dispute was Apex’s attempt to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services against CC Serve’s earlier registration for the plain word ASPIRE covering credit card services. The Board found the parties’ services highly similar — even “legally identical, in part” — and so concluded confusion was likely. Apex appealed. The Federal Circuit affirmed that finding (factor 2) on substantial-evidence grounds, but it found two discrete errors in the Board’s treatment of other DuPont factors.

First, the Board’s factor 6 analysis — which asks whether the field is crowded with similar marks — improperly limited the universe of third-party uses to marks tied expressly to “credit card services,” excluding numerous third‑party uses of ASPIRE in other banking and financing contexts. Yet the Board itself had concluded the parties’ services were highly similar. The court held that, once services are found similar or overlapping, the Board must cast the net for third‑party marks across that same scope. Put differently, you cannot say the services are broad and overlapping for factor 2, then constrict the relevant market for factor 6.

Second, because factor 6 bears on a mark’s commercial strength (crowded fields tend to weaken a term’s source-identifying power), the court reasoned that a re-evaluation of the field could change the commercial impression consumers take from the marks. And commercial impression is the analytic core of factor 1 (similarity in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression). For that reason, vacating factor 6 compelled a do‑over on factor 1. The court remanded so the Board could reexamine the number and nature of third‑party ASPIRE uses across the full field of banking and financing services and then reassess the marks’ overall similarity in light of any revised view of ASPIRE’s strength.

Doctrinal implications: strength and similarity are woven together

The Federal Circuit’s remand reflects two intertwined doctrinal points that practitioners should internalize.

First, factor 6 is not an isolated tally of others using a word; it’s probative on the mark’s commercial strength — how source‑identifying the mark actually is in the marketplace. A term shown to be widely used in a category bears less distinctiveness in consumers’ eyes, which naturally narrows the scope of protection. That narrowed scope changes how much weight should be given to resemblance under factor 1. A weak, descriptive or widely used term will tolerate greater variation before confusion is likely; a strong, arbitrary or fanciful one will not.

Second, the opinion emphasizes consistency across factors. Once the Board takes a position about the breadth or identity of the relevant services (as it did here for factor 2), it should apply that same scope when considering third‑party uses for factor 6. The court refused to let a party have it both ways: broad, overlapping services to support confusion, but a narrow third‑party universe to deny evidence of generic or common usage. This is a commonsense application of the DuPont framework, but it is one that Boards and lawyers sometimes mistake in practice.

Practical lessons for advocates on remand

For challengers (owners of earlier marks): if you claim your mark is strong and entitled to broad protection, be prepared to defend that assertion with robust evidence that the term is unusual, fanciful, or commercially strong across the entire class of services the Board has found overlapping. Relying solely on registrations or uses narrowly focused on a subset of services is risky when the Board has already characterized the services more broadly.

For applicants (those seeking registration): if the challenged mark appears common in the field, assemble and present comprehensive, well-documented third‑party use evidence spanning all similar services the Board finds relevant. The Federal Circuit made clear that excluding uses because they are not word-for-word within a narrower subcategory (e.g., credit cards only) is error when the parties’ services overlap more broadly. On remand, that evidence could materially undercut a likelihood-of-confusion finding by showing ASPIRE is a crowded sign in banking and financing as a whole.

Specific forms of evidence to emphasize on remand include: contemporaneous commercial uses (websites, product names, program names), registrations covering a range of banking/finance services (not just credit cards), advertising materials showing how the word is used in market, and consumer-facing materials that illustrate how the public encounters ASPIRE‑formative names across different financial services. Demonstrating consumer sophistication or differentiation cues across contexts (how consumers actually distinguish brands in banking/finance) can also help calibrate both strength and commercial impression.

Broader downstream effects

Apex Bank v. CC Serve is likely to be cited as a tidy example of the interdependence of DuPont factors. Trademark examiners, TTAB judges, and litigators should expect courts to scrutinize any inconsistent scope choices across factors — for instance, finding wide service overlap to support confusion while simultaneously narrowing the field of third‑party uses to avoid finding weakness. The decision also reinscribes a practical reality: crowded fields dilute protection. In crowded categories like banking and finance — where many players favor aspirational, descriptive, or laudatory terms — it will be harder for any single user to claim exclusive rights absent strong evidence of distinctiveness or substantially different trade channels or consumer perceptions.

Conclusion

The Federal Circuit didn’t create new law in Apex Bank, but it did apply existing DuPont principles in a way that sharpens the lesson for practitioners: strength and similarity are not siloed inquiries. Evidence that ASPIRE is ubiquitous across banking and financing services can narrow the term’s protective reach and change how close ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK feel to consumers. On remand, the fate of Apex’s registrations will turn on whether the Board — now considering the full field of relevant third‑party uses — finds ASPIRE sufficiently weak that the addition of “BANK” and the design elements avoid likely confusion. Whatever the outcome, the decision is a practical roadmap: if you want a reliable resolution on likelihood of confusion, build your record to cover the full competitive field the Board has already acknowledged.



## TTAB Litigation in a Crowded Financial Marketplace: Building the Right Record After Apex v. CC Serve

Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a practical reminder that likelihood-of-confusion battles at the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board turn on careful record-building and consistent legal framing across the DuPont factors. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding that Apex’s planned ASPIRE BANK marks and CC Serve’s long‑registered ASPIRE mark cover highly similar services, but it vacated and remanded the Board’s treatment of two interrelated DuPont factors—six (third‑party use) and one (overall commercial impression)—because the Board narrowed the universe of third‑party marks it considered in a way inconsistent with its own finding on services. The decision is short, pragmatic, and directly relevant to brand owners and challengers in the financial-services sphere, where generic and descriptive terms (Aspire, Aspire‑formative marks) and overlapping service channels (banking, lending, payments, card programs) are common.

What the court did and why

The Federal Circuit applied familiar doctrine. It accepted the Board’s factual finding—supported by substantial evidence—that CC Serve’s “credit card services” and Apex’s identified “banking and financing services” are highly similar and partially legally identical. Having reached that conclusion under DuPont factor two, the court held that the Board erred when it tightened the scope of DuPont factor six to consider only third‑party marks used explicitly for “credit card services,” excluding third‑party ASPIR E–formative uses across other banking and financing services. Because factor six (crowding/consumer education) bears directly on the commercial strength of the prior mark, the court vacated the Board’s factor‑six analysis and—because commercial impression under factor one depends on that strength—also vacated the Board’s factor‑one analysis and remanded.

That sequence is straightforward but revealing: once the Board determined the services were highly similar, it could not then constrict the relevant marketplace for evaluating third‑party use without creating an inconsistent analytical posture. The court’s remedy was not to decide who wins; it directed the Board to consider third‑party ASPIR E uses across the full range of services the Board had already found similar, and to re‑assess commercial impression accordingly.

Analytical perspective

Two doctrinal points follow. First, DuPont is an integrated inquiry; findings on one factor can and should inform the scope of others. The Federal Circuit did not invent a new rule, but it underscored that the Board must be coherent: a broad finding of relatedness requires treating third‑party marks used on any similar services as relevant to crowding/weakness and consumer sophistication questions. Second, evidence of program‑manager or co‑branded structures (as with CC Serve’s role managing bank‑issued ASPIRE cards) will not automatically immunize a mark from being “related” to other financial services. The Board’s original conclusion that the services were highly similar defeated any narrow framing that tried to cabin relevant third‑party uses to a single subcategory.

Practical implications for brand owners and challengers

For applicants and registrants in finance and payments, the decision highlights how to shape a winning record—or defeat one.

For challengers defending older marks, start by establishing the correct universe of comparison. If you argue services are broad and encompassing, accept the logical consequence: the broader the services you claim are related, the broader the set of third‑party uses the Board may consider. That can cut both ways—broad relatedness strengthens confusion arguments but also exposes the mark to more evidence of crowding and consumer sophistication.

For applicants (or opposers challenging registrations), do not assume that a contractual or program‑manager role insulates the prior registrant’s mark from relatedness. If the registrant’s mark is used in connection with credit card issuance and account servicing, show why consumers perceive a distinct source for your banking or deposit services despite program structures—survey evidence, consumer testimony, or examples of branding practices that clearly separate the services can be decisive.

On third‑party evidence and the crowded field

Apex teaches that the Board must consider third‑party marks across all services already found similar. Practically, that means collecting and presenting a full spectrum of third‑party registrations and uses that link the contested term to any banking, lending, payments, card‑program, or account‑servicing context—not just to the hyper‑specific functional label that the opponent’s registration uses. Quantify and contextualize third‑party use. Mere counts of registrations are less persuasive than targeted evidence showing consumer exposure and market practices that teach consumers to distinguish marks (co‑branding, brand architecture, how consumers encounter services in the real world).

The decision also clarifies the role of consumer education. Courts treat crowding evidence not only as a proxy for descriptive weakness but as an indication that consumers in a crowded field will be more adept at distinguishing similarly worded marks. To persuade the Board that factor six favors narrow scope, produce evidence that consumers commonly encounter many similar marks across the relevant service types and thus have been “educated” to differentiate them—real‑world examples, marketing channels, and survey work can help.

Strategic considerations for intent‑to‑use filings and prosecution

Apex filed intent‑to‑use applications for ASPIRE BANK. The case underscores the risks when seeking registration of common or evocative terms in a field populated with prior uses. During prosecution, applicants should anticipate a robust third‑party record and take steps to shape the ID and record: specify services carefully, be ready to argue how the proposed services differ in channels of trade and consumer classes, and when possible, submit evidence showing distinctive branding or service features that reduce the likelihood of source confusion.

On the evidentiary side, don’t over‑rely on the ID Manual or dictionary definitions alone. The Board drew on dictionary definitions and the deletion of a Trademark ID Manual entry to define services; courts will accept such definitions, but real marketplace evidence—how consumers actually encounter and use services—is often dispositive in DuPont analysis. Present clear factual record on how services are marketed, who purchases them, and how the parties’ marks are used in the market.

Positioning factor 6 so it informs factor 1

The Federal Circuit’s remand illuminates a tactical lesson: litigators should present the third‑party use and crowding evidence in a way that naturally feeds into the overall commercial impression analysis. That requires two related moves: first, build the broad record of third‑party uses across all similar service categories; second, explicitly link that crowding evidence to the mark’s commercial strength and to the perception of the mark in the marketplace. Do not treat factor six as a compartmentalized sidebar; show the Board how crowding alters the commercial impression and, accordingly, the scope of protection the prior mark merits.

Bottom line

Apex v. CC Serve is a pragmatic, record‑focused decision. It does not change the law, but it insists on internal coherence: once the Board finds broad service relatedness, it must evaluate third‑party crowding across that same breadth and then re‑examine commercial impression. For practitioners in financial services—where overlapping offerings and shared terminology are the norm—the case is a clear call to marshal comprehensive third‑party evidence, tailor service identifications and market evidence carefully, and connect the dots between crowding, mark strength, and commercial impression in a way the Board cannot ignore.
