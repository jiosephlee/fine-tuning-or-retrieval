Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

Chapter 1: # Foundations of Louisiana Premium-Financed Insurance Cancellations

Insurance premium finance agreements create a triangular commercial relationship: an insured obtains an insurance policy from an insurer but finances the premium through a third-party finance company, which pays the insurer up front and is repaid by the insured in installments. Because the finance company advances the premium, it often acquires contractual rights to protect its loan — most importantly, a power of attorney authorizing the finance company to cancel the policy on the insured’s behalf upon uncured nonpayment. Louisiana has codified a detailed statutory procedure that a premium finance company must follow before it may exercise that power of attorney to effect cancellation. Louisiana courts have long insisted on strict adherence to that statutory procedure; any defect in the process can render the cancellation ineffective and leave coverage intact. Recent federal appellate guidance in Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, 5th Cir. No. 24-30646 (Oct. 3, 2025), illustrates how the statutory text controls in a modern electronic world and helps define which procedural defects are fatal and which are not.

This chapter explains the commercial context for premium-finance cancellations, parses the statutory architecture of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G), describes the strict-compliance doctrine and its purposes, and uses Williams and the Louisiana cases it discusses to illustrate how courts resolve disputes about signatures, electronic transmission, timing, and receipt. The chapter closes with practical compliance steps and litigation considerations that a student should be able to apply to new fact patterns.

## Premium finance agreements: roles of the insurer, insured, and finance company

Premium finance arrangements solve a simple problem: many consumers and businesses lack the liquidity to pay a full annual premium at policy inception. A premium finance company (sometimes called a premium finance lender) pays the insurer the premium in exchange for the insured’s promissory obligation to repay the finance company in installments. Contractually, the parties allocate rights and duties among the insurer (who issues the coverage), the insured (who is the policyholder and beneficiary of the coverage), and the finance company (who finances the premium and protects its collateral — the insurer’s obligation to indemnify losses occurring during the policy term).

To protect its position, the finance company commonly requires a power of attorney in the premium finance agreement. That power is generally limited in scope: it authorizes the finance company to cancel the policy on the insured’s behalf if the insured defaults on installment payments and fails to cure within a specified period. Because a cancellation strips an insured of coverage (and may expose third parties to uninsured risk), Louisiana law subjects the exercise of that power to statutory safeguards designed to ensure adequate notice and to fix the timing of cancellation.

Understanding these roles clarifies why the statute speaks to actions by the finance company rather than direct action by the insured: the finance company acts as an agent with a narrowly defined authority, but the law conditions that agency power on specific procedural steps that concretely inform the insurer, the insured, and other interested parties when coverage terminates.

## Power of attorney in premium finance agreements and its legal effect

A power of attorney in a premium finance agreement is a contractual delegation of authority from the insured to the finance company. Its legal effect is not to create a carte blanche right to make coverage-altering decisions; Louisiana law channels that authority. The statute prescribes what the finance company must do before its act of cancellation will be recognized by the insurer. The power of attorney supplies the underlying agency authority, but the statutory requirements supply a public safeguard: the insurer need not accept the unilateral assertion of default by a finance company unless the finance company has complied with the statutorily specified notice and certification steps.

Because the power of attorney is exercised against the background of the statutory framework, questions frequently arise in litigation: Is the power of attorney valid on its face? Was the finance company in actual default under the financing agreement? Did the finance company send the statutorily required notices? Courts resolve those questions by looking to the contract language for agency scope, the finance company’s records for proof of default and notice, and the statute for formal sufficiency.

## Statutory architecture of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G): when and how a finance company may cancel

La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G) structures the only path by which a premium finance company may effect a cancellation using its power of attorney. The relevant subsections set out a sequence of required events and the contents of a certification the finance company must provide to the insurer. Two aspects are central.

First, the finance company must send a ten-day notice of cancellation to the insured upon default; the notice must specify the date it was sent and thereby create a fixed cure period during which the insured may tender the missed payment. Second, before the insurer may treat the policy as cancelled, the statute requires the finance company to send the insurer a copy of that notice together with a statement “certifying” several enumerated facts: (i) that the finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney; (ii) that the agreement is in default and that the default has not been timely cured; (iii) that upon default a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured and specifying the date of sending; and (iv) that copies of the notice were sent to all persons shown by the finance agreement to have an interest in any loss, with the names and addresses listed. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv) (these are the elements Williams quotes).

Once the insurer has “receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company,” the insurer “shall consider that cancellation of the contract has been requested” and may proceed with cancellation. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). In short: (1) the finance company must provide the insured with a ten-day cure notice; (2) the finance company must provide the insurer with a certification containing specific items; and (3) the insurer must receive that certification before it may cancel.

That statutory architecture does two jobs, and understanding both is essential to doctrinal analysis.

## The ten‑day cure period and timing: fixing the date and time of cancellation

Louisiana courts have repeatedly explained that the statute serves at least two complementary functions. First, it creates a clear method for fixing the exact time and date of cancellation; second, it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured. See Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987) (articulating these twin purposes). The ten-day cure period is thus not a mere formality; it is the statutory protection that allows an insured the opportunity to avoid the loss of coverage by curing the missed premium before the cancellation takes effect.

Practically, litigation about timing reduces to documentary and electronic evidence: When did the finance company mail or otherwise send its ten-day notice to the insured? Did the notice itself specify the date of sending as required? When did the finance company transmit the certification to the insurer? Did the insurer receive and acknowledge it before effecting cancellation? Because Louisiana has held that the process fixes the exact time and date, courts will look for contemporaneous records — mailing affidavits, electronic time stamps, server logs, web-service call traces — to determine whether the statutory timeline was observed. In Britten and the other pre-electronic cases, the documentary record was often a physical affidavit or stamped mailing; in contemporary cases, courts must reconcile electronic methods with the statute’s text and its purposes.

## Required contents of the finance company’s certification to the insurer: elements (i)–(iv)

The statute’s enumerated elements are the engine of strict compliance. The finance company’s certification must, at minimum, assert each of the following:

- (i) That the premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney as provided by the statute. This is a threshold jurisdictional fact for exercising the agency power; absent a valid power of attorney the finance company has no authority to operate on the insured’s behalf to cancel.

- (ii) That the premium finance agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured. The certification must allow the insurer to understand that the finance company asserts an uncured default exists.

- (iii) That upon default a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured as required, specifying the date of sending by the finance company to the insured. This connects to the ten-day cure period and supplies the concrete date by which the insured had the opportunity to cure.

- (iv) That copies of the notice were sent to all persons shown by the premium finance agreement to have an interest in any loss, specifying names and addresses of any governmental agencies, lienholders, or third parties notified. This requirement protects third-party interests that could be affected by a sudden loss of coverage.

The certification functions as both informational predicate and a documentary trigger: the insurer relies on it to know that it has the statutory authorization to cancel; the insured relies on it (and on the underlying ten-day notice) for the opportunity to cure. Courts scrutinize the content — not merely the form — of the certification. If the certification fails to state one of the elements, a court will likely find the cancellation defective.

Williams v. GoAuto makes clear that the statutory requirement is satisfied so long as the finance company’s certification communicates the required facts: the Fifth Circuit rejected a rule that the statutory word “certifying” implies a signature is required. That holding turns on the statute’s text and on the absence of controlling Louisiana precedent to the contrary.

## Who must receive notice: insureds and persons with an interest in any loss

The statute insists that the ten-day notice be sent to the insured and that copies be sent to any persons shown in the finance agreement to have an interest in any loss — e.g., lienholders, governmental agencies, or other named third parties. The requirement of naming the names and addresses in the certification ensures that the insurer (and ultimately a reviewing court) can verify that those parties were informed. The purpose is straightforward: third parties who have a pecuniary interest in the insured property (such as a mortgagee or motor vehicle lienholder) should be able to protect themselves against the momentary lapse of coverage that results from cancellation.

Failure to deliver the notice to such persons, or failure to include their names and addresses in the certification, is one of the defects that Louisiana courts have held can render a cancellation ineffective. The statutory scheme ties the finance company’s power to the performance of these notice obligations — they are not optional niceties.

## Strict adherence (strict compliance) doctrine in Louisiana cancellations

Louisiana courts have repeatedly said that the statutory procedure must be strictly followed when a finance company seeks to cancel a policy under a power of attorney. See Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993) (“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.”); Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990) (“[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.”). Britten v. Reavis explains why: strict compliance (1) fixes the exact time and date of cancellation and (2) guarantees a minimum cure period. These twin concerns explain the doctrine’s force — courts will not lightly excuse deviation from the steps that create those protections.

But “strict adherence” is not an invitation for courts to rewrite the statute by judicial fiat. The Fifth Circuit in Williams emphasized that courts must enforce the statute’s textual requirements but may not layer on extra requirements that the Legislature did not adopt. Thus, a court must demand the requisite content in the finance company’s certification and proof of transmission and receipt, but it need not impose a signature requirement if the statute does not command one and existing precedent does not compel it. The doctrine therefore operates as a strict textualism: the steps required by the statute must be observed to the letter, but judges should not manufacture additional formalities not grounded in statutory text or controlling precedent.

This distinction matters for litigants: a plaintiff challenging a cancellation must show a failure to comply with a statutory element; a defendant seeking to uphold a cancellation can point to compliance with the statute’s text — which in a modern context may be satisfied by electronic communications and metadata, as Williams demonstrates.

## Consequences of defects: ineffective cancellation and continuation of coverage

Louisiana’s remedy for a defective cancellation is severe: courts have held that any defect in the statutorily required process renders the cancellation ineffective. See Eaglin, 558 So. 2d at 287. The legal consequence of an ineffective cancellation is that the policy remains in force as if no cancellation ever took place; the insurer remains liable for losses that occur within the policy period unless some other policy defense applies. This doctrinal rule reflects the statute’s protective aim — to prevent inadvertent or hasty termination of coverage that would leave insureds and third parties exposed.

Because the consequence is so substantial, litigation over the sufficiency of cancellation notices is often fact- and record-intensive. Courts examine mailing affidavits, certified copies of notices, database logs, timestamps, proof of service, and the face of the certification statement. If the statutorily prescribed certification is absent, incomplete, or not received by the insurer before the insurer effects cancellation, the insured (or a third-party claimant) can argue that coverage continued beyond the supposed cancellation date and that the insurer remains on the hook for losses that occurred in that interval.

Williams v. GoAuto exemplifies this dynamic. The plaintiffs challenged a cancellation sequence in which APAC (the finance company) sent e-mail cancellation requests to GoAuto’s computer systems, which automatically cancelled the policies upon receipt, with a printed and stamped signature only added later. The plaintiffs argued that the lack of a signed certification before cancellation and the fact that physical handling of the documents occurred after the electronic cancellation meant that the statutory prerequisites had not been satisfied. The Fifth Circuit, reading the statute’s text and precedents, held that (a) the statute does not require a signature, and (b) “receipt” by the insurer can be accomplished by electronic means. Because the finance company’s e-mailed certification communicated the required facts and GoAuto’s systems received it before effecting cancellation, the cancellation was effective.

## Interaction with extra‑contractual theories: misrepresentation and breach of good faith and fair dealing when cancellation is ineffective

When a cancellation is defective and the policy remains in force, litigants sometimes press extra-contractual theories against insurers and finance companies. Two typical claims are misrepresentation and breach of the insurer’s duty of good faith and fair dealing.

A misrepresentation claim arises when an insurer or finance company falsely represents the status of coverage. If the finance company or insurer tells a claimant (or one of the insureds) that coverage has terminated when, in fact, the statutory prerequisites were not met and the policy remains effective, an injured third party may assert that it relied on a false representation and suffered harm as a result. To prevail, the claimant ordinarily must prove the falsity of the representation, reasonable reliance (in some contexts), and damages flowing from the misrepresentation.

A breach-of-good-faith claim (often framed as an insurance bad-faith action) requires a showing that the insurer breached the duty to act in good faith toward its insured — for example, by misrepresenting coverage or by unreasonably denying or delaying benefits. Louisiana recognizes that insurers owe certain duties to insureds; if an insurer relies on a purported cancellation that is legally ineffective and then refuses to honor a claim arising during the period the policy remained effective, an insured may pursue contractual and tort remedies. The results turn on the facts: whether the insurer honestly believed the cancellation was valid, whether it had a reasonable basis for denying coverage, and whether the insured suffered consequential damages.

Williams is instructive: had the Fifth Circuit concluded that the cancellation was ineffective, plaintiffs might have had viable claims that GoAuto misrepresented coverage status or breached duties of good faith by allowing coverage to lapse in form while representing it had been cancelled. Because the court found compliance, Williams is a reminder that resolution of procedural questions about cancellations often resolves broader extra-contractual claims.

## The signature question and electronic receipt: Williams v. GoAuto as a contemporary guide

Williams v. GoAuto crystallizes two contemporary doctrinal tensions: (1) whether “certifying” requires a person’s signature, and (2) whether electronic transmissions and automated system responses constitute the insurer’s “receipt” under the statute.

On the signature issue, the plaintiffs in Williams argued that the statutory word “certifying” implies a contemporaneous signature — that the finance company must sign the certification before cancellation. The Fifth Circuit rejected that implication. It noted that the statute does not explicitly demand a signature and observed that Louisiana appellate decisions interpreting the statute have not insisted on a signature as a standalone requirement. Where a previous case (Benitez v. Elsayed, 285 So. 3d 572 (La. App. 5 Cir. 2019)) had mentioned the absence of a signature as a criticism of a notice’s shortcomings, it did not hold that lack of signature, alone, invalidated cancellation. The Fifth Circuit therefore concluded that the statutory requirement that “the insurance premium finance company” certify the enumerated facts can be satisfied without an individual employee’s signature and without identifying the employee who prepared the document. The lesson is that, under current Louisiana practice as interpreted by the Fifth Circuit, formality in the sense of an actual handwritten or stamped signature is not automatically required where the content of the certification and method of transmission demonstrate that the finance company has “certified” the facts.

On the receipt and electronic-transmission issue, Williams is more affirmative. The finance company’s computer system automatically e-mailed the certification to GoAuto’s web service; that web service acted like a listener, received the request, and the insurer’s system performed the cancellation automatically. The Fifth Circuit accepted that electronic receipt of the notice and certification by GoAuto’s systems sufficed as receipt under § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). The court relied on dictionary definitions of “receipt” — “the act of receiving something, especially by taking physical possession” and “to take (something offered, given, sent, etc.)” — and concluded that modern corporate computer systems may take “possession, delivery, or custody” of a communication. Put simply, an insurer’s automated system, when it accepts and acts on a finance company’s electronic cancellation request, receives that request for purposes of the statute.

Williams therefore stands for a narrow but important proposition: the statutory prerequisites may be satisfied electronically; courts should examine the operative records (e-mail timestamps, server logs, web-service traces) to determine whether insurer receipt occurred before cancellation; and absence of a signature is not per se fatal where compliance with the statute’s other content and transmission requirements can be proven. But the decision does not authorize sloppy records. Because the statute demands strict compliance, finance companies and insurers are wise to maintain contemporaneous, auditable records that demonstrate both the finance company’s transmission and the insurer’s receipt.

## Practical consequences and litigation strategies

For plaintiffs challenging a cancellation, Williams and the prior Louisiana cases point to several fruitful lines of attack: show that the finance company’s certification omitted an element (i)–(iv); demonstrate that the ten-day notice to the insured was not sent or did not specify a date; show that copies were not sent to persons with an interest; or show that the insurer never received the certification prior to effecting cancellation. Documentary gaps — missing affidavits of mailing, absent log files, or inconsistent timestamps — will often be decisive. Because cancellation effectiveness often turns on dates and moments, small discrepancies can produce large doctrinal results.

For defendants (finance companies and insurers), Williams suggests several defensive steps: preserve digital evidence of transmission and receipt (server logs, e-mail headers, web-service call logs), maintain a contemporaneous affidavit of mailing or proof of delivery, include explicit language in the certification addressing each enumerated statutory element, and — although the law may not require it — consider including an identified signer (with printed name and title) on the certification to preempt signature-based challenges. Even though Williams says a signature is not required, placing a named signer on the certification reduces litigation risk by aligning practice with former courts’ expectations expressed in cases such as Benitez.

On summary judgment strategy, a moving party must present undisputed proof that the statutory prerequisites were or were not satisfied. Williams confirms that in a modern context electronic evidence can be as probative as physical stamps or filing-cabinet entries. Courts will evaluate whether the insurer’s automated processes received the certification before cancellation and whether the finance company produced contemporaneous evidence of its mailing to the insured and to the listed interested parties.

## Policy considerations: balancing protection of insureds and operational efficiency

The statutory scheme embodies a policy balance: it protects insureds and third parties from sudden loss of coverage by requiring clear notice and a cure period, while also enabling finance companies to protect their financial interests cheaply and efficiently. Requiring strict compliance ensures the protective function is not illusory, but slavish formalism would frustrate legitimate commerce — particularly where technology allows reliable electronic proof of transmission and receipt. Williams illustrates a middle way: preserve the statute’s protections by enforcing its content and timing requirements, but allow reasonable modern means of satisfying those requirements when the record proves they were observed.

## Checklist for compliance and litigation preparation

To apply the doctrine to new fact patterns or to ensure compliance, practitioners should focus on these concrete items:

- Confirm the finance agreement contains a clear, valid power of attorney consistent with the statute. Preserve a copy.

- Send a ten-day notice to the insured that specifies the precise date on which the notice was sent and the date by which a cure must be received.

- Send copies of the notice to all persons identified in the finance agreement as having an interest in any loss, and record the names and addresses.

- Prepare a certification to the insurer that expressly states elements (i)–(iv) of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

- Preserve proof of sending the ten-day notice to the insured (mailing affidavits, carrier tracking, e-mail headers) and proof of sending and receipt of the certification by the insurer (e-mail headers, server logs, web-service call traces, automated acknowledgments).

- If practical, include an identifiable signer (printed name and title) on the certification and maintain a contemporaneous log showing when an employee authorized or reviewed the transmission.

- Ensure that the insurer’s systems record the time of receipt and the time of any automatic cancellation, and preserve those logs for litigation.

- If a dispute arises, assemble the chain of documents and electronic records showing the time the ten-day notice was sent, the finance company’s certification was transmitted, and the insurer’s receipt occurred.

## Conclusion

Louisiana’s premium-finance cancellation statute creates a tightly-framed path for a finance company to exercise a contractual power of attorney to cancel an insured’s policy for nonpayment. The statutory elements — a ten-day cure notice to the insured, copies to interested parties, and a certification to the insurer asserting the specified facts — must be strictly observed. But strict adherence means enforcement of the statutory text, not judicial invention of extra formalities. Williams v. GoAuto Insurance (5th Cir. 2025) illustrates how courts reconcile strictness with modern electronic practices: absent statutory text requiring a signature, certification may be effective without one, and an insurer’s electronic receipt of a finance company’s transmitted certification can satisfy the statute’s “receipt” requirement. For students and practitioners alike, the cases teach two enduring lessons: (1) timing and contemporaneous records decide these disputes, and (2) compliance requires attention to both substantive content and proof of the mechanics of transmission and receipt.
