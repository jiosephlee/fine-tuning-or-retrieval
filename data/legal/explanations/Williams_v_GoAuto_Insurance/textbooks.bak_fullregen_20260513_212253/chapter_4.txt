Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

Chapter 4: # Predicting State Law and the Certification Decision

Federal courts sitting in diversity frequently confront unsettled questions of state law. The Erie doctrine requires them to apply the substantive law of the forum state, but Erie does not tell a federal judge how to proceed when the state's highest court has not yet spoken. This chapter explains the federal court’s role in predicting state law, the option of certifying a question to a state supreme court, and the Fifth Circuit’s restrictive posture toward certification. Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, 5th Cir. No. 24-30646 (Oct. 3, 2025), provides a concise illustration of these principles in action: the court predicted Louisiana law rather than certify, resolving statutory ambiguities about how an insurance-premium finance company may effectuate cancellation.

This chapter will (1) situate the task within Erie; (2) describe the Fifth Circuit’s standards and the “chary” approach to certification exemplified by Jefferson v. Lead Industries; (3) explain why the mere absence of controlling precedent ordinarily does not compel certification; and (4) give practical advice to litigants on when to seek certification and how to build a record that helps the federal court either predict state law or decide to certify.

## Erie’s command and the federal court’s task of prediction

Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins and its progeny require a federal court in a diversity action to apply the substantive law of the state in which it sits. Where the state’s highest court has issued controlling authority on an issue, the federal court must follow it. The harder question is what to do when the state high court has not spoken. The settled approach is that the federal court must predict how the state supreme court would rule and then apply that prediction. To do so, the federal court looks first to decisions of the state’s highest court, then to decisions of the state’s intermediate appellate courts, and then to other sources: persuasive opinions from other states, statutes, regulations, restatements, the text and context of the state statute, relevant legislative history, dictionaries and treatises, and policy considerations.

Prediction is an exercise in reasoned judgment: the judge attempts to synthesize whatever guidance exists into the best available answer to the state-law question. Courts generally favor narrow, text-based reasoning anchored in state precedent and analogous authority over broad federal policy pronouncements. The Williams opinion illustrates this approach. There, the Fifth Circuit recognized the Louisiana statute’s text, surveyed Louisiana appellate decisions, and relied on dictionary definitions and the modern technological context to conclude that Louisiana law did not require a physical, signed certification before an insurer could effect cancellation. The court emphasized that it would not, in the name of "strict adherence," add requirements the legislature had not prescribed. That reasoning is a classic Erie-conscious prediction: rely on state law materials and avoid creating de novo state law.

## The certification mechanism and the Fifth Circuit’s standard

When the question is genuinely one of state law and the federal court believes the state supreme court is better positioned to resolve it, the federal court may certify the question to the state’s highest court—if the state’s procedure allows certification. Certification is not a jurisdictional right of the litigants; it is a discretionary tool that federal courts may use to obtain authoritative state-law answers. Many states have rules that permit federal courts to certify questions to their highest courts; the precise mechanism and the state court’s willingness to accept are governed by state law and by the state court’s discretion.

The Fifth Circuit has articulated a cautious, restrictive approach to certification. In Jefferson v. Lead Industries Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245 (5th Cir. 1997), the court said that it is “chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so,” because certification is a valuable, limited resource and federal courts should not overuse it. The Fifth Circuit repeated that posture in Williams: the plaintiffs urged certification of whether the Louisiana statute required a signed certification and what “receipt” meant under modern practices. The Fifth Circuit declined, explaining that the mere absence of a state supreme court decision on the precise point was insufficient to warrant certification.

Although the Fifth Circuit’s “chary” language conveys restraint, it does not erect a bright-line bar to certification. The Fifth Circuit and other courts will certify when special circumstances exist—typically when (1) the state-law question is novel and dispositive of the case; (2) state appellate authority is sparse, conflicting, or insufficient to permit a reasoned prediction; (3) clarification by the state’s highest court would significantly affect the development of state law or resolve an important public or industry question; or (4) when application of incorrect or uncertain law would produce serious adverse practical consequences. Even with these factors, the Fifth Circuit will consider whether certification is likely to yield a timely, authoritative answer, and whether its use is appropriate given the case’s posture and the public interest.

## Jefferson and the "chary" approach: what it means in practice

Jeffer son’s admonition that the court will be “chary” about certification serves several functions. First, it preserves certification as an exceptional remedy for the most suitable cases, preventing routine deferral of questions that federal courts can acceptably predict. Second, it avoids imposing unnecessary burdens and delay on litigants and the state courts. Third, it deters forum-shopping for certification.

In practice, the Jefferson rule means that a federal court will decline to certify where the unsettled issue can be resolved by careful analysis of statutory text and putatively analogous state-court opinions. Williams exemplifies this: the Fifth Circuit identified a number of Louisiana appellate opinions discussing the statute, none of which squarely addressed the signature issue. But the court concluded that existing authorities, the statute’s language, and dictionary definitions provided a sufficient basis for a principled prediction. The court was also mindful of not “risk[ing] [the] continued availability” of certification by using it too often. Thus, the chary approach does not signal hostility to certification in any case where state guidance is truly needed, but it does require a showing that prediction is impractical or likely to risk error that only the state court can avoid.

## Why absence of controlling precedent is usually not enough

A common impulse for litigants and even trial judges is to treat any unsettled question of law as a candidate for certification. The Fifth Circuit’s practice counsels otherwise. Lack of controlling precedent alone rarely justifies certification because federal courts are tasked with predicting state law; they are not expected to wait until a state appellate court has spoken first on every novel issue. Several practical and doctrinal reasons underlie this rule.

First, federal courts are competent to interpret statutes and reconcile lower-court precedents. The Erie principles require them to do so. Williams shows this: faced with no state-high-court ruling on whether “certifying” required a signature, the Fifth Circuit turned to the statute’s text (which refers to “the insurance premium finance company” certifying, not an individual employee), to Louisiana intermediate-court decisions that used language like “statement certifying compliance” or “upon receipt of such notice,” and to a dictionary definition for “receipt.” That synthesis was deemed sufficient.

Second, certification can substantially delay resolution, add cost, and is often discretionary on the receiving state supreme court’s part. State courts curate their dockets and may decline certification, producing wasted effort and further delay. Federal courts must weigh whether a certification will materially help and whether the state court is likely to accept it. If the federal court can reach a defensible prediction without certification, it will often do so.

Third, certification should be reserved for genuinely unsettled legal terrains—issues of wide public importance, conflicting intermediate-court decisions, or ambiguous state law that simple predictive tools cannot resolve. If the question is narrow or can be resolved on textual or closely analogous authorities, the federal court should decide.

## Williams v. GoAuto: how the Fifth Circuit predicted rather than certified

Williams offers a succinct demonstration of the principles above. The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto’s cancellation procedures violated a Louisiana statute governing cancellation of financed policies because (1) APAC’s certification was not signed until after GoAuto’s computer had already effected cancellation, and (2) GoAuto had not “received” the notice in the sense of taking physical possession. The plaintiffs therefore sought certification of the relevant statutory questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court.

The Fifth Circuit did not regard the absence of a state-high-court pronouncement on those precise issues as dispositive. Instead, it surveyed the statutory text, Louisiana intermediate appellate opinions, and even used dictionary definitions to interpret “certifying” and “receipt.” The court highlighted that the statute speaks of “the insurance premium finance company” certifying certain items, and it refused to graft an individualized signature requirement onto that statutory language. On “receipt,” the court adopted ordinary dictionary meanings—possession, delivery, or custody—and concluded that receipt by GoAuto’s automated systems satisfied the statutory requirement, because the insurer’s web service received the cancellation request and acted upon it. The court also rejected the district court’s novel reliance on a broad interpretation of “private carrier.” Because the federal court could, within Erie constraints, arrive at a reasoned answer, certification was unnecessary. The plaintiffs’ request to certify was denied; the federal court’s prediction resolved the case.

Williams thus demonstrates two points: (1) a federal court will use all relevant state-law materials to predict how the state’s highest court would rule; and (2) certification is reserved for those situations where such prediction would be inadequate, impractical, or likely to produce serious error.

## Strategic considerations for litigants: when to seek certification and how to frame the question

Because federal courts are often reluctant to certify, litigants should be strategic about when and how to ask for certification. A few practical guidelines follow.

1. Reserve certification for truly novel, dispositive questions or where there is clear conflict in intermediate appellate courts. Certification has the best chance of success when the question is outcome-determinative, important to the state’s law and policy, and likely to be accepted by the state supreme court because it would clarify an area of public or doctrinal significance.

2. Raise certification early and explain its necessity. A timely, well-supported motion or request for certification will carry more weight. Explain why the federal court cannot responsibly predict the outcome, why the state supreme court’s authoritative answer is likely to be accepted and would resolve important issues, and why certification will not create undue delay.

3. Frame the question narrowly and concretely. State supreme courts are more likely to accept a narrow, clean question than a broad open-ended one. Define the precise statutory term or doctrinal point and the factual context in which it arises. For example, asking “Does the Louisiana statute require a dated signature on a cancellation certification before an insurer’s automated system may lawfully cancel?” is preferable to a sweeping question about the statute’s entire purpose.

4. Demonstrate conflict or uncertainty in state law. If intermediate appellate courts have issued conflicting decisions, make that point explicit. If the issue implicates important statewide interests—insurance regulation, consumer protection, or significant economic consequences—highlight those stakes.

5. Show the question is outcome-determinative. If certification will resolve the litigation or a class certification issue, that strengthens the argument. Identify how an authoritative state answer will dispose of claims, reduce litigation cost, or create uniformity in the law.

6. Predict how the state supreme court will treat the issue. Part of persuading a federal court to certify is showing that the state court is likely to accept the question. Cite the state court’s past willingness to accept certified questions on similar subjects or to clarify the statute at issue.

7. Prepare fallback argumentation. Because certification is uncertain, craft a comprehensive brief that both urges certification and supplies a robust prediction of state law as an alternative. The federal court can then either certify or adopt the offered prediction; the latter preserves appellate review.

## Building the record for prediction or certification

Whether asking for certification or asking the federal court to predict state law, the litigant must help the court assemble the best available material. Useful elements include:

- The statutory text and any relevant statutory scheme. Courts begin with the text; present the provision, its context within the statute, and cross-references.

- Legislative history and regulatory guidance. If available and probative, legislative reports, committee debates, and administrative interpretations can illuminate statutory purpose.

- State appellate opinions—especially those of intermediate courts—and any concurrences or dissents that reveal doctrinal trends or splits.

- Comparable state supreme court rulings on analogous statutes or doctrines in related contexts. These can be very persuasive.

- Decisions from other jurisdictions. While not controlling, other states’ approaches to similar statutory language can be instructive, particularly where the state’s own law is thin.

- Secondary sources: treatises, law review articles, restatements, and practice manuals. These help demonstrate the accepted understanding in the legal community.

- Dictionaries and usage evidence. As Williams shows, ordinary meaning of statutory words (e.g., “receipt,” “certifying”) can be decisive; courts sometimes rely on Black’s or other reputable dictionaries when there is no contrary state authority.

- Industry practice and practical consequences. Evidence about how industry participants understand and implement statutory terms—policies, form contracts, standard operating procedures—can be particularly relevant in statutes that regulate commercial practices.

- Expert evidence, where appropriate, on industry norms or technical matters (for example, how automated systems operate and what constitutes delivery or receipt in modern IT systems).

Carefully assembling these materials helps a federal court both to predict state law and to assess whether certification is warranted. In Williams, the Fifth Circuit relied on multiple sources: the statutory wording, intermediate appellate decisions, and dictionary definitions; the court also considered the practical reality of automated web services as a context for interpreting “receipt.”

## Practical examples of framing certification requests and fallback predictions

A litigant who wishes to persuade a federal court to certify should combine a focused certification petition with a robust predictive brief. A model approach:

- Begin by stating the exact question in single-sentence form. Be as narrow as possible.

- Explain why the question is outcome-determinative and why the state supreme court’s answer would be dispositive.

- Catalog existing state appellate decisions, highlighting inconsistencies or their insufficiency.

- Show the public importance of the question: industry impact, consumer protection, administrative clarity, or widespread uncertainty.

- Offer a proposed answer and the legal reasoning supporting it, including textual analysis, relevant precedents, and policy arguments.

- Address timing and practicability: propose how certification would proceed without undue delay.

- As fallback, present a comprehensive predictive analysis for the federal court—textual interpretation, persuasive authorities, and a proposed holding.

This dual strategy respects the federal court’s duty to predict while making a compelling case for certification where appropriate.

## Conclusion: prudence, precision, and preparation

Predicting state law and deciding whether to certify are twin exercises of judgment. Federal courts are charged with applying state law and thus should undertake reasoned prediction when state-law authority is incomplete. Certification is a valuable but limited tool, and the Fifth Circuit’s “chary” approach in Jefferson and its application in Williams remind litigants that mere novelty does not compel certification. Success in either persuading a federal court to certify or persuading it to adopt a party’s preferred prediction depends on careful, narrow framing of the legal question, a thorough presentation of the state-law materials, and clear articulation of why the state’s highest court’s input is necessary (or, conversely, why the federal court’s prediction is adequate).

Williams v. GoAuto illustrates these points: the Fifth Circuit carefully synthesized statutory text, intermediate appellate decisions, and usage evidence to predict Louisiana law rather than certify, and it explained that leaving the statute unadorned by judicially created requirements (such as an independent signature) was the better course. For practitioners and students, the lesson is practical: build the record, frame narrow dispositive questions, show why state clarification is essential if you seek certification, and otherwise prepare a robust predictive argument grounded in state materials.
