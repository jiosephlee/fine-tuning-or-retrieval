Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

Chapter 5: # Electronic Communications and “Receipt”: From Mailbox Rule to Web Services

This chapter examines the intersection of statutory delivery rules and modern electronic communications. It uses the Fifth Circuit’s decision in Williams v. GoAuto (No. 24-30646) as a focal point to teach how courts treat statutory language that authorizes cancellation “by sending . . . or via electronic mail” and that ties legal effects to the insurer’s “receipt” of a notice. The issues that arise are practical and doctrinal: when does a digital transmission qualify as the sending required by a statute; what does “receipt” mean when a recipient’s systems automatically ingest and act on messages; how do state enactments of the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA) frame the analysis; and what proof is required to establish that an electronic communication was sent and received. Williams illustrates these questions and the analytic tools courts use to answer them.

The chapter proceeds in six parts. First, we canvass statutory options for delivery—traditional mail, private carrier, and electronic mail—and the role of statutory text in delimiting acceptable modes. Second, we define “receipt” as courts and legal dictionaries commonly do and explain why the ordinary meaning matters. Third, we summarize UETA principles (as adopted in Louisiana by La. R.S. 9:2601 et seq.) and how they treat the concepts of sending, receiving, electronic agent, and attribution. Fourth, we explore how automated systems—APIs and web services—function as institutional actors and how their operation can satisfy statutory receipt requirements. Fifth, we address evidentiary means for proving electronic sending and receipt (logs, headers, architecture description, authentication). Finally, we synthesize doctrinal lessons and practical implications for litigants and drafters.

## Statutory options for delivery: mail, private carrier, electronic mail

Many statutes that govern notice and cancellation list specific delivery methods. Louisiana’s statute at issue in Williams allows a premium finance company to request cancellation “by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” certain facts. That listing is a legislative choice. When a statute enumerates delivery methods, the first task of interpretation is to determine whether the list is exclusive or illustrative and to give effect to the methods the legislature named.

Two general interpretive propositions are relevant. First, courts start with the statute’s plain language. If the statute expressly authorizes “electronic mail,” that means electronic transmission of the stated materials is a permitted mode. If the statute also mentions “depositing in the mail” and “private carrier,” those traditional methods remain available and should be given their ordinary meanings. Second, courts are reluctant to add conditions not found in the statutory text. Where Louisiana law requires “strict adherence” to the statutory process for a finance company to exercise a power of attorney to cancel, courts nonetheless must apply the statute as written; they may not graft additional formalities (for example, a handwritten signature) onto the statutory steps unless the statutory language or controlling precedent requires them.

Williams illustrates both propositions. The Fifth Circuit observed the statute’s explicit reference to electronic mail and refused to import a signature requirement that did not appear in the text. A finance company’s electronic transmittal that “certified” the required information was sufficient under the statute, at least where the company’s electronic notice communicated the elements the statute demanded. The presence of mail and private carrier options did not exclude electronic methods; rather, the statute accommodates contemporaneous technologies for effecting delivery.

The enumeration also raises an analytic question that recurs in other contexts: does the statute require actual receipt by the insurer, or is the act of sending sufficient? Louisiana’s statute requires that “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested.” Thus the statutory trigger is the insurer’s receipt of the notice; the permitted means of sending include electronic mail. Courts therefore must determine when an electronic transmittal becomes “received” by the insurer.

Because statutes diverge, practitioners should read the precise statutory text involved. Some statutes still invoke a mailbox or dispatch rule (effect at the time of mailing); others treat receipt as the operative event. When an authorized method is “electronic mail” or similar, UETA and related doctrines often supply interpretive backstops for what “sent” and “received” mean in an electronic environment.

## Defining “receipt”: Black’s Law Dictionary and the ordinary meaning (possession, custody, delivery)

A statutory reference to “receipt” can be deceptively simple. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and defines “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” These ordinary-meaning glosses anchor judicial interpretation. Courts commonly treat “receipt” as requiring some form of possession, custody, or control over the communicated item, whether a physical document or an electronic message.

Two aspects of the ordinary-meaning approach are important. First, receipt need not require physical handling by a human being; courts recognize that “possession” today can mean custody of an electronic file or record in a system under the recipient’s control. Second, the point of receipt is legally significant because statutes often use it to determine time-sensitive consequences—deadlines to cure defaults, effective times for cancellation, or periods for filing claims.

In Williams, the Fifth Circuit embraced the ordinary-meaning definition of receipt: a recipient takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request. The court emphasized that modern company computers perform many acts that previously required a human intermediary. In that environment, the insurer’s automated web service that accepted an e-mailed cancellation request and immediately performed a cancellation amounted to institutional “possession” for purposes of the statute. The district court’s alternative theory—treating the later physical stamping by an APAC employee as delivery of the notice to a private carrier—was unnecessary once the e-mail ingestion was characterized as actual receipt.

This approach aligns with a functional understanding of receipt: the question is whether the recipient’s systems have taken control of the communication in the way the recipient has arranged to accept communications. If the insurer’s processes are configured to treat certain inbound electronic messages as operative and to act on them automatically, then those inbound messages have been “received” for statutory purposes when they enter and are processed by the insurer’s system.

There are limits. If the statute requires that the recipient actually examine or manually acknowledge a communication, or if the parties have contractually agreed on a different benchmark for receipt, courts will not impute receipt merely because a message passed through an intermediary system. The statutory text and the parties’ expectations matter.

## UETA in Louisiana (La. R.S. 9:2601 et seq.): message sending, receipt, and electronic agents

La. R.S. 9:2601 et seq. enacts Louisiana’s version of the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA). UETA supplies foundational rules for electronic records and signatures and addresses when electronic communications are “sent” or “received,” and how actions taken by automated systems should be attributed. Although courts still must construe the particular statutory scheme in which “sending” or “receipt” appear, UETA often provides interpretive guidance and fills gaps left by statutes drafted before electronic commerce became ubiquitous.

Core UETA principles relevant to receipt and automated systems include the following (stated in doctrinal form without relying on precise subsection numbering):

- Electronic records and signatures satisfy statutory requirements for writing and signature where the statute does not preclude electronic form. If a statute allows a notice “by electronic mail,” an electronic record can carry the same force as paper.

- “Sent” in the electronic context generally means the electronic record was transmitted by the sender in a form capable of being received by the recipient’s designated system and left the sender’s control.

- “Received” generally occurs when the electronic record enters the recipient’s designated information processing system in a form capable of being retrieved and processed by the recipient. Where the recipient has not designated an electronic means of receipt, the record is received when it comes to the recipient’s attention.

- UETA recognizes the concept of an “electronic agent” (automation such as a web service or API). An act performed by an electronic agent is attributable to the person that establishes, uses, or controls the agent, even if no individual person reviewed the action at the moment it occurred.

Applying these principles, Louisiana’s UETA endorses the proposition the Fifth Circuit invoked in Williams: an insurer’s information-processing system that accepts and processes a cancellation request is the proper locus of receipt. Where a sender transmits to a system that the recipient has arranged to receive and process inbound messages, UETA treats the record as received when it enters that system.

UETA does not, however, relieve parties of any statutory requirement that a notice’s content include certain confirmations or statements. If a statute requires that the notice “certify” particular facts, an electronic record can suffice to convey that certification, but the sender must ensure the record contains the required language or structured data.

UETA also addresses attribution. When an automated component (an “electronic agent”) sends or receives a message, the action is generally treated as the act of the person who used the agent. That allocation matters when statutes require a party (the finance company) to certify certain facts—if the finance company’s system sends such a certification automatically, UETA supports treating the automated communication as coming from the finance company itself.

The interaction between UETA and a particular statutory scheme can be subtle. UETA typically governs unless a statute explicitly provides otherwise. When a statute lists electronic mail among permitted delivery methods, UETA’s default rules about sending and receipt will often inform courts’ understanding of when a legal effect (like cancellation) occurs.

## Automated receipt via APIs/web services: institutional agency of computer systems

Businesses increasingly use automated interfaces—application programming interfaces (APIs) and web services—to exchange information and trigger actions. Web services that listen for inbound messages and perform actions when the messages conform to expected structure are ubiquitous in commercial systems. The legal questions are twofold: (1) can the activity of an automated system constitute “sending” or “receipt” under a statute; and (2) when can the system’s automated conduct be attributed to the sponsoring entity?

The common-law mailbox rule (dispatch rule) treats certain notices as effective upon proper dispatch; it developed in an era of physical mail. In the electronic setting, UETA and modern statutory drafting often supplant the mailbox rule with receipt-based benchmarks. That shift matters for automated systems: an insurer that configures an automated listener to accept cancellation messages has chosen the means by which it will receive and process communications. When a finance company’s system transmits a cancellation request to that listener, and the listener immediately acknowledges or performs the cancellation, it is sensible—and consistent with UETA—to treat the insurer as having received the notice at the moment of system ingestion.

Williams presents a clear application of this logic. GoAuto’s system hosted a web service that “listens” and when called by APAC’s system performs cancellation for the policy. The insurer’s system processed the incoming electronic request during the early morning hours and triggered the cancellation automatically. Because the insurer’s system was set up to accept and act on this class of messages, the Fifth Circuit concluded that receipt occurred when the insurer’s system took custody of the request. No human intervention at that precise moment was necessary for receipt or for the statutory consequence to follow.

UETA’s attribution rules support this outcome. The statute treats actions performed by an electronic agent—and actions directed to an electronic agent—as attributable to the person who uses or controls the agent. Thus, where APAC’s system invokes GoAuto’s web service and the web service, acting as the insurer’s electronic agent, accepts and processes the request, the law will typically regard the insurer as having received the request at that time.

This institutional-agency framework also clarifies responsibility and risk allocation. A party that elects to automate its receipts cannot later disavow an automated intake as a failure to receive, absent unusual facts (for example, the message was malformed and never entered the system; a malicious actor spoofed the sender; or the parties had an agreement specifying a different receipt mechanism). Conversely, a sender that directs a transmission to the recipient’s designated system bears the risk that the recipient’s system will accept or reject the message according to its programming.

Two caveats warrant emphasis. First, the legal effect of an automated receipt may turn on the particular statutory text. If a statute requires signature by a named individual or a sworn affidavit physically signed and notarized, simple electronic ingestion may not suffice. Second, operational clarity matters: the parties’ configurations, business practices, and any express agreements about which systems and formats constitute effective delivery will shape the legal outcome. Systems should be designed and documented with those legal stakes in mind.

## Evidentiary considerations for proving electronic sending and receipt (server logs, timestamps, system architecture)

When litigation arises, parties must prove electronic sending and receipt. Courts treat these proofs through the familiar lenses of authentication, hearsay exceptions, and business records. The most reliable types of proof are technical records generated contemporaneously by the systems that sent or received messages. The following categories commonly appear in disputes:

- Server logs and system-generated timestamps. SMTP server logs, web server access logs, API gateway logs, and application logs typically record the time a message was received, the source IP, the resource invoked, the HTTP or SMTP status code returned, and other metadata. These logs—properly authenticated—provide powerful evidence that a message left one system and entered another at a specified time.

- Message headers and message bodies. Email message headers include “Received” entries showing the chain of servers traversed. Email headers and message bodies also indicate sender and recipient addresses and message identifiers. When preserved in original form, headers assist in reconstructing the transmission path.

- Mailbox or queue records. For providers that queue messages, the queue’s records can show whether the recipient’s system accepted the message or returned an error.

- Application acknowledgments or responses. An automated web service may return an acknowledgment code or generate a confirmation record. These acknowledgments, like delivery receipts or application-level responses, corroborate receipt and processing.

- System architecture and business process documentation. Diagrams, system specifications, and testimony from IT personnel explain how messages are routed, which systems are “designated” for receipt, and what actions follow ingestion. Courts rely on such context to assess whether an incoming message was capable of being retrieved and processed.

- Authentication under the rules of evidence. Federal Rule of Evidence 901(a) requires authentication for documentary evidence; parties commonly authenticate logs and emails through witness testimony from a custodian or qualified IT employee who can explain the log-generation process and the record’s integrity. The business records exception to hearsay (FRE 803(6)) often admits system-generated logs when the proponent establishes a foundation: the record was made in the regular course of business, at or near the time of the event, by a person (or electronic process) with knowledge, and it is shown to be trustworthy.

- Digital forensics. When disputes involve allegations of tampering, spoliation, or spoofing, forensic analysis of preserved media, server configurations, and security logs may be necessary to demonstrate the provenance and integrity of records.

In Williams, the Fifth Circuit relied on a combination of the parties’ system descriptions and declarations explaining how APAC’s automated process sent an e-mail that GoAuto’s web service received and acted upon. That documentary and testimonial record was sufficient for summary-judgment purposes to show that GoAuto’s system took custody of the notice and that the insurer’s system treated the incoming message as a request for cancellation.

Litigants should anticipate proof challenges. The absence of logs, inconsistent timestamps, or gaps in system documentation can undermine a party’s claim that a notice was sent or received. Conversely, well-maintained logs, persistent message identifiers, and corroborating acknowledgments are highly persuasive. Practically, companies should preserve logs and related artifacts when litigation is foreseeable; failure to do so can lead to adverse inferences or spoliation sanctions.

## Applying the doctrine: synthesis and practice points

Several principles emerge from the doctrinal materials and Williams:

- Statutory text controls. If a statute lists electronic mail as an authorized method, courts will generally enforce the statute without imposing extra-formal signature or notarization requirements unless the text or controlling precedent requires them. Where the statute triggers effects on “receipt,” courts will look for evidence of possession, custody, or control consistent with ordinary meaning.

- UETA provides interpretive scaffolding. La. R.S. 9:2601 et seq. (Louisiana’s UETA) recognizes electronic records and signatures and supplies sensible definitions of “sent,” “received,” and “electronic agent.” When a statute is silent as to how electronic communications operate, UETA’s rules on when an electronic message is received by an addressee’s information processing system are especially useful.

- Automation is attribution. Actions performed by an electronic agent (web service, API listener) are attributable to the party that controls the agent. A business that elects to receive and process notices automatically should expect courts to treat a message processed by that agent as received by the business at that moment.

- Evidence rules are central. Server logs, headers, application acknowledgments, and witness testimony authenticating system records are the primary means of proving electronic sending and receipt. Parties should ensure logs are preserved and capable of being authenticated.

- Process design matters. Contracting parties and regulated entities should define which channels constitute authorized receipt, what formats are acceptable, and what constitutes a valid certification. Doing so reduces disputes and aligns systems with legal requirements. When statutes demand “certifying” language, automated messages should include the mandated verbiage or structured attestations.

Consider two short hypotheticals that test these principles:

- Hypothetical A: A finance company transmits a cancellation message to an insurer’s publicly documented API endpoint. The insurer’s API accepts the request and immediately returns a 200 OK acknowledgment and triggers cancellation. The insurer later claims it never “received” the notice because no human saw the message. Under UETA and Williams, the insurer’s system received the notice when it entered and was processed by the insurer’s designated system; the insurer cannot avoid the statutory effect by insisting on human review.

- Hypothetical B: A finance company e-mails a cancellation notice to an insurer’s generic inbox, but the insurer’s spam filter quarantines the message and never places it in the recipient’s designated processing queue. Which party bears the risk? Courts will look to whether the insurer designated that inbox as the means to receive cancellation notices and whether the sender sent the message in a manner consistent with the insurer’s instructions. If the insurer had not used that mailbox or had specified a different protocol, the sender may bear the risk; if the insurer’s public instructions created an expectation that that address was the correct channel, the insurer may bear the risk of its internal filtering.

## Conclusion: doctrinal contours and litigation posture

Williams v. GoAuto provides a concise, modern example of how courts apply statutory delivery and receipt rules to electronic communications. The decision reiterates that statutory text governs, that ordinary-meaning definitions of “receipt” (possession, custody, delivery) remain operative in the digital era, and that UETA-style principles of message receipt and attribution of electronic agents provide appropriate doctrinal tools for analysis. Automated web services that are the recipient’s chosen means of intake can constitute legal receipt when they ingest a transmitted notice and perform the statutorily relevant action. Parties should design systems and contracts to make intentional choices about how notices are sent and received and should retain contemporaneous technical records that authenticate messages and system behavior. These practices reduce litigation risk and allow courts to apply statutory rules predictably in an age of automated communications.
