Chapter 9: # Comparative Perspectives: Model Acts, Other States, and Technology Trends

The Fifth Circuit’s recent decision affirming dismissal in Williams v. GoAuto University—interpreting Louisiana’s premium-finance cancellation statute, La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(b)(i)—provides a timely point of departure for examining how premium finance statutes, model acts, and courts in other jurisdictions treat the mechanics of cancellation when premiums are financed. The Fifth Circuit confronted three issues that recur in this area of law: (1) what the statute means by a premium-finance company’s “certifying” information to an insurer; (2) whether a signature or the identification of an individual is mandated; and (3) what counts as the insurer’s “receipt” of that certification for purposes of triggering cancellation and the insured’s cure period. The court’s pragmatic answers—no per se signature requirement and acceptance of machine-to-machine electronic delivery as sufficient “receipt”—illustrate one contemporary approach, but they also highlight a landscape in which model acts, state statutes, and case law diverge. This chapter places the Fifth Circuit’s reasoning in comparative perspective: it contrasts Louisiana’s statutory framework with model and other-states’ approaches, surveys judicial treatments of certification contents and signatures, examines judicial and regulatory accommodation of electronic and automated communications, and discusses the consumer-protection tradeoffs and avenues for harmonization through legislative or regulatory updates.

## NAIC Model Premium Finance Act: parallels and divergences from § 9:3550(G)

The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) has long provided model legislation addressing premium finance companies’ conduct, aimed at protecting consumers by regulating premium finance contracts, disclosures, powers of attorney, and cancellation procedures. Although states adopt NAIC model provisions unevenly and often with variation, the model act’s goals mirror those expressed in La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G): to create a clear, verifiable record of when a financed policy may be cancelled and to guarantee the insured a statutory minimum opportunity to cure a default.

Parallels. Like Louisiana’s statute, the NAIC model emphasizes (a) the premium finance company’s duty to provide a notice when it intends to exercise a power of attorney to cancel; (b) certain mandatory contents for notices and certifications so the insurer, insured, and other interested parties can identify the basis and timing of the cancellation; and (c) a cure period for the insured. Both frameworks seek to pin down the exact time and date of cancellation and to ensure documentation is in the record—objectives that courts have repeatedly described as the rationale for “strict adherence” doctrines under state law.

Divergences. The model act and model regulatory guidance frequently—or at least increasingly—address methods of communication and recordkeeping in the age of electronic commerce. Many model provisions and modern statutory updates expressly contemplate electronic delivery of documents and the use of automated systems, often by cross-reference to broader electronic-transaction statutes. By contrast, older formulations of state statutes (like the particular text of Louisiana’s provision as litigated in Williams) do not explicitly mandate or foreclose electronic methods; they instead speak in neutral terms of “sending,” “certifying,” and “[u]pon receipt.” The Fifth Circuit resolved that silence by reading the statute in light of modern practices and ordinary dictionary definitions of “receipt,” but states that adopt NAIC recommendations or that have modernized statutes may provide clearer statutory guidance—for example, by expressly permitting electronic signatures, defining what suffices as “delivery” or “receipt,” or specifying required metadata or audit logs.

Implication. For practitioners and regulators, the distinction matters. Where a statute expressly contemplates electronic means, insurers and finance companies can rely on specified authentication and retention standards. Where the statute is silent, courts will be asked, as in Williams, to interpret statutory language against the backdrop of contemporary business practices and federal or state electronic-transaction law. The NAIC model’s movement toward clarity on electronic methods is thus a potential stabilizer, reducing litigation over whether traditional terms should be read to accommodate automation.

## Other jurisdictions on finance-company cancellations and certification contents

Across the states, courts confronted with premium-finance cancellations occupy a spectrum from strict literalism to pragmatic substantial-compliance doctrines. Louisiana is situated near the strict end: its appellate cases emphasize that any defect in the statutorily prescribed process results in an ineffective cancellation, because the statute’s purpose is to fix the exact time and ensure a minimum cure period. Cases such as Delatte v. Lemotte and Eaglin v. Champion highlight why courts in Louisiana will scrutinize every element of the finance company’s certifications and notices.

Strict compliance jurisdictions. In states and lines of authority that hew to strict compliance, courts will invalidate a cancellation if the finance company fails to include required elements in the certification, fails to send notice to all interested parties, or otherwise deviates from the statutory checklist. The rationale is two-fold: (1) the statutory scheme creates rights—for example, a statutory cure period—that must be protected by clear procedures, and (2) strict rules produce easily administrable bright lines that reduce dispute over when a policy lapsed.

Substantial compliance jurisdictions. Other courts interpret comparable statutes more flexibly, applying a substantial-compliance test: if the finance company’s actions substantially meet the statute’s objectives—so that the insured received meaningful notice and an opportunity to cure—then minor procedural defects will not invalidate cancellation. Courts adopting this approach emphasize functional fairness and the dangers of allowing small procedural omissions to produce windfalls for insureds who have failed to pay premiums.

Certification contents and signatures. One recurring issue is whether the statute’s requirement that the premium finance company “certif[y]” certain facts (valid power of attorney, uncured default, copies of notices, notice to interested parties) demands a signed affidavit or the naming of a responsible company employee. Some courts have insisted on identifiable and verifiable certification—sometimes in the form of a signed affidavit—when the statute explicitly requires a sworn statement. Other courts have permitted an unsigned corporate communication so long as the required substantive information is present and attributable to the finance company. Benitez v. Elsayed, a Louisiana appellate opinion referenced in Williams, noted shortcomings where a notice was unsigned and did not identify an employee, but did not hold that lack of signature alone invalidated a cancellation. The Fifth Circuit in Williams likewise concluded that the statute’s silence as to a signature does not permit courts to add such a requirement; the statute speaks of the finance company certifying, and a corporate act can certify through means other than an individual’s signature.

Practical consequences. For litigators, these divergences mean that advising clients requires attention to local precedent. In strict-compliance jurisdictions, finance companies should use practices that generate signed, individualized certificates or affidavits and robust proof of delivery and service. In more flexible jurisdictions, corporations may achieve compliance through reliable internal systems and contemporaneous documentation that demonstrate the company’s affirmative act of certification and the insured’s receipt of notice.

## Evolving recognition of electronic communications and automated processing in cancellation regimes

The Williams opinion embodies a doctrinal adaptation to automation: the Fifth Circuit accepted an automated e-mail/web-service message and a system-generated cancellation as constituting the insurer’s “receipt” and the finance company’s certification, even though a human signature was affixed only later. This approach reflects two broader legal currents: (1) the widespread statutory adoption of electronic-transaction principles (for example, the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA) and the federal E-SIGN Act) that validate electronic records and signatures in commercial transactions; and (2) judicial recognition that process automation—machine-to-machine messages, API web-service calls, and time-stamped electronic logs—can furnish reliable evidence of who sent what and when.

What courts look for. When assessing electronic or automated cancellation procedures, courts generally examine four aspects:

- Authentication: Is the origin of the message traceable to the finance company or its authorized system? Audit trails, system logs, certificate-based authentication, or other technical evidence help answer this.
- Integrity: Can the contents of the notice be shown to be unchanged since transmission? Hashes, secured logs, or preserved PDFs with metadata bolster a showing of integrity.
- Timing: Does the record reliably establish the date and time of sending and of the insurer’s receipt? Time stamps synchronized to a reliable time source and retained server logs are often decisive.
- Human oversight and error-correction: Are there procedures for preventing, detecting, and correcting erroneous cancellations—e.g., confirmed double checks for accounts flagged as in dispute, or human review for cancellations triggered outside daily business hours?

Benefits of recognizing electronic receipt. Accepting automated delivery as sufficient reduces latency in cancellation processing and aligns legal requirements with modern business operations. The Fifth Circuit emphasized that statutes should not be read to freeze procedures in an analog past; web services and always-listening listeners can effect the statutorily required receipt. This reduces the interval during which an insured might receive coverage for which the premium was not paid, and it produces machine-generated data that can prove compliance.

Risks and limitations. Automated systems are fallible. Erroneous cancellations caused by software bugs, misapplied business rules, or mistaken matches between customer accounts and system identifiers can expose consumers to uninsured loss and companies to liability. The absence of a signature or human-authored affidavit may complicate parties’ ability to demonstrate intention in marginal cases unless the electronic system provides robust logs and authentication. Regulators and courts are increasingly asking for auditability: where corporations rely on automation, they will be expected to show detailed logging, chain-of-command, retention policies, and testing procedures.

Regulatory context. State insurance departments and legislatures have begun to react. Many jurisdictions now incorporate or cross-reference UETA/E-SIGN, and some have issued guidance or bulletins about acceptable electronic practices for notices and for the exercise of powers of attorney in insurance contexts. The NAIC itself has been attentive to digital trends and has advocated clarity in recordkeeping and authentication expectations.

## Consumer-protection tradeoffs: clarity, fairness, and administrative efficiency

At the core of premium-finance cancellation rules lie competing public-policy values. On one hand, the statute protects insureds by guaranteeing clear notice and a defined cure period; on the other hand, insurers and finance companies seek efficient, reliable means to end coverage when premiums are unpaid so that risks are not borne by an insurer without payment.

Advantages of strict, signature-oriented regimes. Requiring signed, individualized certifications and strictly enforced procedures favors consumer protection. It is hard to overstate what strict formalities secure: an identifiable certificate signed by a natural person sends a clear message, provides a direct locus for accountability, and reduces the chance that a computer error will lead to inadvertent cancellation. Courts that invalidate cancellations for procedural defects underline this protective function.

Costs of strict formalities. Formalities can be costly and administratively cumbersome. They can produce transactional friction, delay cancellations, and complicate legitimate enforcement of policy terms—perhaps leaving insurers exposed to uncompensated risk and increasing premium costs to offset that risk. They may also produce waste when identical risk-control objectives could be met by more modern, less paper-bound mechanisms that equally protect insured notice and record accuracy.

Advantages of allowing automated procedures and substantial compliance. Recognizing machine-originated notices and permitting substantial compliance can lower costs, increase speed, and reflect real-world business practices. When electronic processes include good authentication, logging, and retention, they can produce more reliable records than ad hoc paper channels. The Fifth Circuit’s endorsement of system-based receipt reflects this reasoning: a web service that is “always listening” and which produces a traceable call can be as reliable as a stamped paper letter.

Consumer risks in a digital approach. The risk is wrongful cancellation without meaningful human oversight or effective remediation. Electronic systems can propagate systematic errors quickly (e.g., a misconfigured rule cancels many policies at once), and consumers may be ill-equipped to detect or rectify errors before suffering loss. Ensuring consumers have an accessible, prompt mechanism to cure and to reverse incorrect cancellations is essential if electronic methods are permitted.

Balancing mechanisms. Optimal regimes typically combine the two approaches: they accept electronic methods but require safeguards that protect consumers—clear content requirements, mandatory cure periods, mandatory confirmation notices to consumers triggered by cancellation, easy and quick reinstatement procedures upon cure, robust record retention and authentication requirements, and regulatory or statutory standards for auditability. In short, administrative efficiency should not be secured at the expense of meaningful consumer protection.

## Potential harmonization through legislative updates or regulatory guidance

The Williams opinion illustrates the interpretive strain courts face when statutes are silent about technologies that now routinely effect routine commerce. Harmonization—and avoidance of case-by-case judicial interpretation—can be advanced both by legislative amendment and by administrative or regulatory guidance. Below are concrete avenues for reform and harmonization.

Statutory clarifications. Legislatures can amend premium-finance statutes to specify (1) that electronic delivery, signatures, and certifications satisfy statutory requirements (with cross-reference to UETA or E-SIGN where appropriate); (2) a definition of “receipt” tailored to modern communications (e.g., receipt occurs when a message reaches the insurer’s designated electronic address or is received by a specified automated interface); (3) required contents of a certification (e.g., statement of power of attorney, identification of the finance agreement, date of initial notice to insured, and list of parties notified); and (4) minimum recordkeeping and authentication standards (e.g., retention of time-stamped, tamper-evident audit logs for a specified number of years). Such amendments would reduce litigation over whether a system-generated e-mail or API call suffices.

Regulatory guidance. State insurance regulators can issue interpretive bulletins that (a) validate electronic means if accompanied by specified technical safeguards (authenticated messaging, synchronized time stamps, immutable audit logs), (b) require vendors of cancellation software to maintain testing and error-correction protocols, and (c) require reporting to regulators when system-wide errors lead to a material number of improper cancellations. Regulators can also promulgate model compliance checklists for insurers and finance companies, reducing regulatory uncertainty.

Consumer-facing rules. Legislatures or regulators can require consumer-friendly protections, such as mandatory redundancy of notice (a written mail notice when practicable supplemented by an electronic message), minimum grace periods after notice for curing defaults, and expedited procedures for reinstatement without penalty if a cancellation is set aside due to company error. These protections preserve the statute’s remedial purpose while enabling modern practices.

Industry standards and certification. The NAIC and state departments can work with industry to develop standards for audit logs, message formats, authentication protocols, and API interfaces. Certification programs or best-practice frameworks for premium finance vendors would give companies commercial incentives to comply with high technical standards and provide courts with recognized benchmarks when evaluating whether an automated process was adequate.

Judicial deference and clear rules. Finally, courts and litigators benefit from clear statutory language or regulatory norms because judicial creation of ad hoc standards—forcing courts to “add to the procedures,” as the Fifth Circuit warned against—creates unpredictability. If legislatures and regulators supply well-crafted standards, courts can focus on applying those standards to facts rather than manufacturing them ex post.

Conclusion: Toward a predictable, protective, technologically informed regime

The Williams decision is illustrative: it adapts a traditional statutory scheme to modern practice, declining to impose a signature requirement not present in the statute and accepting electronic receipt by a web service. That approach may be defensible as a matter of statutory interpretation and commercial realism, but it also demonstrates the costs of statutory silence. To reduce litigation and to align administrative efficiency with consumer protection, states would do well to harmonize their statutes with model provisions and regulatory guidance that: (1) expressly accommodate electronic delivery and signatures; (2) define “receipt” in terms that recognize machine-to-machine interactions while requiring reliable authentication and time-stamping; (3) specify required certification content and retention standards; and (4) preserve robust cure and reinstatement protections for insureds. With such harmonization, the goals identified by courts—fixing the exact time and date of cancellation and guaranteeing a minimum time to cure—can be achieved in ways that are both technologically current and protective of consumers.
