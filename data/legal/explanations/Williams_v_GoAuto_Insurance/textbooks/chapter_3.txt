Chapter 3: # Strict Adherence Doctrine in Louisiana Cancellation Cases

Louisiana law imposes a demanding regime when an insurance-premium finance company uses a power of attorney to cancel an insured’s policy for nonpayment. The statutory framework, La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G), sets out both the substantive content of what the finance company must “certify” to the insurer and the procedural means by which cancellation may be effected. Louisiana appellate courts have repeatedly characterized those requirements as demanding “strict adherence,” and they have repeatedly held that any defect in the statutorily prescribed process may render a purported cancellation ineffective. This chapter explains the doctrinal baseline for strict adherence, illustrates how courts apply the rule, examines contested points (signature, electronic transmission, when an insurer has “receipt”), and explains the remedial consequences of noncompliance.

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in Williams v. GoAuto Insurance Co. (the opinion excerpted above) is our most recent demonstration of how courts assimilate the strict-adherence principle to modern communications technology. The court reaffirmed two core propositions. First, the statutory checklist in § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) is mandatory in the sense that courts will not give effect to a cancellation unless the finance company has made the required certification and used an allowed transmission method. Second, “strict adherence” does not invite courts to add extra-statutory requirements—courts will not impose a signature requirement or other gloss where the statute itself is silent. At the same time, the Fifth Circuit recognized that modern systems (automated e-mail, web services) can satisfy the statute’s delivery and “receipt” requirements when the electronic process results in the insurer actually coming into possession (physical or electronic) of the certification.

## Delatte v. Lemotte: strict adherence baseline

Delatte v. Lemotte established the core rule often cited in subsequent opinions: when a finance company cancels under the power of attorney provision of the premium finance statute, the company must strictly adhere to the statutory mechanics. The lesson from Delatte is doctrinally simple and practically sweeping. The statute provides a specific sequence of steps and required content: a ten-day notice to the insured, the finance company’s certification to the insurer repeating statutorily enumerated statements, and delivery of copies to interested parties. Delatte holds that courts will treat the statutory process as mandatory rather than directory: compliance is not a suggestion or a matter of substantial compliance in the ordinary contract sense; rather, compliance is a precondition to a legally effective cancellation.

For a student, the important takeaway is two-fold. First, identify the statutory text (what must be certified) and method of transmission (mail, private carrier, or e-mail) before analyzing any case about cancellation by a finance company. Second, when courts say “strict adherence,” they generally mean that any meaningful omission in the statutorily prescribed content or procedure will prevent the insurer from treating the policy as terminated at the asserted time. Put differently, when a finance company claims to cancel under a power of attorney, the burden is on the finance company (and the insurer relying on its certification) to prove compliance with the statute’s checklist; failure to do so ordinarily leaves the policy in force.

## Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co.: defects render cancellation ineffective

Eaglin v. Champion illustrates the remedial consequence that flows from Delatte’s strictness: defects in the cancellation process render the purported cancellation ineffective. Eaglin involved a purported cancellation where the required steps were not fully accomplished according to the statute. The court’s holding — that the cancellation was ineffective — follows from the same doctrinal premise: the statute defines the sole authorized method by which a finance company may cause a cancellation through its power of attorney, and if the finance company does not satisfy that method a court will not retroactively validate the action on equitable grounds.

What follows from Eaglin is both practical and doctrinal. Practically, an insurer who relies on a defective cancellation will be exposed to the obligations of the policy as if it remained in force. Doctrinally, the remedy is not merely a scrivener’s correction; courts will not rewrite or cure a statutorily defective process by reference to equities or fair expectations. Eaglin thus reinforces the deterrent function of strict adherence: finance companies and insurers must get the process right, or they will bear the legal consequences of having failed to do so.

## Britten v. Reavis: purposes of strict compliance — fixing the cancellation time and guaranteeing the cure window

Britten v. Reavis elucidates why Louisiana courts insist on strict adherence. The opinion identifies two essential functions served by the statute: (1) it fixes “the exact time and date of cancellation,” and (2) it “grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” These two functions explain the policy behind the insistence on precise compliance.

Fixing the time and date of cancellation is critical to the allocation of risk among insurer, insured, and third parties. Third parties injured by a driver whose policy was purportedly canceled on a given date must know whether coverage was in effect at the moment of loss. If the cancellation process provides an unambiguous mechanism for fixing that moment — for example, a dated notice and a certification establishing the date of mailing or transmission — insurers and third parties can rely on the date as dispositive. Conversely, if the method of cancellation is ambiguous or defective, courts will not construe a cancellation date that lacks statutory support.

Guaranteeing a cure window explains the consumer-protective dimension. The statute requires the finance company to give the insured a prescribed period (a ten-day notice in the usual statutory formulation) to cure the default before the power of attorney can be exercised. Strict adherence thus ensures insureds are afforded the statutorily chosen period to save their coverage. If a finance company omits, shortens, or obscures the cure window, the company cannot subsequently claim an effective cancellation.

Britten therefore gives us the analytic rule: in any dispute over whether a cancellation was effective, start by asking whether the statutory devices sufficiently specify (1) the time of cancellation in a verifiable way and (2) the existence of the statutorily required opportunity to cure. If either function is compromised by defect, courts will generally find the cancellation ineffective.

## KMJ Services, Inc. v. Hood; Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s; Lewis v. Julien: paraphrases and the evolving procedural posture

A number of Louisiana appellate decisions have paraphrased or applied the statute without resolving all subsidiary ambiguities. KMJ Services, Hodges, and Lewis illustrate two points: first, appellate courts sometimes resolve cancellations on narrow record facts without significant interpretive gloss; second, certain issues (for example, whether a particular informal statement suffices as “a statement certifying compliance”) often recur and are sometimes resolved differently on tight factual records.

Some decisions simply treat the statutory certification as a required “statement certifying compliance” and hold that, upon receipt of such a statement, the insurer may treat a requested cancellation as effective. Other panels have said less; they apply the statute case-by-case, focusing on whether the statement sent by the finance company contained the statutorily required elements and whether the insurer received it by an approved method. The recurring procedural posture is summary judgment: each side tends to litigate whether the statutory boxes were checked. These cases are a reminder that the statute’s language matters, and that appellate courts will often dispose of disputes by reference to whether the written materials in the record contain the required certifications and can be shown to have been delivered in a permitted way.

The lesson in practice is doctrinal modesty: when an opinion recites that a “statement certifying” was sent, examine the record. Was the statement in the record actually a certification of the elements specified in § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv)? Were copies sent to interested parties? Was there proof of mailing or electronic receipt? KMJ, Hodges, and Lewis show that failure to document these items usually decides the case against the party asserting the cancellation.

## Signature, “certifying,” electronic transmission, and “receipt”: tensions and modern adaptations (Williams v. GoAuto)

Two subsidiary issues repeatedly surface under § 9:3550(G): whether the statutorily required “certifying” requires a signature (and, if so, a signature before cancellation), and when an insurer has “receipt” of a finance-company notice — particularly where communications are electronic and cancellations are automated. The Fifth Circuit’s Williams decision confronts both questions and offers an instructive framework for resolving them.

Signature and “certifying.” The statutory text requires “a statement certifying” certain enumerated items; it does not expressly require a signature, nor does it say the certification must be made by a named individual. Louisiana case law has sometimes noted the absence of a signature as one of several defects (see Benitez, which criticized a notice that “is not signed by any individual,” 285 So. 3d at 579), but Benitez did not hold that signaturelessness alone vitiates a cancellation when the document otherwise satisfies the statutory content requirement. The Fifth Circuit in Williams followed that line: it refused to engraft a signature requirement onto the statute where the legislature had chosen the impersonal phrasing of “the insurance premium finance company” who “certifies.” The court concluded that a company may “certify” by sending an un-signed statement that, on its face, contains the statutorily required certifications. The governing principle is that strict adherence requires satisfaction of the statute’s specified items and permitted means of transmission, but it does not allow courts to import additional formalities that the legislature did not include.

Electronic transmission and “receipt.” The statute allows a finance company to send the certification by “depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail.” An unresolved issue in some cases is when an insurer has “receipt” under § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i): does receipt require a human’s physical possession of a printed document, or is a message received by an insurer’s computer system sufficient? The Fifth Circuit answered this question in practical terms: “receipt” occurs when the insurer takes possession, delivery, or custody of the request — and that can happen electronically. The court accepted the ordinary dictionary definitions of “receipt” and “receive” and found that an automated e-mail to a GoAuto web service that triggers an automated cancellation constitutes receipt. Under that factual scenario, the web service acts as the insurer’s agent or listener: when the finance company’s system “calls” the web service, the insurer’s system is in possession of, and acts upon, the cancellation request. The court thus recognized that modern electronic workflows may satisfy a statute written before those technologies were widespread; the decisive question is whether the insurer’s system actually came into custody or control of the certification before it treated the policy as cancelled.

What Williams instructs doctrinally is the following: strict adherence remains the rule, but the statute’s allowed means of transmission include e-mail and the courts will treat an electronically received, time-stamped transmission that is processed by the insurer’s systems as the functional equivalent of physical receipt. Conversely, if the electronic transmission never arrives in the insurer’s possession (for example, it is misaddressed or blocked), then there is no receipt and no effective cancellation. The focus therefore shifts from form (signature, paper) to substance (did the insurer receive the required certification in a manner enabling it to determine the date and plausibly rely on it).

Caveat: Williams rejected a request to read more into the statute (for example, that cancellation could not be effected until a human had stamped “received” the printed notice). The court declined to adopt the district court’s imaginative expansion of “private carrier.” The doctrinal balance respects the statute’s text and purpose while allowing the statutory devices to be satisfied by contemporary methods that accomplish the statute’s two core functions (fixing the time of cancellation and guaranteeing the cure window).

## Remedial consequences of noncompliance: policy remains in force; exposure for coverage and extra‑contractual claims

The immediate legal consequence of a court finding a cancellation ineffective is straightforward: the policy remains in force past the asserted cancellation date, and the insurer remains responsible for covered losses that occur in that period. That consequence serves the protective function of the statute: if the finance company failed to give the insured the statutory opportunity to cure, the insured is not deprived of coverage.

Beyond the core coverage result, there are additional remedial implications that practitioners and students must keep in mind:

- Insurer liability to third parties. An insurer that relies on an invalid cancellation may be forced to provide coverage for losses that occurred during the period the insurer contended the policy was cancelled. This can create awkward commercial consequences, especially where the insurer already issued warranty or cancellation-related communications to third parties. Courts will enforce the statutory protection for third parties: if the cancellation is ineffective, coverage exists to the extent the policy otherwise applies.

- Extra-contractual and bad-faith claims. A plaintiff may assert extra-contractual claims (for example, bad-faith or breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing) against the insurer if the insurer’s actions following an allegedly defective cancellation violate duties beyond the policy language. The viability of such claims depends on the facts: mere procedural error by a finance company may not itself generate a large tort award against an insurer unless the insured can show bad faith, intentional misconduct, or other actionable conduct. State cases display a range of outcomes: some plaintiffs obtain only a declaration that the cancellation was ineffective; others may pursue damages for harms caused by the wrongful cancellation (e.g., increased premiums, loss of insurance-dependent employment, or liability exposure), and courts will consider whether the insurer or finance company acted intentionally, recklessly, or in bad faith. The statutory strictness makes a failure to comply a legally significant event, but it does not automatically convert every procedural failure into an award of extra-contractual damages — plaintiffs must still prove causation and the relevant tort elements.

- Class actions and systemic risk. Where cancellation practices are automated and used consistently across many insureds, a defective process can produce class-action exposure (as in the Williams case). The remedial stakes for insurers and finance companies are therefore much higher when a systemic process is at issue. Courts will examine the regularity and proof of the process to determine whether an identifiable class of insureds was harmed by a systemic failure to comply with the statute.

- Costs and interest. When a cancellation is invalid and coverage attaches, the insurer may be responsible for the cost of defense and indemnity subject to the policy terms, and courts may award statutory or contractual interest on judgments where applicable.

Students should therefore frame remedies in two tiers: (1) the primary, automatic consequence is preservation of coverage for the contested period; (2) secondary consequences (tort damages, extra-contractual relief, class remedies) depend on additional proof of harm and misconduct.

## Practical rules for counsel and companies; avoiding defect

Given the doctrine and the direction of modern cases, prudent practices for finance companies and insurers include the following:

- Follow the statute’s checklist literally. Ensure that every certification sent to the insurer contains the explicit elements required by § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv): (i) the premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney; (ii) the agreement is in default and not timely cured; (iii) upon default the ten-day notice was sent to the insured and the date is specified; and (iv) copies were sent to all listed interested persons (specifying governmental agencies, lienholders, or other named third parties).

- Time-stamp and log electronic transmissions. Because “receipt” can occur electronically, maintain contemporaneous, reliable logs showing when an e-mail or web service call was sent and when it was received by the insurer’s systems. Automatic time stamps and electronic logs are often better proof of compliance than after-the-fact signatures on printed documents.

- Preserve proof of mailing to the insured and interested parties. If the statute permits (and it usually does), keep affidavits of mailing, registered-mail receipts, or electronic confirmation copies demonstrating that the insured received the required ten-day notice before the certification was sent.

- Consider a named certifier when practical. While courts like the Fifth Circuit have refused to require a human signature where the statute does not demand one, including a named certifying individual (with a manual or digital signature and a date) reduces litigation risk by eliminating an argument about who “certified” the statements.

- Coordinate system cutoffs and human review. If an automated process triggers cancellation when a web service call is received, ensure the systems’ recorded timestamp and the certification align; consider building a slight delay to allow human verification when appropriate, or ensure the written e-mail or electronic certification is generated and sent before the automated cancellation action. The objective is to avoid any temporal gap in which a system cancels coverage before the insurer has demonstrable receipt of the certification.

- Document delivery to the insurer’s custody. If the business model involves shared personnel or shared filing rooms (as arose in Williams), maintain documentation showing the movement of records and which corporate entity is the custodian. Ambiguities about whether the insurer took possession of the cancellation request invite litigation.

Finally, attorneys representing insureds should aggressively test the presence of all statutory elements in the finance-company file: the ten-day notice to the insured, proof of sending, the content of the finance-company certification, the method and timestamp of transmission to the insurer, and any logs showing receipt by insurer systems. Absent a clear record that all these elements were present and received before cancellation, courts will often find the cancellation ineffective.

## When to seek state-court clarification: certification of questions

Federal courts can certify unsettled state-law questions to the state supreme court. But as the Fifth Circuit reiterated in Williams, certification is not automatic simply because a term has not been interpreted by the state high court. A party seeking certification should show that the question is novel, outcome-determinative, and of pressing state-wide importance such that a state supreme court’s guidance would materially shape litigation nationwide. Williams illustrates judicial restraint: the Fifth Circuit declined to certify the signature/receipt questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court because the court did not view the absence of a defining precedent as sufficient to justify certification. Practitioners should therefore weigh the likelihood of certification carefully and build a robust factual record to persuade a federal appellate court that state-law resolution is necessary.

***

In sum, Louisiana’s premium-finance cancellation statute embodies a strict-adherence doctrine designed to provide clear, verifiable evidence of when insurance coverage ends and to protect insureds’ opportunity to cure premium defaults. Courts will enforce the statute’s checklist rigorously, but they will not amend the statute to add formalities the legislature omitted. Modern electronic workflows can satisfy the statute so long as the insurer actually receives the required certification and the record shows the date and content of the transmission. Where the required steps are missing, courts will treat the purported cancellation as ineffective, with the attendant coverage and potentially extra-contractual consequences.
