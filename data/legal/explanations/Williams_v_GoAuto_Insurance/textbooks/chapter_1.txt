Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

Chapter 1: # Parties, Facts, and Procedural Posture

## Parties
This case involves four principal groups of parties. The plaintiffs are former insureds Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright and a third-party claimant, Nicholas Jenkins. Jenkins is the owner of a vehicle and the victim of an accident caused by a different GoAuto insured (not a plaintiff here); that driver’s GoAuto policy had been financed through the same financing arrangement as Williams’s and Wright’s. The defendants are GoAuto Insurance Company (the insurer) and American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (APAC), the premium-finance company that paid GoAuto the full premium up front and then collected installment payments from the insureds.

## Premium-finance agreements and the power of attorney
The financing arrangement between the insureds and APAC is central to the dispute. Under those agreements APAC advanced the full premium to GoAuto; the insureds, in turn, repaid APAC in monthly installments. Each financing agreement expressly granted APAC a power of attorney authorizing APAC to cancel the insurance policy if the insured defaulted on the installment payments. That power of attorney is the statutory mechanism by which a finance company may request cancellation of a financed policy under the Louisiana statute at issue in the case (La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)). The statute requires the finance company to send the insurer a notice “certifying” certain enumerated items (e.g., that the finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney, that the agreement is in default, that a ten‑day notice was sent to the insured, and that copies were sent to interested third parties).

## What happened: notices, nonpayment, and cancellation mechanics
In each relevant instance the insureds failed to make the installment payment required by APAC. APAC’s practice in that event was to send the insured a “Ten Day Notice of Cancellation” informing the insured that the policy would be cancelled if payment was not received within ten days. If payment was not received by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system generated an automated e‑mail to GoAuto containing a copy of the Ten Day Notice and a request for cancellation. The automated request was on APAC letterhead and recited the statutory certifications. GoAuto’s computer system was configured to cancel the policy when it received that e‑mail/cancellation request.

Later that same morning, after the computer-initiated cancellation occurred, an APAC employee would print the cancellation materials, affix a signature stamp to the printed documents, and then place the signed, printed documents in a shared filing cabinet. Prior to 2017 the filing cabinet was located in a shared room managed by GoAuto Management Services; since 2017 an APAC employee delivered the signed, printed materials to a GoAuto employee who stamped them as “received” and placed them in a shared cabinet. Thus, the chronological facts relevant to the statutory dispute are: (1) APAC’s automated electronic request reached GoAuto’s systems and triggered an automated cancellation; (2) only later that morning was any human-signed paper copy produced and filed.

## Claims asserted
The plaintiffs asserted that GoAuto’s cancellations were ineffective under Louisiana law because the statutory procedures for cancellation by a premium‑finance company were not strictly followed. The complaints alleged, in substance, that the technical procedures APAC and GoAuto used—most importantly the timing of any human signature and the manner in which GoAuto “received” the cancellation request—failed to satisfy the statute’s requirements, rendering the cancellations ineffective. From those alleged defects the plaintiffs asserted related claims including misrepresentation and breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing (claims that depend on whether the policies were in fact valid at the time of the accidents and communications).

## Procedural path to the Fifth Circuit
The plaintiffs originally sued GoAuto and APAC in Louisiana state court as a putative class action; Williams and Jenkins were the original plaintiffs and Wright was added by amendment. GoAuto removed the action to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties narrowed the litigation to a single legal question for summary-judgment briefing: whether GoAuto’s cancellation procedures complied with the Louisiana statute governing premium-finance cancellations. The district court granted GoAuto’s motion for summary judgment, concluding that GoAuto’s procedures satisfied the statutory requirements, and dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Wright, and Jenkins appealed that adverse judgment to the Fifth Circuit, challenging (among other things) whether the required statutory “certifying” had occurred before cancellation and whether GoAuto had in fact “received” the cancellation requests prior to cancelling the policies. The Fifth Circuit’s decision on those issues determines whether the district court correctly resolved the threshold statutory question that controlled the case.
