Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

Chapter 2: # Louisiana’s Premium Finance Cancellation Framework and Strict Compliance

## Core statute: what must the premium finance company send

At the center of Williams v. GoAuto Insurance is La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a), which authorizes a premium finance company to request cancellation of an insured’s policy upon an uncured default by sending the insurer “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” four discrete items. Those four items are (i) that the premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney, (ii) that the agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured, (iii) that upon default the finance company sent the insured the required notice of cancellation and specifying the date it was sent, and (iv) that copies of the notice were sent to any persons who, under the finance agreement, have an interest in any loss (including named agencies, lienholders, or other third parties), with names and addresses identified. The statute permits delivery by depositing the notice in the mail, by using a private carrier, or “via electronic mail,” and it frames the certifications as the act of “the insurance premium finance company.” Read together, the statute prescribes both content (the four certifications plus the cancellation notice) and permitted means of transmission (mail, private carrier, or email).

Understanding these textual components is essential: the statute does not, on its face, say how the statement must be signed, who must sign it, or the precise mechanics of delivery beyond the three permitted methods. Those lacunae are the source of litigation over whether particular company practices satisfy the statute’s demands.

## Insurer’s action upon receipt: what triggers cancellation

Section 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) supplies the consequence of compliance: “Upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” The operative trigger is the insurer’s receipt of the notice and certification. In Williams the Fifth Circuit considered what “receipt” means in a modern, largely electronic business environment. Because the statute itself does not define “receipt,” the court consulted a leading dictionary definition (Black’s Law Dictionary) treating “receipt” as the act of receiving — especially by taking physical possession — and “receive” as coming into possession or getting from an outside source. Applying that definition to the record, the court concluded that an insurer may proceed to cancel once it has taken possession, delivery, or custody of the request for cancellation. The court accepted that automated electronic delivery to GoAuto’s systems — an APAC process calling a GoAuto web service that the company’s computer “listener” then executes — constitutes the insurer’s receipt for purposes of the statute in this record. Thus, in the court’s view, receipt can occur by electronic, automated transmission and need not await a human review or an imprimatur placed on paper later in the morning.

## The strict-adherence doctrine and its limits

Louisiana precedent requires strict adherence to the statute’s procedural requirements when a finance company exercises a power of attorney to cancel a policy. The Fifth Circuit rehearsed that doctrine through controlling and persuasive state appellate decisions: Delatte v. Lemotte and Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co. stand for the proposition that any defect in the statutorily-prescribed cancellation process can render the cancellation ineffective. The court emphasized the rationale courts have given for strictness: as Britten v. Reavis explains, exact compliance serves two core functions — it fixes the precise time and date of cancellation, and it preserves a minimum cure period during which the insured can remedy the default. These twin aims explain why Louisiana courts have been vigilant about enforcement of the statute’s procedures.

But Williams also illustrates a doctrinal restraint: courts enforcing strict adherence should not, under the guise of strictness, rewrite the statute or impose requirements the legislature did not include. The Fifth Circuit framed its task as interpreting the statutory text, not adding prerequisites (for example, a legislatively unenumerated signature requirement). Thus, strict compliance is enforced against departures from the statutory prescriptions, but courts should be careful not to manufacture additional steps that the statute does not require.

## Prior case treatments of “certifying” and the signature question

A recurring interpretive knot is what the statute’s command that the finance company “certif[y]” requires in practice. Several Louisiana appellate opinions quoted by the court characterize the statute as requiring “a statement certifying compliance” or allow cancellation “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement.” Those prior decisions (KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood; Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins.; Lewis v. Julien) endorse the requirement of a statement certifying certain facts but do not address whether that statement must bear an individual employee’s signature.

One intermediate appellate opinion, Benitez v. Elsayed, did note the absence of a signature on the submitted instrument as a shortcoming, observing that the document “does not identify any particular employee” and “is not signed by any individual,” but the Benitez court did not hold that lack of an individual signature, standing alone, automatically invalidated the cancellation if the other statutory elements were satisfied. In Williams the Fifth Circuit read these cases to mean that while a statement certifying the required facts is essential, Louisiana courts have not established a per se rule that a named or signed individual signature is mandatory. The Fifth Circuit therefore concluded that the statute’s command that “the insurance premium finance company” certify the specified facts can be satisfied without a signature identifying a particular employee, and it declined to graft a signature requirement onto the statute absent clearer state-law authority.

## How the court applied the framework to GoAuto/APAC’s procedures

Applying the statute and the strict-adherence principle to the record, the Fifth Circuit examined APAC’s practices and GoAuto’s receiving process. APAC sent ten‑day notices to insureds and, when the cure period expired, its computer system automatically sent an email to GoAuto with a copy of the notice of cancellation and a certification on APAC letterhead reciting the four required points and stating that copies were enclosed. GoAuto’s web service — an automated listener — received that electronic notice and automatically effected cancellation in the early morning hours. Only later did an APAC employee (using a signature stamp) and, after 2017, a GoAuto employee stamp the printed file as “received” and place the documents in a shared filing cabinet.

The court’s analysis had two key conclusions. First, the word “certifying” in the statute does not, as a matter of Louisiana law as the court read it, impose a separate requirement that the statement be signed by an identifiable individual before cancellation. Second, the insurer’s “receipt” for purposes of § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) can occur when an insurer’s electronic system takes possession of the delivered notice; an automated web-service action that accepts and processes the cancellation request constitutes receipt so the insurer may proceed to cancel. Because the APAC email/statement reached GoAuto’s systems and GoAuto’s system acted on it, the court held the statutory prerequisites were satisfied and that GoAuto’s cancellation was effective. The Fifth Circuit therefore affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment for GoAuto.

The court did note an alternative rationale the district court had used — treating the person who later placed the printed notice into the shared filing cabinet as a “private carrier” — but declined to reach that theory since the email-and-receiver analysis resolved the statutory question. Similarly, the plaintiffs’ request that the Fifth Circuit certify the interpretive questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court was denied because the panel found no compelling reason to invoke certification; the state-law question had an answer sufficient for disposition in the federal appeal.

## Pedagogical takeaway

Williams v. GoAuto illustrates how a federal appellate court reads a state statute that prescribes a strict procedural route for legal consequences. The court enforced Louisiana’s rule of strict adherence to the statutory procedure but stayed within the statute’s text: it required the prescribed content of notice and certification, but it declined to create additional formalities (like a mandated individual signature) not present in the statute or authoritatively required by Louisiana courts. The decision also shows how courts will adapt traditional statutory concepts — here, “receipt” and “certifying” — to contemporary electronic business practices when the statute permits electronic transmission and the record demonstrates that an insurer’s systems have taken possession of the communication. For students learning to read opinions, Williams provides a useful model of statutory interpretation that balances fidelity to strict procedural requirements with fidelity to the statute’s plain language and existing state precedent.
