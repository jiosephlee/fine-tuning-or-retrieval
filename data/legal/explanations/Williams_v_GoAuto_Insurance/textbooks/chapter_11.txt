Chapter 11: # Litigation Strategies and Summary Judgment in Cancellation Disputes

This chapter uses GoAuto Insurance Co. v. Williams et al., No. 24-30646 (5th Cir. 2025), as the concrete vehicle to teach how plaintiffs and defendants frame disputes about the “strict adherence” rules that govern cancellations of financed insurance policies under state statute, the kinds of evidence courts expect at summary judgment, and the tactical choices—particularly whether to seek certification of state-law questions—that litigants make in these cases. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion articulates several durable lessons: (1) courts defer to statutory text and to contemporaneous automated records when the statute does not expressly require additional formalities such as a handwritten signature; (2) plaintiffs remain able to prevail where a statutory element was not satisfied and the defect is material; and (3) federal courts are selective about certifying unsettled state-law questions.

The goal of this chapter is practical and doctrinal. Students should come away able to: (a) identify the levers a plaintiff will pull to try to defeat a cancellation as invalid; (b) organize the documentary and technical evidence a defendant will marshal to show compliance; (c) understand how the burdens at summary judgment operate in this context; (d) assess the utility of expert and technical proof; and (e) evaluate when a federal court ought to certify a controlling question to a state supreme court.

## The statutory and doctrinal framework

Most statutes that allow a premium finance company to cancel an insured’s policy on default require that the finance company “send” or “certify” certain information to the insurer and sometimes to the insured and interested third parties. Under Louisiana law, as interpreted in the GoAuto case, a finance company must send the insurer “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” specified facts: that the finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney, that the agreement is in default and uncured, that a notice was timely sent to the insured, and that copies were sent to interested third parties. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv). Louisiana appellate decisions emphasize “strict adherence” to the statutory requirements, and hold that any defect may render a cancellation ineffective. See Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993); Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990); Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). Those authorities create a real advantage for plaintiffs when there is a genuine shortcoming in the statutorily required content or transmission process.

But “strict adherence” does not license courts to read additional requirements into the statute. As GoAuto demonstrates, courts will start with the statute’s text and interpret undefined words (like “certifying” and “receipt”) in light of ordinary usage and technological realities. When the text says the finance company must “certify,” the question becomes whether that implies a signature, or whether a corporate statement in a record that purports to certify the facts is sufficient. Similarly, if the statute conditions cancellation on the insurer’s “receipt” of the certification, the court must decide whether electronic delivery that an insurer’s systems accept constitutes receipt. These are the two principal interpretive battlegrounds litigants litigate—formalities of signature/certification on one side, and the point of “receipt” on the other.

Understanding those issues is the prerequisite for building the litigation strategies discussed below.

## Plaintiff playbook: attacking statutory elements, demanding a named signatory, and contesting “receipt”

Plaintiffs in cancellation disputes begin by framing the case as a straightforward application of the “strict adherence” principle: the statute requires discrete elements and, if any element is missing or defective, the cancellation is ineffective. That framing has two immediate tactical consequences. First, plaintiffs focus their proof obligations narrowly: they need to create a genuine dispute on at least one statutory element—content of the certification, manner of transmission, timing of delivery, or receipt by the insurer. Second, they aim for bright-line deficiencies that courts have historically treated as material.

Attacking the statutory elements. A plaintiff’s first line of attack is to show that one or more required statements were never made, were inaccurate, or were not sent to all required recipients. For example, if the statute requires the finance company to specify the names and addresses of holders of a security interest or governmental agencies to whom notice was sent, a plaintiff will point out omissions and argue those omissions frustrate the statute’s dual purposes: fixing the effective date of cancellation and preserving a minimum cure period. To prevail, the plaintiff must link the omission to the statutory purpose—i.e., demonstrate that the missing information made it impossible to determine the date of cancellation or deprived a party of its opportunity to cure.

Demanding a named signatory. Where the certification appears on corporate letterhead or in an automated message, plaintiffs commonly assert that the statute’s command to “certify” necessarily implies an identifiable person’s certification, usually evidenced by a signature and printed name. The argument has two variants. The narrow variant says the absence of any signer or printed name means the finance company never truly certified anything and therefore the insured’s policy remains in force. The broader, more aggressive variant argues that the signature must predate or accompany the act of cancellation; a post hoc signature or stamp is ineffective. This is a potent argument when the record shows a human signature was affixed only after a system had already processed the cancellation, as in GoAuto.

Contesting “receipt” and possession. Plaintiffs also attack the insurer’s assertion that it “received” the cancellation request. Because the statute conditions cancellation on the insurer’s receipt of the finance company’s notice and certification, plaintiffs argue that mere transmission is insufficient—especially where transmission was electronic and the tangible paperwork was handled by an employee of the finance company or placed in a shared file room. Plaintiffs will press the argument that the insurer never took “possession” or was never placed on adequate notice as the statute contemplates. In the GoAuto case plaintiffs emphasized that the signed certification did not exist until after the policy had already been cancelled by GoAuto’s automated system and that, before 2017, the documents were placed in a filing room not technically belonging to the insurer. Those facts supported a plausible inference—absent countervailing proof—that the insurer did not “receive” the required certification before cancellation.

Tactical evidence and briefing. On summary judgment plaintiffs should compile evidence that creates a genuine issue about the completeness, timing, and authenticity of certifications and receipts. Useful items include: copies of the cancellation notice lacking a signatory or printed name; deposition testimony about the timing of automated processes; evidence that the signature was affixed after the cancellation action; evidence showing the insurer’s employees did not actually handle the documents; and any affidavits or testimony that the insurer’s systems did not log or acknowledge a receipt prior to cancellation. Importantly, plaintiffs should explain why the defect is material under the statute—why the missing signature, omitted content, or lack of physical possession prevented the statute’s purposes from being met.

Proving materiality is critical. A plaintiff can prevail only if the alleged defect is not merely technical but undermines the statute’s core objectives—fixing the cancellation date and preserving a cure period. If a defendant can show the insurer had contemporaneous electronic evidence establishing the required facts prior to cancellation, a court may treat the defect as immaterial despite the “strict adherence” rhetoric.

## Defense playbook: systematized compliance and contemporaneous records

Defendants, typically insurers and premium finance companies, succeed by translating statutory requirements into routine corporate processes and establishing contemporaneous documentary proof that the statutory elements were satisfied. The modern defense playbook has three overlapping strands: (1) show the certification exists in the corporate record and was transmitted to the insurer in a form that the statute contemplates; (2) show the insurer received the certification—often via automated systems logs or API/webservice events—before or at the time it cancelled the policy; and (3) demonstrate that any informalities (e.g., absence of a handwritten signature) do not appear in the statute and are not required.

Documentary foundations. The quickest way to dispose of a plaintiff’s challenge is to present contemporaneous records—email copies, server logs, system-generated cancellation requests, and database entries—showing that the finance company’s system sent the notice and that the insurer’s systems acknowledged and acted upon it. In GoAuto the defendant produced evidence that APAC’s automated system sent an e-mail and made a call to a GoAuto webservice; the GoAuto webservice functioned as a “listener” and, upon receiving the request, performed the cancellation. This evidence shows (a) the certification content was included in the transmitted record, and (b) the insurer’s systems received the request and acted on it. Timestamped records, API call logs, and mail affidavits are the core of the defense.

Organizational testimony and business-records foundation. Defendants should support technical records with testimony and affidavits from the custodian of records, IT personnel, or system administrators who can explain how the systems operate, how logs are generated and stored, and how timestamps are created and protected from tampering. Such testimony both authenticates the records under FRE 901 and supports their admissibility as business records under FRE 803(6). In GoAuto, the insurer offered deposition testimony explaining the webservice architecture and that the system “listens” and responds to incoming requests—an explanation readily intelligible to a court asked to decide whether the insurer “received” a cancellation.

Neutralizing the signature argument. When the plaintiff insists on a named signatory, the defense should anchor its response in statutory text and legislative purpose. If the statute does not require a handwritten signature, defendants argue courts should not judicially add one. The defendant can show that the certification in the corporate record is unequivocal (e.g., letterhead, explicit certification language) and that a corporate entity can “certify” through its authenticated business record. Further, defendants can document that any signature stamps were never meant to supply additional substantive facts but merely to produce a durable paper copy for shared files.

Chain-of-possession and business practices. If the plaintiff challenges “receipt” because paperwork was deposited in a room shared by both entities or handled by a finance-company employee, the defense can show that internal business practices provided for immediate electronic delivery to the insurer and that physical handling of later printed copies was an administrative convenience not required by the statute. Evidence that the insurer’s systems had control over cancellation actions, even if a human later affixed a stamp, tends to defeat a claim that the insurer never received the certification.

Practical presentation. On summary judgment the defense should present a layered record: (1) authenticated system logs and e-mail headers with timestamps; (2) business-record affidavits explaining record generation and retention; (3) technical witness declarations or depositions explaining how automated messages were processed; (4) contemporaneous printouts showing the policy was cancelled only after—or because—an authenticated cancellation request was received; and (5) routine SOPs (standard operating procedures) that explain the handling of notices. This combination both supplies the evidentiary foundation and answers the plaintiff’s inferences about post hoc signatures or misplaced paper.

## Burden of production and proof on summary judgment; materiality of asserted defects

Understanding the allocation of burdens at summary judgment is essential in cancellation disputes. Under Rule 56, the moving party must show there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and that it is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. How that plays out depends on who moves and what the disputed elements are.

If the defendant moves for summary judgment (the common situation in GoAuto), the defendant may demonstrate that the record contains authenticated, contemporaneous evidence that the finance company transmitted the statutorily required certification and that the insurer’s systems received it and acted upon it. A strong record often includes system logs, e-mail headers, and a custodian affidavit establishing the documents are business records. Once the defendant satisfies that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to produce specific, admissible evidence showing a genuine dispute of material fact. The plaintiff cannot rest on mere allegations or speculation. Instead the plaintiff must point to evidence from which a reasonable juror could find the statutory requirements were not satisfied—evidence, for example, that the certification never existed in a form the law contemplates, that the insurer’s systems did not actually receive the message prior to cancellation, or that the missing element was material to the statute’s purpose.

Materiality is the legal hook by which courts distinguish minor, technical defects from defects that frustrate the statute. When Louisiana appellate courts speak of “strict adherence,” they do so to protect the statute’s core purposes—establishing the cancellation date and ensuring a compliance window. Therefore, to defeat a defendant’s motion the plaintiff must show the defect meaningfully interfered with those objectives. Proof that a signature was omitted will be material if the statute or case law requires a signature or if the absence of a signature resulted in uncertainty about whether the finance company had actually certified the listed items. Conversely, if the insurer can show it possessed contemporaneous electronic evidence establishing the necessary facts—such that the insured had notice and the period to cure was preserved—the omission of a handwritten signature is unlikely to be material.

Summary judgment mechanics in practice. In most contested cases the parties will file cross-motions or oppose one another’s motions by attacking the sufficiency and authenticity of background records. Judges look for the following in deciding whether there is a genuine dispute:

- Are there authenticated, contemporaneous records evidencing that the finance company’s notice contained the required certification?
- Do system logs or e-mail headers show the insurer’s systems received the transmission before or at the time of cancellation?
- Is there testimony from custodians or IT personnel explaining how the systems create and preserve records and why the records are trustworthy?
- Has the plaintiff introduced admissible evidence raising a plausible contrary inference—e.g., that the certification was created later, that the insurer’s systems did not actually receive or act on it, or that the purported certification lacked the statutorily required content?

If the plaintiff cannot point to admissible evidence creating a dispute about one of these points, summary judgment for the defendant usually follows.

## Use of expert or technical testimony to explain automated receipt and processing

Automated systems and web APIs are central to the modern mechanics of cancellations. A judge deciding a summary judgment motion must understand whether an insurer’s computer “receipt” of a cancellation request is functionally equivalent to a person taking physical possession. For this reason, expert and technical testimony often proves decisive.

What the technical testimony should cover. Experts or knowledgeable technical witnesses should explain how the relevant systems work: e-mail transmission paths and headers, server timestamps and time-zone normalization, API/webservice calls, queues and listeners, transactional logging, and retention procedures. Testimony should describe how logs are generated and how their integrity is protected (e.g., write-once logs, access controls, or immutable backup regimes). If the defendant relies on e-mail headers, the technical witness should explain why they can be relied upon to establish timing and origin. When timestamps are critical, experts should address clock synchronization issues and how the system ensures consistent timekeeping across components.

Authenticating electronic records. Technical testimony often supplies the foundation for admitting electronic records as business records under FRE 803(6) and authenticating them under FRE 901. The custodian or an IT specialist should describe the regular course of business, how records are made, where they are stored, and how alteration is prevented. Laying this foundation transforms machine-generated logs from self-serving documents into admissible evidence that can defeat a plaintiff’s purely speculative allegations.

Anticipating and rebutting challenges. Plaintiffs will attack the reliability and meaning of the technical records—arguing, for example, that an e-mail with the right header could have been forged, that a webservice log might have been backfilled, or that the signature stamp was the only real verification. Defendants should preempt those arguments by producing chain-of-custody evidence, system access logs, and audit trails showing that records were created automatically and contemporaneously and that only authorized processes could have generated or modified them. If necessary, a neutral expert can explain why the records are unlikely to have been altered and how the logs show an unambiguous sequence: finance-company system -> transmission -> insurer system receipt -> cancellation action.

Cost and scope of experts. Technical experts are not cheap, and not every cancellation case requires elaborate forensic proof. Use an expert when the plaintiff’s theory turns on the plausibility of forgery, backdating, or when the question is whether an automated transmission equals statutory receipt. A well-chosen lay witness (custodian or IT supervisor) may suffice where the technical matters are straightforward and the logs are clear.

## Role of certification to state courts under Erie; when it is—and is not—appropriate

When a federal court confronts an unsettled question of state law that may be determinative of the case, it can seek certification of that question to the state supreme court. Certification is a powerful tool, but courts employ it sparingly and on predictable grounds. The GoAuto opinion illustrates the Fifth Circuit’s typical approach.

Criteria for certification. A federal court will consider certification when: (1) a controlling question of state law is genuinely unsettled; (2) the question is outcome-determinative; and (3) there is no clear existing state precedent or overwhelming guidance from the state’s intermediate appellate courts. Certification is particularly attractive when the state-law rule will govern many future disputes, or when the federal court is genuinely unsure how the state supreme court would decide the issue.

The Fifth Circuit’s reluctance. The Fifth Circuit in GoAuto refused certification, relying on the principle that certification should be sought only for compelling reasons and not merely because a state supreme court has not yet defined a term. The opinion cited Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997), which underscores that the mere absence of state supreme court authority does not automatically make certification appropriate. Courts worry that overuse of certification could undermine its availability as a “special resource.” Thus, certification is appropriate when the question is truly uncertain and the answer would significantly affect the litigation’s outcome; it is less appropriate where a court can reasonably resolve the issue by ordinary tools of textual interpretation, dictionary definitions, and analogous precedent.

When plaintiffs should seek certification. Plaintiffs should consider asking for certification when: the state law point is dispositive and unsettled; intermediate appellate courts are split or silent; the issue implicates broader policy concerns for which the state supreme court’s guidance would be valuable; and delay occasioned by certification is acceptable relative to the stakes. Plaintiffs should draft a focused certification question that highlights the normative consequences (e.g., whether “certify” requires a handwritten signature prior to cancellation or whether “receipt” requires physical possession by a human).

When defendants should oppose certification. Defendants generally oppose certification when they can win under a plain-text reading of the statute and the record is strong. Certification imposes delay and uncertainty and may invite a stricter rule from the state supreme court. If a federal court can interpret the statute sensibly in context—particularly when contemporary commercial practices (like automated transmissions) make a textual reading clear—defendants should argue against certification.

Practical consequence of certification. Certification can produce clarity and protect clients against protracted, repeated litigation. But it will not necessarily yield a favorable result; state supreme courts may impose the stricter rule plaintiffs wish for. Both sides must weigh the trade-off: the certainty and authoritative guidance certification brings versus the delay and unpredictability of obtaining a state-court ruling.

## Applying the lessons to new fact patterns

The doctrinal and tactical points above distill into a simple analytic method you should apply to new fact patterns.

1. Identify the statutory elements. List every element the statute requires (content of certification, recipients, timing, manner of transmission). Remember that courts emphasizing “strict adherence” will look for each element.

2. Map the record to those elements. For each element, identify the records, logs, or testimony that support or contest compliance. Ask whether the plaintiff can point to admissible evidence that creates a reasonable dispute on any element.

3. Analyze materiality. If a defect exists, ask whether it meaningfully undermines the statute’s dual purposes. A missing signature is material only if the statute requires it or if its absence made it impossible to determine the effective cancellation date or deprived a cure period.

4. Consider the best form of evidence. For transmissions and system events, the best evidence is a composed record: an e-mail with full headers, system logs with synchronized timestamps, an affidavit from the custodian of records, and a technical declaration explaining how the system processes requests. For signature questions, produce contemporaneous notes, policyholders’ files, or witness testimony explaining why a missing name did or did not matter.

5. Decide whether to seek certification. If the controlling state-law question is undecided and outcome-determinative, weigh the likelihood of success before a federal judge against the advantages of definitive state-court guidance. If the federal judge can plausibly resolve the issue on textual grounds, certification is less likely to be granted.

6. Draft dispositive motions to focus on the narrowest determinative legal question. Defendants should move for summary judgment on the ground that the undisputed record shows statutory compliance; plaintiffs should file partial summary judgment or, if more strategically timed, an opposition that isolates the single element most clearly in dispute.

## Conclusion

GoAuto confirms that modern electronic processes can satisfy statutory requirements to “certify” and for an insurer to have “receipt” so long as the relevant information is contained in contemporaneous, authenticated records and the statutory text does not mandate a particular formalism like a handwritten signature. Nevertheless, strict-adherence doctrines remain meaningful: a material omission or a genuine factual dispute about timing or receipt can defeat a cancellation. Summary judgment victories require building a layered, admissible record: authenticated electronic logs, custodian and technical witness testimony, and clear linkage between the transmission event and the insurer’s cancellation act. Plaintiffs must focus on materiality and present admissible evidence that a statutory element was not satisfied; defendants must show reliable system-generated evidence that the element was satisfied. Finally, certification to state courts is a powerful but limited option; it should be pursued only when a settled, outcome-determinative state-law question cannot be reliably resolved by the federal court on the record and the statutory text.
