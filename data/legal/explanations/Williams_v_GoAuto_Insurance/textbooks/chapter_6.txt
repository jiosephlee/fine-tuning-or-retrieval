Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

Chapter 6: # Case Study: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance Co. — Facts, Posture, and Issues

This chapter uses the Fifth Circuit’s decision in Williams v. GoAuto Insurance Co. to examine how Louisiana’s statutory scheme governing cancellations of insurance policies financed through a premium finance company, La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G), applies when cancellation is triggered by modern, automated communications. The opinion is useful for demonstrating (1) how Louisiana’s “strict adherence” rule operates, (2) what the statute actually requires and does not require, and (3) the practical and litigation risks that flow from automated workflows that separate electronic notice, automated processing, and later human verification.

## Parties and relationships: insureds; APAC as premium finance company; GoAuto as insurer

The underlying dispute arises from a common commercial arrangement in the insurance market: an insured may finance the insurance premium through a premium finance company, which pays the insurer up front and obtains a right to receive installment payments from the insured. That financing agreement often contains a power of attorney authorizing the finance company to cancel the insurance policy if the insured defaults on the finance agreement. In Williams, the plaintiffs — Kimberly Williams, Felita Wright, and Nicholas Jenkins (the latter through a driver whose accident implicated him) — were former insureds of GoAuto Insurance Company (GoAuto) who had financed their policies through American Premium Assistance Company (APAC).

APAC’s role as the premium finance company is central because La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G) allocates to the finance company, in defined circumstances, the authority to effect cancellation by providing the insurer with a notice containing a “statement certifying” certain enumerated facts. GoAuto is the insurer that issues the underlying policies and, under the statute, is required to consider a cancellation “requested” upon receipt of the finance company’s notice containing the certification. The factual record also reflects operational intermingling: employees who perform services for APAC and GoAuto work under shared arrangements administered through a management-services entity (GoAuto Management Services, LLC). That shared-services context proved relevant to plaintiffs’ contention about whether GoAuto actually “received” cancellation requests.

Understanding these relationships is important because the statute’s command — and the strict-adherence case law that interprets it — turns on the identity of the actor who must “certify” and the steps that actor must take to make cancellation effective. The finance company’s power to cancel is not merely contractual; it is exercised within the boundaries the Legislature defined. Louisiana courts treat that statutory framework strictly: any procedural defect in the finance company’s statutorily-prescribed process may render the cancellation ineffective, with potential consequences for the insurer and the financed insured.

## Workflow: 10-day notice, no cure, automated email from APAC to GoAuto’s system, auto-cancellation, later signature stamp and filing

A clear timeline of what happened in Williams provides the factual foundation for the statutory analysis.

- APAC sent each insured a 10-day “notice of cancellation” upon missed payments. That notice advised the insured that the policy would be cancelled if payment was not received within ten days.
- If the insured failed to cure by midnight of the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically generated and transmitted an email to a GoAuto web service. That email included a copy of the 10-day notice and a request for cancellation. Most importantly for the statutory inquiry, the cancellation request contained a preformatted certification listing four items: (1) the financing agreement contained a valid power of attorney; (2) the agreement was in default and not timely cured; (3) a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation had been sent to the insured (copies enclosed and mailing affidavit available); and (4) copies of the notice had been sent to all other parties with an interest as required by the finance agreement.
- GoAuto’s computer system was configured as a listener (a web service). When the APAC-generated message called that listener, GoAuto’s system automatically canceled the policy immediately — in the early morning hours — without any human review at that instant.
- Later that morning, a human APAC employee printed the cancellation documents, affixed a signature stamp, and then, depending on the year, either delivered the documents to a shared filing cabinet (pre-2017) or handed them to a GoAuto employee who stamped them “received” and placed them in a shared cabinet (post-2017). The sequence that concerned the plaintiffs was that the human signature and physical filing occurred after the computer had already performed the cancellation.

That workflow raises two related legal questions under the statute: (1) whether the word “certifying” in the statute requires a signature or named individual to sign the certification before cancellation may be effectuated, and (2) whether an insurer’s electronic receipt of an automated cancellation request satisfies the statutory requirement that cancellation be effectuated “upon receipt” of notice and statement from the finance company.

## Procedural posture: cross-motions for summary judgment limited to statutory compliance; district court’s grant of summary judgment and dismissal with prejudice

The litigation began as a Louisiana state-court class action in which plaintiffs alleged GoAuto had not effectively cancelled policies and that GoAuto had failed to act in good faith because of the alleged statutory noncompliance. GoAuto removed the case to federal court. The parties framed their dispute narrowly for summary judgment: the cross-motions were limited to whether GoAuto’s cancellation procedures complied with La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G). The district court concluded the procedures complied with the statute and entered summary judgment for GoAuto, dismissing all claims with prejudice. Plaintiffs appealed that narrow legal ruling to the Fifth Circuit.

Because the parties confined the summary judgment proceedings to statutory compliance, the district court did not undertake a broader trial on facts such as whether communications were actually sent to a particular plaintiff or whether any insured reasonably relied on representations about coverage. The sole legal question was whether, as a matter of law, the procedures in place satisfied the statute’s requirements.

On appeal the Fifth Circuit reviewed de novo whether the undisputed procedural facts established compliance with the statute. The court affirmed the district court’s ruling.

## Issues on appeal

The plaintiffs pressed four principal issues on appeal. Each raises both textual and policy questions under Louisiana law.

1) Does “certifying” in La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) require a pre-cancellation signature or identification of an individual who prepared the document?

The statute authorizes a premium finance company to request cancellation “by sending to the insurer … a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” four enumerated matters. The plaintiffs argued that the ordinary understanding of “certify” implies a contemporaneous, person-signed attestation — something signed by a responsible individual — and that Louisiana’s strict-adherence rule requires that a signature appear on the certification before the insurer is permitted to cancel the policy. The plaintiffs relied on at least one intermediate appellate opinion (Benitez) that criticized a purported notice for lacking any named preparer and any signature, although Benitez did not expressly hold that a signature alone would render a cancellation ineffective if other statutory elements were met.

The Fifth Circuit rejected the plaintiffs’ argument. The court emphasized the statute’s text: the statute speaks of “the insurance premium finance company” making the certification; it does not say the certification must bear an individual’s signature or the printed name of the person who prepared the notice. The court declined to add a signature requirement where the Legislature omitted one. The Fifth Circuit also surveyed Louisiana appellate authority and concluded there is no clear, controlling case requiring a signature as a precondition to cancellation — some opinions used phrases such as “statement certifying” or “upon receipt of such notice and statement,” but none established a categorical pre-cancellation signature requirement. In short, the court held that the statutory word “certifying,” in this context, does not itself import a mandatory, contemporaneous handwritten (or even stamped) signature; a corporation may “certify” by issuing a formal, representational statement in the manner in which APAC’s automated system did so.

Two doctrinal points undergird this holding and are worth emphasizing beyond the facts of Williams. First, Louisiana courts require strict adherence to the statutory mechanics for cancellation to protect the insured’s interest in being afforded the cure period and to fix an exact date and time of cancellation. But “strict adherence” is not license for courts to add requirements not found in the statutory text. Second, the modern corporate form and computerized business processes mean that a company’s written, system-generated representations can serve the same function as a personally signed attestation, provided the statutory elements are otherwise satisfied. For litigators and practitioners, the practical takeaway is that although a signature is often a straightforward way to provide a clear attestation, the absence of one will not automatically invalidate a cancellation if the certification language is present and the record provides other evidence that the finance company in fact made the required statements.

2) Does the insurer have to physically or personally take custody of the notice before it may treat cancellation as “requested”? In other words, what does “receipt” mean under § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i)?

La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) provides that “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation of the contract has been requested” and may proceed to cancel. Plaintiffs argued that the Legislature’s use of the word “receipt,” rather than merely “sent,” imposes a requirement that the insurer actually obtain physical possession of the notice prior to cancellation. Because the APAC employee’s signature stamp and paper filing occurred after GoAuto’s computer system had already canceled the policy, plaintiffs contended that GoAuto never actually “received” the request before cancellation.

The Fifth Circuit again disagreed with plaintiffs. The court adopted standard dictionary definitions of “receipt” and “receive” — i.e., to take into possession or to come into possession — and held that an insurer may “receive” a cancellation request through electronic means. The court credited the uncontroverted technical evidence: APAC’s automated call to a GoAuto web service sent the certification-containing notice; the GoAuto web service is a listener that, on being called, performs the activity requested. That interaction constituted taking possession of the notice. Once the web service received the electronic request, GoAuto’s system had “receipt” and was authorized to proceed with cancellation. The court treated modern electronic transmission and automated processing as functionally equivalent to the physical handoff that the plaintiffs emphasized.

A collateral theory — which the district court had entertained but the Fifth Circuit did not need to resolve — was whether the delivery by an APAC employee to the shared filing cabinet constituted delivery by a “private carrier.” The district court posited a theory that a private carrier might be analogized to the APAC employee’s delivery duties, but the Fifth Circuit found it unnecessary to adopt that route given its resolution that electronic receipt by the insurer satisfied the statutory requirement.

This part of the opinion is important doctrinally because it recognizes that Louisiana’s statutory scheme, written in an earlier era, applies to contemporary electronic processes. The key is possession or custody — not the particular mode — so long as the electronic process reliably establishes that the insurer received the finance company’s statement. For practitioners, this emphasizes the importance of maintaining electronic logs, timestamps, system records, and other contemporaneous evidence that an electronic notice was transmitted and a recipient system processed it at a particular time.

3) Did the district court err in denying plaintiffs’ motion for partial summary judgment?

The plaintiffs had filed a motion for partial summary judgment arguing, in essence, that GoAuto’s procedures failed to satisfy the statute and that such failure required judgment in plaintiffs’ favor on statutory noncompliance. Because the district court — and the Fifth Circuit on appeal — concluded as a matter of law that APAC’s certification and GoAuto’s receipt (via automated email and web service) satisfied the statute, the court held plaintiffs were not entitled to summary judgment. The denial of plaintiffs’ partial summary judgment follows directly from the construction of the statute explained above: there was no statutory violation to adjudicate in plaintiffs’ favor.

Two points about the summary-judgment posture are noteworthy. First, summary judgment is appropriate where, on the undisputed record and applying the controlling law, no reasonable factfinder could find a statutory violation. The parties had stipulated many operational facts about the notification chain and the automation, which allowed the courts to resolve the legal issues as a matter of law. Second, plaintiffs’ argument that post-automation human acts (stamping, filing) were essential to create a valid certification failed because the court found that the statutory elements had already been met before those later steps. That reasoning underscores that summary-judgment disputes about statutory compliance with administrative processes often turn on close textual interpretation combined with factual demonstrations of system behavior and timing.

4) Should the Fifth Circuit have certified the unsettled issue(s) of statutory interpretation to the Louisiana Supreme Court?

Plaintiffs asked the Fifth Circuit to certify one or more questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that resolution by the state’s highest court would be valuable given the importance of the subject to the insurance industry and the lack of a definitive Louisiana Supreme Court decision on the signature and receipt issues. Certification of state-law questions from a federal court sitting in diversity or on state-law questions in a removed case is a recognized procedure, but federal courts exercise their discretion in deciding whether to certify. The Fifth Circuit invoked its usual caution: certification is reserved for truly compelling circumstances, and the mere absence of a state supreme court decision does not automatically justify certification. The court declined certification, citing precedent that prohibits overuse of the certification device and concluding there was no compelling reason to ask the Louisiana Supreme Court to decide the narrow textual questions presented.

The practical consequence is that the Fifth Circuit’s interpretation stands as the controlling federal appellate resolution of these issues within the Fifth Circuit. Litigants in Louisiana federal courts (and perhaps litigants elsewhere looking to persuasive authority) will be able to point to Williams for the propositions that (a) the statute does not necessarily require a pre-cancellation signature and (b) electronic receipt by an insurer’s system suffices as “receipt.”

## Doctrinal context and practitioner implications

Williams sits at the intersection of several doctrinal themes in Louisiana insurance law.

- Strict adherence doctrine. Louisiana courts have repeatedly said finance companies must strictly follow § 9:3550(G)’s procedures. The justifications are twofold: (1) the statute fixes an exact time and date of cancellation (important for coverage disputes) and (2) it guarantees a minimum cure period for the insured. Earlier cases (Delatte, Eaglin, Britten) have emphasized that any defect in the statutory process may render the cancellation ineffective. Williams does not dilute that principle; instead, it constrains courts not to import additional requirements (like a mandatory human signature) absent clear statutory language.
- Modernization of statutory application. Williams reflects judicial recognition that statutes written before the ubiquity of electronic commerce should be applied to modern business processes. The function of the statutory requirement — an identifiable, provable certification and an insurer’s receipt of that certification — can be satisfied by automated messages and system listeners, provided the electronic processes transfer custody and permit reliable proof of what occurred and when.
- Proof and liability risks. Although Williams holds that a signature is not per se required and that receipt can occur electronically, the absence of a signature or a physical handoff does not immunize parties from liability if other elements are missing or the finance company cannot prove what it claims. Louisiana’s strict-adherence doctrine means that if a court finds a deficiency in the statutory steps (for example, failing to send the 10-day notice to the insured, failing to send copies to other interested parties, or failing to create adequate proof of mailing), the cancellation can be treated as ineffective and give rise to bad-faith or misrepresentation claims against the insurer or other responsible parties. Thus, the Williams holding is narrow: it resolves particular issues — signature and electronic receipt — in favor of allowing automated processes to satisfy the statute where the record supports that result.
- Best practice recommendations. Practitioners advising insurers and premium finance companies should take away several pragmatic lessons from Williams:
  - Maintain contemporaneous, robust electronic records: system logs, timestamps, message receipts, and durable copies of the transmitted notice and certification language.
  - Where possible, include clear, corporate-authority language identifying the finance company as the certifying entity and make the certification language explicit in the transmitted request.
  - Consider including an identifiable signature (even a stamped or system-generated signature) or an audit trail showing who authorized the transmission contemporaneously; although Williams says a signature per se is not required, an immediately verifiable attestation reduces litigation risk.
  - Ensure notices to insureds and other interested parties are sent in compliance with the statute’s substantive and timing requirements, and preserve proof of mailing or service.
  - Build redundancy into receipt confirmation mechanisms: in addition to automated cancellation triggers, retain human process steps that corroborate electronic evidence.

## Conclusion and disposition

Williams v. GoAuto Insurance Co. presents a focused application of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G) to a modern, automated cancellation workflow. The Fifth Circuit held that (1) the statute’s use of the term “certifying” does not, by itself, impose a mandatory pre-cancellation human signature requirement; (2) an insurer’s electronic receipt of a finance company’s cancellation request via an automated web service suffices as “receipt” under the statute; and (3) because GoAuto’s procedures satisfied the statute as a matter of law, the district court did not err in denying plaintiffs’ motion for partial summary judgment or in entering summary judgment for GoAuto. The Fifth Circuit declined to certify the questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court and affirmed the district court.

For students and practitioners, Williams demonstrates how courts interpret statutory requirements in light of evolving technologies, while reaffirming that Louisiana’s strict-adherence principle remains a central constraint — courts will not add to statutory requirements, but failures to comply with the statutory elements continue to carry significant legal consequences.
