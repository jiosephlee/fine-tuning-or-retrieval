Law Stack Exchange Q&A: Williams_v_GoAuto_Insurance

### What was the federal subject-matter jurisdiction basis for removal of this Louisiana class action?
Question:
The opinion says GoAuto removed the putative class action from Louisiana state court to the Middle District of Louisiana, but it doesn’t identify the jurisdictional hook. Was this under CAFA (minimal diversity, 100+ class members, $5M+) or traditional diversity jurisdiction? If CAFA, did any exceptions (e.g., home-state, local controversy) apply and get litigated? I’m trying to understand why the Fifth Circuit didn’t address jurisdiction explicitly.
Answer:
Short answer: The Fifth Circuit opinion does not say. From the opinion alone you cannot determine whether GoAuto removed under CAFA or ordinary (complete) diversity, and the opinion contains no discussion of any CAFA exceptions.

Why: the opinion merely states that the plaintiffs “filed a class action lawsuit” in Louisiana state court and “GoAuto removed the case to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana,” and then proceeds straight to the merits. (See “FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND,” first paragraph.) The court never identifies the statutory basis for federal jurisdiction, never cites 28 U.S.C. § 1332(d) (CAFA) or § 1332(a) (traditional diversity), and never mentions the numerosity, amount-in-controversy, minimal/complete diversity, or any CAFA exceptions (home-state, local controversy, etc.). The Discussion and holding are limited to Louisiana statutory interpretation and summary-judgment rulings; there is no jurisdictional analysis or remand briefing summarized or cited.

Relevant doctrinal points (what you would expect to see if jurisdiction were addressed):
- CAFA (28 U.S.C. § 1332(d)) requires (1) a class action, (2) minimal diversity (any class member and any defendant citizens of different states), (3) class size >100, and (4) aggregate amount in controversy > $5,000,000. CAFA also contains enumerated exceptions (e.g., home-state, local controversy) that removing parties or plaintiffs may litigate in district court.  
- Traditional diversity (28 U.S.C. § 1332(a)) requires complete diversity between plaintiffs and defendants and that each plaintiff’s claim exceed $75,000 (or the amount-in-controversy be satisfied for the representative plaintiff(s) under controlling law).

Why the Fifth Circuit likely didn’t address jurisdiction explicitly:
- Courts often omit a jurisdictional discussion when federal jurisdiction is neither contested on appeal nor central to the disposition. This opinion focuses on statutory interpretation and summary judgment; the panel affirmed on the merits without reaching jurisdictional issues. Nothing in the opinion indicates a jurisdictional challenge was presented to the Fifth Circuit, and the court gave no reason to reach or analyze subject-matter jurisdiction.

What to do to resolve the question for this case:
- Check the district-court docket and filings (the Notice of Removal, any Motion to Remand, and the district court’s order(s)). The Notice of Removal will state whether removal was premised on CAFA or on § 1332(a). If there was a remand motion, the district-court order resolving it (or noting it was not filed) will show whether jurisdiction was contested.  
- If you want the Fifth Circuit record, examine the briefs and the district-court record referenced in the Fifth Circuit docket (the appeal record will include the removal papers and any jurisdictional briefing, if any).

Conclusion: based solely on the Fifth Circuit opinion you provided, the jurisdictional hook is unspecified. To know whether GoAuto invoked CAFA or traditional diversity (and whether any CAFA exception was pressed), you must consult the Notice of Removal and related district-court filings.

### How does Jenkins (a third-party accident victim) have standing to challenge the insured’s policy cancellation?
Question:
Jenkins wasn’t an insured; he was hit by a GoAuto insured whose policy was allegedly canceled. On what legal theory does he have Article III standing and a Louisiana cause of action to contest the effectiveness of cancellation? Is this via the Louisiana Direct Action Statute, or some derivative right tied to coverage at the time of the accident? The opinion doesn’t explain why his injury is cognizable in this procedural posture.
Answer:
Short answer
The Fifth Circuit’s opinion never separately analyzes or rejects Jenkins’s Article III standing; it treats him as a co‑plaintiff and moves directly to the statutory‑interpretation question. Reading the opinion itself, Jenkins’s ability to sue is supported by two points the court emphasizes: (1) the Louisiana cancellation statute is designed to protect “persons…to have an interest in any loss” (so third‑party claimants are within the statute’s protective scope), and (2) the statute creates a procedural right (strict notice/certification requirements) whose violation can produce a concrete, redressable injury (loss of coverage at the time of an accident). Those features supply the legal bases—derivative/third‑party rights under state law and a concrete procedural injury—for Jenkins’s standing even though the panel did not separately discuss Article III.

Why that follows from the opinion (legal doctrine + textual support)
1) The statute contemplates third‑party interests. The court quotes La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(iv) and emphasizes that the finance company must send “Copies of the notice of cancellation…to all persons shown by the premium finance agreement to have an interest in any loss which may occur thereunder.” The opinion treats that requirement as part of the protective scheme for persons who may suffer loss if a policy is cancelled. That statutory text and the court’s repeated emphasis on it (see pp. discussing § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv)) make clear the legislature contemplated notice and certification duties owed with respect to third‑party loss interests. If a third party (like Jenkins) is someone who might suffer a loss under the policy, the statute’s procedural protections apply to the cancellation process that determines whether coverage exists.

2) The statute creates a procedural right whose breach can cause a concrete injury. The panel repeatedly characterizes Louisiana law as requiring “strict adherence” to the statutory cancellation procedures and explains the two functions of that strictness: “(1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” (quoting Britten). Those functions show the statute does more than regulate internal relationships between insurer and finance company: they protect the temporal and substantive scope of coverage, which directly affects third parties who rely on insureds’ policies. The court therefore treats failure to comply with the statute as producing an “ineffective cancellation” (Eaglin) — a legal consequence that could preserve coverage and remedy an injured third party’s exposure to an uninsured driver.

3) Redressability is implicit in the relief sought and litigated. The plaintiffs (Williams, Wright, and Jenkins) litigated whether GoAuto’s cancellation procedure “satisfied Louisiana law” and sought, effectively, judicial recognition that the cancellations were ineffective (and attendant damages/good faith remedies). The Fifth Circuit resolves that statutory question on the merits and affirms dismissal because procedures did comply. By entertaining and deciding that dispute, the court treats the plaintiffs’ asserted injury (loss of coverage, or the risk thereof, from allegedly ineffective cancellation) as cognizable and remediable under state law. The opinion therefore implies redressability: a favorable state‑law ruling would have preserved coverage or created other remedies for harmed third parties.

Practical label: derivative/third‑party right rather than a free‑standing federal cause of action
The opinion does not use the phrase “direct action statute” or treat the case as a classic Louisiana “direct action against insurer” provision; instead it treats Jenkins as a plaintiff in the class action challenging the effectiveness of cancellations under the premium‑finance statute. That posture is best described (consistent with the opinion) as a state‑law, derivative/third‑party interest in coverage protected by the cancellation statute — i.e., a third party asserting that the statutory cancellation procedures were not followed, so the policy remained in force at the time of the accident. The court’s reliance on the statute’s requirement to send notices to persons with an interest in a loss supports that characterization.

What the opinion does not do (and what you should not assume)
- The Fifth Circuit does not give a standalone Article III standing analysis for Jenkins. It simply proceeds on the parties’ framing of the case and decides the statutory question on the merits. The panel therefore implicitly accepts that Jenkins may litigate the statutory issue, but it does not articulate an explicit federal‑standing doctrine in the opinion.  
- The opinion does not invoke (and does not purport to decide) whether Louisiana’s “direct action” provisions elsewhere (if any) independently authorize third‑party suits against an insurer; instead, it resolves the case under the premium‑finance statutory scheme quoted and the state cases (Britten, Eaglin, Delatte) that treat the cancellation procedure as protective of third‑party interests.

Bottom line
Although the court didn’t separately analyze Article III standing, the opinion’s statutory text and its characterization of the statute’s protective, procedural functions make clear why a third‑party accident victim like Jenkins can be a plaintiff: the cancellation rules are written to protect “persons…to have an interest in any loss,” and a statutory defect in cancellation can produce the concrete, redressable injury (loss or risk of loss of coverage) that state law remedies. The Fifth Circuit simply accepted that posture and resolved the statutory question on the merits.

### What standard of review governed the appeal from summary judgment and statutory interpretation here?
Question:
The panel affirms summary judgment but doesn’t expressly state the standard of review. I assume de novo review of both the grant of summary judgment and the underlying legal question of interpreting La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3). Is there any reason the court omitted the usual summary judgment standard recitation, and does that omission matter for understanding the analysis?
Answer:
Short answer: The panel applied de novo review of both the grant of summary judgment and the statutory issues. The court simply omitted the formal boilerplate recitation of the summary-judgment standard, but the opinion’s structure and language make clear it treated the legal issues as questions of law and reviewed them anew. The omission does not change how you should read the analysis.

Why: two points from the opinion support this.

1) The nature of the issues: The panel framed the appeal as legal challenges to whether GoAuto’s procedures “comply with state law” and repeatedly treated interpretation of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3) as a pure question of law. For example, the panel “begin[s] by setting out Louisiana law for the cancellation of financed insurance policies, then examine[s] GoAuto’s procedures for complying with that law.” That is the standard approach when a court reviews legal questions de novo rather than factual findings.

2) The court’s language shows it reviewed and reached its own legal conclusions: the panel states legal conclusions in categorical terms—“we conclude that Louisiana law does not require a signature on the notice” and “we accept these definitions and conclude that GoAuto may cancel a policy once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation.” Those are not deferential formulations; they are direct legal holdings. The panel also affirms the district court because “GoAuto’s procedures comply with the statute,” not because it found the district court’s factfinding was plausible—again consistent with de novo review of legal interpretation and summary judgment.

Why the opinion omitted the usual boilerplate: Opinions sometimes skip the customary recital of the summary-judgment standard when the dispute turns on pure questions of statutory interpretation and the panel’s analysis proceeds directly to the governing law and its application. The panel’s choice to state the statute, discuss prior Louisiana authority, and then explain why GoAuto’s automated email/web-service process satisfies the statute serves the same function as an explicit “we review summary judgment de novo” sentence. There is no suggestion anywhere in the opinion that the court applied an abuse-of-discretion or clearly-erroneous standard.

Does the omission matter? No. The court’s analysis and the form of its holdings make plain the standard actually applied: the panel reviewed the statute and the parties’ legal arguments and resolved them as questions of law. The refusal to certify the question to the Louisiana Supreme Court (citing Jefferson v. Lead Indus.) further indicates the panel considered itself capable of deciding the legal question rather than deferring.

Relevant quotations to support this reading:
- The panel frames the task as legal: “we begin by setting out Louisiana law for the cancellation of financed insurance policies, then examine GoAuto’s procedures for complying with that law.”
- The panel states legal conclusions directly: “we conclude that Louisiana law does not require a signature on the notice sent by the premium finance company to the insurer.” “We accept these definitions and conclude that GoAuto may cancel a policy once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation.”
- The disposition: “The district court held there was no statutory violation and dismissed all claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM.”

So your initial assumption—de novo review of both summary judgment and the statutory interpretation—is correct, and the omitted formal recitation does not undermine that reading.

### Under Erie, how does the Fifth Circuit decide Louisiana law when the Louisiana Supreme Court hasn’t spoken?
Question:
The court relies on intermediate Louisiana appellate decisions (Delatte, Eaglin, Britten, Benitez) and Black’s Law Dictionary to interpret § 9:3550(G)(3). What is the Fifth Circuit’s methodology for predicting Louisiana Supreme Court rulings, especially given Louisiana’s civil law tradition and Civil Code interpretive canons? Should the court have anchored its analysis in Civil Code arts. 9–13 (plain meaning, context) rather than Black’s definitions?
Answer:
Short answer
Under Erie the Fifth Circuit predicts how the Louisiana Supreme Court would decide by (1) starting with the text of the statute, (2) giving effect to controlling Louisiana precedent (including intermediate appellate decisions) where the high court is silent, and (3) using persuasive aids (like Black’s) only to fill gaps. That is what the panel did here: it relied on the statute and Louisiana intermediate-appellate decisions (Delatte, Eaglin, Britten, Benitez), concluded the statute does not require a signature, adopted a common-law dictionary definition of “receipt” to resolve an ambiguity, and declined to certify the question to the Louisiana Supreme Court because no compelling reason to do so (citing Jefferson).

What the opinion shows about the Fifth Circuit’s Erie methodology
- Text-first: The panel repeatedly returns to the statute’s language. The court frames the legal question by quoting La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(b) and asks whether the statute’s express requirements have been met (Opinion, pp. 3–5, 9–12). It rejects adding requirements not in the statute: “Strict adherence … is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures.” (Op. at 10.)
- Precedent hierarchy: Because the Louisiana Supreme Court had not spoken on the precise questions (signature requirement; definition of “receipt”), the panel relied on intermediate Louisiana appellate decisions interpreting the statute (Delatte, Eaglin, Britten, Benitez) to see how Louisiana courts have treated similar facts (Op. at 7–10). The panel treats those decisions as the best available evidence of how the state’s highest court would decide.
- Gap-filling with persuasive sources: Where the statute and Louisiana appellate decisions were silent or inconclusive — for example, the meaning of “receipt” — the court used a leading law dictionary (Black’s) to supply a reasonable meaning and applied it to modern electronic processes (Op. at 11–13).
- Certification as last resort: The court invoked the well-known standard that it will not certify state-law questions lightly (citing Jefferson), and concluded that the absence of a Louisiana Supreme Court decision, by itself, did not compel certification (Op. at 13–14).

Why the panel used Black’s instead of Civil Code canons in this opinion
- The opinion does not cite Civil Code arts. 9–13 or other Civil Code interpretive canons. Instead the panel (correctly under Erie) looked first to the statute and then to existing Louisiana case law. Because the Louisiana high court had not defined “receipt” and lower courts had not settled on a single rule, the panel elected to use Black’s for a commonsense, widely accepted definition to resolve the statutory ambiguity (Op. at 11).
- The panel explains a policy reason for restraint: it will not “add to the procedures” set by the legislature or import requirements that do not appear in the statute (no signature requirement) (Op. at 10). Using Black’s to define an ordinary word in the statute fits that restrained approach: it supplies a neutral meaning rather than imposing a new substantive rule.
- The court’s choice is consistent with the Erie task: when the state’s highest court has not spoken, a federal court should look to existing state decisions and other evidence of state law. Dictionaries and other authorities are an accepted form of evidence about how a term is understood if state courts have not defined it.

A short assessment (limited to what the opinion itself shows)
- The Fifth Circuit followed a conventional Erie approach as reflected in the opinion: statutory text + Louisiana appellate precedent + secondary aids to fill silence. It declined to impose additional requirements (signature, etc.) that the statute does not contain and which Louisiana appellate courts have not uniformly required.
- One can reasonably critique that the panel did not invoke Civil Code interpretive articles (arts. 9–13) in its analysis. But the opinion shows the panel believed the answer could be reached from the statute, the intermediate-appellate decisions, and a dictionary definition without adding interpretive apparatus; and it expressly refused to certify the issue to the Louisiana Supreme Court because the absence of high-court guidance alone was not “a compelling reason” to certify (Op. at 13–14).

Bottom line
Under Erie the Fifth Circuit used the statute’s text and Louisiana intermediate-appellate precedent as the primary guides, and employed Black’s to resolve an ambiguity where the Louisiana Supreme Court had not spoken. The panel declined to read in requirements (like a signature) that are not in the statute. The opinion shows that using a dictionary as a gap-filler is an acceptable, restrained Erie technique when state high-court authority and lower-court consensus are lacking; the panel did not treat the absence of a Louisiana Supreme Court decision as sufficient reason to certify.

### Why did the court decline to certify the statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court?
Question:
The panel acknowledges the Louisiana Supreme Court hasn’t defined “receipt” or addressed whether “certifying” requires a signature, yet it declines certification, citing Jefferson’s general reluctance. What factors did the court implicitly weigh (e.g., clarity of statutory text, availability of intermediate decisions, lack of split)? Would the industry-wide importance of cancellation mechanics typically weigh in favor of certification under Fifth Circuit practice?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel declined to certify because, applying Jefferson, the question was not sufficiently novel or unsettled to justify using the certification tool: the statutory text and existing Louisiana appellate authority (though not dispositive) gave the court a principled basis to decide; there was no clear state-court split or compelling uncertainty that only the Louisiana Supreme Court could resolve. The industry importance of cancellation mechanics is a relevant consideration but, under Fifth Circuit practice, importance alone ordinarily does not overcome the court’s reluctance to certify.

Why the court declined — the factors it implicitly weighed
1. Jefferson presumption against certification. The opinion cites Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247, and repeats the Fifth Circuit’s well‑established reluctance to certify “absent a compelling reason.” The court treated that rule as the starting point and required more than the mere absence of a Louisiana Supreme Court opinion to warrant certification (“That a term has not yet been defined by a state supreme court ‘is not sufficient to warrant certification.’”).

2. Clarity (or workable guidance) in the statutory text. The court read La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) and (b) and found the statute’s plain language permitted the court to interpret “certifying” and “receipt” without creating new requirements. For example, the panel observed the statute does not mention a signature and rejected importing one where the text is silent: “The statute makes no explicit reference to a signature… ‘Strict adherence’… is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures. We conclude that Louisiana law does not require a signature on the notice.” That reliance on textual clarity undercuts the need for certification.

3. Existing intermediate appellate decisions and prior state authorities. The court surveyed Louisiana appellate decisions (KMJ Servs., Hodges, Lewis, Benitez, Delatte, Eaglin, Britten) and found that, while none expressly resolved the signature/receipt questions in a controlling way, they supplied enough points of comparison and precedent to allow the panel to reach a reasoned decision. The opinion cites those cases and distinguishes Benitez (which noted absence of signature but did not hold that absence was dispositive) to show the court was not deciding in a factual or doctrinal vacuum.

4. Ability to interpret statutory language using ordinary legal tools. The court supplemented the statutory text with dictionary definitions (Black’s) and common‑law understandings of receipt and electronic processes to conclude GoAuto’s machine receipt satisfied “receipt.” That methodological availability indicated the federal court could resolve the question predictably without state‑court input.

5. Lack of a clear or material split among Louisiana courts that would make certification necessary. The opinion does not identify a definitive conflict in state appellate decisions that only the state supreme court could harmonize; the cited cases are described as “similarly unhelpful” rather than conflicting. In that procedural posture, certification was not compelled.

How industry importance figures in Fifth Circuit practice
- Relevance is acknowledged but not dispositive. The plaintiffs argued the interpretation is important to Louisiana’s insurance industry and statutory scheme. The panel explicitly recognized the argument (the plaintiffs asked for certification because of industrywide importance) but still declined under Jefferson. That demonstrates the Fifth Circuit treats practical importance as a factor but will not allow importance alone to overcome the circuit’s presumption against certification.
- When certification is more likely. Certification is more likely when (a) the state supreme court has not spoken, (b) intermediate appellate authority is sparse or in direct conflict, (c) the question is novel or uniquely calls for the state court’s policy judgment, or (d) there is a significant risk that a federal court’s interpretation would create disharmony with state law or statewide uncertainty. The opinion shows none of those threshold conditions were met here — the text was workable and existing authorities gave guidance — so industry importance did not tip the balance.

Bottom line
The panel weighed Jefferson’s default reluctance, the statute’s plain text, available (if imperfect) Louisiana appellate authority, and the court’s ability to apply ordinary interpretive tools (including dictionary definitions and an understanding of modern electronic receipt). Those considerations collectively made certification unnecessary even though the Louisiana Supreme Court had not previously defined “receipt” or addressed a signature requirement. In Fifth Circuit practice, practical or industry importance is a relevant but not controlling consideration; it would not by itself justify certification absent the additional features (novelty, conflict, or inadequate state guidance) that Jefferson requires.

### Does 'certifying' under La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3)(a) require a signature by a natural person?
Question:
Plaintiffs argued the cancellation was ineffective because the APAC certification wasn’t signed until after automated cancellation. The statute doesn’t mention a signature. The panel held a company can 'certify' without an identified employee’s signature. Under Louisiana law, does 'certify' carry any implication of a signed attestation? How (if at all) do Louisiana’s UETA or ESIGN principles bear on whether an electronic or automated certification suffices?
Answer:
Short answer
No. Under the Fifth Circuit’s reading of La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3)(a) in this opinion, “certifying” does not carry a statutory requirement that a natural person sign the certification. The court held that a premium finance company may “certify” by sending an electronic notice (here, an automated e‑mail/web‑service request) and that Louisiana law does not require a named or handwritten signature on the notice.

Why — doctrine and the court’s reasoning
- The statute is silent about signatures. The court repeatedly notes that La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3)(a) requires “a statement certifying” certain facts but “makes no explicit reference to a signature.” The panel refused to add a signature requirement that the legislature did not include: “Strict adherence to the statute ‘is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures.’ We conclude that Louisiana law does not require a signature on the notice sent by the premium finance company to the insurer.” (Op. pp. discussing I. Strict Adherence and quoted language.)  
- Prior Louisiana decisions offered no clear rule requiring a human signature. The panel reviewed appellate decisions (including Benitez, which noted the absence of a signature but did not hold that absence alone invalidated the cancellation) and found no authority imposing a signature requirement. The court relied on that gap to conclude a company may certify without identifying a particular employee. (Op. ¶s summarizing prior cases and Benitez.)  
- The court treated “certifying” as something a corporate actor may do through its processes. The statute speaks of “the insurance premium finance company” certifying; the panel read that language to allow a company to certify through its established procedures rather than through a named, signed individual. (Op. text noting statute refers to the company as the certifying actor.)

What the opinion says about electronic/automated certification (UETA/ESIGN)
- The court explicitly accepted that modern, computerized processes can satisfy the statute. It described APAC’s automated e‑mail/web‑service request that triggers GoAuto’s computer system to cancel the policy and said: “This is a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did. GoAuto’s method of receiving the notice and reacting to it satisfies the statute.” (Op. paragraph discussing receipt and web service.)  
- The opinion does not analyze or cite Louisiana’s UETA, the federal ESIGN Act, or any electronic‑signature statutes. It therefore does not rest its holding on UETA/ESIGN principles; rather it relies on the statute’s text, prior case law (or lack thereof), and the practical reality of automated corporate processes. Because the court did not invoke UETA/ESIGN, you cannot read the opinion as declaring those statutes dispositive here — only that electronic/automated transmission and machine processing satisfied La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3)(a) as interpreted.

Bottom line for a law student
- Under this Fifth Circuit opinion, “certifying” under La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3)(a) need not involve a signature by a natural person; a company can certify via its corporate/automated processes. (See the court’s holding that the statute does not require a signature and its description of the automated e‑mail/web‑service cancellation process.)  
- The court accepted electronic/automated certification as sufficient on the facts presented, but it did so by construing the statute and existing state case law (or the absence of decision requiring a signature), not by applying UETA/ESIGN authority. If you need a definitive statement about how Louisiana’s electronic‑signature statutes interact with § 9:3550, that specific statutory interaction was not addressed by the opinion.

### How did the court conclude 'receipt' includes automated intake by an insurer’s web service?
Question:
The statute triggers cancellation 'upon receipt' of the certification by the insurer. The panel uses Black’s to define 'receipt' and holds that GoAuto’s automated web service taking custody of the email request counts. Is there Louisiana authority treating electronic receipt by an automated system as 'receipt' by a legal person? Does statutory text or Louisiana interpretive practice require some human agency before an insurer may 'proceed to cancel'?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel read “receipt” in the statute according to its ordinary meaning (using Black’s), and held that taking “possession, delivery, or custody” by GoAuto’s automated systems satisfies that meaning. The court therefore rejected the plaintiffs’ argument that a human signature or human physical possession must precede cancellation. The opinion notes there is no Louisiana authority requiring human agency and declines to add such a requirement.

Why the court reached that conclusion (doctrinal and textual points)
- No controlling state definition: The Louisiana Supreme Court has not defined “receipt” in § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i), and the statute itself contains no definition. The panel therefore relied on the ordinary-meaning approach rather than importing a new procedural requirement. (Op. ¶ beginning “The Louisiana Supreme Court has not defined ‘receipt’…”)

- Ordinary meaning from Black’s: The court quoted Black’s Law Dictionary definitions of “receipt” as “the act of receiving…by taking physical possession” and “receive” as “to take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” The court adopted those definitions and concluded the insurer may cancel once it “takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation.” (Op. ¶ beginning “A leading law dictionary defines ‘receipt’…”)

- Application to automated systems: The panel described how APAC’s system sends an e‑mail that a GoAuto web service (a “listener”) receives and immediately triggers cancellation. Because the web service takes custody of the emailed request and the GoAuto computer system effects cancellation upon that electronic intake, the court treated that event as “receipt.” The court explicitly framed this as a modern-statement point: “This is a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did. GoAuto’s method of receiving the notice and reacting to it satisfies the statute.” (Op. ¶ beginning “We accept these definitions…” and the paragraph starting “No person is involved until later in the morning…”)

- Refusal to add a signature/human-possession requirement: Plaintiffs asked the court to treat “certifying” as imposing a signature/human-actor requirement before cancellation. The panel examined prior Louisiana decisions, observed that none requires a signature as a condition of cancellation (and one case that criticized lack of signature did not hold it fatal), and held the statute does not require a signature. The court warned against judicially adding requirements not in the statutory text: “Strict adherence … is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures.” (Op. ¶s beginning “With an exception we will discuss…” and “We conclude that Louisiana law does not require a signature…”)

What the opinion says about Louisiana authority and certification
- There is no Louisiana appellate or supreme-court holding definitively treating automated electronic intake as “receipt”; the panel expressly notes the state high court has not defined the term. The court therefore relied on dictionary meaning and the undisputed facts about the web service. (Op. ¶ beginning “The Louisiana Supreme Court has not defined ‘receipt’…”)

- The panel declined the plaintiffs’ invitation to certify the question to the Louisiana Supreme Court, explaining that merely because a state court has not defined a term is not by itself a sufficient reason to certify. (Op. ¶ beginning “The Plaintiffs request we certify…” and Jefferson citation.)

Bottom line for how courts will treat this issue
- Under this Fifth Circuit opinion, “receipt” in § 9:3550 includes electronic receipt by an automated system that takes custody of a sent notice and triggers cancellation. The statute’s text does not, by its terms and as interpreted here, require a human actor, a human signature, or physical hand‑to‑hand delivery before the insurer “may proceed to cancel.” The panel’s holding rests on (1) the lack of a conflicting Louisiana definition, (2) ordinary‑meaning dictionary definitions, and (3) the undisputed operational facts showing the insurer’s system took custody of the cancellation request.

### What satisfies subpart (iv)’s requirement to specify names and addresses of interested parties?
Question:
Section 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(iv) requires the certification to specify the names and addresses of any governmental agencies, secured parties, or third parties to whom notice was sent. The sample certification in the record says copies were sent but doesn’t list specific names/addresses. Did the court infer there were no such parties, making a list unnecessary, or does a general statement meet 'strict adherence' to subpart (iv)?
Answer:
Short answer
The Fifth Circuit treated APAC’s blanket certification that “Copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation were sent to all persons shown by the Premium Finance Agreement to have an interest…” (and that copies were enclosed/affidavit available) as meeting the statutory certification requirement. The court did not require a printed list of names and addresses and did not (on this record) infer there were no interested parties; instead it declined to add to the statutory requirements and accepted the company’s certified statement as sufficient.

Why — the court’s reasoning tied to the opinion
- The statute requires the premium finance company to “certif[y]” that “Copies of the notice of cancellation were sent to all persons shown by the premium finance agreement … specifying the names and addresses of any governmental agencies, holders of a security interest … or third parties to whom the insurance premium finance company has sent notice of cancellation.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(iv) (quoted in the opinion).
- The actual APAC cancellation request (quoted in the opinion) said, in relevant part: “(4) Copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation were sent to all persons shown by the Premium Finance Agreement to have an interest in any loss which may occur there under.” It also noted copies were enclosed and affidavit proof of mailing was available upon request.
- The court emphasized that Louisiana law requires “strict adherence” to the statute but also warned that courts should not judicially add requirements beyond the statute: “Strict adherence to the procedures for cancellation is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures.” (Opinion, discussion of signature requirement.)
- Applying that principle, the Fifth Circuit rejected imposing extra-formalities (e.g., a named signatory) and concluded that the company can “certify information without a named employee being identified.” By parity, the court accepted the company’s certification that copies were sent to the interested parties rather than insisting on a separately itemized list in every instance.

What the opinion does and does not hold (important for doctrinal limits)
- The opinion does not hold that an express, itemized list of names and addresses can never be required under Louisiana law; it holds that in this case the statutorily required “certifying” was satisfied by APAC’s statement that copies were sent to all persons shown on the finance agreement (plus the enclosures/affidavit-available statement), and that courts should not add extra formal requirements not in the statute.
- The court also did not treat the absence of a list as meaning there were no interested parties; it accepted the certification as a substantive assertion by the finance company that the notices were sent to whoever the agreement showed as interested.

Bottom line for practice
If a premium finance company issues a certification repeating the statutory language that copies were sent to all persons shown by the finance agreement (and can produce the enclosures or proof of mailing on request), this opinion treats that as sufficient under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(iv). A practitioner seeking to avoid dispute should still consider including an appended list of recipients and addresses or contemporaneous proof of mailing to make the factual record clear, but the Fifth Circuit here declined to require that extra step as a matter of statutory interpretation.

### How does 'strict adherence' to § 9:3550 reconcile with the court declining to require a signature?
Question:
Louisiana courts insist on strict compliance for finance-company cancellations, yet the panel rejects reading a signature requirement into 'certifying.' How does the court square strict-compliance doctrine (fixing date/time and cure period) with a more functional approach to certification content and form? Is the line drawn at textual requirements only, with no room to add formalities by implication?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel treats “strict adherence” as requiring that the statute’s substantive, textually specified elements be met (power of attorney, default, sending the ten‑day notice with date, notice to interested parties, and insurer receipt), and that the statutory objectives (fixing date/time of cancellation and preserving the cure period) be accomplished. But the court refuses to read additional formalities—like a handwritten or named signature—into the word “certifying” where the statute does not require one. In other words, Louisiana’s strict‑compliance rule constrains courts from excusing defects or tolerating omissions of the statute’s required items, but it does not empower courts to add extra signature formalities by implication.

Explanation tied to the opinion

1) What “strict adherence” protects
The opinion explains the two policy functions Louisiana courts attach to strict adherence: “(1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten, 503 So. 2d at 1154 (quoted at pp. 3–4). The Fifth Circuit evaluates GoAuto’s procedures against those goals: the e‑mailed request and the automated web‑service cancellation provided an electronic record/time stamp (fixing time/date) and the ten‑day notice furnished the cure period. Because those statutory objectives were achieved, the court found the statutory scheme’s purpose satisfied.

2) What “certifying” requires (and what it does not)
The statute requires “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” four specified items. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv) (quoted at pp. 2–3). The plaintiffs argued “certifying” necessarily means a signature before cancellation. The panel rejects that reading for two reasons in the opinion:

- The statute itself does not say “signed” or identify any signature requirement (p. 6). The court observed that when the legislature wants a signature it could say so, but the text here does not.
- Existing Louisiana decisions are equivocal and do not treat a signature as an indispensable component. The opinion cites Benitez (which noted lack of a signature but did not hold it fatal) and concludes “we see no reason that a company cannot certify information without a named employee being identified” (pp. 5–6).

The panel states the principle succinctly: “Strict adherence to the procedures for cancellation is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures. We conclude that Louisiana law does not require a signature on the notice sent by the premium finance company to the insurer.” (p. 6.)

3) How formality is assessed practically
The court treats the email/web‑service/timestamp process as legally sufficient “receipt” by the insurer. After surveying dictionary definitions and the statute’s text, it held that “GoAuto may cancel a policy once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation” and that the automated receipt by the GoAuto web service satisfied that requirement (pp. 6–7). Thus a functional, contemporary method of transmitting and recording the certification can meet the statute so long as the statutory elements and purposes (time stamp, cure period, notice to interested parties, etc.) are satisfied.

Bottom line — where the line is drawn
The court draws the line at the statute’s text and objectives. Courts will enforce the statute’s listed, substantive requirements strictly (any defect in those required items can render a cancellation ineffective), but the courts will not graft on additional formalities (like a handwritten signature or identification of the particular employee) that the statute does not demand. Put another way: strict compliance controls the content and the policy goals the statute protects; the content can be supplied in a functionally equivalent modern form unless the statute itself prescribes a particular formality.

This explains why the panel both invoked Louisiana’s strict‑adherence rule and declined to require a signature: strict adherence prevents courts from overlooking the statute’s required elements or its protective functions, but it does not allow courts to create extra requirements not found in the statute.

### What is the legal significance of the district court’s 'private carrier' rationale that the Fifth Circuit labeled 'imaginative'?
Question:
The district court apparently analogized the APAC employee who delivered notices to a 'private carrier' to satisfy the statute’s 'by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier' language. The Fifth Circuit declined to reach that ground and affirmed based on email 'receipt' instead, calling the definition 'imaginative and unconventional.' Is the district court’s reasoning dicta now, and can future litigants rely on it? More generally, when will the Fifth Circuit affirm on alternative grounds versus expressly disavowing them?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — the district court’s “private carrier” theory is effectively dicta and has weak precedential force. The Fifth Circuit explicitly declined to adopt that theory, describing it as “imaginative and unconventional” and saying it would not consider its validity because the court resolved the case on a different ground (email “receipt”). Future litigants may cite the district-court reasoning as persuasive authority, but not as binding precedent, and should expect the Fifth Circuit and other courts to treat it accordingly.

Explanation and authority from the opinion
- The Fifth Circuit resolved the appeal on whether GoAuto’s automated e-mail/receipt process satisfied La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3). After finding that the e-mail/web-service interaction “satisfies the statute,” the court expressly declined to reach the district court’s alternative rationale: “We mention that the district court held GoAuto’s cancellation procedures complied with the statute in a different way: ‘the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.’ … The district court’s definition of ‘private carrier’ was both imaginative and unconventional. Because we find the e‑mail satisfies the statute, we will not consider the validity of the definition.” (Op. pp. [quoted text].)
- That language shows two things: (1) the Fifth Circuit did not adopt the district court’s private‑carrier definition; and (2) the panel explicitly declined to rule on it because the email ground was dispositive. Where an appellate court refuses to adopt and expressly disclaims an alternative ground, that alternative is not part of the panel’s holding.

Doctrinal rule about holdings versus dicta
- Binding precedent is the court’s holding — the rule necessary to decide the case. Statements that are unnecessary to the disposition are dictum and are not binding, though they may be persuasive.
- Here the holding is that GoAuto’s email/web‑service process constituted “receipt” under the statute; the district court’s private‑carrier analogy was unnecessary to the Fifth Circuit’s disposition and was in fact singled out and rejected. That makes the district court’s theory (and any adoption of it) nonbinding dicta.

Practical consequences for future litigants
- Don’t treat the district court’s “private carrier” reasoning as authoritative precedent in the Fifth Circuit. The Fifth Circuit criticized it and chose not to adopt it.
- You may cite the district‑court reasoning as persuasive (especially in another district court), but be prepared to answer that the Fifth Circuit declined to validate it and that the Fifth Circuit’s own holding rests on electronic receipt, not the private‑carrier concept.
- If you want a binding rule on “private carrier” under § 9:3550(G)(3), the safe route is to pressure the issue (e.g., preserve it on appeal and seek an express ruling from an appellate court, or seek certification to the Louisiana Supreme Court). The Fifth Circuit here declined certification and declined to reach the question.

When the Fifth Circuit affirms on alternative grounds vs disavows them
- The panel will affirm on an alternative ground when that alternative ground is properly presented, preserved, and sufficient to sustain the judgment; in that circumstance the appellate court may adopt that alternative as its holding.
- The panel will disavow or decline to adopt district-court reasoning when (a) the panel resolves the case on a different, dispositive ground and thus the alternative is unnecessary, or (b) the panel believes the alternative is legally suspect and chooses not to endorse it. The opinion here is an example of (a) coupled with (b): the e‑mail ground was dispositive, and the panel criticized the alternative as “imaginative and unconventional,” so it declined to adopt that reasoning.

Bottom line
The Fifth Circuit’s opinion is the binding precedent on the points it decided (notably that e‑mail/automated receipt satisfied the statute). The district court’s “private carrier” rationale is dicta with limited persuasive value and should not be relied on as controlling authority in the Fifth Circuit.

Short answer: Yes — under this opinion, the presence on the face of the notice of an explicit certification that it contains the four statutory items in La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv) is what matters; a signature or a named individual is not required.

Why: the panel applies strict-adherence principles (citing Delatte, Eaglin, Britten) only to the statutory content, not to additional formalities the statute does not demand. The court contrasted Benitez, where the cancellation document “does not identify any particular employee . . . is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein,” with the notices here which “certif[y] the relevant information.” The panel explained that Benitez was distinguishable because the Benitez notice lacked an affirmative certification of the required statements, whereas the APAC notices here expressly “APAC certifies that:” and then list the four required items. Op. at pp. ___ (discussion I & Benitez paragraph).

Key textual points from the opinion:
- The statute prescribes that the premium finance company must send “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the four specific matters. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv) (quoted in the opinion).
- The court held that nothing in the statute requires a signature or named preparer: “we see no reason that a company cannot certify information without a named employee being identified” and “we conclude that Louisiana law does not require a signature on the notice.” Op. ¶¶ discussing Benitez and signature requirement.
- The practical consequence the court enforces is content: the notice must actually certify the statutory items; absence of that certification (as in Benitez) is problematic.

Limits and caveats:
- The court enforces strict adherence to the statutory content — the “magic word” alone is not sufficient if the document does not in substance assert the enumerated statutory items. The opinion treats the presence of the explicit certification plus the four listed assertions as satisfying the statute.
- The court grounded its conclusion in the statute’s language that it is “the insurance premium finance company” who effects cancellation by “certifying” the information, not in an independent policy requiring signatures or named employees. Op. ¶¶ discussing statutory text.

Bottom line: Benitez teaches that a document that neither purports to certify nor identifies its preparer fails the statutory requirement; by contrast, an unsigned electronic or template notice that nonetheless expressly “certifies” the four statutory items on its face satisfies the statute under this opinion.
