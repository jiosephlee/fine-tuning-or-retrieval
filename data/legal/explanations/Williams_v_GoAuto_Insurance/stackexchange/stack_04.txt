### Under Erie, how does the Fifth Circuit decide Louisiana law when the Louisiana Supreme Court hasn’t spoken?
Question:
The court relies on intermediate Louisiana appellate decisions (Delatte, Eaglin, Britten, Benitez) and Black’s Law Dictionary to interpret § 9:3550(G)(3). What is the Fifth Circuit’s methodology for predicting Louisiana Supreme Court rulings, especially given Louisiana’s civil law tradition and Civil Code interpretive canons? Should the court have anchored its analysis in Civil Code arts. 9–13 (plain meaning, context) rather than Black’s definitions?
Answer:
Short answer
Under Erie the Fifth Circuit predicts how the Louisiana Supreme Court would decide by (1) starting with the text of the statute, (2) giving effect to controlling Louisiana precedent (including intermediate appellate decisions) where the high court is silent, and (3) using persuasive aids (like Black’s) only to fill gaps. That is what the panel did here: it relied on the statute and Louisiana intermediate-appellate decisions (Delatte, Eaglin, Britten, Benitez), concluded the statute does not require a signature, adopted a common-law dictionary definition of “receipt” to resolve an ambiguity, and declined to certify the question to the Louisiana Supreme Court because no compelling reason to do so (citing Jefferson).

What the opinion shows about the Fifth Circuit’s Erie methodology
- Text-first: The panel repeatedly returns to the statute’s language. The court frames the legal question by quoting La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(b) and asks whether the statute’s express requirements have been met (Opinion, pp. 3–5, 9–12). It rejects adding requirements not in the statute: “Strict adherence … is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures.” (Op. at 10.)
- Precedent hierarchy: Because the Louisiana Supreme Court had not spoken on the precise questions (signature requirement; definition of “receipt”), the panel relied on intermediate Louisiana appellate decisions interpreting the statute (Delatte, Eaglin, Britten, Benitez) to see how Louisiana courts have treated similar facts (Op. at 7–10). The panel treats those decisions as the best available evidence of how the state’s highest court would decide.
- Gap-filling with persuasive sources: Where the statute and Louisiana appellate decisions were silent or inconclusive — for example, the meaning of “receipt” — the court used a leading law dictionary (Black’s) to supply a reasonable meaning and applied it to modern electronic processes (Op. at 11–13).
- Certification as last resort: The court invoked the well-known standard that it will not certify state-law questions lightly (citing Jefferson), and concluded that the absence of a Louisiana Supreme Court decision, by itself, did not compel certification (Op. at 13–14).

Why the panel used Black’s instead of Civil Code canons in this opinion
- The opinion does not cite Civil Code arts. 9–13 or other Civil Code interpretive canons. Instead the panel (correctly under Erie) looked first to the statute and then to existing Louisiana case law. Because the Louisiana high court had not defined “receipt” and lower courts had not settled on a single rule, the panel elected to use Black’s for a commonsense, widely accepted definition to resolve the statutory ambiguity (Op. at 11).
- The panel explains a policy reason for restraint: it will not “add to the procedures” set by the legislature or import requirements that do not appear in the statute (no signature requirement) (Op. at 10). Using Black’s to define an ordinary word in the statute fits that restrained approach: it supplies a neutral meaning rather than imposing a new substantive rule.
- The court’s choice is consistent with the Erie task: when the state’s highest court has not spoken, a federal court should look to existing state decisions and other evidence of state law. Dictionaries and other authorities are an accepted form of evidence about how a term is understood if state courts have not defined it.

A short assessment (limited to what the opinion itself shows)
- The Fifth Circuit followed a conventional Erie approach as reflected in the opinion: statutory text + Louisiana appellate precedent + secondary aids to fill silence. It declined to impose additional requirements (signature, etc.) that the statute does not contain and which Louisiana appellate courts have not uniformly required.
- One can reasonably critique that the panel did not invoke Civil Code interpretive articles (arts. 9–13) in its analysis. But the opinion shows the panel believed the answer could be reached from the statute, the intermediate-appellate decisions, and a dictionary definition without adding interpretive apparatus; and it expressly refused to certify the issue to the Louisiana Supreme Court because the absence of high-court guidance alone was not “a compelling reason” to certify (Op. at 13–14).

Bottom line
Under Erie the Fifth Circuit used the statute’s text and Louisiana intermediate-appellate precedent as the primary guides, and employed Black’s to resolve an ambiguity where the Louisiana Supreme Court had not spoken. The panel declined to read in requirements (like a signature) that are not in the statute. The opinion shows that using a dictionary as a gap-filler is an acceptable, restrained Erie technique when state high-court authority and lower-court consensus are lacking; the panel did not treat the absence of a Louisiana Supreme Court decision as sufficient reason to certify.
