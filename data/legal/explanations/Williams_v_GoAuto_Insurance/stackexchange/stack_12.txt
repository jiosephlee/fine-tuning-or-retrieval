Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

### What is the legal significance of the district court’s 'private carrier' rationale that the Fifth Circuit labeled 'imaginative'?
Question:
The district court apparently analogized the APAC employee who delivered notices to a 'private carrier' to satisfy the statute’s 'by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier' language. The Fifth Circuit declined to reach that ground and affirmed based on email 'receipt' instead, calling the definition 'imaginative and unconventional.' Is the district court’s reasoning dicta now, and can future litigants rely on it? More generally, when will the Fifth Circuit affirm on alternative grounds versus expressly disavowing them?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — the district court’s “private carrier” theory is effectively dicta and has weak precedential force. The Fifth Circuit explicitly declined to adopt that theory, describing it as “imaginative and unconventional” and saying it would not consider its validity because the court resolved the case on a different ground (email “receipt”). Future litigants may cite the district-court reasoning as persuasive authority, but not as binding precedent, and should expect the Fifth Circuit and other courts to treat it accordingly.

Explanation and authority from the opinion
- The Fifth Circuit resolved the appeal on whether GoAuto’s automated e-mail/receipt process satisfied La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3). After finding that the e-mail/web-service interaction “satisfies the statute,” the court expressly declined to reach the district court’s alternative rationale: “We mention that the district court held GoAuto’s cancellation procedures complied with the statute in a different way: ‘the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.’ … The district court’s definition of ‘private carrier’ was both imaginative and unconventional. Because we find the e‑mail satisfies the statute, we will not consider the validity of the definition.” (Op. pp. [quoted text].)
- That language shows two things: (1) the Fifth Circuit did not adopt the district court’s private‑carrier definition; and (2) the panel explicitly declined to rule on it because the email ground was dispositive. Where an appellate court refuses to adopt and expressly disclaims an alternative ground, that alternative is not part of the panel’s holding.

Doctrinal rule about holdings versus dicta
- Binding precedent is the court’s holding — the rule necessary to decide the case. Statements that are unnecessary to the disposition are dictum and are not binding, though they may be persuasive.
- Here the holding is that GoAuto’s email/web‑service process constituted “receipt” under the statute; the district court’s private‑carrier analogy was unnecessary to the Fifth Circuit’s disposition and was in fact singled out and rejected. That makes the district court’s theory (and any adoption of it) nonbinding dicta.

Practical consequences for future litigants
- Don’t treat the district court’s “private carrier” reasoning as authoritative precedent in the Fifth Circuit. The Fifth Circuit criticized it and chose not to adopt it.
- You may cite the district‑court reasoning as persuasive (especially in another district court), but be prepared to answer that the Fifth Circuit declined to validate it and that the Fifth Circuit’s own holding rests on electronic receipt, not the private‑carrier concept.
- If you want a binding rule on “private carrier” under § 9:3550(G)(3), the safe route is to pressure the issue (e.g., preserve it on appeal and seek an express ruling from an appellate court, or seek certification to the Louisiana Supreme Court). The Fifth Circuit here declined certification and declined to reach the question.

When the Fifth Circuit affirms on alternative grounds vs disavows them
- The panel will affirm on an alternative ground when that alternative ground is properly presented, preserved, and sufficient to sustain the judgment; in that circumstance the appellate court may adopt that alternative as its holding.
- The panel will disavow or decline to adopt district-court reasoning when (a) the panel resolves the case on a different, dispositive ground and thus the alternative is unnecessary, or (b) the panel believes the alternative is legally suspect and chooses not to endorse it. The opinion here is an example of (a) coupled with (b): the e‑mail ground was dispositive, and the panel criticized the alternative as “imaginative and unconventional,” so it declined to adopt that reasoning.

Bottom line
The Fifth Circuit’s opinion is the binding precedent on the points it decided (notably that e‑mail/automated receipt satisfied the statute). The district court’s “private carrier” rationale is dicta with limited persuasive value and should not be relied on as controlling authority in the Fifth Circuit.
