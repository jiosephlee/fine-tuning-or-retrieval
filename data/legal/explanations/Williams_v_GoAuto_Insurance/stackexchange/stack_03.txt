Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

### How does the "strict adherence" requirement square with the court’s refusal to add a signature requirement not in the text?
Question:
Louisiana courts demand "strict adherence" to § 9:3550 when a finance company cancels under a power of attorney, and any defect invalidates cancellation. Plaintiffs argued that strict adherence means insisting on a signature and other formalities to fix timing and ensure accuracy. The Fifth Circuit responded that strict adherence isn't an invitation to add requirements not in the statute. Do Louisiana cases define 'strict adherence' as compliance with the express text only, or do they sometimes infer procedural safeguards (like signatures or named preparers) from terms such as 'certifying'? How do cases like Delatte, Eaglin, Britten, and Benitez frame the line between strict textual compliance and judicially implied formalities?
Answer:
Short answer
Louisiana’s “strict adherence” doctrine enforces the statute’s express requirements and the statutory purposes those requirements serve (fixing the time/date of cancellation and protecting the insured’s cure period). It does not license courts to invent additional formalities (like a handwritten signature or a named preparer) that the Legislature did not require. That is exactly what the Fifth Circuit held in Williams v. GoAuto: absent clear textual or controlling state-court authority, courts should not graft a signature requirement onto La. R.S. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Williams also explains the flip side: Louisiana cases will invalidate cancellations for defects that defeat the statute’s functions.

How the cases fit together
- Delatte: the opinion quotes Delatte for the broad principle that “Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Williams treats Delatte as the source of the strict-adherence rule — i.e., courts will enforce the statute’s requirements closely. (Williams opinion, quoting Delatte at 633 So. 2d 686, 689.)

- Eaglin: Williams also cites Eaglin for the related proposition that “[a]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” That language supports invalidating cancellations when statutorily required elements are missing or when the process has a material defect. (Williams quoting Eaglin, 558 So. 2d at 287.)

- Britten: Williams quotes Britten as explaining the function of “strict adherence”: it (1) “provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation,” and (2) “grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Those functional goals are the touchstone for determining whether a procedural shortcoming is fatal. (Williams quoting Britten, 503 So. 2d at 1154.)

- Benitez: Williams discusses Benitez, which criticized a notice for “not identif[ying] any particular employee … [and] is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” But crucially, Williams points out that Benitez did not hold that lack of a signature alone automatically invalidates a cancellation. Instead Benitez identified the absence of signature/named preparer as a shortcoming among others. (Williams citing Benitez, 285 So. 3d at 579.)

What Williams adds and the governing line
Williams articulates the controlling line: strict adherence requires compliance with the statute’s text and the statutory purposes (the Britten functions). Courts will strike down cancellations that fail to meet the statute’s express requirements or that defeat those purposes. But courts will not create additional procedural requirements — such as a mandatory handwritten signature or a requirement that the notice name the individual preparer — unless the statute says so or Louisiana precedent clearly demands it. As Williams puts it, “Strict adherence … is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures.” Williams then concludes that an e‑mailed company statement on letterhead that “certifies” the required facts is consistent with the statute and that receipt can occur electronically. (Williams, Oct. 3, 2025.)

Practical implications
- To satisfy § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) under current Louisiana authority (as read in Williams), make sure the statutorily listed items are actually stated and that the insurer can show receipt of that statement. Those are the hallmarks courts enforce.  
- A handwritten signature or named preparer is helpful evidence but, standing alone, is not required by the statute as currently interpreted; however, absence of those elements can be cited by a court (as in Benitez) when other defects are present that undermine the statute’s functions.  
- The safe practice is to draft the certification to mirror the statutory text, document delivery/receipt, and preserve contemporaneous evidence (whether electronic or physical) that the insurer actually received the certifying statement.

Bottom line: “Strict adherence” means strict compliance with the statute and its purposes, not judicial creation of extra formalities. The cases cited (Delatte, Eaglin, Britten, Benitez) show that courts will invalidate cancellations that fail to accomplish the statute’s objectives, but they will not invent signature or naming requirements that the statutory text does not contain.
