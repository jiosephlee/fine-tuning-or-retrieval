### How do plaintiffs’ misrepresentation and good-faith claims depend on the cancellation’s validity under Louisiana law?
Question:
The plaintiffs alleged misrepresentations and breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The panel affirms dismissal after finding the cancellations complied with § 9:3550. Are these extra-contractual claims in Louisiana effectively derivative of the cancellation’s legal effectiveness (i.e., no bad faith if cancellation is valid), or could they have survived on a theory independent of statutory noncompliance?
Answer:
Short answer
Under the Fifth Circuit’s reading of Louisiana law in this opinion, the extra‑contractual misrepresentation and bad‑faith/good‑faith claims were treated as derivative of (i.e., dependent on) the legal effectiveness of the premium‑finance cancellation. Because the panel concluded GoAuto complied with La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550, the district court’s dismissal of the plaintiffs’ misrepresentation and duty‑of‑good‑faith claims was affirmed. The opinion thus treats the statutory cancellation question as a threshold legal prerequisite: if cancellation was effective under § 9:3550, the plaintiffs’ theory that they were harmed by an ineffective cancellation collapses.

Why (doctrine and reasoning)
- Louisiana law makes the mechanics of a premium‑finance cancellation dispositive of whether the policy actually was cancelled: the state appellate cases require “strict adherence” to § 9:3550 and hold that “any defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation.” The panel cites Delatte, Eaglin, and Britten for this proposition (Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689; Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287; Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154). Those cases show that the statutory cancellation procedure fixes the operative date and the cure period — matters central to any claim that an insurer improperly represented coverage or acted in bad faith.
- The plaintiffs’ pleaded misrepresentation/bad‑faith theory in this case depended on the premise that APAC/GoAuto had not validly cancelled the policies under § 9:3550. The district court granted summary judgment on the statutory‑compliance question, and the Fifth Circuit affirmed: it concluded the emailed certification/notice and the automated receipt/cancellation satisfied the statute’s certification and “receipt” requirements. The panel thus disposed of the case at the statutory‑compliance stage and affirmed dismissal of the extra‑contractual claims because the predicate statutory violation was not shown.

What the opinion does and does not allow as independent theories
- The panel expressly focused on whether the cancellation procedures complied with § 9:3550 and treated that as the controlling legal issue. The opinion declines to add extra statutory requirements (for example, it rejects reading a signature requirement into the word “certifying”) and holds that computer receipt of the e‑mail/webservice call sufficed for “receipt.” That analysis directly undercuts the plaintiffs’ claim that the cancellations were ineffective on the grounds they pressed.
- The opinion does not establish a broad rule that no extra‑contractual claim can ever proceed after a valid statutory cancellation; it simply shows that where the plaintiffs’ extra‑contractual claims rest on an asserted statutory defect in cancellation, proof of statutory compliance defeats those claims. The court did not recognize any distinct, independent bad‑faith or fraudulent‑misrepresentation theory that survives despite statutory compliance here. Put differently, the panel resolved the case on the dispositive statutory question and therefore did not carve out or endorse an alternative independent theory of misrepresentation or bad faith in the presence of a valid cancellation.

Practical takeaways for future pleading or appeal
- If an extra‑contractual claim against an insurer or premium‑finance company in Louisiana rests on an allegation that the policy was not effectively cancelled under § 9:3550, a defendant that shows compliance with § 9:3550 can defeat that claim at the threshold. The opinion makes clear that Louisiana’s statutory cancellation mechanics are central to that determination.
- To survive summary judgment despite a defendant’s showing of statutory compliance, a plaintiff would need to plead and develop an independent theory of misrepresentation or bad faith that does not rest solely on the cancellation being ineffective under § 9:3550 — i.e., specific, actionable false statements or other tortious conduct separate from the statutory cancellation mechanics. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion does not endorse such a theory on these facts and dismisses the claims once it finds § 9:3550 satisfied.

Citation anchors from the opinion
- The court’s starting point: Louisiana requires “strict adherence” to § 9:3550 and “any defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation.” (Delatte; Eaglin citations in opinion.)
- The dispositive holding: the panel concluded GoAuto’s procedures satisfied the statute’s “certifying” and “receipt” requirements (no signature required; computer/webservice receipt sufficient), and therefore affirmed dismissal of the plaintiffs’ claims.
