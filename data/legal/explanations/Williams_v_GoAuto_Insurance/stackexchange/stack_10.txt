### Was the evidentiary record adequate to grant summary judgment on the timing and content of 'receipt'?
Question:
The court relies on a deposition describing APAC’s system calling a GoAuto web service, after which cancellation is executed automatically, with human stamping later. For Rule 56 purposes, was there admissible, undisputed evidence (e.g., authenticated logs, business records) establishing the exact timing/content of the emails and the system’s receipt? How did the court handle possible hearsay or authentication issues with computerized processes at summary judgment?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — the Fifth Circuit treated the defendants’ undisputed, admissible deposition testimony about APAC’s automated process (the APAC system calling a GoAuto web service, the e‑mail/request being received by GoAuto’s system, and the system’s automatic cancellation followed later by a stamped printout) as sufficient for summary judgment on the timing and content of “receipt.” The court did not require separate authenticated logs or a business‑records package, and it did not separately analyze hearsay/authentication objections to the computerized evidence — it relied on the parties’ summary‑judgment record (principally the GoAuto deponent’s explanation) as admissible and uncontested proof.

Relevant legal standard
As the court summarized by applying Rule 56 principles, a district court may grant summary judgment only when there is “no genuine dispute as to any material fact” and the moving party supports its motion with admissible evidence (the usual Rule 56 framework). Under that framework, a party may rely on deposition testimony and other admissible evidence to establish undisputed facts about processes and timing that permit the court to conclude no statutory violation occurred.

How the opinion applied that standard to the computerized receipt question
- The court explicitly recited the dispositive factual sequence the parties put before the district court: APAC’s system sends an e‑mail to GoAuto with the notice/certification; GoAuto’s computer system “receives” that e‑mail/call and automatically cancels the policy in the early morning hours; only later does an APAC employee stamp/sign the printed cancellation documents (Op. pp. — see paras describing APAC sending e‑mail, GoAuto system canceling, and later stamping). The court quoted a GoAuto deponent describing the interaction: “the APAC process . . . makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation” and the web service “is always listening . . . and when it hears its name . . . it performs the activity.” (Opinion ¶ discussing GoAuto deponent.)
- Relying on that evidence, the court concluded GoAuto “takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation” when its computer system receives the e‑mail/call, and that this method of receipt satisfies the statute’s requirement that the insurer have “receipt” of the cancellation request. The court thus resolved the statutory timing issue against the plaintiffs on the summary‑judgment record.
- The court also addressed the plaintiffs’ signature argument and held Louisiana law does not require a human signature pre‑cancellation; that issue was resolved by statutory interpretation rather than by excluding evidence.

What the court did not do (and what that means for Rule 56 challenges)
- The opinion does not contain a separate, detailed hearsay or authentication analysis of computerized logs or the e‑mail itself. Instead, it treated the GoAuto deposition and the record description of the automated process as admissible and sufficient to establish the dispositive facts. The court did not demand production of authenticated system logs, business‑records affidavits, or a foundation for the e‑mail/web service call in terms of explicit hearsay‑rule exposition.
- Because the defendants’ evidence as presented in the summary‑judgment record was undisputed on the key points (timing of receipt and automated cancellation), the court resolved the legal question on that record. The court also noted the district court had an alternative theory (private carrier) but did not rely on it because the e‑mail/web service approach already satisfied the statute.

Practical takeaway for future summary‑judgment practice
- At summary judgment, a party can and did rely on clear deposition testimony describing a computerized process to establish when a communication was “received” and what it contained. But if an opponent wants to contest timing/content of electronic communications, it should raise admissibility/authentication or create a genuine factual dispute in the summary‑judgment record (for example, by offering contrary testimony, disputing the foundation for system logs, or moving to exclude the proffered evidence). The Fifth Circuit’s opinion shows that, absent such an evidentiary challenge in the record, courts may accept deponent testimony about automated systems as sufficient to satisfy Rule 56’s admissibility requirement.

Bottom line
The Fifth Circuit found the deposition‑based description of APAC’s automated e‑mail/web‑service interaction and the undisputed sequence of automated cancellation followed by later stamping adequate to grant summary judgment on receipt and timing. The opinion does not go through a separate hearsay/authentication ruling about electronic logs or e‑mails; it treated the summary‑judgment record (principally the GoAuto deponent’s testimony) as admissible and dispositive.
