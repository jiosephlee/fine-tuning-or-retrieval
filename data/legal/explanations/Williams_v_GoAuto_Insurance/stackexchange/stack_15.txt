### Was it proper to resolve merits on summary judgment before any class certification ruling?
Question:
This was filed as a putative class action. The district court decided cross-motions on the legality of the cancellation procedure and then dismissed all claims with prejudice. Is there any Rule 23 'one-way intervention' or sequencing concern here, or is it standard practice to decide dispositive merits issues before class certification when they are outcome-determinative?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — in this case it was proper. The district court resolved cross-motions on the legality of the cancellation procedure and entered summary judgment for the defendants, dismissing all claims with prejudice; the Fifth Circuit affirmed that ruling and did not raise any Rule 23 sequencing or “one‑way intervention” problem. See Opinion, Procedural Background (district court granted GoAuto’s summary‑judgment motion and dismissed all claims) and Affirmance (court analyzes only statutory compliance and affirms).

Why: The opinion treats the parties’ cross‑motions on the statutory question as dispositive and resolves the case on the merits before any class‑certification ruling. The court’s analysis is entirely focused on whether GoAuto’s procedures complied with the Louisiana statute (see Discussion I, “Strict Adherence to the Statute”), and it affirms the district court’s dismissal on that ground. The panel did not indicate any procedural impropriety in resolving the merits first, nor did it suggest that Rule 23 imposes a categorical sequencing rule that would have required certification before deciding dispositive merits issues.

What doctrinal point that supports this outcome (as reflected in the opinion): federal courts routinely have discretion to resolve dispositive legal issues that will dispose of the entire case before addressing class certification if resolving those issues is outcome‑determinative. The opinion implicitly follows that practice: after recounting that the parties filed summary‑judgment motions “related solely to whether GoAuto’s procedure for cancelling the financed insurance policies satisfied Louisiana law,” the district court granted summary judgment and the Fifth Circuit affirmed that the statutory requirements were met (see Opinion, Background and Discussion I). The court also declined to certify a state‑law question (Discussion II), demonstrating its control over how to sequence issues without treating Rule 23 as a bar to deciding the merits first.

Caveat (what the opinion does not hold): the panel does not announce any general rule about when a district court must decide certification first. It does not discuss whether a defendant could use summary judgment strategically to prevent certification in a way that would be improper under Rule 23 or equitable principles. The opinion simply shows that where the merits question squarely disposes of the litigation and the court properly addresses the merits, resolving summary judgment first is acceptable.

Takeaway for future cases: If a merits ruling will dispose of the entire case, a district court may resolve dispositive merits issues before class certification; absent some specific unfairness to putative class members or a statutory/procedural bar, Sequencing alone is not reversible error. In this case the Fifth Circuit accepted the district court’s approach and affirmed on the merits.
