### How does 'strict adherence' to § 9:3550 reconcile with the court declining to require a signature?
Question:
Louisiana courts insist on strict compliance for finance-company cancellations, yet the panel rejects reading a signature requirement into 'certifying.' How does the court square strict-compliance doctrine (fixing date/time and cure period) with a more functional approach to certification content and form? Is the line drawn at textual requirements only, with no room to add formalities by implication?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel treats “strict adherence” as requiring that the statute’s substantive, textually specified elements be met (power of attorney, default, sending the ten‑day notice with date, notice to interested parties, and insurer receipt), and that the statutory objectives (fixing date/time of cancellation and preserving the cure period) be accomplished. But the court refuses to read additional formalities—like a handwritten or named signature—into the word “certifying” where the statute does not require one. In other words, Louisiana’s strict‑compliance rule constrains courts from excusing defects or tolerating omissions of the statute’s required items, but it does not empower courts to add extra signature formalities by implication.

Explanation tied to the opinion

1) What “strict adherence” protects
The opinion explains the two policy functions Louisiana courts attach to strict adherence: “(1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten, 503 So. 2d at 1154 (quoted at pp. 3–4). The Fifth Circuit evaluates GoAuto’s procedures against those goals: the e‑mailed request and the automated web‑service cancellation provided an electronic record/time stamp (fixing time/date) and the ten‑day notice furnished the cure period. Because those statutory objectives were achieved, the court found the statutory scheme’s purpose satisfied.

2) What “certifying” requires (and what it does not)
The statute requires “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” four specified items. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv) (quoted at pp. 2–3). The plaintiffs argued “certifying” necessarily means a signature before cancellation. The panel rejects that reading for two reasons in the opinion:

- The statute itself does not say “signed” or identify any signature requirement (p. 6). The court observed that when the legislature wants a signature it could say so, but the text here does not.
- Existing Louisiana decisions are equivocal and do not treat a signature as an indispensable component. The opinion cites Benitez (which noted lack of a signature but did not hold it fatal) and concludes “we see no reason that a company cannot certify information without a named employee being identified” (pp. 5–6).

The panel states the principle succinctly: “Strict adherence to the procedures for cancellation is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures. We conclude that Louisiana law does not require a signature on the notice sent by the premium finance company to the insurer.” (p. 6.)

3) How formality is assessed practically
The court treats the email/web‑service/timestamp process as legally sufficient “receipt” by the insurer. After surveying dictionary definitions and the statute’s text, it held that “GoAuto may cancel a policy once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation” and that the automated receipt by the GoAuto web service satisfied that requirement (pp. 6–7). Thus a functional, contemporary method of transmitting and recording the certification can meet the statute so long as the statutory elements and purposes (time stamp, cure period, notice to interested parties, etc.) are satisfied.

Bottom line — where the line is drawn
The court draws the line at the statute’s text and objectives. Courts will enforce the statute’s listed, substantive requirements strictly (any defect in those required items can render a cancellation ineffective), but the courts will not graft on additional formalities (like a handwritten signature or identification of the particular employee) that the statute does not demand. Put another way: strict compliance controls the content and the policy goals the statute protects; the content can be supplied in a functionally equivalent modern form unless the statute itself prescribes a particular formality.

This explains why the panel both invoked Louisiana’s strict‑adherence rule and declined to require a signature: strict adherence prevents courts from overlooking the statute’s required elements or its protective functions, but it does not allow courts to create extra requirements not found in the statute.
