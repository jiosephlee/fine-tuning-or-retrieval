### Are there any constitutional due process concerns with policy cancellation by a private finance company’s power of attorney?
Question:
The process provides a 10-day cure and requires specific notices, but there’s no court hearing. I realize due process generally requires state action, which seems absent here. Still, is there any Louisiana or federal constitutional doctrine that would constrain § 9:3550 cancellations (e.g., if state action were implicated), or is the 'strict adherence' statutory scheme intended to be the sole procedural safeguard?
Answer:
Short answer
There is no constitutional due‑process problem in the facts and law the Fifth Circuit analyzed because (1) the cancellation here was carried out under a private power of attorney and governed by a detailed Louisiana statutory scheme, and (2) absent state action there is no constitutional due‑process claim. If state action were somehow present, the Fifth Circuit’s opinion shows the State of Louisiana has already supplied a targeted statutory procedure (notice + a ten‑day cure period) and Louisiana courts require “strict adherence” to that statutory procedure rather than any additional judicially‑created pre‑deprivation hearing or signature formality.

Why state action is the gating issue
The Due Process Clause constrains only government action. The opinion proceeds on the state‑law question of whether the premium finance company’s cancellation complied with La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G). The court treated the transaction as a private cancellation authorized by statute (APAC’s power of attorney), not as state action triggering constitutional restraints. So the constitutional question only arises if some governmental actor caused or ratified the deprivation — the opinion does not find that here.

What the Fifth Circuit says about procedural safeguards (and what would matter if state action were implicated)
- The statute itself sets the procedure: the finance company must send the insurer “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” several enumerated facts and the insured must be given notice that includes a ten‑day cure period. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(iv) and (G)(3)(b)(i) (quoted and discussed throughout the opinion).
- Louisiana courts require “strict adherence” to § 9:3550’s requirements because that strictness (1) fixes “the exact time and date of cancellation” and (2) “grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” The opinion cites Delatte v. Lemotte, Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., and Britten v. Reavis for these principles (Opinion, pp. quoting Delatte, Eaglin, Britten).
- The Fifth Circuit refused to add extra procedural requirements not in the statute. It rejected the plaintiffs’ contention that a pre‑cancellation human signature on APAC’s certification was required, reasoning the statute authorizes the finance company itself to “certify” and does not demand an actual named signature (Opinion: discussion concluding “Louisiana law does not require a signature on the notice”).
- The court also accepted electronic delivery and automatic cancellation on receipt by the insurer’s systems as satisfying the statute’s “receipt” requirement (Opinion: discussion of GoAuto’s web service, e‑mail trigger, and Black’s Law Dictionary definition of “receipt”).

What a constitutional challenge would have to show
If a plaintiff wanted to mount a due‑process argument despite the private nature of the conduct, they would need to show:
- State action (e.g., the insurer is a state actor, the state compels or authorizes the private actor to effect governmental deprivations without adequate safeguards, or the state has so entwined itself with the private actor that constitutional restraints apply). The Fifth Circuit did not find such entanglement here — the dispute was resolved by applying the statutory text and Louisiana common law.
- That the statutory procedure (notice + ten days to cure) itself is constitutionally inadequate to protect property interests. The opinion strongly suggests Louisiana’s statutory notice + cure framework is the legislature’s chosen procedural safeguard; the court declined to require additional process (signature, pre‑deprivation hearing). Under established constitutional doctrine, pre‑deprivation hearings are not always required where the state provides an adequate post‑deprivation remedy or where the state’s statutory procedures supply fair process; the Fifth Circuit’s treatment indicates that § 9:3550’s mandated notice/cure is the relevant procedural baseline.

Bottom line for the student
- In the posture of this case, there is no federal constitutional due‑process obstacle to a finance company cancelling a financed policy under § 9:3550 so long as the statutory notice-and‑cure requirements are strictly followed. That is the exact practical result reached by the Fifth Circuit: the court enforced the statutory scheme and declined to read additional constitutional or signature/hearing requirements into it.
- If you want to pursue a constitutional angle in another case, you must first plead and prove state action or sufficient entanglement; absent that, the courts will treat § 9:3550’s notice + ten‑day cure as the procedural safeguard against unfair deprivation.
