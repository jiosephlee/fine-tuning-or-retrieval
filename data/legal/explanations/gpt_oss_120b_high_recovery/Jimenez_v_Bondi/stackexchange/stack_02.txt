Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### When do threats or psychological harm rise to the level of ‘past persecution’ under INA § 1101(a)(42)(A)?
Question:
The opinion states that "threats alone are insufficient" and that emotional distress from threats does not constitute persecution, citing *Karki v. Holder* and others. However, I have seen other cases where sustained threats were considered sufficient for past persecution. What threshold must be met for conduct to qualify as past persecution? Specifically, how do courts evaluate cumulative threats, physical harm, and lasting psychological injury?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Under INA § 1101(a)(42)(A) “past persecution” is satisfied only when the applicant can show that he suffered **actual harm‑or‑suffering**—not merely a threat or a fleeting fear—that is **offensive**, **serious**, and **on account of a protected ground**.  The Tenth Circuit has articulated three practical checkpoints for evaluating whether a series of threats, assaults, or psychological effects rise to that level:

1. **The conduct must go beyond “mere restrictions or sporadic threats.”**  
   *Karki v. Holder*, 715 F.3d at 805 (10th Cir. 2013) holds that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.”

2. **Cumulative facts may be considered, but the total must produce a *significant* injury—either physical or severe mental—to qualify as persecution.**  
   *Ritonga v. Holder*, 633 F.3d at 975 (10th Cir. 2011) says courts look at the incidents collectively; however *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 2025 WL … (10th Cir.) emphasizes that “the suffered harm must be offensive and more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats” (*Brandy v. Holder*, 590 F.App’x at 747).

3. **Psychological injury counts only when it is **clinically documented** and **serious enough** to be comparable to bodily injury** (e.g., PTSD, major depressive disorder with functional impairment).  The Tenth Circuit has repeatedly declined asylum where the claimed mental distress was limited to anxiety or sleeplessness stemming from isolated threats (*Chhetri v. Rosen*, 844 F.App’x at 27; *Carias‑Mejia v. Garland*, 860 F.App’x at 124).

When those thresholds are met—­for example when an applicant suffers repeated violent attacks culminating in permanent physical disability **or** when a pattern of threats causes a medically‑verified serious mental disorder—the conduct satisfies the “persecution” prong of INA § 1101(a)(42)(A).  

---

**How the courts walk through the analysis**

| Step | What the court asks | Key case law (as applied in *Jimenez*) |
|------|--------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **(a) Identify the incident(s)** | Is there an “incident, or incidents,” that can be characterized as more than harassment? | *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d at 893 — “infliction of suffering … must entail more than just restrictions or threats.” |
| **(b) Assess severity** | Does the conduct cause *actual* suffering (bodily injury, loss of liberty, serious fear of death/serious bodily harm) *or* severe mental injury? | *Karki* – “threats alone … insufficient.” <br> *Brandy* – emotional distress not enough unless it rises above ordinary anxiety.<br> *Chhetri* & *Carias‑Mejia* – repeated threats without physical injury were rejected. |
| **(c) Look at the totality** | If multiple episodes exist, do they together create a pattern that amounts to serious harm? | *Ritonga* – “We consider them collectively.” <br> *Jimenez* – Even taken together, the plaintiff’s threats + brief gunpoint encounter did not amount to persecution because there was no lasting physical injury and only “unfortunate circumstances.” |
| **(d) Evaluate psychological impact** | Is there objective evidence (medical records, expert testimony) showing a serious psychiatric condition caused by the persecutors? | *Jimenez* notes judges have “rarely … granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone.” The court points to cases where even a ten‑hour detention plus threats failed because no medical documentation of severe trauma was presented (*Brandy*). |
| **(e) Nexus** *(not asked here but required overall)* | Is the harming motive tied centrally to a protected ground? | In *Jimenez* the court found political opinion was merely tangential—illustrating that even if severe harm existed, without a nexus the claim fails.|

---

### Practical take‑aways for students

1. **Physical injury is not required**, but whatever harm occurred must be “offensive” enough to rise above ordinary intimidation.
2. **Psychological injury can substitute**, but you need credible, documented evidence—self‑reported sleeplessness or panic attacks without professional diagnosis rarely suffice.
3. **A single isolated threat never qualifies**; you need either:
   - A pattern of violent acts that creates a credible risk of death/serious bodily harm, **or**
   - A proven serious mental disorder directly caused by that pattern.
4. **Cumulative analysis is permissible**, yet it does not lower the severity requirement—adding together harmless threats still yields no persecution claim.
5. **Cite controlling precedent** when arguing either side:
   - For *insufficiency*: *Karki* (threats alone); *Brandy* (emotional distress alone); *Chhetri* & *Carias‑Mejia* (repeated low‑grade intimidation).
   - For possible sufficiency: *Ritonga* (the court may consider aggregate impact); cases outside this opinion where courts have found PTSD from ongoing gang intimidation qualifies—but note those decisions contain medical evidence absent here.

---

### How courts have treated specific fact patterns

| Fact pattern | Result in Tenth Circuit (per *Jimenez*) | Reason |
|-------------|-------------------------------------------|--------|
| One phone call + occasional vague death threat → no physical assault | No past persecution | Pure threat = insufficient (*Karki*) |
| Repeated gunpoint encounters + assault on family members + forced relocation → no lasting physical injury but severe fear | Still denied (“unfortunate circumstances” = not enough) | No demonstrated serious mental injury; harms not deemed “offensive” enough (*Brandy*) |
| Ongoing campaign of beatings causing permanent disability OR medically diagnosed PTSD from sustained kidnapping/kidnapping attempts | Likely qualifies as past persecution | Meets “actual suffering” standard; subsumes both physical and severe psychological harm (*Wiransane*, *Ritonga*) |
| Multiple machete-wielding robberies with documented injuries + PTSD diagnosis | Qualifies | Physical harm plus documented mental illness satisfies both severity requirements |

---

**Bottom line:** Under INA § 1101(a)(42)(A), “past persecution” requires more than fear—it demands concrete, serious suffering (physical or medically verified psychological). Courts will weigh all incidents together only if together they cross that seriousness line; otherwise repetitive threats remain legally “insufficient.”
