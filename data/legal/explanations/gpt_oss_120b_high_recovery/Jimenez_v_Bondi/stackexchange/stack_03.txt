Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### How must an applicant prove that political opinion is a ‘central reason’ for persecutor’s motive?
Question:
The court held that Jimenez’s political affiliation was "tangential" because the primary motive for the attacks was his involvement in a property dispute with Don Rafa. What evidence is required to show that political opinion was at least one central reason for persecution? In mixed‑motive cases, how do courts allocate weight among multiple motives, and what distinguishes a "central" reason from an incidental one?
Answer:
**Short answer**

To satisfy the nexus requirement for asylum (8 U.S.C.§ 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)), an applicant must show—by a pre‑ponderance of the evidence—that his or her *political opinion was at least one **central** reason* for whatever harm was inflicted.  “Central” means more than a passing or peripheral influence; it must be a *principal* or *core* motive rather than an ancillary one.  In mixed‑motive cases the reviewing court looks at the totality of the record and asks whether a protected‑ground motive can plausibly be said to have been decisive in the persecutor’s decision to act.  If the record demonstrates that another non‑protected motive alone would have been sufficient to explain the conduct, then politics is deemed *tangential* or *incidental* and does not satisfy the statutory nexus (see *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 2025 WL _____, ¶ D‑2–4).

---

### 1️⃣ The statutory/precedential standard

The Ninth/​Tenth Circuit construction quoted in *Jimenez* makes clear what “central” entails:

> “To show persecution on account of a political opinion, the applicant must establish the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.”  
> (*Rivera‑Barrientos* v. Holder, 666 F.​3d 641, 646) (quoting § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)).  

> “…the political opinion ‘cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate … or play a minor role.’”  
> (*Dallakoti* v. Holder, 619 F.​3d 1264, 1268) (cited in *Jimenez*, ¶ D‑2).

The same paragraph adds that even when several motives coexist:

> “…the persecutor must be motivated by **at least one protected ground that is a *central* reason** for the inflicted harm.” (*Jimenez*, ¶ D‑3).

Thus “central” is tied directly to **causation**: politics has to be a *key factor* driving the persecutor’s decision‐making.

---

### 2️⃣ What proof satisfies “central”?

Because the applicant bears *the burden of proof by a pre‑ponderance of the evidence* (see § 1158(b)(1) discussion in *Jimenez*), he/she must produce credible factual material showing that politics was more than an explanatory backdrop.  Evidence commonly accepted by this Court includes any of the following **when they are tied directly to the act of persecution**:

| Type of proof | What it shows | Why it matters |
|---------------|---------------|----------------|
| **Explicit statements** by perpetrators linking harm to politics (“You’re a snitch because you belong to the Liberal Party”). | Demonstrates that politics motivated the act *on its face*. | Direct language leaves little doubt about causation; see how *Jimenez* treated men’s accusations that he was a “snitch for the prosecutor’s office” as part of their rationale—but only after they also linked his actions to Don Rafa’s property dispute did politics appear secondary.* |
| **Political symbols or slogans** on threatening materials (e.g., pamphlets reading “death to snitches from Liberal Party”). | Shows politicians were singled out as part of a campaign against dissenters.| Such symbolism can elevate politics from background noise if coupled with other factual support that perpetrators identified themselves politically.* |
| **Pattern of targeting** individuals who share the same partisan activity while unrelated non‑political persons are left untouched.| Indicates hostility grounded in party affiliation rather than random criminality.| Courts consider comparative treatment when assessing whether politics drove selection.* |
| **Correlation between expressed political activity and timing/targeting** (e.g., escalated threats immediately after an activist intervened in an opponent’s business).| Establishes causation through temporal proximity plus explicit reference.| In *Jimenez*, escalation coincided with Jiménez’s property dispute; however because threats also mentioned his role as a prosecutor’s aide rather than only his Liberal Party work, politics was judged secondary.* |
| **Testimony from witnesses** who heard perpetrators state politicized motives.| Provides corroboration when documentary evidence is thin.| The record showed no independent witnesses who linked Don Rafa’s retaliation solely to Jiménez’s partisan work.* |

In practice an applicant should assemble **more than isolated references**; isolated mentions may still count as *incidental* unless they are supplemented by material indicating that without those mentions there would have been no basis for attack.

---

### 3️⃣ How Courts allocate weight among multiple motives

When an alleged persecutor has both *political* and *non‑political* reasons—as many criminal enterprises do—the appellate court conducts a **totality‑of‑circumstances inquiry**:

1️⃣ **Identify all plausible motives** presented in the record (political affiliation, business dispute, personal vendetta).  

2️⃣ **Ask whether each non‑political motive alone suffices** to explain why the persecutor acted exactly as he did. If it does—e.g., protecting illicit revenue—then politics cannot be considered *central*.  

3️⃣ **Gauge which motive appears dominant** based on direct statements and contextual facts (such as who gave orders, which grievances were repeatedly voiced).  

This approach is reflected verbatim in *Jimenez*:  

> “…the BIA found…that …the predominant motive behind the threats was due …to Jiménez’s professional and personal involvement with Don Rafa rather than …political opinion.” (*Jimenez*, ¶ D‑4).  

Because Don Rafa’s retaliation could have been explained entirely by his desire to protect his property and revenue stream—both demonstrated independently—the Court concluded politics was merely “tangential.”

---

### 4️⃣ Central vs. Incidental – practical distinction

| Feature | **Central/political** | **Incidental/political** |
|---------|----------------------|--------------------------|
| **Persecutor’s language** | Politically charged insults or demands are primary content (“You’re targeted because you support X”). | Politics appears only as an afterthought (“You’re also out there supporting X”). |
| **Causation test** | Removing all references to politics leaves no rational explanation for why harm occurred.| Removing references still leaves an adequate conventional criminal explanation (e.g., debt collection). |
| **Evidence weight** | Multiple independent sources corroborate politicized intent.| One isolated instance amidst overwhelming non‑political justification.|

In short: while any mention of politics satisfies *some* degree of relevance under § 1158(b)(1), it satisfies *the nexus* only when doing so pushes politics up into the driver category rather than letting it sit on a sideline.

---

### 5️⃣ Takeaways for applicants

1️⃣ **Collect direct statements** linking hostile acts to your partisan activity—ideally from perpetrators themselves or credible third parties.  
2️⃣ **Show that non‑political explanations are insufficient**, i.e., demonstrate that ordinary criminal motives would not explain why you specifically were singled out while others similar in business dealings were left alone.  
3️⃣ **Present comparative evidence** indicating you were targeted precisely because you belonged to—or publicly Supported—a particular party or ideology.  
4️⃣ **Avoid reliance solely on vague threats**, especially when those threats could reasonably be rooted in other disputes (as happened here where property conflicts formed an alternative explanatory framework).

When these elements are present on balance—so that removing all references to politics would cripple any rational narrative for persecution—the applicant meets the “central reason” threshold described in *Jimnez v. Bondi*. Absent such proof, courts will treat political affiliation as tangential and deny asylum based on lack of nexus.
