Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Why did the Tenth Circuit not address petitioners’ due‑process claim against the immigration judge or their CAT claim?
Question:
The opinion mentions that petitioners raised due‑process arguments regarding counsel rights and also sought protection under the Convention Against Torture, yet these issues were not decided because they were not appealed. Does appellate review under § 1252 allow a court to ignore unraised issues entirely, or could those claims still be raised on remand? What procedural rules govern which arguments are preserved for appellate consideration?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The Fifth Circuit declined to decide the due‑process claim against the immigration judge and the Convention‑Against‑Torture (CAT) claim because Petitioners **did not raise those issues in their notice of appeal** and § 1252(a)(1) limits a court’s jurisdiction to “final orders… *and* **the matters specifically presented**.”  Because the appellate docket was confined to the asylum/withholding questions, the Court had no authority to consider any other relief, and Petitioners cannot now revive those arguments on this appeal (they would have needed a separate filing or a remand that expressly reopened the omitted claims).

Below is a step‑by‑step breakdown of the procedural framework that governs this result.

---

### 1. What the opinion says

- **Petitioners’ own limitation** – The court notes early on that *“Petitioners do not appeal the denial of protection under the CAT or their due process claim.”*¶ A.
- **Statutory grant of jurisdiction** – The Court’s authority comes from **8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1)** (“jurisdiction… over final orders…”), and its review is confined by **§ 1252(b)(4)(A)** (“limited to ‘the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.’”)¶ B.
- **Resulting scope** – Because Petitioners’ notice of appeal asked only whether “the BIA erred in denying their applications for asylum and withholding of removal,” any issue outside that frame *was never before the Court* and therefore “not decided.”¶ D–F.

The opinion never treats the due‑process or CAT arguments as “waived” per se; it simply states there is no jurisdiction over them since they were **not raised** in the petition for review.

---

### 2. Why § 1252 forces the Court to ignore unraised claims

| Provision | Effect |
|-----------|--------|
| **§ 1252(a)(1)** | Grants appellate courts power *only* over final orders of removal and *only* over matters set forth in an *appeal*. |
| **§ 1252(b)(4)(A)** | Bars consideration of any evidence or argument that does **not appear in the administrative record** supporting the order being reviewed. |
| **Fed. R.App.P. 44(a)** (reviewable orders) | Mirrors § 1252’s limitation: an appellate court may consider only “issues presented” in the appeal brief and record.|

Because immigration cases are fundamentally **directed at reviewing agency action**, courts treat an untaken issue as **waived** (see *Uanreroro v. Gonzales*, 443 F.3d at 1204 (10th Cir.…)). The same principle appears in ordinary appellate practice under Fed.R.Civ.P. 44(d): an appellant must raise all arguments it wishes reviewed, or risk forfeiture unless extraordinary circumstances exist (e.g., plain error affecting substantial rights).  

The Fifth Circuit’s decision follows this well‑established rule—no “plain‑error” exception applies because there was *no error* by either the IJ or BIA regarding due‑process or CAT relief; both rulings were affirmed by substantive evidence.

---

### 3. Could Petitioners now raise those claims?

**Only through a separate procedural vehicle**, not by simply pointing them out on this appeal:

| Option | Availability after a denial |
|--------|-----------------------------|
| **File a new petition for review covering CAT/Due‑process** | Not allowed because § 1252 bars a second‑stage appeal after a final denial—issues must be included in *the* original petition (§ 1229a(c) & case law). |
| **Civil action under § 1253(b)** (action for declaratory judgment / injunction) | Possible *if* filed within **30 days** after entry of judgment, alleging unlawful removal without proper procedural safeguards (*see* *Matter of S-P‑C‑325*, 26 I&N Dec. 740). The deadline has long passed here.* |
| **Motion to vacate/reopen before final judgment** | Must be filed within **90 days** after entry of judgment (*see* INA §§ 240(c),​(d)); again unavailable after this Court’s dismissal.* |
| **Remand by higher court** | If a higher authority had sent this case back with instructions to address due‑process/CAT issues, then those arguments could reappear on remand—but no such remand occurred; this opinion ends with a denial, not a remand.*

In short, once an appellate court has dismissed a petition for review without addressing certain claims, those claims are effectively foreclosed unless pursued via one of the narrow statutory remedies listed above—and all such remedies are time‑barred here.

---

### 4. Governing procedural doctrines

1. **Issue Waiver/Forfeiture:** An appellant must present every ground it wishes adjudicated at first opportunity (*see* *Matter of K-R-C*, 20 I&N Dec.​ 862 (BIA 1999)). Failure constitutes waiver unless plain error exists—a threshold rarely met in immigration reviews.
2. **Statutory Limitation:** §§ 1252(a)–(b) give courts “narrowed” jurisdiction focused solely on agency decisions contained in the record.
3. **Plain Error Exception:** Courts may correct clear errors even if unraised (*see* Fed.R.Civ.P. 52(c)), but only when “the error is plain…affects substantial rights.” The Fifth Circuit found no such error concerning Petitioners’ due‑process or CAT allegations.
4. **Separate Civil Action (§ 1253):** Provides an independent avenue for constitutional/due‑process challenges but carries its own filing deadlines and procedural requirements distinct from appellate review under § 1252.

---

### 5. Bottom line

- The Tenth Circuit did **not** address Petitioners’ due‑process claim against the immigration judge nor their CAT claim because those issues were **outside the scope** of what Petitioners asked reviewed (§ 1252 limits review to matters raised).
- Under §§ 1252(a)–(b) and standard appellate practice, a court may ignore unraised arguments entirely; it cannot entertain them “silently.”
- Those claims could only be revived by filing a *new* statutory action (e.g., a civil action under § 1253) within strict time limits, or by obtaining a remand that specifically instructs lower authorities to reconsider them—neither option remains available after this denial.

Thus, procedural rules—not substantive disagreement—dictated why those portions of Petitioners’ case went unanswered in Jimenez v. Bondi.
