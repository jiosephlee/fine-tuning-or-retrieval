# Political Opinion or Personal Grudge? The Central‑Reason Test in **Jimenez v. Bondi**

*By [Your Name],* *July 10 2026*

---

## Why this case matters  

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* sharpens a line that has been inching forward for years: when an asylum seeker points to a non‑state actor as the source of his persecution, he must show that “political opinion” was more than a peripheral justification—it must be a **central reason** for the harm.  The panel’s rejection of Jimenez’s claim underscores how demanding that inquiry has become, especially when the alleged persecutor is a narco‑guerrilla leader whose interests overlap both criminal enterprise and partisan politics.  For practitioners representing Latin American claimants who routinely confront drug cartels, paramilitary groups, or other armed factions intertwined with politics, *Jimenez* offers a clear—if unsettling—template for how courts may dissect “political” motives in future cases.

---

## A quick recap of the facts  

Wilson Leonardo Canon Jiménez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons fled Bogotá in late 2022 after an escalating series of threats tied to Jiménez’s work as a provisional lawyer and his volunteer activity for Colombia’s Liberal Party.  While canvassing neighborhoods for a 2022 presidential election, Jiménez received phone threats from unknown callers—a pattern that intensified after he intervened on behalf of a client whose property was being used by “Don Rafa,” a narco‑guerrilla leader allied with President Gustavo Petro’s faction.  

After confronting Don Rafa’s tenant (the client’s ex‑husband) and learning that the property served as a hub for drug trafficking and kidnappings, Jiménez received a death‑threat pamphlet (“death to snitches from Liberal Party”).  Two weeks later—while working night shifts at his family’s furniture factory—he was stopped at gunpoint by men who accused him of being a “snitch” for prosecutors and warned him off “the neighborhood.”  The same men later threatened an employee who asked about Jiménez’s whereabouts.  The climax came when an armed man tried to silence Jiménez’s younger son at school; an ensuing escape prompted the family to take temporary refuge on Jiménez’s mother’s rural farm before fleeing to the United States.

In immigration court they pleaded asylum, withholding of removal, and CAT protection.  The immigration judge found them credible but concluded they had not shown *past persecution*, *a nexus* between any harm and a protected ground, nor *internal relocation* feasibility.  The Board of Immigration Appeals affirmed; the Tenth Circuit denied review on those grounds.

---

## The legal scaffolding: asylum’s nexus requirement  

To succeed on an asylum claim under §1158(b)(1)(A), an applicant must prove **(1)** past persecution (or its threat), **(2)** that the persecution was on account of one of five protected grounds—including **political opinion**—and **(3)** that it was committed by either the government or by forces it is unwilling or unable to control.  The second prong—the *nexus*—has been clarified repeatedly by this circuit through what scholars now call the **central‑reason test**.

In *Dallakoti v. Holder*, 619 F.3d at 1268 (10th Cir. 2010), the court held that “the political opinion must be at least one central reason for the persecution.”  A motive that is “incidental, tangential… or subordinate” does not satisfy §1158(b)(1)(B).  Likewise, *Yuk v. Ashcroft*, 355 F.3d at 1236 (10th Cir. 2004) emphasized that appellate courts do not reweigh evidence but merely determine whether substantial evidence supports the finding that the protected ground was *the* driving factor behind the harm.

These precedents give judges a three‑part yardstick:

1. **Identify** all alleged motives disclosed in the record.
2. **Weight** each motive against one another.
3. **Determine** whether any protected-ground motive rises above mere background noise to become “central.”

If it does not, the claim fails even if other harms are severe enough to qualify as “persecution” under *Rivera‑Barrientos*’s broader definition.

---

## How *Jimenez* applied (and extended) that test  

Judge Tymkovich’s opinion meticulously applied each step of the central‑reason analysis:

1. **Motivation inventory:** The record shows two plausible motives behind the threats against Jiménez—the family’s affiliation with Colombia’s Liberal Party and their personal entanglement with Don Rafa over a disputed property.
2. **Evidence weighting:** While political campaigning preceded their first threats (phone calls in May–June 2022), all subsequent escalations—including gunpoint confrontations and school intimidation—were linked explicitly by perpetrators to “snitching” on Don Rafa and his criminal operations.
3. **Centrality determination:** The majority concluded that “the predominant motive… was due to Jiménez’s professional and personal involvement with Don Rafa,” not his Liberal Party allegiance.  As such, politics was “merely tangential” (citing *Dallakoti*) rather than central.

The panel thus affirmed both the immigration judge’s and BIA’s findings without rebasing its analysis on independent speculation—a classic application of “substantial evidence” review under §1252(b)(4)(A). In essence, even though Jiménez was an active Liberal Party member—a classic political actor—the court ruled that his physical safety concerns stemmed from private retaliation rather than government‑sanctioned persecution based on his views.

---

## Non‑state actors muddy the waters  

What makes *Jimenez* noteworthy beyond its strict nexus parsing is its handling of **non‑state actors** who enjoy de facto ties to state power. Don Rafa is described as “a leader of a group of narco‑trafficking guerrillas politically aligned with President Petro.”  In prior Tenth Circuit decisions—most notably *Aviles‑Gonzalez v. Garland*, No. 23‑9547 (10th Cir.—June 20 2024)—the court has held that an applicant need not show direct government participation if it can prove government unwillingness or inability to control those actors after notice is given.

Here, however, Jiménez never reported these threats formally beyond an informal conversation with two police officers who advised him simply “to leave.”  As Judge Tymkovich observed, isolated officer statements do not satisfy proof of systemic governmental failure; thus there was no independent showing that even an informally aligned non‑state actor could be deemed effectively governmental for purposes of §1158(b)(1)(C).  

The decision underscores an emerging doctrinal tension: **When non‑state coercion resides within politically connected networks**, does it suffice as “political persecution”? Some dissenting voices have argued—citing Ninth Circuit cases such as *Mattera v. Gonzales*, 560 F.Supp 2d 1087 (2008)—that an applicant need only demonstrate that “the persecutor acted because they perceived their victim as aligned with opposing politics,” even if no formal party apparatus is involved. *Jimenez* pushes back against such lenient readings within its jurisdiction, demanding documentary clarity that politics were indeed at heart—not just collateral.

---

## A missed avenue: particular social group  

One strategic shortfall evident from Jiménez’s briefing record is its complete omission of any **particular social group (PSG)** argument—a ground often employed when political motives are contestable but membership in an identifiable group can supply an alternative nexus foothold.

Had counsel framed Jiménez and his family as members of “Liberal Party activists targeted by drug‑linked paramilitaries,” they could have marshaled persecution evidence under both political opinion *and* PSG doctrines—a dual prong sometimes enough to tip preservation analyses in favor of applicants (see *Ada v. Gonzales*, 552 F.Supp 2d 1085 (2006)). Moreover, recent Tenth Circuit jurisprudence acknowledges overlap between PSGs defined by political affiliation and political opinion per se (*Lopez-Perez v.\ Gonzales*, No. 23-1027). By sidelining this avenue entirely—perhaps due to lack of time or resources—the petitioners left themselves vulnerable should any court require “central” proof of pure political motivation while being more forgiving when group identity supplies part of the causal chain.

---

## Doctrinal ripples & practical consequences  

*Jimenez* reinforces several trends:

1️⃣ **Hardening of the central‑reason bar** – Courts will likely demand concrete evidence linking each act directly back to protected‐ground motives rather than relying on schemata like “politically aligned criminal groups.” Applicants facing threat from cartel‐linked militias will need affidavits or country‑condition reports explicitly tying those groups’ animus to political opposition rather than mere business disputes.

2️⃣ **Higher evidentiary burden on government unwillingness** – Even when perpetrators are alleged allies of state officials, mere anecdotal police indifference will not satisfy inability/unwillingness criteria absent documented attempts at official protection or systemic patterns indicating state complicity (*Aviles–Gonzalez*’s emphasis remained salient).

3️⃣ **Strategic diversification becomes essential** – Counsel should habitually identify every viable protected ground early—political opinion *and* PSG—as well as any potential Convention Against Torture claim rooted in serious non‐political harm—so that jurisdictions like Colorado can later salvage claims based on whichever prong survives asymmetrical scrutiny.

4️⃣ **Potential circuit divergence** – While other circuits have sometimes emphasized “perceptual politics” where perpetrators merely thought they were targeting someone because of party affiliation (*Mattera*, etc.), Tenth Circuit may now stand apart by insisting on demonstrable centrality through factual corroboration—a development likely to appear in forthcoming district court opinions citing *Jimenez*. If higher courts remain split over this interpretive hinge, we might see another circuit split basis for Supreme Court review—a prospect many practitioners will watch closely given its immigration‐policy stakes.

5️⃣ **BIA guidance pressure** – The Board has long struggled with drafting policy guidance concerning non‐state actors tied loosely to governments (*Mattera Memo* vs.*Mihailovic guidance). After *Jimenez*, we can expect renewed internal memoranda urging adjudicators to scrutinize whether purported political motives survive substantive evidentiary interrogation rather than relying on broad characterizations like “politically motivated cartel”.

---

## Bottom line  

*Jimenez v. Bondi* demonstrates how nuanced—and unforgiving—the Tenth Circuit has become regarding asylum's nexus requirement when non‐state actors buttress their claims with vague political connections. By insisting that political opinion must rise above incidental relevance into "central" causality, Judge Tymkovich sent a clear message: threats dripping with criminal undertones cannot be cloaked in partisan rhetoric unless there is concrete proof that politics—not personal vendetta—is driving them forward.

For lawyers representing clients caught between criminal syndicates and partisan strife across Latin America, this decision signals two immediate priorities:

1️⃣ Gather explicit contemporaneous evidence tying persecutors’ actions directly back to your client’s expressed or perceived politics (e.g., threatening letters citing party affiliation alongside criminal accusations).

2️⃣ Immediately explore alternative protected grounds—particularly particular social group claims—so you aren’t forced into an all–or–nothing battle over one potentially fragile nexus theory.

If Congress ever revisits statutory language concerning non‑governmental persecutors—or if higher courts embrace a broader definition encompassing perceived politics—the tide may shift dramatically toward applicants like Jiménez who find themselves trapped between ruthless cartels and wavering state protectionists alike.

Until then, *Jimenez* stands as both cautionary tale and roadmap: your clients’ grievances must be more than just politically flavored grievances—they must be politically motivated at their core—or risk being dismissed as personal grudges masquerading as asylum claims.
