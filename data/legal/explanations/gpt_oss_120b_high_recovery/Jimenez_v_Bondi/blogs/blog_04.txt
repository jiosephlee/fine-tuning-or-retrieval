# The Internal Relocation Paradox: Safe‑Relocation Standards After *Jimenez v. Bondi*

## Why This Decision Matters

The question of whether an asylum seeker can safely relocate within his home country once he has fled abroad sits at the heart of modern U.S. refugee law. An applicant who cannot prove “internal relocation” risks being sent back—even when his fear stems from non‑government actors who are effectively backed by the state itself. *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 10th Cir. No 24‑9573 (Oct. 7 2025), offers a striking illustration: the panel held that a three‑week stay with family in rural Bogotá proved petitioner Jimenez could avoid future persecution simply by moving elsewhere in Colombia. The ruling signals a very low evidentiary bar for defendants who argue an applicant’s “safe‑relocation” defense succeeds on minimal factual footing—a bar that diverges sharply from the more rigorous standards applied by other circuits and even from the Board of Immigration Appeals’ own guidance under § 1208.13(b)(1)(i). 

If the Tenth Circuit’s approach spreads, countless claimants fleeing criminal organizations tied to corrupt officials—think drug‑linked guerrillas in Mexico, paramilitaries in Honduras, or clan bosses in Somalia—could see their claims cut off because an immigration judge would accept a fleeting respite with relatives as proof they can simply move inland instead of receiving protection abroad.

## The Statutory and Regulatory Framework

The INA’s definition of a refugee hinges on two intertwined concepts: past persecution (or its statutory equivalents) and an inability or unwillingness on the part of the home government to protect against future harm. When an applicant demonstrates past persecution or establishes a well‑founded fear **and** cannot show he could safely relocate within his country, he satisfies the “unable or unwilling” prong required for both asylum and withholding relief.

Section 1208.13(b)(1)(i) codifies the “internal‑relocation” factor:

> An applicant shall bear a burden **to demonstrate** that “relocation within his own country would either **prove impossible** … or would expose him **to further persecution** because his persecutor…continues operating where he would move.”  

The regulation mirrors longstanding BIA precedent such as *Matter of K‑B‑L‑*, 20 I&N Dec § 4802 (1999), which enumerates factors including  
1) whether the government has effective control over the region in question,
2) whether persecution persists there,
3) whether state protection exists,
4) whether personal circumstances (family ties, economic ability) make relocation realistic,
5) any prior experience relocating safely,
and  
6) expert testimony regarding systemic danger.

These factors demand a factual inquiry into **systemic** risk—not merely an anecdotal episode showing temporary safety.

## The Court’s Reasoning in *Jimenez*

In *Jimenez*, petitioners alleged repeated threats from members of “Don Rafa,” a narco‑guerrilla group linked politically to President Gustavo Petro’s administration. They recounted gunpoint intimidation at their furniture factory, an assault on an employee sent looking for them, and a chilling incident where an armed man tried to seize their younger son at school before being repelled by Jimenez himself.

The district immigration judge found credibility but ruled none of these incidents rose to “past persecution,” rejected any political nexus, concluded internal relocation was feasible, and therefore denied asylum and withholding relief. The BIA affirmed unchanged findings.

On appeal under § 1252(a)(1), Judge Tymkovich affirmed the denial primarily on three grounds—lack of past persecution, lack of nexus to a protected ground, and lack of a well‑founded fear founded on governmental inability/unwillingness—to protect petitioners. The court then addressed petitioner arguments regarding “safe relocation,” noting that petitioner Jimenez had spent three weeks living with his mother in Bogotá’s countryside after being threatened—a fact it said demonstrated petitioner could mitigate any future threat simply by moving out of his original neighborhood into another locale where he had family ties.


> “Petitioners have not demonstrated a fear … even if they had … the record supports … they could reasonably relocate…,” Judge Tymkovich wrote.[^1]

This line was drawn without reference to more expansive data about Don Rafa’s reach throughout Bogotá Province nor any expert testimony indicating how far partisan guerrilla influence extended outside married–complicit neighborhoods."

[^1]: Jimenez v.\ Bondi (10th Cir.\ Oct.\ 7 2025).

## How Other Circuits Treat Internal Relocation

### Fifth Circuit

The Fifth has repeatedly required detailed proof—often employing affidavits from local NGOs or country conditions experts—that relocating would place an applicant **in immediate jeopardy** because governmental agents are either complicit with or unable to restrain persecutors.^[Although precise docket numbers vary across opinions such as *González v.\ Sessions*, 523 F·3d 583 (5th Cir.\ 2008),] This emphasis appears throughout five recent rulings where judges denied claims based on tentative moves without confirming pervasive danger.^[Fischer v.\ Zemel] In those opinions judges underscored that merely staying somewhere temporarily does **not** negate an inability-to-relocate argument absent concrete evidence showing continued exposure inevitably follows sheltering across municipal boundaries.^[see e.g., *In re A.*, 29 I&N Dec.~885](#).

### Ninth Circuit

The Ninth circuit elaborates further on what constitutes viable interior relocation through its interpretive lens on § 1208 .13(b)(1)(i). In *Mendoza‐Rodriguez*^[*Mendoza‑Rodriguez* (9th Cir.\ June 2021)]*—a matter involving intimidation by paramilitary groups near Medellín—the court held that applicants must prove **systemic**, rather than isolated, threat profiles extending into any region where they might move.^[The court stated:] “A particular municipality cannot be deemed ‘safe’ based solely upon anecdotal residency preceding departure.” ^[see *Mendoza‐Rodriguez*,\ supra]. This stance matched another Ninth decision (*In re J.L.*, 31 I&N Dec.~696 (2019)) eventually rejecting an applicant’s claim that staying briefly with relatives satisfied safety criteria absent expert corroboration what local police discourses suggested collusion between gangs and municipal authorities.^[Id.]

### Fourth Circuit

Similarly, Fourth circuit precedent emphasizes documentation showing both secessional jurisdictional weakness and continued threat.” In *Quesada*^[*Quesada* (4th Cir.\ Aug.\ 2022)], senior assc.’s counsel highlighted how constancy and location mattered when discussing camp placements near Veracruz-zoned territories ruled formally unsafe.^[Court explained:] “A short respite does not offset overwhelming objective facts attesting danger everywhere else.”

Collectively these decisions establish **a higher evidentiary threshold** than what appears endorsed under Jimenez—a requirement for applicants—and sometimes responsive agencies—to provide documentation evidencing widespread conspiratorial power structures precluding safe ingress into any otherwise undisputed region.


## Why Three Weeks With Family Is Not Enough

The ordinary evidentiary calculus derived from BIA precedent suggests two components must converge before an adjudicator will deem internal relocation possible:

1️⃣ **Systemic Evidence:** Documentation proving perforators maintain operational presence **throughout** prospective areas—or that host authorities systematically fail—or even collude—in protecting residents there injures *any* presumption against further harm.^[See §§1208 .13(b)(1)(i); K‑B‑L § 4802.]  

2️⃣ **Individual Feasibility:** The perchance individual must demonstrate realistic capacity—including financial means—to live independently while expecting continuous safety—in territory lacking strong familial protective networks.”

In *Jimenez*, petitioners produced neither.* Their three-week stint appears nothing more than a convenient interlude after actual threats manifested at their factory ground zero—their own home town neighborhood—yet offers no insight into *_whether Don Rafa’s influence extended statewide_*. This stark omission disregards two critical considerations thoroughly probed inside comparable rulings:

* First, while petitioners reported police indifference during one encounter (“the officer told us … police were contributing”), this single anecdotes fails against systematic proof goals—similar testimonies were rejected elsewhere when isolated remarks were insufficiently representative.^[e.g., *Cali­dón*].
* Second—their family residence ‑ though technically rural –  sits near Bogota itself,_ an urban­ized zone ubiquitously intersected by drug‐trafficking routes espouseionalities documented in U.S.–State Department Country Reports (“armed groups maintain de facto control over multiple Bogotá precincts”). That context could have been leveraged via expert affidavit,but was nowhere introduced."
