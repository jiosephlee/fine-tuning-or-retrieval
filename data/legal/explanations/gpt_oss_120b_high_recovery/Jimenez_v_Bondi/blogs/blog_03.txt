Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# “Unable or Unwilling” Without a Police Report: Reporting Requirements After *Jimenez v. Bondi*

## Why *Jimenez* matters for every asylum practitioner  

When a non‑citizen asks whether his home government can protect him from persecution, the answer often hinges on the “unable or unwilling” prong of the refugee definition. In the Tenth Circuit’s October 7 2025 decision *Jimenez v. Bondi*, Judge Tymkovich reaffirmed a stringent evidentiary gate: without a concrete showing—typically a police report—that the government refused or could not intervene, an asylum claim is unlikely to survive. The ruling leans heavily on *Aviles‑Gonzalez* and underscores how claimants who fear retaliation for reporting abuse are left with an almost impossible burden of proof. For counsel representing vulnerable clients from countries where law‑enforcement collusion is the norm, *Jimenez* is both a warning sign and a roadmap for how to navigate—or sidestep—the reporting requirement.

## The court’s reasoning in a nutshell  

The petitioners, Colombian nationals who fled after a series of threats tied to a property dispute with a narco‑linked local boss (“Don Rafa”), asked the immigration judge and then the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) for asylum and withholding of removal. The Ninth Circuit affirmed both denials on three fronts: lack of past persecution, lack of nexus to a protected ground, and failure to prove that Colombia was unable or unwilling to protect them.

On the “unable or unwilling” query, Judge Tymkovich reiterated that an applicant must show—by a preponderance of the evidence—that the government either failed to act against persecutors or could not be expected to do so (8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(A)). The opinion cites *Singh v. Bondi*, 130 F.4th 848, 861 (10th Cir. 2025) for the standard and leans on *Aviles‑Gonzalez*, No. 23‑9547, 2024 WL 3066987 (10th Cir. June 20 2024) for its discussion of when “failure to report” does not automatically defeat an applicant’s claim.

The record showed that Jimenez’s family spoke informally with two neighborhood police officers who advised them simply “to leave.” There was no filing of an official complaint, no request for protective custody, and no documented follow‑up with municipal authorities. The court deemed these isolated conversations insufficient evidence that Colombia’s government was either unaware of—or unwilling to address—the threats. In its view, “the record does not show … that Petitioners sought help outside their immediate neighborhood or through Jimenez’s law‑enforcement contacts,” rendering their argument about governmental complicity speculative at best.

Because there was “substantial evidence” that the petitioners had not pursued any formal avenue of protection—or could have done so without serious danger—the Tenth Circuit concluded that they failed the second element of the “unable or unwilling” analysis and consequently could not meet either the asylum or withholding standards.

## The reporting requirement under *Aviles‑Gonzalez*  

*Aviles‑Gonzalez* set out two circumstances where an applicant’s failure to file a police report does **not** automatically defeat an asylum claim:

1. **Futility** – when any reasonable person would know that contacting authorities would be pointless because they are systematically incapable of protecting victims; and  
2. **Danger** – when filing would expose the applicant—and often their family—to further harm due perhaps to corrupt officials or armed groups intertwined with state actors.

The *Jimenez* panel did not find either circumstance justified here. Although it acknowledged “the officer told Jimenez he and his family should leave,” it placed the burden back on petitioners to produce more than vague warnings—a formal complaint, corroborating affidavits about prior retaliation against reporters, or country‑condition evidence showing systematic impunity regarding crimes linked to political actors such as Don Rafa.

That expectation aligns with prior Ninth Circuit authority (*Matter of S-P‑*, 21 I&N Dec.* 2009) but diverges from many UNHCR guidelines which allow an applicant’s inability—or perceived inability—to seek state protection as part of establishing “government unwillingness.” Internationally accepted practice acknowledges that refugees may lack any realistic avenue for reporting without incurring additional risk; U.S. jurisprudence has been slower to embrace this nuance.

## Practical guidance for counsel  

The *Jimenez* opinion offers three takeaways for practitioners:

First, **anticipate the reporting question** early in case preparation rather than treating it as an afterthought during oral argument. If clients fear retaliation for filing a complaint—common in contexts where state actors collude with criminal enterprises—the record should contain concrete evidence that such fear is well‑founded. This may include newspaper articles documenting reprisals against whistleblowers, human‑rights reports detailing patterns of police non‑response in specific regions, or sworn statements from community leaders attesting that any official complaint would have been ignored or would have escalated danger.

Second, **document every interaction with officials**, even informal ones. A short conversation where an officer says “stay away” is insufficient; however, if you obtain a written acknowledgment—an officer’s note stating they received your client’s description of threats yet could do nothing because …—that becomes admissible proof of governmental futility or danger under *Aviles‑Gonzalez*. When formal police logs are unavailable because officers refuse to file them, counsel can request FOIA releases from municipal precincts or submit affidavits from NGO workers who observed attempts at seeking protection.

Third, **frame arguments around futility versus danger** rather than simply pointing out that there is no police report at all. In *Aviles‑Gonzalez*’s language those two concepts are distinct pathways past the reporting hurdle:

- For **futility**, rely heavily on country conditions indicating systemic corruption—e.g., U.S. State Department reports describing how widespread collusion between narcotraffickers and local law enforcement renders official channels useless.
  
- For **danger**, marshal specific instances where victims who reported were subsequently harmed—perhaps through testimony from other Colombian dissidents who experienced exactly this pattern after contacting authorities about narco‑linked intimidation.

A well‑crafted brief will intertwine these factual strands with scholarly commentary on refugee law norms and case precedent showing courts willing to infer inability when there is credible corroboration—even absent a classic police document.

## Doctrinal implications and potential downstream effects  

*Jimenez* reinforces an already robust trend across several circuits toward narrowing what counts as proof that a government is “unable or unwilling.” The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Sanchez‑Lopez* (2023) similarly required petitioners to offer more than anecdotal statements about corrupt policing; likewise, the Fourth Circuit’s recent holding in *Elizondo* demanded documentary evidence linking state actors directly to alleged persecutors before excusing lack of formal complaints.

Together these rulings inch U.S./asylum law closer to what scholars have called a “reporting nexus”—the idea that an applicant must produce some official record linking themselves—or an organization acting on their behalf—to any attempt at state protection before they can invoke unavailability as a basis for relief. Critics argue this creates an absurd loop for victims from countries where precisely those official records cannot exist because making them would trigger lethal reprisals.

Internationally recognized standards caution against such circularity. The UNHCR Handbook on Determining Refugee Status states unequivocally that “[i]f obtaining state protection would expose refugees …to further persecution …an asylum claim should not be denied on account of failure to seek such protection.” By insisting on concrete documentation—essentially demanding clients prove something they are structurally barred from obtaining—U.S.’s approach diverges increasingly from global norms and may become fertile ground for future constitutional challenges under due process grounds if it systematically precludes relief based on evidentiary impossibility rather than mere credibility concerns.

Should this line persist, we may see:

1. **Increased reliance on secondary evidence** such as expert country-condition reports which may become more central in establishing futility/danger arguments.
2. **Higher appellate caseloads** as appellants contest lower‐court dismissals rooted primarily in procedural deficits rather than substantive factual disputes.
3. **Potential legislative response** if Congress decides accreditation agencies like USCIS must adopt clearer guidelines allowing abstraction beyond formal police filings—a step already contemplated in previous immigration reform proposals but yet undone.
4. **Strategic tightening by criminal organizations** aware that coercing victims into silence about reporting removes one legal avenue for asylum seekers—a self-reinforcing cycle that benefits repressive regimes.

## Conclusion  

*Jimenez v. Bondi* serves as both warning sign and practical playbook for immigration attorneys handling persecution claims where governments are either complicit with—or powerless against—non‑state actors such as narco‑guerrillas in Colombia or analogous contexts elsewhere worldwide. By confining “unable or unwilling” analyses largely behind a door marked “police report,” the panel has set a high bar that only diligent documentation—and sometimes creative evidentiary tactics—can meet.

Counsel must therefore front-load efforts to document any attempt at state protection—formal complaints where possible, detailed affidavits describing refusals by officials, contemporaneous news coverage evidencing systemic impunity—and be ready at oral argument to argue futility or danger using both domestic precedent (*Aviles‑Gonzalez*, *Singh*) and international refugee norms.

If litigation strategies adapt accordingly, many claimants whose lives depend on establishing governmental inability will survive this procedural snare even without an official complaint on file—a modest but crucial step toward aligning U.S.’s asylum adjudication framework with its own humanitarian commitments under international law.
