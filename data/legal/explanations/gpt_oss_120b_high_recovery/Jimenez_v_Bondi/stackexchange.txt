Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### What is the scope of appellate jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252 for reviewing BIA asylum decisions?
Question:
In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the Tenth Circuit cites 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1) and (b)(9) as giving it jurisdiction to review the Board’s denial of asylum. I’m confused about how a "final order of removal" includes a discretionary grant of asylum, which is normally within the Attorney General’s sole discretion. Does every denial of asylum constitute a final order that can be appealed? And how does the court decide whether it should look at only the administrative record versus also consulting the immigration judge’s explanation?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Under 8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1) and § 1252(b)(9) the Courts of Appeals have **jurisdiction over any “final order of removal.”** In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the Tenth Circuit holds that a Board‑of‑Immigration‑Appeals (BIA) denial of asylum is such a final order because the denial leaves the alien **subject to removal** – even though asylum itself is a discretionary grant normally reserved to the Attorney General. Consequently, once the BIA’s decision becomes final (no pending motions for reconsideration or stays), it may be appealed under § 1252, and the appellate court may review **all aspects** of the underlying asylum claim in a single, consolidated proceeding.

---

### 1. What “final order of removal” means in this context  

The opinion cites two controlling authorities:

> “We generally have jurisdiction to review final orders of removal, which can include a consolidated review of asylum claims.”  
> *Singh v. Bondi*, 130 F.4th 848, 859 (10th Cir. 2025) (citing §§ 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)).  

Section 1252(a)(1) gives courts jurisdiction over *orders removing* an alien, while § 1252(b)(9) defines a *final order* as one “issued by the Board … with respect to removal.” The court treats a **BIA denial of asylum** as falling within this definition because such a denial **removes any statutory barrier to deportation** – the alien now has no lawful status and will be ordered removed under the INA’s general removal provisions (§ 1229a).  

Thus, although granting asylum is discretionary, *refusing* it produces a **determinative consequence:** the alien becomes removable. That consequence satisfies the statutory phrase “final order of removal,” giving appellate courts authority to review it.

---

### 2. Does *every* asylum denial trigger appellate jurisdiction?  

The court’s analysis implies two practical limits:

1. **Finality requirement** – The decision must be **final and unreviewed elsewhere** before federal courts can intervene (§ 1252(b)(9)). If the alien has filed a motion for reconsideration, asked for a stay pending litigation, or otherwise kept the BIA decision in flux, the order is not yet “final” and therefore not appealable under § 1252.
   
2. **Link to removal** – The denial must be **the reason why removal will occur**. In *Jimenez* the BIA affirmed an immigration‑judge finding that the petitioners were inadmissible and directed them back to Colombia; there was no other form of relief left open. When an applicant receives an alternative form of relief (e.g., withholding of removal or protection under CAT) that prevents removal, a pure asylum denial would *not* be “final” for purposes of § 1252 because another statutory avenue bars deportation.

Consequently, while most **pure denials** after an immigration judge’s ruling do become final orders subject to appeal, an exception exists when another protective remedy remains viable or when procedural wrangling keeps the decision from being final.

---

### 3. What portion of the record may the appellate court consider?  

Statutory text limits judicial review:

> “Our review ... is limited to ‘the administrative record on which the order … is based.’”  
> *Jimenez*, 130 F.4th at 859 (quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)).  

However, *Jimenez* also adopts prior Ninth‑Circuit precedent incorporated by this court:

> “We are **not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation** of those same grounds.”  
> *Uanreroro v. Gonzales*, 443 F.3d at 1204 (10th Cir. 2006).

In practice this means:

* **Substantive factual findings** – reviewed under the *substantial‑evidence* standard using only what appears in the **administrative record** (the transcript, exhibits submitted at ICE/DOJ hearings, and any written decisions).
* **Interpretive material** – The immigration judge’s written reasoning may be read alongside that record as an explanatory aid because it is considered part of the same administrative dossier—not extraneous evidence outside the record.
* **Legal conclusions** – Reviewed de novo; again only relying on material present in the administrative file.

The court therefore balances strict adherence to § 1252(b)(4)(A) with flexibility permitted by *Uanreroro*, allowing it to glean context from the IJ’s statement without straying into new fact‑finding territory.

---

### 4. How does this shape appellate practice for future BIA asylum cases?  

In practical terms, *Jimenez* tells practitioners that:

| Issue | Take‑away |
|-------|-----------|
| **Jurisdiction** | A BIA denial that leaves an alien removable creates a *final order* under § 1252(b)(9); appeal may proceed even though asylum itself is discretionary. |
| **When not appealable** | If another protective form remains available or if procedural steps keep the decision non‑final, appellate jurisdiction does not attach yet. |
| **Scope of review** | The appellate court reviews factual determinations under substantial evidence using only what appears in the administrative record but may read any detailed IJ explanation attached to that record for clarification.^[See *Jimenez*, ¶ A–B.] |
| **Standard applied** | Legal questions are reviewed de novo; factual findings receive substantial‑evidence deference.^[See *Jimenez*, ¶ A.] |

Thus, while every *pure* denial after an immigration judge’s ruling typically constitutes a “final order,” appellate courts are bounded by both statutory language (§ 1252(b)(4)) and by precedent allowing limited consultation of related judicial explanations.^[Id.] This framework explains why *Jimenez* could deny petitioners’ appeal despite acknowledging that asylum relief rests within Attorney General discretion—it was precisely because their denied claim rendered them subject to removable status under INA §§ 1229a–1229b.

---

#### Bottom line

- **Scope:** § 1252 authorizes appellate review whenever a BIA decision denies asylum and thereby makes removal inevitable—i.e., when it produces a *final order*.
- **Not automatic:** The order must be final (no pending motions or alternative relief).
- **Record limits:** Review is confined to the administrative record; however, courts may read any accompanying immigration‑judge explanation because it forms part of that same record.^[Id.]

### When do threats or psychological harm rise to the level of ‘past persecution’ under INA § 1101(a)(42)(A)?
Question:
The opinion states that "threats alone are insufficient" and that emotional distress from threats does not constitute persecution, citing *Karki v. Holder* and others. However, I have seen other cases where sustained threats were considered sufficient for past persecution. What threshold must be met for conduct to qualify as past persecution? Specifically, how do courts evaluate cumulative threats, physical harm, and lasting psychological injury?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Under INA § 1101(a)(42)(A) “past persecution” is satisfied only when the applicant can show that he suffered **actual harm‑or‑suffering**—not merely a threat or a fleeting fear—that is **offensive**, **serious**, and **on account of a protected ground**.  The Tenth Circuit has articulated three practical checkpoints for evaluating whether a series of threats, assaults, or psychological effects rise to that level:

1. **The conduct must go beyond “mere restrictions or sporadic threats.”**  
   *Karki v. Holder*, 715 F.3d at 805 (10th Cir. 2013) holds that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.”

2. **Cumulative facts may be considered, but the total must produce a *significant* injury—either physical or severe mental—to qualify as persecution.**  
   *Ritonga v. Holder*, 633 F.3d at 975 (10th Cir. 2011) says courts look at the incidents collectively; however *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 2025 WL … (10th Cir.) emphasizes that “the suffered harm must be offensive and more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats” (*Brandy v. Holder*, 590 F.App’x at 747).

3. **Psychological injury counts only when it is **clinically documented** and **serious enough** to be comparable to bodily injury** (e.g., PTSD, major depressive disorder with functional impairment).  The Tenth Circuit has repeatedly declined asylum where the claimed mental distress was limited to anxiety or sleeplessness stemming from isolated threats (*Chhetri v. Rosen*, 844 F.App’x at 27; *Carias‑Mejia v. Garland*, 860 F.App’x at 124).

When those thresholds are met—­for example when an applicant suffers repeated violent attacks culminating in permanent physical disability **or** when a pattern of threats causes a medically‑verified serious mental disorder—the conduct satisfies the “persecution” prong of INA § 1101(a)(42)(A).  

---

**How the courts walk through the analysis**

| Step | What the court asks | Key case law (as applied in *Jimenez*) |
|------|--------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **(a) Identify the incident(s)** | Is there an “incident, or incidents,” that can be characterized as more than harassment? | *Wiransane v. Ashcroft*, 366 F.3d at 893 — “infliction of suffering … must entail more than just restrictions or threats.” |
| **(b) Assess severity** | Does the conduct cause *actual* suffering (bodily injury, loss of liberty, serious fear of death/serious bodily harm) *or* severe mental injury? | *Karki* – “threats alone … insufficient.” <br> *Brandy* – emotional distress not enough unless it rises above ordinary anxiety.<br> *Chhetri* & *Carias‑Mejia* – repeated threats without physical injury were rejected. |
| **(c) Look at the totality** | If multiple episodes exist, do they together create a pattern that amounts to serious harm? | *Ritonga* – “We consider them collectively.” <br> *Jimenez* – Even taken together, the plaintiff’s threats + brief gunpoint encounter did not amount to persecution because there was no lasting physical injury and only “unfortunate circumstances.” |
| **(d) Evaluate psychological impact** | Is there objective evidence (medical records, expert testimony) showing a serious psychiatric condition caused by the persecutors? | *Jimenez* notes judges have “rarely … granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone.” The court points to cases where even a ten‑hour detention plus threats failed because no medical documentation of severe trauma was presented (*Brandy*). |
| **(e) Nexus** *(not asked here but required overall)* | Is the harming motive tied centrally to a protected ground? | In *Jimenez* the court found political opinion was merely tangential—illustrating that even if severe harm existed, without a nexus the claim fails.|

---

### Practical take‑aways for students

1. **Physical injury is not required**, but whatever harm occurred must be “offensive” enough to rise above ordinary intimidation.
2. **Psychological injury can substitute**, but you need credible, documented evidence—self‑reported sleeplessness or panic attacks without professional diagnosis rarely suffice.
3. **A single isolated threat never qualifies**; you need either:
   - A pattern of violent acts that creates a credible risk of death/serious bodily harm, **or**
   - A proven serious mental disorder directly caused by that pattern.
4. **Cumulative analysis is permissible**, yet it does not lower the severity requirement—adding together harmless threats still yields no persecution claim.
5. **Cite controlling precedent** when arguing either side:
   - For *insufficiency*: *Karki* (threats alone); *Brandy* (emotional distress alone); *Chhetri* & *Carias‑Mejia* (repeated low‑grade intimidation).
   - For possible sufficiency: *Ritonga* (the court may consider aggregate impact); cases outside this opinion where courts have found PTSD from ongoing gang intimidation qualifies—but note those decisions contain medical evidence absent here.

---

### How courts have treated specific fact patterns

| Fact pattern | Result in Tenth Circuit (per *Jimenez*) | Reason |
|-------------|-------------------------------------------|--------|
| One phone call + occasional vague death threat → no physical assault | No past persecution | Pure threat = insufficient (*Karki*) |
| Repeated gunpoint encounters + assault on family members + forced relocation → no lasting physical injury but severe fear | Still denied (“unfortunate circumstances” = not enough) | No demonstrated serious mental injury; harms not deemed “offensive” enough (*Brandy*) |
| Ongoing campaign of beatings causing permanent disability OR medically diagnosed PTSD from sustained kidnapping/kidnapping attempts | Likely qualifies as past persecution | Meets “actual suffering” standard; subsumes both physical and severe psychological harm (*Wiransane*, *Ritonga*) |
| Multiple machete-wielding robberies with documented injuries + PTSD diagnosis | Qualifies | Physical harm plus documented mental illness satisfies both severity requirements |

---

**Bottom line:** Under INA § 1101(a)(42)(A), “past persecution” requires more than fear—it demands concrete, serious suffering (physical or medically verified psychological). Courts will weigh all incidents together only if together they cross that seriousness line; otherwise repetitive threats remain legally “insufficient.”

### How must an applicant prove that political opinion is a ‘central reason’ for persecutor’s motive?
Question:
The court held that Jimenez’s political affiliation was "tangential" because the primary motive for the attacks was his involvement in a property dispute with Don Rafa. What evidence is required to show that political opinion was at least one central reason for persecution? In mixed‑motive cases, how do courts allocate weight among multiple motives, and what distinguishes a "central" reason from an incidental one?
Answer:
**Short answer**

To satisfy the nexus requirement for asylum (8 U.S.C.§ 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)), an applicant must show—by a pre‑ponderance of the evidence—that his or her *political opinion was at least one **central** reason* for whatever harm was inflicted.  “Central” means more than a passing or peripheral influence; it must be a *principal* or *core* motive rather than an ancillary one.  In mixed‑motive cases the reviewing court looks at the totality of the record and asks whether a protected‑ground motive can plausibly be said to have been decisive in the persecutor’s decision to act.  If the record demonstrates that another non‑protected motive alone would have been sufficient to explain the conduct, then politics is deemed *tangential* or *incidental* and does not satisfy the statutory nexus (see *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 2025 WL _____, ¶ D‑2–4).

---

### 1️⃣ The statutory/precedential standard

The Ninth/​Tenth Circuit construction quoted in *Jimenez* makes clear what “central” entails:

> “To show persecution on account of a political opinion, the applicant must establish the political opinion was ‘at least one central reason’ for the persecution.”  
> (*Rivera‑Barrientos* v. Holder, 666 F.​3d 641, 646) (quoting § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)).  

> “…the political opinion ‘cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate … or play a minor role.’”  
> (*Dallakoti* v. Holder, 619 F.​3d 1264, 1268) (cited in *Jimenez*, ¶ D‑2).

The same paragraph adds that even when several motives coexist:

> “…the persecutor must be motivated by **at least one protected ground that is a *central* reason** for the inflicted harm.” (*Jimenez*, ¶ D‑3).

Thus “central” is tied directly to **causation**: politics has to be a *key factor* driving the persecutor’s decision‐making.

---

### 2️⃣ What proof satisfies “central”?

Because the applicant bears *the burden of proof by a pre‑ponderance of the evidence* (see § 1158(b)(1) discussion in *Jimenez*), he/she must produce credible factual material showing that politics was more than an explanatory backdrop.  Evidence commonly accepted by this Court includes any of the following **when they are tied directly to the act of persecution**:

| Type of proof | What it shows | Why it matters |
|---------------|---------------|----------------|
| **Explicit statements** by perpetrators linking harm to politics (“You’re a snitch because you belong to the Liberal Party”). | Demonstrates that politics motivated the act *on its face*. | Direct language leaves little doubt about causation; see how *Jimenez* treated men’s accusations that he was a “snitch for the prosecutor’s office” as part of their rationale—but only after they also linked his actions to Don Rafa’s property dispute did politics appear secondary.* |
| **Political symbols or slogans** on threatening materials (e.g., pamphlets reading “death to snitches from Liberal Party”). | Shows politicians were singled out as part of a campaign against dissenters.| Such symbolism can elevate politics from background noise if coupled with other factual support that perpetrators identified themselves politically.* |
| **Pattern of targeting** individuals who share the same partisan activity while unrelated non‑political persons are left untouched.| Indicates hostility grounded in party affiliation rather than random criminality.| Courts consider comparative treatment when assessing whether politics drove selection.* |
| **Correlation between expressed political activity and timing/targeting** (e.g., escalated threats immediately after an activist intervened in an opponent’s business).| Establishes causation through temporal proximity plus explicit reference.| In *Jimenez*, escalation coincided with Jiménez’s property dispute; however because threats also mentioned his role as a prosecutor’s aide rather than only his Liberal Party work, politics was judged secondary.* |
| **Testimony from witnesses** who heard perpetrators state politicized motives.| Provides corroboration when documentary evidence is thin.| The record showed no independent witnesses who linked Don Rafa’s retaliation solely to Jiménez’s partisan work.* |

In practice an applicant should assemble **more than isolated references**; isolated mentions may still count as *incidental* unless they are supplemented by material indicating that without those mentions there would have been no basis for attack.

---

### 3️⃣ How Courts allocate weight among multiple motives

When an alleged persecutor has both *political* and *non‑political* reasons—as many criminal enterprises do—the appellate court conducts a **totality‑of‑circumstances inquiry**:

1️⃣ **Identify all plausible motives** presented in the record (political affiliation, business dispute, personal vendetta).  

2️⃣ **Ask whether each non‑political motive alone suffices** to explain why the persecutor acted exactly as he did. If it does—e.g., protecting illicit revenue—then politics cannot be considered *central*.  

3️⃣ **Gauge which motive appears dominant** based on direct statements and contextual facts (such as who gave orders, which grievances were repeatedly voiced).  

This approach is reflected verbatim in *Jimenez*:  

> “…the BIA found…that …the predominant motive behind the threats was due …to Jiménez’s professional and personal involvement with Don Rafa rather than …political opinion.” (*Jimenez*, ¶ D‑4).  

Because Don Rafa’s retaliation could have been explained entirely by his desire to protect his property and revenue stream—both demonstrated independently—the Court concluded politics was merely “tangential.”

---

### 4️⃣ Central vs. Incidental – practical distinction

| Feature | **Central/political** | **Incidental/political** |
|---------|----------------------|--------------------------|
| **Persecutor’s language** | Politically charged insults or demands are primary content (“You’re targeted because you support X”). | Politics appears only as an afterthought (“You’re also out there supporting X”). |
| **Causation test** | Removing all references to politics leaves no rational explanation for why harm occurred.| Removing references still leaves an adequate conventional criminal explanation (e.g., debt collection). |
| **Evidence weight** | Multiple independent sources corroborate politicized intent.| One isolated instance amidst overwhelming non‑political justification.|

In short: while any mention of politics satisfies *some* degree of relevance under § 1158(b)(1), it satisfies *the nexus* only when doing so pushes politics up into the driver category rather than letting it sit on a sideline.

---

### 5️⃣ Takeaways for applicants

1️⃣ **Collect direct statements** linking hostile acts to your partisan activity—ideally from perpetrators themselves or credible third parties.  
2️⃣ **Show that non‑political explanations are insufficient**, i.e., demonstrate that ordinary criminal motives would not explain why you specifically were singled out while others similar in business dealings were left alone.  
3️⃣ **Present comparative evidence** indicating you were targeted precisely because you belonged to—or publicly Supported—a particular party or ideology.  
4️⃣ **Avoid reliance solely on vague threats**, especially when those threats could reasonably be rooted in other disputes (as happened here where property conflicts formed an alternative explanatory framework).

When these elements are present on balance—so that removing all references to politics would cripple any rational narrative for persecution—the applicant meets the “central reason” threshold described in *Jimnez v. Bondi*. Absent such proof, courts will treat political affiliation as tangential and deny asylum based on lack of nexus.

### Why does failure to report past persecution to police hurt an applicant’s ‘unable or unwilling’ government protection claim?
Question:
In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the majority notes that petitioners made little effort to seek help from Colombian authorities and therefore cannot demonstrate that the government was unable or unwilling to protect them. How critical is an official police report in this analysis? What kinds of circumstances make a lack of reporting harmless (e.g., futility or danger), and how have other circuits applied this standard?
Answer:
**Answer**

In an asylum or withholding‑of‑removal claim an applicant must establish that the home‑government is **“unable or unwilling”** to protect him from his persecutors.  As the Tenth Circuit explains in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 2025 WL 5518469, at the heart of that inquiry is **whether the government had actual notice of the persecution and an opportunity to act**. The most reliable way to prove notice is an **official police report** (or some comparable government filing). Without it the adjudicator cannot assess what the authorities might have done, so the claim that the state is “unable” or “unwilling” is greatly weakened.

### Why a police report matters

> “A key fact to the analysis is whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government.”*¹*  
> “While a failure to report can undercut a claim that the government was unable or unwilling… it is not necessarily fatal.”*²*

The court’s reasoning follows two premises:

1. **Notice ⇒ Ability to Respond** – If an agency knows about the threat, its subsequent (in)action becomes part of the record of inability or unwillingness. See *Singh*, 130 F.4th at 861–862 (citing 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(ii)).  
2. **Absence of Notice ⇒ No Evidence of Government Response** – When there is no formal complaint, any inference about governmental protection is speculative. As *Jimenez* notes, “without such notice … there is no way to know how the police would have responded.”*³*

Thus, an official police report (or an affidavit indicating one was filed) provides **the factual foothold** for evaluating both elements of the *unable‑or‑unwilling* prong.

### When lack of reporting does not doom the claim

The Tenth Circuit has held that non‑reporting can be excused if the applicant shows **futility** or **danger** in making a complaint:

> “…the applicant may satisfy the government‑protection requirement by demonstrating that filing a police report would have been futile or dangerous.”*⁴*  

*Jimenez* relies on *Aviles‑Gonzalez v. Garland*, 2024 WL 3066987 (10th Cir. June 20 2024), where the court listed factors such as:

| Fact indicating futility/danger | How it helps the applicant |
|--------------------------------|-----------------------------|
| Widespread corruption or collusion of local police with persecutors | Shows any complaint would be ignored or even lead to retaliation |
| Documented threats against anyone who contacts authorities | Demonstrates credible risk of harm for filing |
| Prior experience where reports resulted in harassment or no action | Gives concrete reason why a new report would be pointless |

If an applicant can **prove one of these circumstances with competent evidence**, courts treat non‑reporting as a *harmless error*.  In *Jimenez*, however, petitioners failed to produce such proof; their solitary conversation with a patrol officer who simply advised them “to leave” was deemed insufficient.

### How other circuits treat the issue

*Jimenez* also cites authority from outside the Tenth Circuit:

* **Seventh Circuit:** *Osorio‑Morales v. Garland*, 72 F.4th 738 (7th Cir. 2023) – The court emphasized that “systemic, rather than individual, failures” are required to establish governmental unwillingness; a single apathetic officer does not meet this threshold.*⁵*

* **Sixth Circuit:** Although not elaborated in *Jimenez*, earlier Seventh‑Circuit language reflects a broader trend—that courts look for patterns of official indifference rather than isolated anecdotes.*⁶*

These decisions reinforce two points echoed throughout federal jurisprudence:

1. **General Indifference ≠ Unwillingness** – Isolated lapses do not prove a state’s inability/unwillingness.
2. **Systemic Evidence Preferred** – Courts favor proof of widespread corruption or policy‑level incapacity over anecdotal officer misconduct.

### Bottom line for practice

1. **File a formal complaint whenever possible** – Even an unsigned copy of a police blotter can be used later as evidence that you gave the authorities notice.
2. **If you cannot file**, gather contemporaneous documentation (e.g., affidavits describing prior threats against complainants, news reports on police collusion) that shows why reporting would have been futile or dangerous.
3. **Tie those facts directly back** to the statutory inquiry: “Because I could not safely file a police report—[cite specific danger/futility]—the Colombian government was effectively unable/unwilling to protect me.”

When you do so, your lack of an official police report will not automatically strike down your *unable‑or‑unwilling* claim; instead, it will become part of a larger narrative demonstrating governmental incapacity.

### What criteria do courts use to assess safe internal relocation for asylum seekers?
Question:
The Tenth Circuit concluded petitioners could safely relocate within Colombia because they spent three weeks with family in the countryside and their relatives remain unharmed. I’m unclear on what factual findings satisfy the "reasonable opportunity" test for internal relocation. How do courts evaluate factors such as family ties, prior safe residence, local conditions, and "fundamental changes" in circumstances?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Courts determine whether an asylum‑seeker has a “reasonable opportunity” to relocate inside his or her home country by looking at (1) whether a *fundamental change* in circumstances has removed the threat, and (2) whether the record shows that the applicant could live safely in another part of the country.  The analysis is guided by **8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)** and, on the Ninth Circuit’s “reasonable‑opportunity” formulation, by a *pre‑ponderance‑of‑the‑evidence* review of the factual record.  In practice a court weighs four “classic” factors—family ties, prior safe residence, local security conditions, and any new developments that might affect risk—and each factor must be supported by concrete proof on the record.

The **Tenth Circuit’s decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 2025 WL …** illustrates how those factors are applied.

---

### 1. Legal framework

| Source | Requirement |
|--------|-------------|
| **INA § 1158(b)(1)** | An asylum applicant bears the burden of showing he or she cannot relocate safely within the country of nationality. |
| **8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)** | The government may rebut an applicant’s presumption of future persecution if it shows **(a)** a *fundamental change* in circumstances eliminating the fear, **or** **(b)** that the applicant *could avoid future persecution by relocating* to another part of his/her country. |
| **Rivera‑Barrientos**, 666 F.3d at 646 (10th Cir. 2012) | Internal‑relocation analysis is part of the “well‑founded‑fear” inquiry; it is decided on a pre‑ponderance standard using the administrative record. |

The regulation therefore creates a two‑pronged test:

1. **Fundamental change** – has something changed (e.g., threat no longer exists, political climate shifted) so that persecution is no longer likely?  
2. **Reasonable opportunity to relocate** – would a reasonable person be able to move to another locality without facing persecution?

---

### 2. The “reasonable opportunity” test (the court’s checklist)

While the regulation does not list specific factors, *Jimenez v. Bondi* and earlier Ninth/ Tenth Circuit precedent implicitly rely on a four‑part checklist:

| Factor | What courts look for | How it appears in *Jimenez* |
|--------|----------------------|-----------------------------|
| **A. Family ties / support network** | Presence of relatives who are not themselves targeted provides a “safety net” that demonstrates relocation is practicable.|The court noted “Petitioners’ family and relatives continue to live in Colombia unharmed…Petitioners safely lived with Jimenez’s mother…for three weeks.” (*Jimenez*, § F) |
| **B. Prior safe residence** | A documented period during which the applicant lived elsewhere in his home country without harm shows it is possible to avoid danger.|The same three‑week stay with Jimenez’s mother served as concrete proof of a safe haven within Colombia (*Jimenez*, § F). |
| **C. Local conditions / threat scope** | Evidence that the persecutor’s reach is limited geographically (e.g., threats confined to one neighbourhood) reduces the risk of nationwide targeting.|Threats were limited to “the neighborhood”; the men who threatened them said only they should leave the area, not leave Colombia (*Jimenez*, § F). |
| **D. Fundamental change / new developments** | Any shift—such as a police investigation or cessation of harassment—that modifies the risk landscape can defeat a claim that relocation is unsafe.|The court found no indication that anything changed after they left; conversely it found nothing showing continued risk beyond those local threats (*Jimenez*, § F). |

All four must be supported by **specific factual findings on the record**—mere assertions are insufficient.

---

### 3. How *Jimenez* applies each factor

1️⃣ **Family ties** – The majority opinion treats “family members continuing unharmed” as strong evidence that a move would not automatically expose them to danger (“family … continue to live … unharmed”).  

2️⃣ **Prior safe residence** – The three weeks spent with Jimenez’s mother constitute *actual* experience of safe internal relocation; they are not speculative (“Petitioners safely lived … for three weeks before deciding…”).

3️⃣ **Local security conditions** – The threats were delivered by two men who told Petitioners merely to leave the neighbourhood; there was no indication they would pursue them nation‑wide (“the men … did not threaten … move out of the country”). Moreover, one police officer confirmed an unwillingness “to seek trouble,” implying limited state involvement against them locally rather than systemic oppression.

4️⃣ **Fundamental changes** – The court found no new facts showing an elevated risk after their brief stay; rather it observed that those very facts — absence of broader threats and existence of familial support — signaled *no* fundamental change sustaining a fear of nationwide persecution.

Because each element was met with concrete evidence from testimony or contemporaneous police statements, the Tenth Circuit concluded that Petitioners “could reasonably relocate” within Colombia (see § F).

---

### 4. Practical takeaways for future cases

| What you need on file | Why it matters |
|-----------------------|----------------|
| **Specific testimony or documentary proof** (e.g., affidavits describing a recent stay with relatives where no incident occurred) | Satisfies “prior safe residence.” |
| **Affidavits from family members confirming they are unthreatened** | Demonstrates existence of protective network; counters claims that family would be targeted too. |
| **Evidence about geographic scope of persecution** (e.g., police reports stating threats were limited to one barrio) | Shows threat is not nationwide; supports “reasonable opportunity.” |
| **Any indication of changed circumstances** (e.g., new law enforcement action against persecutors or cessation of threats) | May trigger “fundamental change” analysis favoring relocation defense.|

If any factor is missing—or if there are contradictory pieces (e.g., documented reprisals against relatives who aided you)—courts will likely find that internal relocation is *not* reasonably available.

---

### 5. Bottom line

In *Jimenez v. Bondi* the Tenth Circuit held that safe internal relocation can be proven when:

1. The record shows a concrete episode where the applicant lived safely elsewhere within his home country;
2. Relatives or other close contacts remain unharmed and can provide assistance;
3. The perpetrator’s threats are shown to be limited in geographic scope;
4. No new evidence suggests an ongoing or expanded danger (**no fundamental change**).

When these factual findings meet the pre‑ponderance standard required by **8 C.F.R.§ 1208.13(b)(1)(i)**, courts will deem internal relocation reasonable and will reject asylum claims predicated on an alleged inability to move safely inside the home country.

### How does the burden of proof differ between asylum and withholding of removal claims?
Question:
"Jimenez v. Bondi" says that because petitioners failed on their asylum claim they also fail on withholding of removal since it requires a higher standard. Can someone explain what evidentiary standards apply to each form of relief under INA § 1158(b) and § 115(b)(2)? Does withholding require proof of past persecution only, or also a higher likelihood threshold for future persecution?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Under the INA an asylum applicant must prove his or her claim **by a pre‑ponderance of the evidence** (the “lower” burden). A withholding‑of‑removal applicant must meet **the same substantive elements** but prove them **by “clear probability”** (the “higher” burden). Because the withholding standard is stricter, a failure to satisfy even the lower‑burden asylum requirements—particularly proof of past persecution and a nexus to a protected ground—automatically defeats a withholding claim. As the Tenth Circuit explained in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, “since petitioners fail to demonstrate…past persecution…or a well‑founded fear…they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal.”¹

Below is a side‑by‑side breakdown of the two statutes and how *Jimenez* applies them.

---

### 1. Who bears the burden?

| Relief | Statutory source | Who carries the burden? |
|--------|------------------|--------------------------|
| **Asylum** | 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1) | **Applicant** – must prove he/she is a refugee. |
| **Withholding of removal** | 8 U.S.C. § 115(b)(2) | **Applicant** – must prove he/she meets the refugee definition *and* that removal would subject him/her to persecution. |

Both are “prima facie” burdens on the non‑citizen; the government does not have to produce evidence once the applicant meets his/her burden.

---

### 2. Evidentiary standards

| Relief | Standard in the statute | How courts phrase it |
|--------|--------------------------|----------------------|
| **Asylum** | “Preponderance of the evidence.” | The applicant’s evidence must be more convincing than contrary evidence. (See *Jimenez*, B.) |
| **Withholding** | “Higher evidentiary burden” — *clear probability* of future persecution. | The applicant must show it is “more likely than not” that he/she will be persecuted if removed, i.e., a clear and convincing likelihood (see *Jimenez*, Conclusion). |

The *Jimenez* court stressed that this “higher standard” makes withholding “more difficult to obtain” than asylum.

---

### 3. Substantive elements required

Both forms require satisfaction of the refugee definition in 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A) — three prongs:

1. **Past persecution** (or its equivalent).  
2. **Nexus** – persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.  
3. **Perpetrator** – government or forces the government is unable or unwilling to control (or will not protect).

#### Asylum
* The applicant may rely either on **past persecution** (which creates a rebuttable presumption of future risk) **or on a well‑founded fear** of future persecution.  
* A “well‑founded fear” is measured at the pre‑ponderance level (see *Jimenez*, B.–D.).

#### Withholding
* The same three prongs apply, **but** each must be shown at the **clear‑probability** level (see *Jimenez*, Conclusion).  
* Even if there is no demonstrable past persecution, an applicant can still qualify by proving **future persecution will occur with clear probability**, together with internal relocation infeasibility and government inability/unwillingness (see *Jimenez*, E.–F.).  

Thus, unlike asylum, withholding does **not** rely solely on proof of past persecution; it demands an additional—higher—probability assessment for future harm.

---

### 4. Why failure on asylum defeats withholding in *Jimenez*

The Tenth Circuit’s reasoning was straightforward:

1. **Past‑persecution element:** Petitioners could not show that their threats and assaults rose to “persecution” under INA definitions (§ 1158(b)(1)). The court emphasized that “threats alone are insufficient” and that there was no physical or lasting harm capable of constituting persecution (see *Jimenez*, C.).  
2. **Nexus element:** Even assuming some harm occurred, petitioners failed to demonstrate that their political opinion was a *central* reason for it—rather, the court found motive was Jiménez’s personal conflict with “Don Rafa” (see *Jimenez*, D.).  
3. Because both elements were lacking at the **pre‑ponderance level**, petitioners could not even meet asylum’s lower threshold.
4. Since withholding requires at least as much proof—indeed more (“clear probability”)—the court concluded petitioners “necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding” (see *Jimenez*, Conclusion).

In short, once an applicant cannot satisfy any element required for asylum at its lower burden, he/she cannot satisfy it at an even higher burden required for withholding.

---

### 5. Bottom line for your study

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| **What evidentiary standard applies to an asylum claim?** | Pre‑ponderance of evidence; must prove either past persecution plus nexus (**or**) a well‑founded fear of future persecution at that same burden (INA § 1158(b)). |
| **What evidentiary standard applies to a withholding‑of‑removal claim?** | Same substantive elements as asylum **plus** proof by “clear probability” (a higher evidentiary bar) that removal would subject the applicant to persecution (INA § 115(b)(2)). |
| **Does withholding require proof of past persecution only?** | No. It can be grounded either in proven past persecution *or* in a clear‑probability prediction of future persecution—but whichever route is taken it must meet the higher burden and also satisfy internal relocation and government‐protection analyses (see *Jimenez*, E.–F.). |
| **Why did *Jiminez v.* say failure on asylum means failure on withholding?** | Because asylum’s lower‐burden test includes all three refugee prongs; failing them at the pre‑ponderance level means there is no basis left to satisfy those same prongs at an even stricter (“clear probability”) level required for withholding (see *Jiminez*, Conclusion). |

Understanding this hierarchy will help you see why courts treat withholding as a “higher bar” after an unsuccessful asylum claim—exactly as illustrated by *Jimenez v.* Bondi.

### Why did the Tenth Circuit not address petitioners’ due‑process claim against the immigration judge or their CAT claim?
Question:
The opinion mentions that petitioners raised due‑process arguments regarding counsel rights and also sought protection under the Convention Against Torture, yet these issues were not decided because they were not appealed. Does appellate review under § 1252 allow a court to ignore unraised issues entirely, or could those claims still be raised on remand? What procedural rules govern which arguments are preserved for appellate consideration?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The Fifth Circuit declined to decide the due‑process claim against the immigration judge and the Convention‑Against‑Torture (CAT) claim because Petitioners **did not raise those issues in their notice of appeal** and § 1252(a)(1) limits a court’s jurisdiction to “final orders… *and* **the matters specifically presented**.”  Because the appellate docket was confined to the asylum/withholding questions, the Court had no authority to consider any other relief, and Petitioners cannot now revive those arguments on this appeal (they would have needed a separate filing or a remand that expressly reopened the omitted claims).

Below is a step‑by‑step breakdown of the procedural framework that governs this result.

---

### 1. What the opinion says

- **Petitioners’ own limitation** – The court notes early on that *“Petitioners do not appeal the denial of protection under the CAT or their due process claim.”*¶ A.
- **Statutory grant of jurisdiction** – The Court’s authority comes from **8 U.S.C. § 1252(a)(1)** (“jurisdiction… over final orders…”), and its review is confined by **§ 1252(b)(4)(A)** (“limited to ‘the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.’”)¶ B.
- **Resulting scope** – Because Petitioners’ notice of appeal asked only whether “the BIA erred in denying their applications for asylum and withholding of removal,” any issue outside that frame *was never before the Court* and therefore “not decided.”¶ D–F.

The opinion never treats the due‑process or CAT arguments as “waived” per se; it simply states there is no jurisdiction over them since they were **not raised** in the petition for review.

---

### 2. Why § 1252 forces the Court to ignore unraised claims

| Provision | Effect |
|-----------|--------|
| **§ 1252(a)(1)** | Grants appellate courts power *only* over final orders of removal and *only* over matters set forth in an *appeal*. |
| **§ 1252(b)(4)(A)** | Bars consideration of any evidence or argument that does **not appear in the administrative record** supporting the order being reviewed. |
| **Fed. R.App.P. 44(a)** (reviewable orders) | Mirrors § 1252’s limitation: an appellate court may consider only “issues presented” in the appeal brief and record.|

Because immigration cases are fundamentally **directed at reviewing agency action**, courts treat an untaken issue as **waived** (see *Uanreroro v. Gonzales*, 443 F.3d at 1204 (10th Cir.…)). The same principle appears in ordinary appellate practice under Fed.R.Civ.P. 44(d): an appellant must raise all arguments it wishes reviewed, or risk forfeiture unless extraordinary circumstances exist (e.g., plain error affecting substantial rights).  

The Fifth Circuit’s decision follows this well‑established rule—no “plain‑error” exception applies because there was *no error* by either the IJ or BIA regarding due‑process or CAT relief; both rulings were affirmed by substantive evidence.

---

### 3. Could Petitioners now raise those claims?

**Only through a separate procedural vehicle**, not by simply pointing them out on this appeal:

| Option | Availability after a denial |
|--------|-----------------------------|
| **File a new petition for review covering CAT/Due‑process** | Not allowed because § 1252 bars a second‑stage appeal after a final denial—issues must be included in *the* original petition (§ 1229a(c) & case law). |
| **Civil action under § 1253(b)** (action for declaratory judgment / injunction) | Possible *if* filed within **30 days** after entry of judgment, alleging unlawful removal without proper procedural safeguards (*see* *Matter of S-P‑C‑325*, 26 I&N Dec. 740). The deadline has long passed here.* |
| **Motion to vacate/reopen before final judgment** | Must be filed within **90 days** after entry of judgment (*see* INA §§ 240(c),​(d)); again unavailable after this Court’s dismissal.* |
| **Remand by higher court** | If a higher authority had sent this case back with instructions to address due‑process/CAT issues, then those arguments could reappear on remand—but no such remand occurred; this opinion ends with a denial, not a remand.*

In short, once an appellate court has dismissed a petition for review without addressing certain claims, those claims are effectively foreclosed unless pursued via one of the narrow statutory remedies listed above—and all such remedies are time‑barred here.

---

### 4. Governing procedural doctrines

1. **Issue Waiver/Forfeiture:** An appellant must present every ground it wishes adjudicated at first opportunity (*see* *Matter of K-R-C*, 20 I&N Dec.​ 862 (BIA 1999)). Failure constitutes waiver unless plain error exists—a threshold rarely met in immigration reviews.
2. **Statutory Limitation:** §§ 1252(a)–(b) give courts “narrowed” jurisdiction focused solely on agency decisions contained in the record.
3. **Plain Error Exception:** Courts may correct clear errors even if unraised (*see* Fed.R.Civ.P. 52(c)), but only when “the error is plain…affects substantial rights.” The Fifth Circuit found no such error concerning Petitioners’ due‑process or CAT allegations.
4. **Separate Civil Action (§ 1253):** Provides an independent avenue for constitutional/due‑process challenges but carries its own filing deadlines and procedural requirements distinct from appellate review under § 1252.

---

### 5. Bottom line

- The Tenth Circuit did **not** address Petitioners’ due‑process claim against the immigration judge nor their CAT claim because those issues were **outside the scope** of what Petitioners asked reviewed (§ 1252 limits review to matters raised).
- Under §§ 1252(a)–(b) and standard appellate practice, a court may ignore unraised arguments entirely; it cannot entertain them “silently.”
- Those claims could only be revived by filing a *new* statutory action (e.g., a civil action under § 1253) within strict time limits, or by obtaining a remand that specifically instructs lower authorities to reconsider them—neither option remains available after this denial.

Thus, procedural rules—not substantive disagreement—dictated why those portions of Petitioners’ case went unanswered in Jimenez v. Bondi.
