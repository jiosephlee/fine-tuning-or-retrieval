Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Defunding Refugees Without a Rule? How the APA Shapes Challenges to Executive Suspension of Resettlement Funding  

## Why Pacito v. Trump Matters  

When President Trump ordered a blanket suspension of refugee admissions in early 2025 and simultaneously defunded the very agencies that assist newly arrived refugees, he set off a constitutional‑statutory clash that reaches far beyond a handful of nonprofits in Seattle. The case tests two seemingly opposite doctrines that have long guided immigration litigation: the near‑absolute deference accorded to the President’s “detrimental‑to‑the‑interests” finding under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), and the rigorous procedural safeguards built into the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) for any agency action that alters an existing regulatory or contractual scheme.  

If the Ninth Circuit ultimately finds that the government “cannot engage in ‘a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications’” regarding the refugee moratorium—an approach rooted in *Trump v. Hawaii*—but does have to obey at least one APA principle when it ripped up cooperative agreements overnight, Pacito will become a touchstone for how courts balance immigration deference against procedural accountability in other “policy‑by‑budget” actions.

## The Procedural Backdrop  

The district court in Western Washington entered two preliminary injunctions in February 2025: one blocking enforcement of §§ 3(a)‑(c) and 4 of Executive Order 14163 (the refuge‑admission moratorium), and another ordering the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements it had terminated after those orders went into effect. The government appealed both injunctions and secured partial stays from the Ninth Circuit’s motions panel in March, April and May 2025—except for a narrow carve‑out protecting individuals who had already received conditional refugee approvals.

After months of briefing, plaintiffs asked the Ninth Circuit to vacate those stays while it considered its own appeal. In an interim order issued September 12 2025 (the document we’re analyzing), the panel stayed **both** district‑court injunctions **in their entirety**, **except** for one crucial command: it required the federal government to “reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services” under 8 U.S.C. § 1522.

The panel’s brief rationale rests on two key observations:  

1. **Immigration Deference Wins on Section 1182(f).** The judges affirmed that “the sole prerequisite … is that the President ‘find[]’ … would be detrimental,” citing *Hawaii*’s flat‑out deference language (“The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement”).  
2. **At Least One APA Claim Fails for the Government.** Because agency action under § 1522 carries mandatory statutory language (“shall”), the panel concluded that the abrupt termination of contracts without notice‑and‑comment rulemaking—or any semblance of an agency reasoned decision—might constitute an arbitrary or capricious violation of the APA.

Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent adds a layer of nuance by arguing that § 1522 is *authorization* rather than *mandate*, meaning no statutory duty exists to provide those services at all—a contention we’ll return to later.

## The APA Lens: Arbitrary‑and‑Capricious Review  

Under **APA § 706(2)(A)**, courts may set aside agency actions found “arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law.” The standard requires agencies to show they examined relevant data reasonably and articulated a rational connection between facts found and decisions made (*Motor Vehicle Mfrs.*). In Pacito:

- **No Notice‑and‑Comment Rulemaking:** The State Department’s sudden termination of all cooperative agreements—a fundamental shift in how federal refugee resettlement funding is allocated—did not involve any notice published in *the Federal Register* nor any opportunity for interested parties (the nonprofit resettlement organizations) to comment on alternative approaches or mitigation measures.
- **Absence of Agency Fact‑Finding:** The record shows only vague statements such as “we are actively preparing a request for proposals.” The agency offered no evidentiary basis linking contract terminations directly to its national‑interest justification under § 1182(f). That disconnect opens a classic arbitrary‑and‑capricious hole.
- **Procedural Requirements Under FAR:** Even if one argues § 1522 merely authorizes funding rather than obligates it, federal procurement rules still impose notice‐and‐competition duties when discharging contracts funded by appropriations (§ 5520(a)–(e), FAR Subpart 15). Abrupt contract cancellations without due process could violate both procurement statutes and ‘fair‐dealing’ principles underlying § 706(1).

Thus, while courts will defer heavily on *why* Congress gave the President leeway over admissions decisions—a realm squarely within executive discretion—the *how* matters when Congress also enacts mandatory service provisions (or at least authorizations interpreted as mandates). The Ninth Circuit appears prepared to enforce those procedural safeguards regardless of immigration deference.

## Interpreting 8 U.S.C.§ 1522: Authorization or Obligation?  

The divergence between Judges Lee and his colleagues centers on whether § 1522 erects an enforceable duty for “reception and placement services.”  

- **A Textualist View (Lee):** The statute’s heading (“Authorization for programs…”) signals permissiveness; every subsection uses “may/shall … if … available appropriations,” implying discretionary spending authority rather than an unqualified command to spend millions each fiscal year.
- **A Mandatory Reading (Panel Majority):** Although § 1522 begins with “to the extent of available appropriations,” it also mandates specific conditions (“shall make available sufficient resources…”) if funding occurs—these are conditions attached **to** an authorized program rather than optional actions left entirely to agency whim. Moreover, subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A) contain binding “shall” commands once assistance is decided upon for unaccompanied minors or recent arrivals.
  
Both readings have precedent support:

- In *Negusie v. Holder*, 555 U.S. 511 (2009), Justice Ginsburg noted that Congress intended “a general rule” for ongoing treatment—a phrasing akin to permanent obligations rather than fleeting authorizations.
- Conversely, courts have upheld bipartisan interpretations where “authorization” can be read as mere permission—as in *NLRB v*. Rogers*, 446 U.S., where certain statutory titles were deemed indicative but not determinative.

Pacito forces us closer: if courts adopt Lee’s permissive construction, agencies could simply close down existing service programs when they deem them politically undesirable—a stark inversion of congressional intent underlying Section 1522’s elaborate funding scheme ($3.178 billion appropriated before rescission). If they follow Lee’s colleagues’ mandatory construction, any abrupt funding cuts would trigger AP­A scrutiny irrespective of how much discretion Congress left them under Ok!

## Doctrinal Tension: Immigration Deference Meets Administrative Formalism  

The case crystallizes a growing tension between two doctrines:

1️⃣ **Deference Doctrine (Chevron/Casemix/Skidmore):** Usually invoked for statutory interpretation concerning immigration powers (`§1182(f)`), giving agencies—and particularly Presidents—a high ceiling (‘ample power’) once a ‘finding’ is made.

2️⃣ **Procedural Foundations Under APA/FAR:** Even when substantive policy is left unsupervised, agencies cannot ignore procedural requirements when changing existing frameworks tied directly to statutes containing `shall` language or significant appropriations.

If appellate courts accept that these doctrines operate in parallel—deference shields substantive "why" but not process—the implication is profound: future presidents may wield blunt instruments like blanket suspensions more freely so long as they retain full procedural rigor when tinkering with associated funding streams or contracts eyeing Congress-mandated outcomes.

## Potential Downstream Effects  

### 1️⃣ Litigating Funding Cuts Across Policy Domains  
Pacito signals that plaintiffs can survive an initial deference hurdle if they focus their challenge on procedural missteps—no notice-and-comment rulemaking—and on violating statutory `shall` provisions embedded elsewhere (e.g., housing assistance under HUD). We might see similar suits arise against abrupt cuts to veteran benefits (Veterans Affairs), pandemic relief programs (CDC), or climate grant rollbacks (EPA).

### 2️⃣ Heightened Scrutiny Over Cooperative Agreements  
Nonprofit partners often rely on long‑standing federal grant mechanisms rather than full open competition due largely to congressional authorizations emphasizing continuity (“cooperative agreements”). Once these mechanisms are used as leverage points for sweeping policy changes—as here—the judicial bar may rise regarding how quickly agencies can dissolve them without alternate processes completing first.

### 3️⃣ Congressional Checks & Appropriation Strategies  
Congress may respond by tightening language around spend-authorizations—from permissive “may” toward mandatory “shall,” tying receipt‐and–placement duties explicitly to appropriation bills rather than leaving them within agency discretion suffixes (`authorizes` vs `requires`). Alternatively—if lawmakers want more leeway—they might embed clearer sunset clauses permitting automatic termination upon presidential dire‐interest findings `with appropriate notice`.

### 4️⃣ Potential Supreme Court Review  
Given its dual nature—a presidential immigration moratorium next door from*Trump v.* Hawai`i plus robust administrative law concerns—the case stands as prime material for SCOTUS if either side persists after final Ninth Circuit judgment (perhaps over whether § 1522 imposes enforceable spending obligations).
