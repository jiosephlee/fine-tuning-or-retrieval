# Mandate or Permission? Decoding Section 1522’s Statutory Language in the Refugee Resettlement Dispute  

*By [Your Name]*  

## Why this case matters  

The Ninth Circuit’s interim order in *Pacito v. Trump* has thrust a decades‑old question onto the national stage: does Congress obligate the executive branch to fund refugee “reception and placement” services, or does it merely give agencies permission to do so when funds are available?  The answer will determine whether a President can simultaneously halt refugee admissions while allowing the government to shirk its statutory responsibilities toward refugees already on U.S. soil.  Because the Refugee Act of 1980 is the statutory backbone of America’s humanitarian‑refugee system, any reinterpretation of its funding provisions reverberates far beyond Washington state courts—affecting future appropriations battles, agency discretion under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), and even the structure of other “authorizing” statutes that devote limited appropriations language to charitable programs.  

## The statutory text at issue  

At the heart of *Pacito* lies 8 U.S.C. § 1522, a clause that sits inside the broader Refugee Act framework.  The section is divided into subparts that use both “shall” and “is authorized.”  For example:

* “The Director… **shall** make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement…**to the extent of available appropriations**” (§ 1522(a)(1)(A)).  
* “The Director **is authorized** to provide cash assistance…**to the extent of available appropriations**” (§ 1522(e)(7)(A)).  

The conflicting verbs have prompted divergent readings.  The majority panel of the Ninth Circuit interpreted § 1522 as imposing a **mandatory** duty: because Congress pairs “shall” with concrete programmatic requirements (training, placement, cash assistance), it cannot be reduced to a mere permission.  Accordingly, the panel ordered the State Department to reinstate cooperative agreements that furnish these services—even while it affirmed presidential deference under § 1182(f) for suspending new admissions.

Judge Lee’s partial dissent challenges that view.  He emphasizes that § 1522’s very title – “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees” – signals congressional intent toward permissiveness rather than compulsion.  Lee argues that “authorization” is defined at Black’s Law Dictionary as “permission,” and each operative subsection likewise uses “is authorized,” not “shall” (except where “shall” merely qualifies conditions attached to discretionary spending).  

The textual dispute thus hinges on whether “shall” creates an independent command or simply serves as a conditional clause attached to an underlying authorization.

## Interpretive frameworks: Chevron versus Skidmore  

When agency officials claim discretion over statutory spending decisions—especially where Congress declares a program “authorized”—courts typically apply one of two interpretive doctrines.  

Under **Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council**, courts first ask whether Congress has spoken directly on an issue; if not, they defer to an agency’s reasonable construction so long as it is not contrary to congressional intent.  The framework presumes that statutes are often ambiguous by design and delegates resolution to agencies possessing expertise—in this case, HHS (the Office of Refugee Resettlement) and State Department officials who administer USRAP contracts.

By contrast, **Skidmore v. Swift Corp.** offers a more nuanced approach when Chevron deference is unavailable—for instance when a statute presents an impermissibly vague command or when an agency interprets language outside its core expertise (such as whether an authorization imposes an enforceable duty).  Under Skidmore, courts give weight to an agency’s interpretation according to its persuasiveness rather than automatically deferring.

Judge Lee implicitly nudges toward a Skidmore‑type analysis: because § 1522 mixes permissive language (“is authorized”) with conditional “shall” clauses tied closely to funding availability (“to the extent of available appropriations”), he suggests that any mandatory reading should be grounded in clear congressional intent—not simply inferred from ordinary grammatical usage.

The Ninth Circuit’s majority appears willing to apply Chevron deference in favor of HHS’ longstanding practice of providing reception‑and‑placement services via contracts—a practice long alleged by Congress through appropriations bills rather than via explicit mandatory language.  By treating § 1522 as creating enforceable obligations regardless of budget cuts (notably after an $800 million reduction under the Rescissions Act), the panel effectively elevates HHS’s interpretive stance into law without subjecting it again to scrutiny under Skidmore principles.

## What Judge Lee says—and why his reading matters  

Judge Lee’s dissent pivots on three observations:

1. **Title signals permissiveness** – Section headings are often indicative of congressional intent; here “Authorization …” sets expectations that Congress provided space for agency action but did not compel it.
   
2. **Mixed verbage reflects conditional authority** – While subsections contain “shall,” those mandates are bounded by *“to the extent of available appropriations,”* suggesting that if Congress fails to appropriate funds (as occurred with FY 2025), there is no duty triggered.
   
3. **Legislative history backs discretionary funding** – Lee notes past appropriations reports where Congress repeatedly urged agencies “to seek innovative partnerships” and framed receiving services as optional pilots rather than mandatory entitlements.

If Lee is correct, then requiring agencies to fund reception‑and‑placement services despite appropriation cuts would effectively create a **de facto appropriation**, contravening Article I’s power of the purse by converting an authorization into a binding spending mandate without explicit congressional vote.

Beyond *Pacito*, such reasoning could reshape numerous statutes written with similar dual language—disaster relief authority under FEMA, health assistance under P.L. 104‑208 (the Hill‑Burton Act), or child welfare grants under Title IV-E—all of which pair permissive authorizations with conditional “shall” requirements contingent upon available funding.

## Doctrinal ripple effects  

### Congressional drafting practices

If courts follow Lee’s guidance, legislatures may be prompted either (a) to draft more precise statutes when they genuinely intend compulsory spending—using clear language like “the Secretary shall allocate X dollars each fiscal year”—or (b) explicitly declare certain programs as non‑mandatory so agencies can adjust rapidly during budget shortfalls without fear of judicial injunctions.

### Agency risk management

Agencies currently operating under presumptions of mandatory service provision may reassess their contractual arrangements during fiscal volatility. In *Pacito*, had Lee’s view prevailed fully, the State Department could have left cooperative agreements terminated without violating any statutory duty—a relief for agencies grappling with sudden funding curtailments.

### Judicial scrutiny under APA

The Ninth Circuit already signaled skepticism toward plaintiffs’ APA claims concerning defunding procedures—suggesting possible uncertainties around notice-and-comment requirements applicable when agencies rescind contracts without formal rulemaking. If § 1522 is read as non‑mandatory, agencies can argue that rescinding contracts does not constitute an ‘agency action’ subject to substantive APA review; consequently future challenges will focus squarely on statutory interpretation rather than procedural propriety.

### Broader immigration policy

By separating authority over admissions (unquestionably vested in § 1182(f)) from obligations once admission occurs (§ 1522), courts might enable future Presidents—even those hostile toward refugee admissions—to suspend new entries while nevertheless preserving existing support structures for those already in limbo. Conversely, a strict mandatory reading would force administrations either to fund resettlement despite reduced intake or risk judicial orders mandating service provision even absent new refugees—a tension likely exploited by both advocates and opponents alike.

## Where we go from here  

The full merits opinion remains pending, but Judge Lee’s dissent crystallizes a structural fault line within immigration law: does congressional compromise embed non‑negotiable duties within ostensibly permissive authorizations?  Should courts lean heavily on textual grammar (“shall”), or ought they heed legislative labeling (“authorization”) and contextual cues about appropriations?

Given recent trends toward aggressive executive control over entry restrictions—championed by *Trump v. Hawaii*’s wide‐ranging deference—the resolution of § 1522 will serve as an essential counterbalance if it imposes any substantive check on presidential discretion after admission has occurred. A majority endorsement would affirm procedural protections for refugees already accepted into America; a dissent‑driven reversal would grant administrations near‑total latitude over both intake and aftercare provided funding dries up.

In any event, practitioners should watch closely how lower courts apply Chevron versus Skidmore analyses in contexts where statutory authorizations intertwine with conditional “shall” commands—especially where those conditions intersect contested budgetary realities.

## Conclusion  

*Pacito* presents more than a tug‑of‑war over refugee admissions; it spotlights how precise word choice can expand—or contract—the reach of federal obligations during politically charged moments. As Judge Lee reminds us, words matter: an “authorization” can be read either as permission or as implied command depending upon grammatical scaffolding and legislative purpose both celebrated in statute construction tools dating back centuries yet brought freshly alive by today’s partisan politics.

Whether practitioners will ultimately rely on *Granting agencies breathing room* or *Enforcing congressional mandates* will hinge upon how this Ninth Circuit panel adjudicates § 1522 after hearing full arguments—and consequently how other courts resolve similar authorizing statutes across our nation’s humanitarian programs.
