Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# The President’s Pen Is Mightier Than Ever? Deference to Executive Power Under § 1182(f) After *Pacito v. Trump*

## Why *Pacito* Matters

The September 2025 Ninth Circuit order in *Pacito v. Trump* placed a fresh spotlight on one of the most sweeping grants of authority in U.S. immigration law—8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which lets a president suspend the entry of any class of aliens when he finds it “detrimental to the interests of the United States.”  The panel’s explicit statement that courts may not conduct a “searching inquiry” into the president’s justification revives—and arguably expands—the robust deference doctrine articulated in *Trump v. Hawaii*.  Because refugee admissions are both a humanitarian program established by Congress and an instrument of foreign‑policy discretion, any shift in the judicial posture toward § 1182(f) reverberates through invasion‑policy debates, congressional oversight battles, and future attempts to curtail not just refugees but other categories of non‑citizens.

## The Ninth Circuit’s Reasoning

Faced with emergency appeals from both sides, the panel first applied the well‑settled four‑factor stay test from *Nken v. Holder*.  It found the government “likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge” to *the validity* of Executive Order 14163—the president’s blanket moratorium on refugee admissions—on two grounds.  First, it noted that the statutory text requires only a presidential “find” that entry would be detrimental; second, it emphasized that determining whether such a finding satisfies Congress is a matter for the political branches, not for courts engaged in an interlocutory stay.  Citing *Trump v. Hawaii*, the opinion declared:

> “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’…the entry…would be detrimental…The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.”

By framing the statutory threshold as purely formal—an unfettered factual determination—the majority effectively insulated Executive Order 14163 from substantive judicial scrutiny at this stage.  It reasoned further that even if plaintiffs could show an abuse of discretion under an APA challenge concerning funding cuts (and indeed the court ordered reinstatement of certain cooperative agreements), such concerns did not intrude upon the separate question of whether § 1182(f) gave the president authority to halt refugee admissions altogether.

Judge Lee’s partial concurrence underscored this line divide within the panel.  While she agreed that § 1182(f) grants unambiguous power to suspend admissions—and therefore she would stay those injunctions—the dissent part centered on whether § 1522 obligates the government to continue providing resettlement services once refugees have entered under an earlier regime.  Although her disagreement does not affect deference under § 1182(f), it highlights how even panels united on immigration authority can fracture over statutory interpretation elsewhere in a single case.

## Deference Under § 1182(f): From *Jordan* to *Hawaii*

The Ninth Circuit did not invent its approach; it sits atop a lineage dating back to *Jordan v. INS* (1979), where Chief Judge Woods warned against judicial second‑guessing when Congress gave “broad discretion” over alien admission categories.  In *Kleindienst v. Mandel* (1972), the Supreme Court articulated what later became known as “the doctrine of consular nonreviewability”: when an agency acts within statutory discretion, courts may only inquire whether the decision was “facially valid.”  

Laying out modern boundaries for immigration waivers and bans, *Trump v. Hawaii* affirmed that Section 1182(f)’s “presidential finding” clause is itself an expression of unreviewable discretion so long as there is no palpable constitutional violation or contravention of clear statutory commands.  The Ninth Circuit’s refusal here mirrors Justice Thomas’s plurality assertion that courts should avoid wading into policy justifications — especially when national security or foreign policy is invoked — unless Congress has foreclosed discretion altogether.

Thus *Pacito* reaffirms another layer: when evaluating an emergency stay, lower courts may treat compliance with § 1182(f)’s procedural trigger as a matter-of-fact conclusion rather than a substantive gateway subject to evidentiary testing.

## The Search for a “Detriment” Finding

The key substantive word is “detrimental.”  The statute gives no definition; historically agencies have interpreted it broadly—from economic impacts to threats against public safety—and presidents have used it as shorthand for foreign‑policy concerns ranging from terrorism financing to diplomatic leverage.

In *Hawaii*, Chief Justice Roberts warned against treating every presidential assertion as automatically valid but concluded that conspiracy arguments breach constitutional rights only if they target protected classes or involve explicit animus—a point not raised here because refugees are statutorily authorized only via congressional appropriation cuts rather than categorical discrimination based on nationality or religion.  

By contrast, *Pacito*’s language appears even more categorical: “The President has undoubtedly fulfilled…the requirement.”  No request was made for evidence showing why refugees constitute a detriment—no intelligence reports or economic analyses were introduced into the record before stay deliberations began—yet this absence was dismissed as irrelevant under deference doctrine.

Critics argue this brandishes an ultra‑large brushstroke over what should be at least a minimal evidentiary showing—perhaps akin more closely with traditional administrative law standards where agencies must demonstrate substantial evidence supporting factual findings (“*Motor Vehicle Mfrs.* v.*United Auto Workers*,” **467 U.S.* 822**, 1990).  

If Supreme Court justices view this pronouncement as throttling judicial review beyond historic bounds, they could reframe how far deference extends when an agency’s policy implicates statutory rights vested in identifiable individuals (here, refugees already approved for admission).

## Separation‑of‑Powers Concerns

At stake is more than interpretive style—it is interbranch equilibrium. Congress enacts refugee caps and funding formulas precisely because it wishes some say over how many people seek asylum or resettlement assistance preserves domestic priorities while honoring humanitarian commitments embedded in international oblig­ations (*e.g.,* ARSLA). By allowing an unfettered presidential find without any evidentiary floor, legislators indirectly cede oversight power each time they rely upon §§ 1151–1155 authorizing refugee admissions annually through appropriations acts instead of policy directives alone.

Moreover, although Section 1522 imposes grant conditions once appropriations are made (“the Director shall …”), Judge Lee’s dissent points out there is no statutory command mandating those services at all—the lower courts already implied otherwise by ordering reinstatement prior to any final adjudication about funding adequacy or agency capability today.

If future administrations were able merely to issue an executive memorandum announcing—as did Trump's EO—a “temporary suspension,” then supporters could argue such action merely invokes Section 215(b)(1)(A) solidifying executive control over nonimmigrant visas without needing Congressional appropriations beyond existing levels—a potent tool foreshadowed by past travel bans and now extended explicitly toward refugee vouchers and aid contracts.
 
## Do We Have A Limit?

Two doctrinal lines suggest possible curbs:

1️⃣ **Statutory Exhaustion** – Congress explicitly enumerates certain pathways for restricting refugee admissions: annual numerical caps set by Congress (Section 1151(a)(1)), security vetting provisions under §§ 1154–1157(b), and discretionary suspensions hinging on findings tantamount to national security threats (“detrimental”). While these provisions grant latitude, they do not preclude Congress from imposing additional procedural safeguards—e.g., requiring a written intelligence memorandum demonstrating specific harm rather than relying only on an executive claim.
   
2️⃣ **Constitutional Checks** – Even robust deference cannot shield actions violating obviously unconstitutional gender discrimination or religious animus per *Reno v.*‌ Ashcroft (*549 U.S.* 926* analysis). Though plaintiffs haven’t alleged such animus here; preserved precedent indicates courts retain jurisdictional leverage when classification touches suspect classifications—even though refugees are once‐outside US nationals not traditionally covered by Equal Protection analysis—courts have sometimes applied rational‑basis scrutiny (*Kleindienst*, **335 U.S.* 125**).

Thus while *Pacito* doesn’t overturn any precedent outright—a full merits opinion remains pending—it does stretch existing deference quite far toward presumptive acceptance of presidential findings absent any evidentiary foundation beyond assertion alone.


## Implications for Future Refugee Admissions

If Supreme Court declines review and upholds this approach:

- **Policy volatility will accelerate** – Presidents could respond swiftly (via EO) whenever geopolitical developments render particular source countries “detrimental,” without prior consultation with Congress or filing detailed memoranda.
  
- **Legislative countermeasures will intensify** – We can expect bipartisan bills attempting either (a) amending §§ 1151–1155 to require congressional notification before suspending refugee programs above annually set caps or (b) adding an express delineation defining “‘detrimental’” limitations aimed at reasserting substantive oversight.
  
- **Litigation strategy shifts** – Plaintiffs will likely focus next‑level attacks at almost every subsequent step—for example challenging delayed implementation periods (*Hawaii* linchpin was indefinite duration harming certain nationals); contesting whether emergency suspensions exceed temporal limits set by ISA tradition; demanding concrete evidence through discovery before any stay can be freely granted.
   
- **Agency behavior adapts** – Agencies such as State Department and USCIS may preemptively prepare fact sheets substantiating detriment claims precisely because those documents now constitute crucial “record” material if challenged procedurally at appellate level rather than through substantive merits review.
   
- **Non‑refugee categories feel ripple effects** – The same logic might be deployed against complementarily protected groups — e.g., asylum seekers (+nonrefugee parolees)—by citing broad national security “detriment,” prompting new waves of litigation over whether section 212(a)(3)(A)’s scope permits comparable sweeping suspensions absent concrete evidence.


## Concluding Perspective

The Ninth Circuit’s succinct pronouncement—that “[t]he President has undoubtedly fulfilled …the ‘find’ requirement”—distills what some view as an inevitable trajectory for immigration law since Hawai‘i: once Congress hands down a bare procedural trigger (“find detrimental”), courts will rarely probe deeper unless faced with unmistakable constitutional infirmities or egregious statutory contradictions.
  
Yet this very abstraction risks eroding meaningful checks on executive power at a time when refuge matters intersect imminently with America’s global leadership role and domestic political polarization over immigration influxes.
  
The real battle now lies ahead—in whatever eventual merits opinion emerges from *Pacito*, and in subsequent appellate steps whether this model stands up before an ever‑more fragmented federal judiciary—or whether it becomes another footnote prompting Congress recoil by tightening section 1182(f)’s vague criterion into something more ascertainable.
  
Until then scholars should watch how this decision dovetails with parallel disputes over Section 1522 funding mandates (a point fiercely argued by Judge Lee) and prepare arguments urging our highest court either reaffirm limited rational‑basis review consistent with longstanding precedents—or reinstate a modest evidentiary gate ensuring presidents articulate—not merely assert—a credible basis whenever they decide who may cross our borders next.
