# The President’s Pen Is Mightier Than Ever? Deference to Executive Power Under § 1182(f) After *Pacito v. Trump*

## Why *Pacito* Matters

The September 2025 Ninth Circuit order in *Pacito v. Trump* placed a fresh spotlight on one of the most sweeping grants of authority in U.S. immigration law—8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which lets a president suspend the entry of any class of aliens when he finds it “detrimental to the interests of the United States.”  The panel’s explicit statement that courts may not conduct a “searching inquiry” into the president’s justification revives—and arguably expands—the robust deference doctrine articulated in *Trump v. Hawaii*.  Because refugee admissions are both a humanitarian program established by Congress and an instrument of foreign‑policy discretion, any shift in the judicial posture toward § 1182(f) reverberates through invasion‑policy debates, congressional oversight battles, and future attempts to curtail not just refugees but other categories of non‑citizens.

## The Ninth Circuit’s Reasoning

Faced with emergency appeals from both sides, the panel first applied the well‑settled four‑factor stay test from *Nken v. Holder*.  It found the government “likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge” to *the validity* of Executive Order 14163—the president’s blanket moratorium on refugee admissions—on two grounds.  First, it noted that the statutory text requires only a presidential “find” that entry would be detrimental; second, it emphasized that determining whether such a finding satisfies Congress is a matter for the political branches, not for courts engaged in an interlocutory stay.  Citing *Trump v. Hawaii*, the opinion declared:

> “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’…the entry…would be detrimental…The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.”

By framing the statutory threshold as purely formal—an unfettered factual determination—the majority effectively insulated Executive Order 14163 from substantive judicial scrutiny at this stage.  It reasoned further that even if plaintiffs could show an abuse of discretion under an APA challenge concerning funding cuts (and indeed the court ordered reinstatement of certain cooperative agreements), such concerns did not intrude upon the separate question of whether § 1182(f) gave the president authority to halt refugee admissions altogether.

Judge Lee’s partial concurrence underscored this line divide within the panel.  While she agreed that § 1182(f) grants unambiguous power to suspend admissions—and therefore she would stay those injunctions—the dissent part centered on whether § 1522 obligates the government to continue providing resettlement services once refugees have entered under an earlier regime.  Although her disagreement does not affect deference under § 1182(f), it highlights how even panels united on immigration authority can fracture over statutory interpretation elsewhere in a single case.

## Deference Under § 1182(f): From *Jordan* to *Hawaii*

The Ninth Circuit did not invent its approach; it sits atop a lineage dating back to *Jordan v. INS* (1979), where Chief Judge Woods warned against judicial second‑guessing when Congress gave “broad discretion” over alien admission categories.  In *Kleindienst v. Mandel* (1972), the Supreme Court articulated what later became known as “the doctrine of consular nonreviewability”: when an agency acts within statutory discretion, courts may only inquire whether the decision was “facially valid.”  

Laying out modern boundaries for immigration waivers and bans, *Trump v. Hawaii* affirmed that Section 1182(f)’s “presidential finding” clause is itself an expression of unreviewable discretion so long as there is no palpable constitutional violation or contravention of clear statutory commands.  The Ninth Circuit’s refusal here mirrors Justice Thomas’s plurality assertion that courts should avoid wading into policy justifications — especially when national security or foreign policy is invoked — unless Congress has foreclosed discretion altogether.

Thus *Pacito* reaffirms another layer: when evaluating an emergency stay, lower courts may treat compliance with § 1182(f)’s procedural trigger as a matter-of-fact conclusion rather than a substantive gateway subject to evidentiary testing.

## The Search for a “Detriment” Finding

The key substantive word is “detrimental.”  The statute gives no definition; historically agencies have interpreted it broadly—from economic impacts to threats against public safety—and presidents have used it as shorthand for foreign‑policy concerns ranging from terrorism financing to diplomatic leverage.

In *Hawaii*, Chief Justice Roberts warned against treating every presidential assertion as automatically valid but concluded that conspiracy arguments breach constitutional rights only if they target protected classes or involve explicit animus—a point not raised here because refugees are statutorily authorized only via congressional appropriation cuts rather than categorical discrimination based on nationality or religion.  

By contrast, *Pacito*’s language appears even more categorical: “The President has undoubtedly fulfilled…the requirement.”  No request was made for evidence showing why refugees constitute a detriment—no intelligence reports or economic analyses were introduced into the record before stay deliberations began—yet this absence was dismissed as irrelevant under deference doctrine.

Critics argue this brandishes an ultra‑large brushstroke over what should be at least a minimal evidentiary showing—perhaps akin more closely with traditional administrative law standards where agencies must demonstrate substantial evidence supporting factual findings (“*Motor Vehicle Mfrs.* v.*United Auto Workers*,” **467 U.S.* 822**, 1990).  

If Supreme Court justices view this pronouncement as throttling judicial review beyond historic bounds, they could reframe how far deference extends when an agency’s policy implicates statutory rights vested in identifiable individuals (here, refugees already approved for admission).

## Separation‑of‑Powers Concerns

At stake is more than interpretive style—it is interbranch equilibrium. Congress enacts refugee caps and funding formulas precisely because it wishes some say over how many people seek asylum or resettlement assistance preserves domestic priorities while honoring humanitarian commitments embedded in international oblig­ations (*e.g.,* ARSLA). By allowing an unfettered presidential find without any evidentiary floor, legislators indirectly cede oversight power each time they rely upon §§ 1151–1155 authorizing refugee admissions annually through appropriations acts instead of policy directives alone.

Moreover, although Section 1522 imposes grant conditions once appropriations are made (“the Director shall …”), Judge Lee’s dissent points out there is no statutory command mandating those services at all—the lower courts already implied otherwise by ordering reinstatement prior to any final adjudication about funding adequacy or agency capability today.

If future administrations were able merely to issue an executive memorandum announcing—as did Trump's EO—a “temporary suspension,” then supporters could argue such action merely invokes Section 215(b)(1)(A) solidifying executive control over nonimmigrant visas without needing Congressional appropriations beyond existing levels—a potent tool foreshadowed by past travel bans and now extended explicitly toward refugee vouchers and aid contracts.
 
## Do We Have A Limit?

Two doctrinal lines suggest possible curbs:

1️⃣ **Statutory Exhaustion** – Congress explicitly enumerates certain pathways for restricting refugee admissions: annual numerical caps set by Congress (Section 1151(a)(1)), security vetting provisions under §§ 1154–1157(b), and discretionary suspensions hinging on findings tantamount to national security threats (“detrimental”). While these provisions grant latitude, they do not preclude Congress from imposing additional procedural safeguards—e.g., requiring a written intelligence memorandum demonstrating specific harm rather than relying only on an executive claim.
   
2️⃣ **Constitutional Checks** – Even robust deference cannot shield actions violating obviously unconstitutional gender discrimination or religious animus per *Reno v.*‌ Ashcroft (*549 U.S.* 926* analysis). Though plaintiffs haven’t alleged such animus here; preserved precedent indicates courts retain jurisdictional leverage when classification touches suspect classifications—even though refugees are once‐outside US nationals not traditionally covered by Equal Protection analysis—courts have sometimes applied rational‑basis scrutiny (*Kleindienst*, **335 U.S.* 125**).

Thus while *Pacito* doesn’t overturn any precedent outright—a full merits opinion remains pending—it does stretch existing deference quite far toward presumptive acceptance of presidential findings absent any evidentiary foundation beyond assertion alone.


## Implications for Future Refugee Admissions

If Supreme Court declines review and upholds this approach:

- **Policy volatility will accelerate** – Presidents could respond swiftly (via EO) whenever geopolitical developments render particular source countries “detrimental,” without prior consultation with Congress or filing detailed memoranda.
  
- **Legislative countermeasures will intensify** – We can expect bipartisan bills attempting either (a) amending §§ 1151–1155 to require congressional notification before suspending refugee programs above annually set caps or (b) adding an express delineation defining “‘detrimental’” limitations aimed at reasserting substantive oversight.
  
- **Litigation strategy shifts** – Plaintiffs will likely focus next‑level attacks at almost every subsequent step—for example challenging delayed implementation periods (*Hawaii* linchpin was indefinite duration harming certain nationals); contesting whether emergency suspensions exceed temporal limits set by ISA tradition; demanding concrete evidence through discovery before any stay can be freely granted.
   
- **Agency behavior adapts** – Agencies such as State Department and USCIS may preemptively prepare fact sheets substantiating detriment claims precisely because those documents now constitute crucial “record” material if challenged procedurally at appellate level rather than through substantive merits review.
   
- **Non‑refugee categories feel ripple effects** – The same logic might be deployed against complementarily protected groups — e.g., asylum seekers (+nonrefugee parolees)—by citing broad national security “detriment,” prompting new waves of litigation over whether section 212(a)(3)(A)’s scope permits comparable sweeping suspensions absent concrete evidence.


## Concluding Perspective

The Ninth Circuit’s succinct pronouncement—that “[t]he President has undoubtedly fulfilled …the ‘find’ requirement”—distills what some view as an inevitable trajectory for immigration law since Hawai‘i: once Congress hands down a bare procedural trigger (“find detrimental”), courts will rarely probe deeper unless faced with unmistakable constitutional infirmities or egregious statutory contradictions.
  
Yet this very abstraction risks eroding meaningful checks on executive power at a time when refuge matters intersect imminently with America’s global leadership role and domestic political polarization over immigration influxes.
  
The real battle now lies ahead—in whatever eventual merits opinion emerges from *Pacito*, and in subsequent appellate steps whether this model stands up before an ever‑more fragmented federal judiciary—or whether it becomes another footnote prompting Congress recoil by tightening section 1182(f)’s vague criterion into something more ascertainable.
  
Until then scholars should watch how this decision dovetails with parallel disputes over Section 1522 funding mandates (a point fiercely argued by Judge Lee) and prepare arguments urging our highest court either reaffirm limited rational‑basis review consistent with longstanding precedents—or reinstate a modest evidentiary gate ensuring presidents articulate—not merely assert—a credible basis whenever they decide who may cross our borders next.

# Mandate or Permission? Decoding Section 1522’s Statutory Language in the Refugee Resettlement Dispute  

*By [Your Name]*  

## Why this case matters  

The Ninth Circuit’s interim order in *Pacito v. Trump* has thrust a decades‑old question onto the national stage: does Congress obligate the executive branch to fund refugee “reception and placement” services, or does it merely give agencies permission to do so when funds are available?  The answer will determine whether a President can simultaneously halt refugee admissions while allowing the government to shirk its statutory responsibilities toward refugees already on U.S. soil.  Because the Refugee Act of 1980 is the statutory backbone of America’s humanitarian‑refugee system, any reinterpretation of its funding provisions reverberates far beyond Washington state courts—affecting future appropriations battles, agency discretion under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), and even the structure of other “authorizing” statutes that devote limited appropriations language to charitable programs.  

## The statutory text at issue  

At the heart of *Pacito* lies 8 U.S.C. § 1522, a clause that sits inside the broader Refugee Act framework.  The section is divided into subparts that use both “shall” and “is authorized.”  For example:

* “The Director… **shall** make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement…**to the extent of available appropriations**” (§ 1522(a)(1)(A)).  
* “The Director **is authorized** to provide cash assistance…**to the extent of available appropriations**” (§ 1522(e)(7)(A)).  

The conflicting verbs have prompted divergent readings.  The majority panel of the Ninth Circuit interpreted § 1522 as imposing a **mandatory** duty: because Congress pairs “shall” with concrete programmatic requirements (training, placement, cash assistance), it cannot be reduced to a mere permission.  Accordingly, the panel ordered the State Department to reinstate cooperative agreements that furnish these services—even while it affirmed presidential deference under § 1182(f) for suspending new admissions.

Judge Lee’s partial dissent challenges that view.  He emphasizes that § 1522’s very title – “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees” – signals congressional intent toward permissiveness rather than compulsion.  Lee argues that “authorization” is defined at Black’s Law Dictionary as “permission,” and each operative subsection likewise uses “is authorized,” not “shall” (except where “shall” merely qualifies conditions attached to discretionary spending).  

The textual dispute thus hinges on whether “shall” creates an independent command or simply serves as a conditional clause attached to an underlying authorization.

## Interpretive frameworks: Chevron versus Skidmore  

When agency officials claim discretion over statutory spending decisions—especially where Congress declares a program “authorized”—courts typically apply one of two interpretive doctrines.  

Under **Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council**, courts first ask whether Congress has spoken directly on an issue; if not, they defer to an agency’s reasonable construction so long as it is not contrary to congressional intent.  The framework presumes that statutes are often ambiguous by design and delegates resolution to agencies possessing expertise—in this case, HHS (the Office of Refugee Resettlement) and State Department officials who administer USRAP contracts.

By contrast, **Skidmore v. Swift Corp.** offers a more nuanced approach when Chevron deference is unavailable—for instance when a statute presents an impermissibly vague command or when an agency interprets language outside its core expertise (such as whether an authorization imposes an enforceable duty).  Under Skidmore, courts give weight to an agency’s interpretation according to its persuasiveness rather than automatically deferring.

Judge Lee implicitly nudges toward a Skidmore‑type analysis: because § 1522 mixes permissive language (“is authorized”) with conditional “shall” clauses tied closely to funding availability (“to the extent of available appropriations”), he suggests that any mandatory reading should be grounded in clear congressional intent—not simply inferred from ordinary grammatical usage.

The Ninth Circuit’s majority appears willing to apply Chevron deference in favor of HHS’ longstanding practice of providing reception‑and‑placement services via contracts—a practice long alleged by Congress through appropriations bills rather than via explicit mandatory language.  By treating § 1522 as creating enforceable obligations regardless of budget cuts (notably after an $800 million reduction under the Rescissions Act), the panel effectively elevates HHS’s interpretive stance into law without subjecting it again to scrutiny under Skidmore principles.

## What Judge Lee says—and why his reading matters  

Judge Lee’s dissent pivots on three observations:

1. **Title signals permissiveness** – Section headings are often indicative of congressional intent; here “Authorization …” sets expectations that Congress provided space for agency action but did not compel it.
   
2. **Mixed verbage reflects conditional authority** – While subsections contain “shall,” those mandates are bounded by *“to the extent of available appropriations,”* suggesting that if Congress fails to appropriate funds (as occurred with FY 2025), there is no duty triggered.
   
3. **Legislative history backs discretionary funding** – Lee notes past appropriations reports where Congress repeatedly urged agencies “to seek innovative partnerships” and framed receiving services as optional pilots rather than mandatory entitlements.

If Lee is correct, then requiring agencies to fund reception‑and‑placement services despite appropriation cuts would effectively create a **de facto appropriation**, contravening Article I’s power of the purse by converting an authorization into a binding spending mandate without explicit congressional vote.

Beyond *Pacito*, such reasoning could reshape numerous statutes written with similar dual language—disaster relief authority under FEMA, health assistance under P.L. 104‑208 (the Hill‑Burton Act), or child welfare grants under Title IV-E—all of which pair permissive authorizations with conditional “shall” requirements contingent upon available funding.

## Doctrinal ripple effects  

### Congressional drafting practices

If courts follow Lee’s guidance, legislatures may be prompted either (a) to draft more precise statutes when they genuinely intend compulsory spending—using clear language like “the Secretary shall allocate X dollars each fiscal year”—or (b) explicitly declare certain programs as non‑mandatory so agencies can adjust rapidly during budget shortfalls without fear of judicial injunctions.

### Agency risk management

Agencies currently operating under presumptions of mandatory service provision may reassess their contractual arrangements during fiscal volatility. In *Pacito*, had Lee’s view prevailed fully, the State Department could have left cooperative agreements terminated without violating any statutory duty—a relief for agencies grappling with sudden funding curtailments.

### Judicial scrutiny under APA

The Ninth Circuit already signaled skepticism toward plaintiffs’ APA claims concerning defunding procedures—suggesting possible uncertainties around notice-and-comment requirements applicable when agencies rescind contracts without formal rulemaking. If § 1522 is read as non‑mandatory, agencies can argue that rescinding contracts does not constitute an ‘agency action’ subject to substantive APA review; consequently future challenges will focus squarely on statutory interpretation rather than procedural propriety.

### Broader immigration policy

By separating authority over admissions (unquestionably vested in § 1182(f)) from obligations once admission occurs (§ 1522), courts might enable future Presidents—even those hostile toward refugee admissions—to suspend new entries while nevertheless preserving existing support structures for those already in limbo. Conversely, a strict mandatory reading would force administrations either to fund resettlement despite reduced intake or risk judicial orders mandating service provision even absent new refugees—a tension likely exploited by both advocates and opponents alike.

## Where we go from here  

The full merits opinion remains pending, but Judge Lee’s dissent crystallizes a structural fault line within immigration law: does congressional compromise embed non‑negotiable duties within ostensibly permissive authorizations?  Should courts lean heavily on textual grammar (“shall”), or ought they heed legislative labeling (“authorization”) and contextual cues about appropriations?

Given recent trends toward aggressive executive control over entry restrictions—championed by *Trump v. Hawaii*’s wide‐ranging deference—the resolution of § 1522 will serve as an essential counterbalance if it imposes any substantive check on presidential discretion after admission has occurred. A majority endorsement would affirm procedural protections for refugees already accepted into America; a dissent‑driven reversal would grant administrations near‑total latitude over both intake and aftercare provided funding dries up.

In any event, practitioners should watch closely how lower courts apply Chevron versus Skidmore analyses in contexts where statutory authorizations intertwine with conditional “shall” commands—especially where those conditions intersect contested budgetary realities.

## Conclusion  

*Pacito* presents more than a tug‑of‑war over refugee admissions; it spotlights how precise word choice can expand—or contract—the reach of federal obligations during politically charged moments. As Judge Lee reminds us, words matter: an “authorization” can be read either as permission or as implied command depending upon grammatical scaffolding and legislative purpose both celebrated in statute construction tools dating back centuries yet brought freshly alive by today’s partisan politics.

Whether practitioners will ultimately rely on *Granting agencies breathing room* or *Enforcing congressional mandates* will hinge upon how this Ninth Circuit panel adjudicates § 1522 after hearing full arguments—and consequently how other courts resolve similar authorizing statutes across our nation’s humanitarian programs.

# Defunding Refugees Without a Rule? How the APA Shapes Challenges to Executive Suspension of Resettlement Funding  

## Why Pacito v. Trump Matters  

When President Trump ordered a blanket suspension of refugee admissions in early 2025 and simultaneously defunded the very agencies that assist newly arrived refugees, he set off a constitutional‑statutory clash that reaches far beyond a handful of nonprofits in Seattle. The case tests two seemingly opposite doctrines that have long guided immigration litigation: the near‑absolute deference accorded to the President’s “detrimental‑to‑the‑interests” finding under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), and the rigorous procedural safeguards built into the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) for any agency action that alters an existing regulatory or contractual scheme.  

If the Ninth Circuit ultimately finds that the government “cannot engage in ‘a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications’” regarding the refugee moratorium—an approach rooted in *Trump v. Hawaii*—but does have to obey at least one APA principle when it ripped up cooperative agreements overnight, Pacito will become a touchstone for how courts balance immigration deference against procedural accountability in other “policy‑by‑budget” actions.

## The Procedural Backdrop  

The district court in Western Washington entered two preliminary injunctions in February 2025: one blocking enforcement of §§ 3(a)‑(c) and 4 of Executive Order 14163 (the refuge‑admission moratorium), and another ordering the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements it had terminated after those orders went into effect. The government appealed both injunctions and secured partial stays from the Ninth Circuit’s motions panel in March, April and May 2025—except for a narrow carve‑out protecting individuals who had already received conditional refugee approvals.

After months of briefing, plaintiffs asked the Ninth Circuit to vacate those stays while it considered its own appeal. In an interim order issued September 12 2025 (the document we’re analyzing), the panel stayed **both** district‑court injunctions **in their entirety**, **except** for one crucial command: it required the federal government to “reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services” under 8 U.S.C. § 1522.

The panel’s brief rationale rests on two key observations:  

1. **Immigration Deference Wins on Section 1182(f).** The judges affirmed that “the sole prerequisite … is that the President ‘find[]’ … would be detrimental,” citing *Hawaii*’s flat‑out deference language (“The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement”).  
2. **At Least One APA Claim Fails for the Government.** Because agency action under § 1522 carries mandatory statutory language (“shall”), the panel concluded that the abrupt termination of contracts without notice‑and‑comment rulemaking—or any semblance of an agency reasoned decision—might constitute an arbitrary or capricious violation of the APA.

Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent adds a layer of nuance by arguing that § 1522 is *authorization* rather than *mandate*, meaning no statutory duty exists to provide those services at all—a contention we’ll return to later.

## The APA Lens: Arbitrary‑and‑Capricious Review  

Under **APA § 706(2)(A)**, courts may set aside agency actions found “arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law.” The standard requires agencies to show they examined relevant data reasonably and articulated a rational connection between facts found and decisions made (*Motor Vehicle Mfrs.*). In Pacito:

- **No Notice‑and‑Comment Rulemaking:** The State Department’s sudden termination of all cooperative agreements—a fundamental shift in how federal refugee resettlement funding is allocated—did not involve any notice published in *the Federal Register* nor any opportunity for interested parties (the nonprofit resettlement organizations) to comment on alternative approaches or mitigation measures.
- **Absence of Agency Fact‑Finding:** The record shows only vague statements such as “we are actively preparing a request for proposals.” The agency offered no evidentiary basis linking contract terminations directly to its national‑interest justification under § 1182(f). That disconnect opens a classic arbitrary‑and‑capricious hole.
- **Procedural Requirements Under FAR:** Even if one argues § 1522 merely authorizes funding rather than obligates it, federal procurement rules still impose notice‐and‐competition duties when discharging contracts funded by appropriations (§ 5520(a)–(e), FAR Subpart 15). Abrupt contract cancellations without due process could violate both procurement statutes and ‘fair‐dealing’ principles underlying § 706(1).

Thus, while courts will defer heavily on *why* Congress gave the President leeway over admissions decisions—a realm squarely within executive discretion—the *how* matters when Congress also enacts mandatory service provisions (or at least authorizations interpreted as mandates). The Ninth Circuit appears prepared to enforce those procedural safeguards regardless of immigration deference.

## Interpreting 8 U.S.C.§ 1522: Authorization or Obligation?  

The divergence between Judges Lee and his colleagues centers on whether § 1522 erects an enforceable duty for “reception and placement services.”  

- **A Textualist View (Lee):** The statute’s heading (“Authorization for programs…”) signals permissiveness; every subsection uses “may/shall … if … available appropriations,” implying discretionary spending authority rather than an unqualified command to spend millions each fiscal year.
- **A Mandatory Reading (Panel Majority):** Although § 1522 begins with “to the extent of available appropriations,” it also mandates specific conditions (“shall make available sufficient resources…”) if funding occurs—these are conditions attached **to** an authorized program rather than optional actions left entirely to agency whim. Moreover, subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A) contain binding “shall” commands once assistance is decided upon for unaccompanied minors or recent arrivals.
  
Both readings have precedent support:

- In *Negusie v. Holder*, 555 U.S. 511 (2009), Justice Ginsburg noted that Congress intended “a general rule” for ongoing treatment—a phrasing akin to permanent obligations rather than fleeting authorizations.
- Conversely, courts have upheld bipartisan interpretations where “authorization” can be read as mere permission—as in *NLRB v*. Rogers*, 446 U.S., where certain statutory titles were deemed indicative but not determinative.

Pacito forces us closer: if courts adopt Lee’s permissive construction, agencies could simply close down existing service programs when they deem them politically undesirable—a stark inversion of congressional intent underlying Section 1522’s elaborate funding scheme ($3.178 billion appropriated before rescission). If they follow Lee’s colleagues’ mandatory construction, any abrupt funding cuts would trigger AP­A scrutiny irrespective of how much discretion Congress left them under Ok!

## Doctrinal Tension: Immigration Deference Meets Administrative Formalism  

The case crystallizes a growing tension between two doctrines:

1️⃣ **Deference Doctrine (Chevron/Casemix/Skidmore):** Usually invoked for statutory interpretation concerning immigration powers (`§1182(f)`), giving agencies—and particularly Presidents—a high ceiling (‘ample power’) once a ‘finding’ is made.

2️⃣ **Procedural Foundations Under APA/FAR:** Even when substantive policy is left unsupervised, agencies cannot ignore procedural requirements when changing existing frameworks tied directly to statutes containing `shall` language or significant appropriations.

If appellate courts accept that these doctrines operate in parallel—deference shields substantive "why" but not process—the implication is profound: future presidents may wield blunt instruments like blanket suspensions more freely so long as they retain full procedural rigor when tinkering with associated funding streams or contracts eyeing Congress-mandated outcomes.

## Potential Downstream Effects  

### 1️⃣ Litigating Funding Cuts Across Policy Domains  
Pacito signals that plaintiffs can survive an initial deference hurdle if they focus their challenge on procedural missteps—no notice-and-comment rulemaking—and on violating statutory `shall` provisions embedded elsewhere (e.g., housing assistance under HUD). We might see similar suits arise against abrupt cuts to veteran benefits (Veterans Affairs), pandemic relief programs (CDC), or climate grant rollbacks (EPA).

### 2️⃣ Heightened Scrutiny Over Cooperative Agreements

Once these mechanisms are used as leverage points for sweeping policy changes—as here—the judicial bar may rise regarding how quickly agencies can dissolve them without alternate processes completing first.

### 3️⃣ Congressional Checks & Appropriation Strategies  
Congress may respond by tightening language around spend-authorizations—from permissive “may” toward mandatory “shall,” tying receipt‐and–placement duties explicitly to appropriation bills rather than leaving them within agency discretion suffixes (`authorizes` vs `requires`). Alternatively—if lawmakers want more leeway—they might embed clearer sunset clauses permitting automatic termination upon presidential dire‐interest findings `with appropriate notice`.

Given its dual nature—a presidential immigration moratorium next door from*Trump v.* Hawai`i plus robust administrative law concerns—the case stands as prime material for SCOTUS if either side persists after final Ninth Circuit judgment (perhaps over whether § 1522 imposes enforceable spending obligations). # Stays, Injunctions, and National Interest: Navigating Judicial Review in High‑Stakes Immigration Cases  

## Why *Pacito v. Trump* matters  

The Ninth Circuit’s interim stay order in *Pacito v. Trump* offers a front‑row seat to how federal courts balance presidential power over immigration against congressional mandates and humanitarian concerns when time‑sensitive litigation erupts. At issue is a January 2025 executive order suspending refugee admissions under the United States Refugee Admissions Program (“USRAP”) and an accompanying cut‑off of federal funding for resettlement services. A district court placed an immediate injunction on both the suspension and the termination of cooperative agreements that deliver critical placement assistance to newly admitted refugees. The appellate panel’s decision—to lift the injunctions concerning admissions while ordering reinstatement of funding contracts—hinges largely on the four‑factor test from *Nken v. Holder* (2009). Because stays determine which policies operate while appeals unfold, the reasoning applied here foreshadows how future challenges to swift executive immigration actions will be handled both procedurally and substantively.  

## The Nken framework recast by the Ninth Circuit  

### Likelihood of success on the merits  

The panel’s first factor leans heavily on precedent granting broad deference to presidential authority under **8 U.S.C.§ 1182(f)**. Citing *Trump v. Hawaii* (2018), it observes that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” and “vests [the President] with ample power” to suspend entry whenever he finds it “detrimental to the interests of the United States.” The order explicitly notes that “the sole prerequisite … is that the President ‘find[]’ … would be detrimental,” adding that “the President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement.” By treating the statutory language as self‑executing—requiring only a finding, no evidentiary record—the court signals its expectation that challengers will struggle to demonstrate inadequacy in that finding. Consequently, on that front alone it believes “the government is likely to prevail” and therefore satisfies the first Nken prong quite comfortably.

Judge Lee’s partial dissent underscores how tight this analysis can become when a different statutory provision enters play. He agrees with the majority that § 1182(f) bestows ample discretion over admissions but insists that **8 U.S.C.§ 1522** does **not** impose an enforceable duty upon the Executive Branch—to continue providing reception‑and‑placement services once funding contracts are terminated. While his view does not sway the majority’s assessment concerning admissions, it highlights an alternate avenue where plaintiffs could seek success: arguing a mandatory statutory duty independent of presidential discretion.

### Irreparable injury  

Having deemed success likely on admission authority, the panel next examined whether either party would suffer an injury incapable of adequate remediation later—a classic sketch of irreparability tailored here toward national interests rather than individual hardship alone. By leaving in place an entry ban before appellate review concludes, the government contends it would endure ongoing damage to what it describes as “domestic and foreign interests” essential to public safety—a point emphasized when the order declares those remaining factors “favor…the United States.” Although refugees themselves face prolonged uncertainty absent any resettlement pathway—a serious personal harm—the majority appears convinced that any such injury is outweighed by what it characterizes as unique governmental exposure resulting from premature implementation or reversal of a presidentially sanctioned suspension. 

Judge Lee again diverges here because he sees no comparable impermissible harm arising from maintaining service contracts pending resolution; he argues any disruption would violate nothing more than discretionary policy choices rather than concrete statutory rights requiring immediate protection through a stay extension.



### Injury to non‑parties  

The third factor forces courts to weigh collateral consequences beyond litigants—to ask whether parties standing aside from this dispute are unexpectedly disadvantaged by granting—or denying—a stay. In *Pacito*, non‑parties include both refugees awaiting placement abroad and domestic NGOs whose cooperation agreements were abruptly terminated after February 2025.  
The majority concludes these parties experience limited injury relative either to broader national security considerations or potential congressional oversight mechanisms already built into Section 1522’s reporting duties (“a detailed report…not later than January 31”). In effect it finds any temporary loss of services does not rise above a threshold requiring halting presidential action over entry suspensions—a judgment premised heavily upon deference rather than an empirical calculus about displaced persons’ welfare.
   
Conversely, Lee’s dissent highlights precisely what he perceives as an overlooked injury: removal of cooperative agreements threatens existing contractual expectations without clear statutory compulsion for continued assistance absent appropriation authority. By insisting Section 1522 merely authorizes—not mandates—grant-making decisions, his position frames prospective contract terminations as potentially unlawful—but only insofar as they contravene administrative procedures prescribed by the APA rather than causing direct harms enforceable through injunctive relief.


### Public interest   

Public policy considerations win out decisively during crises where immigration intersects with perceived threats abroad. In emphasizing “broad discretion” granted by § 1182(f), the panel takes counsel from longstanding plenary‑power doctrine which holds foreign affairs—including admission decisions—to lie squarely within exclusive executive territory unless Congress unequivocally imposes limits (“whereon all political branches share…a common concern”). When measuring public interest against humanitarian imperatives enumerated in Congress’s Refugee Act (1980), it sides ultimately with maintaining governmental flexibility amid evolving geopolitical conditions—a stance consistent with *Hawaii*’s assertion that courts should eschew probing deep into presidential rationales absent evidence suggesting constitutional infirmity.

Judge Lee cautions against letting abstract notions of national interest drown out concrete statutory language governing resettlement assistance yet concedes his reading still supports allowing total regulatory suspension pending further clarification—“I thus respectfully dissent in part.” His separate standpoint hints at an alternate doctrinal balance wherein public interest could equally sanction continuing support services because Congress expressly allocated funds for them regardless of entry moratoria.


## Balancing national security against humanitarian responsibilities  

The *Pacito* dispute encapsulates a recurrent tension: Presidents routinely cite national‑security rationales when curtailing admission avenues—as Obama did with Parts 1a/3–5 revisions after Hurricane Katrina—and courts often defer absent demonstrable constitutional violations threatening fundamental rights (e.g., due process). Yet reflexive deference obscures whether filters embedded in statutes such as §§ 1521–1523 anticipating holistic refugee support remain operative during moratoria periods where admissions pause yet ongoing assistance duties linger for already admitted individuals.

By parsing § 1182(f)’s minimal hurdle—merely a presidential determination—the Ninth Circuit effectively draws two concentric circles around refugee regulation: outwardly sweeping sovereign immunity over entrance controls; inwardly limiting compulsory service provisions only where legislation unmistakably commands expenditures irrespective of intentional appropriations decisions (“shall …to the extent of available appropriations”). This bifurcated approach—that admission bans spark little judicial resistance while service funding defeats executives must satisfy ordinary administrative standards—creates practical consequences beyond doctrinal elegance:
1️⃣ Agencies now possess wide latitude simply citing “detriment” without elaborating evidence;
2️⃣ But once they act upon statutes bearing operative language (“shall”) relating directly to resources already appropriated—especially donations through cooperative agreements—the judiciary retains robust oversight power under the APA’s arbitrary‑and‑capricious bar.


The practical upshot is each new immigration emergency threatens different corners of federal law simultaneously—a fact litigants will press strategically depending upon which wedge offers them strongest appeal against entrenched deference.


## Expedited briefing schedules & procedural punch  

Another notable aspect lies in how swiftly both sides navigated motions practice. After initial district rulings were issued in late February–early March 2025, appeals were filed within days; panels heard arguments†on September 3 following abbreviated briefing deadlines set months earlier (§ rise). Such accelerated timetables underline appellate courts’ willingness—or necessity—to act quickly when policy changes have immediate life‑affecting repercussions across borders.
  
From a procedural point-of-view these fast tracks amplify incentives for governments aiming either at preserving policy continuity or seeking rapid reversal after emergent circumstances fade outrun slower district responses:
* **Reduced opportunity** for oppositional parties means reliance upon concise record‑based arguments rather than extensive discovery;
* **Heightened importance** attached now lies upon persuasive legal framing—as seen where plaintiffs emphasized literal contradictions between EO suspensions and explicit Refugee Act provisions;
* **Precedent formation risk increase**, because judges issuing short‑order stays quickly craft middle‑ground rulings lacking full substantive development—their wording anticipates eventual meritorious resolves while simultaneously carving interim obligations (as with reinstating contracts).

For litigants devising future strategies moments akin steps warrant anticipating brief period standstill plans—all­the‑while preserving bright‑line preservation routes should their cases survive prolonged appellate review.


## Doctrinal ripples beyond *Pacito*  

The panel’s logic reserves longstanding Deference doctrine for admission questions yet subjects ancillary programmatic components grounded in appropriation statutes tangibly directed at vulnerable populations toward ordinary administrative scrutiny (§ 1522). That bifurcation could nurture two emerging lines:
* **Immigration‑entry jurisprudence** solidifies its foundation upon narrow premises fortified through EU-style “detriment” findings—a low barrier making wholesale bans harder contestable outside constitutional channels;
* **Refugee-resettlement liability** gains clarity regarding when agencies must honor existing contracts after abrupt policy turns—it suggests contractual forks exist unless unequivocally foreclosed via explicit congressional command—not merely through budgetary rescission alone.


Should similar disputes surface—for instance challenges contesting post‑presidential regulatory rollback affecting asylum adjudication structures—courts may replicate this “dual prong” methodology dampening objections wherever clear congressional intent arises regarding outlays.


Potential legislative reaction looms obvious too: Congress could clarify whether § 1522 creates mandatory services independent from appropriations language—or insert language akin `"shall provide... notwithstanding future suspensions"` thereby insulating crucial assistance from ad hoc shutdowns.
