## Why **Cohen v. Consilio** matters  

When a dispute over overtime collapses into a tidy “all wages paid” stipulation, it can feel like everybody’s winner: employees get their back‑pay, employers avoid protracted discovery, and the docket clears quickly. Yet the October 2025 decision from the Eighth Circuit shows how such clauses can also create hidden traps—particularly when statutory penalties and injunctive relief remain on the table.  

Bruce Cohen’s case is a cautionary tale for anyone drafting—or signing—settlement‑type stipulations in wage‑class actions. By agreeing that he had been fully compensated for overtime wages and liquidated damages, Cohen removed any factual dispute about those elements and handed the district court a clean path to summary judgment on his core wage claims. At the same time, his agreement said nothing about civil penalties under Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), its Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), or its Wage Theft Act (MWTA), nor about an injunction rescinding Consilio’s “premium overtime” policy. Those unresolved strands forced the appellate court into a more nuanced analysis—vacating part of the district court’s ruling on grounds of jurisdiction and standing.

The decision underscores three practical lessons: (1) stipulations that obligate an employer to pay “all wages” may unintentionally waive penalty claims; (2) employees cannot rely on an unstated reservation of injunctive relief once they’ve reshaped their complaint around a payment agreement; and (3) courts will scrutinize whether a plaintiff retains a live controversy when it seeks civil penalties—even when those penalties flow to a state agency rather than directly to the employee.

---

## The court’s reasoning on summary judgment and stipulations  

The Eighth Circuit began its review where every appellate analysis starts: Did any genuine issue of material fact survive? Under *Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle*, summary judgment is proper when the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law after viewing evidence in the light most favorable to the non‑moving party.  

Because Cohen and Consilio jointly stipulated—by express contract—that “Cohen has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed” and “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages,” there was no factual dispute over those elements. The district court therefore correctly entered summary judgment on Cohen’s wage‑and‑liquidated‑damages claims under both Minnesota law and the MFLSA.  

The controversy persisted only where the parties’ written agreement was silent: civil penalties under §§ 181.101 (MPWA), § 177.25 (MFLSA), and §§ 181.032/181.171 (MWTA); and an injunction compelling Consilio to rescind its overtime policy. The appellate panel held that Minnesota’s MPWA expressly limits penalty enforcement to the Commissioner—a point reinforced by *Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.* The language “the ability to enforce a penalty is limited to the [Commissioner]” left no room for an employee‑directed claim, so the district court’s grant of summary judgment stood.

In contrast, the MFLSA portion raised novel standing questions. While statutes allow civil penalties payable **to** the Commissioner, *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw* permits citizen plaintiffs to pursue such sanctions when they serve a remedial purpose—deterring ongoing violations—and when plaintiffs demonstrate an injury traceable to those violations. The Eighth Circuit concluded it could not determine jurisdiction over Cohen’s MFLSA penalty claim without first deciding whether Cohen retained standing after Consilio purportedly ceased its offending practice—a factual determination better suited for trial. Consequently, it vacated that portion of the judgment and remanded for further proceedings.

The MWTA claim was dismissed outright because Cohen offered no legal argument beyond his broad stipulation that he had been fully paid—a classic case where “no meaningful argument” equals waiver, per *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*.

Finally, regarding injunctive relief, Cohen’s shifting pleading strategy left his request unripe for appellate review. He moved from seeking “specific forms” of injunction at filing time to asking merely that Consilio withdraw its policy at briefing—a procedural gap insufficient for appellate review under *InfoDeli* and Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a).

## Penalties versus wages: A statutory tug‑of‑war  

Cohen’s settlement illustrates why employers push hard for blanket “all wages paid” clauses while plaintiffs often overlook penalty language embedded in state and federal labor statutes.

### Minnesota’s MPWA  
Section 181.101(a) mandates that before any penalty can be imposed, **the Commissioner** must first serve a demand on behalf of an employee and collect unpaid wages within ten days. Only then can she assess a daily‑average‑wage penalty—and any monies collected are remitted directly **to** the employee **through** the Commissioner’s office. Because Congress deliberately kept enforcement authority with an agency rather than individual workers, an employee who signs away all wage claims also relinquishes any independent avenue toward those statutory damages unless she explicitly reserves them.

### Federal MFLSA  
Similarly, § 216(b) authorizes civil monetary penalties payable **to** a designated federal agency (the Department of Labor). Although *Friends of the Earth* permits citizen suits seeking such sanctions when aimed at deterrence, standing remains fact‑dependent: plaintiffs must demonstrate either ongoing violation or imminent recidivism. In Cohen’s case, both parties argued that payment satisfied his wage liability but disagreed whether Consilio’s premium‑overtime policy still existed—a factual question now poised for trial after remand.

### Wage Theft Act  
Minnesota’s MWTA mirrors MPWA in granting enforcement discretion primarily—but not exclusively—to state officials rather than employees themselves. Again, absent language preserving penalty rights beyond “payment of all wages,” an “all wages” stipulation effectively extinguishes future penal recovery avenues.

The practical upshot is simple: **If you agree you’ve been fully compensated without expressly retaining penalty rights, you may have waived them**—even though those remedies flow through an agency rather than into your pocket directly.

## Injunctive relief gets left behind  

In wage cases where employers implement policies designed expressly to avoid premium pay—for example superseding statutes requiring overtime premiums—the ultimate objective is often broader than back pay alone: it is about stopping unlawful conduct permanently.

Cohen attempted two distinct injunctive strategies across his pleadings:

1️⃣ **Specific injunctions** tied directly to his class action at complaint filing (e.g., prohibiting any future “premium overtime” structures).  

2️⃣ **General rescission** later asked only in his summary‑judgment brief (“withdraw its 2019 policy”).

Because procedural rules demand new substantive claims be raised via amended complaints—not amended briefing—Courts treat such post‑settlement shifts as untimely or waived unless there is clear notice paired with opportunity for response.*InfoDeli* remains firm on this point because it preserves fairness in litigation and prevents surprise stakes after parties have already executed settlements predicated upon finality.

Consequently, even though Cohen secured back pay via settlement agreement, he abandoned his chance at an injunction simply by failing to preserve it explicitly before settling—or at least before filing an amendment reflecting his change in strategy prior to briefing.*Fed.R.Civ.P.*§ 15(a). On remand we may see another attempt by Cohen (or other putative class members) to raise injunctive claims separately—a move most practitioners would preemptively address within any comprehensive settlement framework.

## Drafting stipulations that protect both sides  

### For Employers  

1️⃣ **Write “full settlement” language wide enough**—but precise enough—to capture not just back pay but **any civil penalties**, **interest**, **liquidated damages**, **statutory damages**, **injunctions**, and **attorney fees** imposed under every relevant statute (MPWA § 181.xxx; MFLSA § 216(b); MWTA § 181.xxx). Use explicit language such as “the Parties hereby release each other from any current or future liability—including civil monetary penalties—arising from violations of ….” 

2️⃣ **Flag agency‐only remedies** early in negotiations (“We understand certain penalties are payable only to state/federal agencies; this settlement includes those amounts as calculated based on current statutes”). Fully acknowledging these nuances reduces later disputes over whether an employee inadvertently waived them by signing an ‘all wages’ clause.

3️⃣ **Include survivorship provisions** tying cessation obligations together with monetary payments (“Employer shall maintain compliance with applicable wage laws post‑settlement”), thereby preserving grounds for injunctive relief should recidivism arise despite payment compliance.

4️⃣ **Seek explicit acknowledgment** from plaintiff counsel that they understand banded remedies flow through agencies rather than directly into their client’s bank account—as part of informed consent documentation—but still elect not to pursue them personally.



### For Plaintiff Counsel  

1️⃣ **Separate payment from penalty waivers** wherever possible—draft distinct releases addressing back pay (“Employee acknowledges receipt”) while leaving language open (“Employee reserves right...”) concerning statutory civil penalties unless expressly waived with consideration (“In exchange for $X in back pay […], Employee hereby waives any claim …”).

2️⃣ **Preserve injunctive rights early** —in addition—or even separate—from monetary settlements (*see* *Veliz v…*, where courts honored joint requests for injunctions alongside payment). Even if you accept back pay now, filing an amendment before final pre‑trial orders guarantees future injunctive relief remains viable should evidence emerge indicating continued noncompliance after settlement execution.

3️⃣ **Clarify standing implications** upfront if you intend your client or class members should drive agencies’ enforcement actions (e.g., petitioning DOL). Highlight potential hurdles (Direct vs indirect injury) so clients understand risks if they rely solely on private suit avenues.



4️⃣ **Conduct statutory mapping** prior to closing deals—the set‐off process touches sometimes obscure provisions (e.g., MPWA § 181.​13 vs § 181.​101) dictating who may impose/receive penalities—and indicate any divergent treatment between states so client expectations match reality.



By integrating these carveouts into settlement drafts experts provide both sides clarity about what has truly been relinquished—and what remains alive as potential leverage against future violations.

## Broader implications & possible future battles  

Cohen highlights how strategic ‘all wages paid’ stipulations shape litigation beyond what parties anticipate:

*First,* courts will continue reading these agreements strictly against-wage recovery but leave open any statutory remedy whose source lies outside an employee’s personal recovery stream unless expressly surrendered.”

*Second,* we may see more litigants turning toward **state agency enforcement** after private wage recoveries conclude—but only if settlements carve out space for agencies’’ continued action.*Kelley v….

*Third,* given divergent circuit precedent on standing for private penal actions (*e.g.*, *Friends v.* Laidlaw versus tighter approaches in Ninth Circuit cases), there is fertile ground for Supreme Court review once multiple circuits split over whether employees retain concrete injury sufficient merely to buttress civil‐penalty deterrence goals after they're paid everything else.*

In practice:

- Employers will likely intensify pressure during early negotiations (“sign our ‘full release’ now or we’ll keep pursuing unique pen­alties potentially worth much more”). 

- Plaintiff counsel will become more meticulous documenting every remedy being disclaimed—not just overt back‑pay sums—to protect client interests against later agency redress expectations or class‐wide objections.


If Minnesota joins other states making civil‐penalty enforcement exclusively agency driven—an approach reflected neatly in §§ 181.\*—the cautionary tale expands nationally across labor jurisdictions sharing similar hybrid structures (*California Labor Code §§ 203–205*, New York Labor Law §§ 191–193). 

As wage‑class litigation continues its resurgence post–COVID era—with companies reaping efficiencies from gig workforces and document‐review crowdsourcing—the interplay between private settlements and public enforcement mechanisms will shape lawyer negotiations in ways far beyond simple paycheck calculations.

## Bottom line  

“**All wages paid**” clauses are powerful weapons—but they’re double edged when statutory criminal or civil penalties remain dangling off statute books yet off-limits under narrow language linking those sanctions solely upending agency processes.* In *Cohen v.* Consilio *(2025)* we saw how one well‑written stipulation closed off wage claims while leaving penalty disputes alive enough for appellate intervention—and forced both sides onto R​E​MAND land concerning jurisdictional footing.


Whether you’re representing companies eager to seal off exposure quickly or workers hoping their class action does more than just fill bank accounts once each check clears,
the takeaway is unequivocal:

> *Never sign—or draft—a settlement without explicitly spelling out which remedies survive.*  

If your goal is synergy between cash recovery *and* lasting compliance guarantees against future illegal overtime practices,
that synergy begins long before either party inks their names.
Instead it begins during negotiation table talk about which statutory knives you’re willing—or unwilling—to hand over.
Only then do you avoid being stuck later wondering whether you’ve unintentionally amputated your own right—or your class members’ right—to hold violators accountable beyond past due payroll.
