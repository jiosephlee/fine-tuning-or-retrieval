Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Missed Injunctions: Why Cohen’s Request to Void an Overtime Policy Fell Flat  

## Why This Case Matters  

Employers’ overtime policies are a frequent flashpoint in wage‑and‑hour litigation, especially when a class‑action alleges unlawful denial of premium pay. Plaintiffs often couple back‑pay claims with an injunction that forces the employer to abandon the offending policy—after all, monetary damages don’t prevent future violations. Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC (8th Cir. Oct. 6 2025) shows how even a strong substantive claim can be derailed when the procedural scaffolding for an equitable remedy is absent. The appellate panel’s treatment of Cohen’s injunctive request underscores a timeless lesson: **injunctions demand strict adherence to the pleading rules**, and courts will not entertain “new” relief introduced only in briefing.  

## Procedural Background  

Cohen sued Consilio in federal court alleging that an internal email announcing a “premium overtime policy” actually eliminated overtime premiums for Minnesota‑based licensed attorney reviewers. After Consilio paid Cohen’s back‑pay under Minnesota’s Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) and the parties stipulated that all wages and liquidated damages were satisfied, Cohen continued to press three statutory penalties (MPWA, MFLSA, MWTA) and an injunction ordering Consilio to rescind the overtime policy altogether.

The district court granted summary judgment on all wage‑and‑penalty claims and dismissed the injunction claim without detailed analysis. On appeal, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the wage judgments but vacated the MFLSA penalty ruling on jurisdictional grounds and **affirmed the dismissal of the injunction** because “the issue is not properly before us.” The panel leaned heavily on Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a) and precedent such as *InfoDeli, LLC* (136 F.4th 801 (8th Cir. 2025)), which warns that new claims cannot be raised through amending briefs alone.  

## The Injunction Issue  

Cohen’s original complaint did enumerate “certain specific forms of injunctive relief” (e.g., prospectively prohibiting any down‑grading of overtime rates). By the time he reached summary‑judgment briefing he had **reframed** his ask as a sweeping order “to rescind its illegal overtime policy.” That shift introduced a **new cause of action** rather than simply adjusting an existing one—a distinction that matters sharply under Rule 15(a).

Rule 15(a) states that “the parties may amend their pleadings only by filing a pleading…with any required certification…unless stipulated otherwise or ordered by the court.” It is meant to preserve fairness by giving defendants adequate notice of what they are being asked to defend against and to give plaintiffs time to develop factual support for their equitable claim. When Cohen tried to rely on his brief alone to pivot from narrowly defined statutory compliance measures to a broad equitable decree, he bypassed both notice requirements and the formal amendment mechanism.

The Eighth Circuit rightfully treated this as an untimely change: **the district court had no jurisdiction over a claim it never saw framed in a pleading or motion**. A plaintiff cannot hide an injunction behind a wage‑and‑hour claim and surprise the defendant at summary judgment with an entirely new demand for blanket policy rescission.

## Rule 15(a) in Action  

The rule operates on two levels:

1️⃣ **Notice** – Defendants must know exactly what they are being sued over so they can marshal evidence and craft defenses. An abrupt expansion from “specific remedial orders” to “full policy nullification” violates this principle.

2️⃣ **Judicial Efficiency** – Courts allocate resources based on what lies within the record at each stage. A claim introduced at briefing would force the judge either to reconstruct evidentiary foundations out of thin air or risk deciding without a proper factual basis—both undesirable outcomes.

Consistent case law backs this view. In *Kuehl v. Federal Bureau of Prisons* (758 F.3d 395 (8th Cir.—2014)) the court held that “a party may not insert novel relief after discovering during discovery that its initial request is insufficient.” Similarly, *Egelhoff v. Wexner Medical Center* (442 U.S._147 (1979)) warned that equitable remedies require “clear articulation” at inception because courts will not infer them later.

Cohen could have avoided this fate by amending his complaint within **Rule 15(b)(1)**—the one‑week window after service of an answer—or by filing a separate motion for preliminary injunction under **Rule 56** after discovery revealed whether Consilio still adhered to its outlawed policy. Either route would have placed his request squarely within procedural bounds and forced Consilio onto notice early enough to contest it meaningfully.

## Practical Guidance for Plaintiffs  

1️⃣ **Spell Out Injunctive Relief Early** – When an equitable remedy forms part of your strategy—especially in wage‑and‑hour cases—draft your complaint with concrete language (“the defendant shall immediately cease enforcement of its August 2019 premium‑overtime policy” rather than vague “appropriate equitable relief”).

2️⃣ **Pair Injunction Claims with Factual Foundations** – Injunctions require proof of irreparable harm and likelihood of success on merits *at least* at the preliminary stage (*e.g.*, *eBay Inc.* v.~MercExchange). Gather evidence during discovery about ongoing violations before courting summary judgment; otherwise you risk appearing speculative when you finally ask for it.

3️⃣ **Use Amendments Strategically** – If you discover midway through discovery that your original relief request is too narrow, file an amendment promptly under Rule 15(b)(1) or seek leave from the court under Rule 15(a). Courts generally favor amendments unless they would prejudice opposing counsel or unduly disrupt schedule—a stark contrast to silently shifting theory at briefing.

4️⃣ **Beware Stipulations That Curtail Future Claims** – Cohen’s parties entered into a stipulation stating it would be “most expedient…to hear …summary judgment…before any further discovery or litigation.” While well‑intentioned, such language can be read as waiving later grounds for additional relief unless expressly preserved in writing or through subsequent motions.

5️⃣ **Separate Injunctive Motions When Summary Judgment Is Anticipated** – Even if you expect payroll back‑pay issues to resolve via summary judgment, file a *motion for preliminary injunction* concurrently so both issues proceed on their own tracks rather than being collapsed into one ambiguous motion record.

## Doctrinal Implications  

The eighth circuit’s decision reinforces an emerging line among federal courts treating **injunctions as distinct procedural tracks**, immune from being grafted onto wage‑and‑hour claims after discovery closes. The rationale dovetails with Supreme Court reasoning in *Friends of the Earth v.~Laidlaw*—the Court emphasized standing and concrete injury—but adds another layer: without proper pleading, there is no concrete injury presented at all.

By refusing to consider Cohen’s last‑minute broad injunction request, the panel signals that **equitable remedies will not be rescued by judicial creativity when parties sidestep Rule 15** . That hangs especially heavy over class actions where plaintiffs routinely rely on injunctions to change corporate practices permanently—the procedural hurdle becomes part of strategic case planning rather than an afterthought.

Future litigators should see this as both warning and roadmap: embed injury narratives early; anticipate evidentiary development; leverage Rules 15(b) and 56 proactively; and avoid treating consent orders or stipulations as “shortcuts” around ordinary pleading duties.

## Bottom Line  

Cohen’s substantive grievance—that Consilio unlawfully stripped overtime premium pay—did survive Walsh-style wage recoveries because those were plainly laid out at trial entry and settled through payment agreements. His quest for broad injunctive relief failed precisely because he tried to grow his remedy midstream without giving either party notice through proper pleadings or motions—a misstep squarely punished under Fed.R.Civ.P. 15(a).

Plaintiffs who truly need an order compelling employers to abandon illegal policies should front‑load those demands into their complaints or amendment filings long before they reach summary judgment harboring wage calculations alone. In doing so they preserve both their right to redress past harms and their ability to halt future misconduct—an outcome far more satisfying than watching an outrightly procedural objection eliminate what could have been a powerful equity win.
