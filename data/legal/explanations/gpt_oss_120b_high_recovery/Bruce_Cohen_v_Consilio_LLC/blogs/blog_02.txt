Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Federal Standing Meets State Wage Penalties — The MFLSA Conundrum  

## Why This Case Matters  

When a company changes its overtime policy and decides not to pay premium rates, employees often look to state wage‑law statutes for remedies beyond back pay—namely civil penalties meant to deter unlawful practices. Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC forces courts to confront a thorny question: **Can an aggrieved employee sue for a statutory penalty that, by statutory design, is payable not to the employee but to a state labor commissioner?**  The Eighth Circuit’s decision is a bellwether for how federal courts will treat standing in state wage‑penalty schemes, especially when the underlying conduct may have ceased by the time the case reaches appellate review.  Practitioners across the country will watch how this reasoning shapes class‑action strategy, employer compliance programs, and the broader architecture of wage‑law enforcement.

## Factual and Procedural Snapshot  

Consilio—an attorney document‑review firm with Minnesota operations—issued an internal email in July 2019 announcing that its “premium overtime” program would be eliminated in August 2019.  From that point forward hourly licensed attorney reviewers would be paid at their base rate regardless of hours worked.  Cohen, a Minnesota‑licensed attorney who began work in 2018, sued in August 2020 alleging violations of three Minnesota statutes:  

1. The **Minnesota Payment of Wages Act** (MPWA);  
2. The **Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act** (MFLSA); and  
3. The **Minnesota Wage Theft Act** (MWTA).  

The suit sought back‑pay overtime, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief.  After settlement of the back‑pay and liquidated‑damage components—Cohen received $3,225 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages—the parties stipulated that no further wages were owed.  They also agreed that “the most expedient” path was for the district court to decide Consilio’s summary‑judgment motion on all claims before any further discovery.

The district court granted summary judgment on every claim.  Cohen appealed.  The Eighth Circuit affirmed on the MPWA and MWTA claims but **vacated** the district court’s grant of summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remanded for further consideration of jurisdiction/standing issues.

## The Court’s Reasoning on Private Penalties Under the MPWA  

The first question was whether Cohen could recover “average daily wage” penalties under MPWA §§ 181.101 & 181.171.  Section 181.101(a) makes clear that **only** the Minnesota Labor Commissioner may serve a demand for payment and, if unpaid within ten days, impose a penalty that must be turned over to the employee **via** the commissioner’s collection process.  The court emphasized prior Minnesotan precedent (Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.) that plain‑language statutes control: because § 181.101 expressly limits penalty enforcement to the commissioner, a private employee lacks a direct cause of action for those penalties.

The panel noted that when Minnesota wished to grant a private right to penalties it did so explicitly in other statutes—for example § 181.13(a) authorizes an employee who files a demand after discharge to recover both wages *and* a penalty directly from the employer.  The omission of such language in § 181.101 therefore signals legislative intent—not oversight—to confine penalty collection to the commissioner’s office.  Consequently, Cohen’s MPWA penalty claim was properly dismissed.

## The Heart of the Matter: MFLSA Penalties and Federal Standing  

Cohen’s remaining claim hinged on his entitlement under **Section 6(c)** of the **Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act** (the “MFLSA”) to civil penalties “payable to the commissioner.”  Unlike ordinary overtime wages or liquidated damages—which Cohen had already received—the statutory penalties would flow directly into a state fund administered by Minnesota’s Labor Commissioner.

### A Standalone Private Right?  

The Eighth Circuit found itself confronting *standing* doctrine rather than whether Minnesota “authorizes” such penalties—a question already settled by statute.  Because the statute designates **the commissioner** as both plaintiff and beneficiary of any civil penalty, an employee must demonstrate a personal injury sufficient to confer standing under Article III of the Constitution.

Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000) offers two prongs for standing in civil‑penalty actions: (1) an injury “in fact” traceable to conduct alleged by plaintiff; and (2) a redressability component whereby granting relief would give plaintiff a concrete benefit beyond general public interest vindication.  Applying this framework:

* **Injury:** Cohen had already been compensated for all back wages and liquidated damages owed under MFLSA § 6(b).  The only alleged injury left was an abstract institutional injury—the notion that Consilio continued illegal conduct without paying its mandated penalty.
* **Redressability:** Even if a penalty were imposed it would flow into state coffers—not into Cohen’s pocket—so there is no direct monetary benefit to him personally.

Because those elements were lacking—or at best uncertain—the panel concluded there is **no clear basis** for treating Cohen as an “injured party” capable of sustaining a private civil‑penalty action against Consilio.

### Mootness Concerns  

Even assuming standing were established, Cohen still faced a *mootness* hurdle.  Under *Friends of the Earth*, claims may become moot when unlawful conduct has ceased such that no future injury is foreseeable (“the alleged wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur”).  Here, Consilio had already paid back-wages and liquidated damages; whether its “no premium overtime” policy remained in effect was disputed but could be resolved through discovery or additional pleading.

Eighth Circuit jurisprudence stresses that courts should not retain jurisdiction over cases where parties plainly lack any continuing interest (*Sisney v.* Kaemingk). The panel therefore vacated summary judgment on the MFLSA claim—not because it disagreed with its merits—but because it could not adjudicate liability without first determining whether Cohen satisfies standing requirements or whether his claim is moot absent ongoing violation.

Consequently, on remand the district court must decide:

1. Whether Cohen can meet *Friends of the Earth* standing criteria for an employee seeking statutory penalties payable solely to a state official.
2. Whether any residual violation remains capable of recurrence—i.e., does Consilio still enforce its base‑rate‑only overtime policy?

Only after those threshold questions are resolved can substantive liability be addressed.

## Comparative Perspective: Penalty Schemes in Other States  

Minnesota is not unique in routing wage‑penalties through a state agency rather than directly to employees:

* **California** – Civil Code § 2034 provides workers’ compensation awards plus *civil penalties* enforced by labor regulators; however California authorizes private workers’ claims for “civil money penalties” if they prove willful violations.
* **New York** – Labor Law §§ 191–196 allow private employees an action for accrued wages *and* civil fines imposed by either department officials or via private litigation where “the Department shall receive” any fine collected.
* **Illinois** – The One Day Rest Act imposes civil penalties payable directly into an administrative fund but also gives employees limited private rights of action (*Steinberg v.* City of Chicago).

In each jurisdiction courts have grappled with essentially identical standing problems when employees attempt to capture agency‑directed fines as partisans rather than merely as indirect beneficiaries (“the payer”).   A common thread emerges: when statutes explicitly vest enforcement discretion—and receipt of funds—in a governmental official or agency, courts require plaintiffs either (a) explicit textual authorization granting them private rights or (b) an independent injury beyond abstract public interest—often rare indeed.

The Eighth Circuit’s approach dovetails with these precedents by treating *private inability* as fatal unless Congress or state legislatures expressly carve out an exception.

## Practical Takeaways for Plaintiffs  

1️⃣ **Confirm Whether Penalties Are Private Rights:** Before filing class actions targeting statutory fines, scrutinize legislative history and statutory text for language granting employees direct entitlement.\  
2️⃣ **Preserve Procedural Prerequisites:** Many statutes require first filing a demand with—​or obtaining notice from—the labor commissioner before an employee may bring her own suit (e.g., Tennessee's Wage Payment Act).   Attempting litigation without satisfying those prerequisites almost invariably results in dismissal on standing grounds.\  
3️⃣ **Seek Injunctive Relief Simultaneously:** Even if penalty recovery is barred, injunctive relief can forcibly change employer policy—eliminating future exposure—and maintain ongoing injury sufficient for standing.\  
4️⃣ **Structure Claims Around Redressability:** Demonstrating personal financial loss distinct from any government benefit strengthens standing \(e.g., seeking accrual interest on unpaid wages rather than simply civil fines\).\  
5️⃣ **Plan For Settlement Timing:** If settlement covers back wages but leaves policy unchanged, plaintiffs should negotiate contractual clauses compelling policy reversal—a concrete remedy preserving sustained injury necessary for future enforcement.\

## Potential Downstream Effects  

The Eighth Circuit’s vacatur injects uncertainty into how wage‑penalty provisions will be litigated moving forward:

* **Class‑Action Viability:** Plaintiffs’ ability to aggregate “penalty” claims across large employee groups may be curtailed unless they can demonstrate personal injury beyond generalized deterrence.\  
* **Employer Compliance Strategies:** Employers may feel emboldened by knowing even if they pay back wages promptly they might avoid exposure to large statutory fines—provided they cease offending policies before litigation ages into enforceable standing.\  
* **Legislative Reactions:** Observers anticipate Minnesota lawmakers revisiting § 6(c) of MFLSA—or analogous statutes elsewhere—to clarify whether employees possess an independent right-of-action or remain dependent upon commissioner initiation.\  
* **Federal Courts’ Role:** By foregrounding standing rather than substantive liability, this decision underscores federal courts’ willingness to prune wage cases at early procedural stages when Congress has allocated enforcement duties primarily at state level.\

All told, *Cohen v.* signals that plaintiffs cannot rely solely on “penalty‐of‐the‑statute” language as armor against employer defenses; they must articulate personalized stakes that survive constitutional scrutiny.

## Looking Ahead  

The remand will force district judges within the circuit—and perhaps beyond—to issue concrete guidance on two fronts: 

1) How strictly should courts interpret statutory designations of "commissioner" as precluding all private action?  
2) At what point does cessation of offending conduct render an employee’s remaining claims moot?

If lower courts adopt a narrow view akin to what Judge Kelly outlined here—requiring either explicit private rights or demonstrable ongoing injury—we may see fewer successful attempts at recovering agency‑directed civil penalties from private actors nationwide.

Conversely, should appellate precedent emerge permitting broader standing where employees prove likely repeat violations—a more permissive approach—it could reinvigorate wage‑law advocacy across jurisdictions where similar penalty schemes exist.

Either way, lawyers representing hourly professionals should now audit client policies not only through paycheck calculations but also through procedural lenses: do we have legitimate avenues to summon agencies *and* capture whatever remedial bounty lies behind them? As *Cohen* reminds us, dissecting statutory anatomy matters as much as tallying overtime dollars—and perhaps more so when constitutional doctrine intersects with state labor law minutiae.\
