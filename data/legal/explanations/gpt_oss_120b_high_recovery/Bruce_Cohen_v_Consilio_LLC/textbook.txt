Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# Chapter 1 – Factual and Procedural Background  

This chapter sets out the factual landscape that frames *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, and outlines the procedural posture that brought the dispute before the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit. Understanding who the parties are, how their employment relationship was structured, what statutory regimes were invoked, and how the lower court disposed of those claims is essential before delving into the appellate analysis of the court’s reasoning.

## Consilio’s Business Model and Minnesota Employment Setting  

Consilio, LLC is a nationwide legal‑document‑review firm headquartered outside Minnesota but operating a substantial Minnesota office where it employs attorneys who perform document‑review work on a contract basis. Under Minnesota law the company is deemed an “employer” for purposes of wage‑and‑hour statutes because it controls the hours worked, sets rates of pay, and issues paychecks directly to its employees. The firm’s business model hinges on hiring licensed attorneys as hourly “document reviewers” rather than as salaried staff attorneys; consequently its workers are covered by state wage‐payment regimes that require overtime compensation when hours exceed forty per week unless an exemption applies.

## Cohen’s Role as an Hourly Licensed‑Attorney Document Reviewer  

Bruce Cohen joined Consilio in 2018. He was classified as an hourly employee despite being a licensed attorney—a status common among document reviewers who perform substantive legal work without being assigned case responsibilities typical of salaried counsel. As an hourly worker he was subject to Minnesota’s overtime provisions, which generally obligate employers to pay “premium” overtime (time–and–a‑half) for hours worked beyond forty in a workweek unless a valid exemption exists. Cohen performed his duties exclusively at Consilio’s Minnesota location, making him subject both to Minnesota statutory protections and to federal Fair Labor Standards Act coverage through the state’s mini‑FLSA (“Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act,” MFLSA).

## The August 2019 “Premium Overtime” Policy Eliminating Overtime Premiums  

In July 2019 Consilio circulated an email to all Minnesota–based hourly licensed‑attorney reviewers announcing a change effective August 2019. The communication stated that Consilio would implement a new “premium overtime” policy that **eliminated** any overtime premium pay. Instead, employees would receive **their ordinary base‐rate** for **all** hours worked regardless of whether weekly totals exceeded forty hours. The policy therefore supplanted the statutory requirement—or at least the customary practice—of paying time–and–a‑half for overtime work with flat base‐rate compensation. No new exemption was identified in the memo; rather, Consilio relied on its own classification scheme rather than express statutory authority. This policy shift formed the factual nucleus of Cohen’s wage‐and‐hour claims because it arguably contravened both state and federal requirements that employees receive premium pay absent a valid exemption.

## The 2020 Wage‑and‑Hour Class‑Action Complaint and Its Statutory Bases  

In August 2020 Cohen filed suit in federal district court on his own behalf **and** on behalf of a *putative* class consisting of similarly situated hourly licensed attorneys employed by Consilio in Minnesota. A putative class is one whose members share common legal questions but whose status has not yet been certified by the court; pleading such a class preserves potential collective relief while allowing individual claims to proceed if certification fails.

Cohen anchored his complaint in three distinct Minnesota statutes:

1. **Minnesota Payment of Wages Act** (“MPWA”), §§ 181.101 et seq.—a remedial statute authorizing employees who have been “wronged” under its wage provisions to bring civil actions seeking compensatory damages **and** any civil penalties expressly provided in the violated section;
2. **Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act** (“MFLSA”), §§ 177.25–177.27—which mirrors many provisions of the federal FLSA by requiring payment of overtime premiums where applicable and additionally permitting recovery of liquidated damages equal to unpaid wages plus *civil penalties* payable **to** the State Commissioner;
3. **Minnesota Wage Theft Act** (“MWTA”), §§ 181.032(f), § 181.171 et seq.—which imposes notice requirements on employers altering wage terms and authorizes *penalties* if those notice requirements are breached.

Cohen demanded four categories of relief across these statutes:

* **Overtime wages** calculated at one‑and‑a half times his regular rate;
* **Liquidated damages** equal to his unpaid overtime;
* **Statutory civil penalties** (average daily‐wage penalties under MPWA; Commissioner‐collectible penalties under MFLSA; notice‐violation penalties under MWTA); and
* **Injunctive relief** compelling Consilio to rescind its August 2019 premium policy so that future work would be compensated at lawful rates.

By pleading both monetary compensation *and* punitive sanctions Cohen emphasized that even though he could recover backpay through ordinary wage provisions, he also sought deterrent benefits embedded within each statute’s remedial scheme.

## Stipulated Payments Covering Overtime Wages and Liquidated Damages  

Before any dispositive ruling was entered, Consilio made a series of payments intended to satisfy Cohen’s monetary claims under his MFLSA allegations. Specifically:

* Total payments released by Consilio amounted to **$256,010.01**, earmarked broadly as “overtime wages and liquidated damages” owed pursuant to MFLSA;
* Of this sum, Cohen personally received **$3,225.21** representing backpay for overtime hours and **$462** representing liquidated damages;
* Both parties entered into **stipulations** agreeing that these amounts satisfied *all* outstanding overtime wages *and* liquidated damages owed under Cohen’s MFLSA theory;
* The stipulations expressly noted that while compensation was settled, **no agreement existed concerning statutory penalties**, which remained contested topics between plaintiff and defendant;
* Neither side could agree on any figure representing such penalties—Cohen asserted entitlement totalling $172,…$42,…$42 thousand respectively while Consilio denied any statutory authority supporting them.

Stipulated facts are treated as incontrovertible evidence between litigants; consequently the trial court was free from any dispute concerning backpay or liquidated damage calculations when assessing whether further relief might be warranted under MPWA or MWTA or whether its own determination regarding civil penalties warranted reversal on appeal.

## Unsettled Issue of Statutory Penalties Under Each Statute  

While monetary compensation was resolved through stipulation, each statute invoked by Cohen contains distinct *penalty* mechanisms whose availability rested on divergent textual schemes:

* **MPWA:** Section 181.101 makes clear that an employer may be held liable for “the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.” However, Section 181.101(a) also limits enforcement authority—it requires a demand from the State Commissioner before any penalty may be assessed or collected, thereby raising whether an *employee* may directly recover those “average daily wage” penalties absent such commissioner involvement.
* **MFLSA:** Here civil penalties are explicitly payable **to** the State Commissioner—not directly to an employee—even though they serve a deterrent function similar to those discussed in *Friends of the Earth v.* Laidlaw (where punitive civil sanctions were deemed redressable by private plaintiffs). Whether an aggrieved employee has standing—or even mootness concerns—when all compensatory sums have been paid forms part of this unresolved question.
* **MWTA:** This act imposes notice obligations before changes such as eliminating premium pay may take effect; violations trigger civil penalties outlined in § 181.032(f) / § 181.…(d)(4). Yet Cohen offered no legal authority showing how those specific provisions create private rights enforceable absent commissioner's demand or explicit congressional intent authorizing employee suits solely for notice violations.


These divergent textual frameworks created genuine disputes about whether any plaintiff could recover statutory penalties independent from compensatory awards—a point both sides highlighted during briefing and which set the stage for appellate review.


## The Parties’ Agreement to Resolve the Case via Summary‑Judgment Motion  

Recognizing that most factual disputes had been resolved through stipulation—and mindful of litigation costs—the parties entered into another written agreement shortly before trial. They jointly stipulated:

> “The most expedient and efficient path forward would be for this Court [the district court] to hear and decide Defendants’ motion for summary judgment on [the] MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims before any further discovery or litigation.”

Both sides also acknowledged a consequential principle: *if* defendants received total summary­ judgment defeat ("grant[ed] Defendants’ summary judgment motion in full"), then “no claims would remain,” thereby terminating all aspects—including any pending injunctive request—and concluding the case entirely without additional proceedings.

Summary judgment is a dispositive mechanism governed by Federal Rule 56 permitting a party moving for judgment “to obtain such judgment if there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact.” In effect, once both sides agree their material facts are settled—or one side demonstrates conclusively there exists no factual controversy—the moving party may succeed without going before a jury or even taking additional discovery.


The stipulation therefore reflected strategic consent: since payment controversies had been eliminated via stipulation, both plaintiff and defendant believed only pure questions of law remained—namely whether each statute permitted private recovery of its prescribed sanctions.


## District Court’s Grant of Summary Judgment on All Three Claims  

Acting upon Defendants’ Rule 56 motion—and relying upon its own reading that none of Cohen's asserted penalty remedies survived either textual or legislative scrutiny—the district court entered an order granting summary judgment *in its entirety.* This order dismissed:

1️⃣ All liability under **MPWA**, finding no private right vested solely with employees for average daily wage penalities without prior commissioner demand;

2️⃣ All liability under **MFLSA**, concluding statutory civil penalties were unavailable absent further ongoing violations capable of producing injury—and further holding jurisdiction improper given mootness concerns;

3️⃣ All liability under **MWTA**, determining none existed either because Cohen offered no viable argument supporting employee recovery for alleged notice violations nor identified any applicable penal provision actionable against him directly.


Because summary judgement extinguishes every claim presented without new factual development or referral back unto trial counsel counsel instructions weigh drastically weight--the adverse order prompted Cohen promptly filing notice​of​appeal within thirty days pursuant­to Fed.R.Civ.P 4(b). Appellate jurisdiction conferred upon this panel likewise required de novo appraisal—a fresh look at evidentiary materials together with conclusions from well‐established precedents controlling constructive resolution interpretation where “[t]he standard…is viewing…the evidence…and reasonable…inferences…in light most favorable…to nonmoving party”—though substantive legal questions remained reserved for subsequent sections.


Thus concludes our factual narrative: an hourly attorney employed by a national document‐review firm challenged an employer‐initiated elimination of premium‐overtime pay using three overlapping state statutes; after settling backpay obligations through stipulation yet leaving penalty theory unresolved And agreeing upon expedited resolution via dispositive motion , consensus culminated with outright dismissal at trial level—a dismissal now perched before this Court's review.

# Chapter 2 – Governing Law and Judicial Standards  

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* the appellate court grappled with three distinct Minnesota wage‑payment statutes and applied a set of procedural doctrines familiar to every Eighth Circuit appellate practitioner.  Understanding the court’s reasoning requires mastering the substantive provisions at issue—namely the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (“MPWA”), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (“MFLSA”), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (“MWTA”)—as well as the interpretive tools and procedural standards that frame appellate review.  This chapter walks through each statute, explains how the court constructed its meaning, and then ties those constructions to the summary‑judgment standard, standing requirements for a private “citizen plaintiff,” and the mootness analysis that ultimately compelled a remand on the MFLSA claim.

---

## Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) – §§ 181.101 & 181.171  

The MPWA is Minnesota’s principal remedial scheme for an employer’s failure to pay lawful wages or commissions. § 181.101(a) sets out a two‑step enforcement process:  

1. The State Commissioner may serve a written demand on behalf of an employee for payment of all wages earned but not yet paid;  
2. If the employer does not satisfy the demand within ten days, the Commissioner may collect both the unpaid wages **and** a civil penalty on the employer’s behalf.  

The statute expressly provides that any money “collected by the Commissioner [must] be paid … to the employee concerned” (§ 181.101).  

Section 181.171 creates a private right of action allowing an aggrieved employee “to bring a civil action seeking redress” for violations covered by § 181.101 (and other MPWA provisions). The remedial clause states that an employer “who is found … liable … [is] liable for compensatory damages **and other appropriate relief**” and further adds that “the aggrieved party may recover **civil penalties or damages** so long as they are **provided for in the section**” violated (§ 181.171(subd 1)).

In *Cohen*, plaintiff argued that § 181.101 implicitly authorizes an individual employee to recover “average daily wage” penalties directly—an approach typical in some states where private actions can recover liquidated damages identical to statutory penalties. The Eighth Circuit rejected this construction on two grounds:

* **Plain‑language reading** ― The text limits penalty assessment to actions taken by the Commissioner after a demand is made (§ 181.101(a)). No phrase grants employees an independent right to impose those penalties.“When the language … is plain and unambiguous … it is assumed … manifest legislative intent” (*Allan* 869 N.W.​2d at 33).  
* **Legislative design** ― The legislature’s decision elsewhere in Chapter 181—to vest penalty‑collection authority solely with the Commissioner—demonstrates intentional limitation (“the Legislature knows how…if it wishes”). The court cited *Lagasse* 982 N.W.​2d at 199 n·2 acknowledging that selective statutory drafting signals purposeful restriction.

Because *Cohen*’s reading required reading around clear textual commands—the commissioner's exclusive role—the appellate court concluded that Cohen could not sue for average‑daily‑wage penalties under § 181.101, and therefore affirmed the district court’s summary‑judgment disposition on his MPWA claim.

---

## Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) – Overtime Wage Provisions & Civil‑Penalty Scheme  

The MFLSA mirrors many federal FLSA requirements but operates as a state statute designed specifically for “Minnesota employees.” Its overtime provision obligates covered employers to pay “time‐and‐a‑half” compensation for hours worked beyond twenty‑four hours per week or eight hours per day (§ 177․25). Violations trigger liquidated damages equal to one‑times owed overtime (§ 177․27(subd 8)) **and**, importantly, a separate statutory penalty regime designed for governmental enforcement rather than private recovery:

* Upon an employee’s demand for unpaid wages that remains unsatisfied after ten days, **the Commissioner may assess a civil penalty** against the employer; collected funds go directly into state coffers rather than into the employee’s pocket (§ 177․27(subd 8); cf. § 181.​101).

In *Cohen*’s case, parties stipulated that all overtime wages and liquidated damages were already paid pursuant to his MFLSA claim (<em>see Stipulation</em>). Yet Cohen sought additional statutory penalties amounting to $42,000—the amount prescribed by § 177․27(subd 8). The appellate panel recognized two intertwined doctrinal hurdles:

1️⃣ **Standing** – While *Friends of the Earth v.* Laidlaw clarified that citizen plaintiffs may pursue civil penalties because such sanctions serve deterrence goals (*Friends*, 528 U.S.? at 186), those statutes implicitly require an injury “as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct.” Here Cohen had already been compensated for his wage loss, raising doubt whether he maintained a concrete personal stake distinct from a generalized public interest ([*Town of Chester* 581 U.S.? at 439]).

2️⃣ **Mootness** – The panel noted uncertainty whether Consilio’s discriminatory overtime policy remained operative after its settlement payments were made (<em>see Vacatur</em>). If Consilio had definitively abandoned “premium‐pay elimination,” any resulting penalty claim would be rendered moot because there would be no foreseeable future violation (“a defendant’s voluntary cessation … does not deprive … power,” *Laidlaw*, 528 U.S.? at 189). Conversely, if policy persisted, Cohen’s claim retains live controversy.[^1]

Because those jurisdictional questions were unresolved—particularly whether any injury existed sufficient for standing—the circuit vacated summary judgment on Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remanded so that the district court could first determine jurisdictional competence before addressing merits.

---

## Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) – Notice‑of‑Policy Requirement & Penalties  

The MWTA targets deceptive wage practices through mandatory disclosure duties imposed upon employers who change wage rates or overtime structures ([§ 181​.032(f)]). An employer must provide written notice at least three days before any change becomes effective ([§ 181​.032(d)(4)]); failure triggers “civil penalties” calibrated similarly to those found elsewhere in Chapter 181.

Cohen alleged Consilio breached this notice requirement by unilaterally instituting its August 2019 premium‑pay elimination via email without satisfying MWTA notice mandates—a violation he claimed should yield statutory penalties under §§ 181​.032(f) & (d)(4). However:

* No legal authority was cited establishing a private right–of–action specifically for MWTA penalties,
* Cohen had already received all money he claims is owed,
* He offered no substantive argument supporting why his complaint survived dismissal absent such authority,

Thus according️ly applying *Milligan v.* Red Oak (“If a brief does not support its assertion ...the issue is waived”),the appellate panel affirmed dismissal without further discussion.

---

## Statutory Interpretation Principles Used by Minnesota Courts  

Minnesota courts follow a hierarchical approach when construing statutes:

1️⃣ **Plain Text Dominance** – If wording is clear, courts apply it directly (*Allan* 869 N.W.? at 33). In *Cohen*, § 181.​101(a)’s explicit reference to “the commissioner” left little room for an alternative private‐penalty reading.

2️⃣ **Legislative Intent Through Structural Analysis** – When ambiguity exists—or when different statutory schemes coexist—the legislature’s “knowledge” approach signals intentionality (*Lagasse* 982 N.W.? at 199 n·2 quoting *In re Hubbard*, 778 N.W.? at 323). Thus contrasting sections granting private actions ([§ 181​.171]) with sections delegating enforcement solely to commissioners highlighted deliberate limitation rather than oversight.

3️⃣ **Presumption Against Implied Penalties** – Courts refrain from attributing penal provisions absent clear textual source (*Hull*​ v.​ ConvergeOne,*570 F.​Supp.? at 695), reinforcing that treating § 181​.101 penetration as granting private damages would run counterto expressed design placing penalty collection exclusively in agency hands.

These interpretive tools collectively guided judges toward reading each wage statute narrowly enough “to give effect” only what legislators plainly enacted.

---

## Summary‑Judgment Standard in the Eighth Circuit (De Novo Review)  

The Eighth Circuit evaluates summary judgment under **de novo** standards:

*“Viewing the evidence and inferences…in light most favorable to nonmoving party,”* (*Karlen* 766 F.​3d at 866).  
To prevail, moving parties must demonstrate there are *no genuine issues of material fact* and they are entitled “as a matter of law” ([*Placzek*] 18 F.​4th at 1014).

The burden sequence follows *Petroski*’s two‐step format:

1️⃣ Moving party presents evidentiary materials sufficient on its own face;
2️⃣ If satisfied, burden shifts briefly—but only insofar as evidence is still admissible—to nonmoving party who must create *some* evidence showing genuine dispute (*Petroski*, 750 F.​3d at 978).

In *Cohen*, both sides stipulated payment satisfaction concerning overtime wages/ liquidated damages—removing factual conflict about amount owed—and thus left only legal questions regarding who may recover statutory penalties under each act. Because those legal questions were resolved against Cohen under clear statutory construction (§ MPWA) or remained unsettled pending jurisdictional determination (§ MFLSA), summary judgment was proper where applicable but required vacatur where jurisdiction was doubtful.

---

## Standing Requirements for Citizen Plaintiffs Seeking Civil Penalties  

Standing traditionally requires three elements:

1． **Injury in fact** – concrete and particularized harm;
2． **Causal connection** between injury and challenged conduct;
3． **Redressability** – relief likely will remedy injury (*Town of Chester*​ v.​ Laroe Ests.*, 581 U.S.? at 439).

When plaintiffs pursue government‐imposed civil penalties—as opposed merely seeking back wages—a nuanced question arises: does paying a fine constitute sufficient redress? *Friends of Earth v.*​Laidlaw clarified that citizens may have standing because penal sanctions serve deterrence functions benefiting them personally (*Friends*,528 U.S.? at 186). However this permissive holding assumes plaintiffs suffer an ongoing injurious effect beyond mere monetary recovery—for example continued exposure to unlawful employment practices or depletion of public funds earmarked for enforcement activities affecting them indirectly.

*Cohen* presented an ambiguous picture: his wage loss was fully remedied by settlement stipulations; his remaining interest lay solely in extracting additional monies payable not directly into his pocket but into state coffers—a classic public‐interest remission rather than individualized injury.^[<sup>2</sup>] Consequently judges expressed concern over whether he satisfied *Town of Chester*’s personal stake requirement when seeking MFLSA penalties.^[<sup>3</sup>]

Because neither party raised explicit arguments concerning this standing question during briefing—and because standing determines subject–matter jurisdiction—the panel chose constitutional prudence over substantive merits: it vacated summary judgment pending clarification whether Cohen possesses requisite personal stake or whether his allegation constitutes merely an abstract desire for governmental enforcement funds.\[⁴\]

---

## Mootness Doctrine as Applied to Civil‑Penalty Claims  

Mootness bars adjudication when “the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or ‘ripe’,” requiring a case‐oriented controversy throughout litigation(*Sisney*​ v.​Kaemingk**,15 F.​4th at 1194). Two facets illuminate its application here:

1️⃣ **Voluntary Cessation Does Not Render Claim Moot Per Se** – Federal precedent holds courts may evaluate legality even if defendant stops alleged conduct (*Mesquite v.*​Aladdin’s Castle,*455 U.S.? at 289). Nonetheless cessation can make claims moot if there is no reasonable expectation another violation will occur (**Friends**,528 U.S.? at·189).

2️⃣ **Continuing Violation Possibility Sustains Case** – In *Cohen*, defendants argued their August–2020 settlement eliminated any future breach because policy was abandoned post‑payment; plaintiffs insisted policy persisted without amendment—a factual dispute critical because statutory penalty schemes penalize continuing noncompliance rather than isolated historical failures.^[<sup>5</sup>] Because parties disagreed whether Consilio would continue imposing its zero–premium pay rule—a point central even after settlements—the district court could not definitively declare litigants moot without further factfinding.^[<sup>6</sup>]

The appellate panel therefore vacated summary judgment on MFLSA grounds until lowercourts could decide whether any live controversy existed regarding Consilio’s ongoing practice—a prerequisite before imposing any further statutorily prescribed sanctions.\[⁷\]

# Chapter 3 – The MPWA Penalty Claim: Why Only the Commissioner May Enforce Penalties  

Bruce Cohen sued his former employer, Consilio, LLC, under Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (“MPWA”) alleging that the company owed him overtime wages *and* statutory “average‑daily‑wage” penalties provided by §§ 181.101 and 181.171 of the MPWA.  After Consilio paid Cohen all overtime wages and liquidated damages owed under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (“MFLSA”), the parties stipulated that nothing further remained payable except any statutory penalties they could still recover.  Consilio moved for summary judgment on all three state‑law claims; the district court granted it, holding that Cohen could not recover MPWA penalties because those penalties are enforceable only by the state labor commissioner pursuant to § 181.101(a).  On appeal, the Eighth Circuit affirmed that portion of the ruling, concluding there was **no genuine issue of material fact** as to whether an individual employee may sue directly for average‑daily‑wage penalties under § 181.101(a).  

The following sections break down the Court’s textual, historical, and precedential analysis and show why it concluded that **only the commissioner—not an aggrieved employee—may enforce MPWA penalties**.



## Textual Analysis of § 181\.101(a): Demand‑for‑Payment Requirement & Commissioner’s Authority  

Section 181.101(a) reads:

> “When an employer fails to pay wages or commissions due an employee, **the Commissioner shall serve a written demand** on the employer … If payment is not made within ten days after service of the demand, **the Commissioner may charge and collect** the wages earned … **and a penalty**.”  

Two phrases are decisive.^1 First, **“the Commissioner shall serve a written demand.”** The statute makes *the* commissioner—not an employee—the entity authorized to initiate any enforcement proceeding concerning unpaid wages or commissions under this subsection.  

Second, **“the Commissioner may … charge and collect … a penalty.”** The conditional language (“may”) ties the imposition of any monetary penalty directly to *the commissioner’s* exercise of authority after an unsatisfied demand has been served. The statute does **not** contain language granting an employee an independent right either to serve such a demand or to sue for a penalty without involving the commissioner.^2  

Because both elements are mandatory—the commissioner's demand and subsequent authority—the ordinary grammatical construction leaves no room for an alternative private right concurrent with—or independent from—the statutory scheme.^3 The Court therefore read § 181.101(a) as creating **an exclusive enforcement mechanism** whereby “average‑daily‑wage” penalties flow only through a state‐directed process.



## Distinction Between a Civil Action Under § 181\.171 and Penalty Enforcement Under § 181\.101  

Minnesota law provides two parallel pathways for wage‐related relief:

1️⃣ **A civil action** expressly authorized by § 181.171(1). That provision states an aggrieved person may bring “a civil action seeking redress” for violations of certain MPWA sections—including §§ 181.​101—and that “when an employer is found… liable for compensatory damages **and other appropriate relief**.”^^4  

2️⃣ **The commissioner’s enforcement power** embodied in § 181.101(a), which permits collection *and* assessment of a statutory penalty *only* after a commissioner's written demand has been ignored.^5  

The critical distinction lies in **who initiates relief** and **what kind of relief is available** through each route:

*Under § 181.​171*, an employee may sue directly for traditional damages (back wages, liquidated damages) *and* any civil penalties **if those penalties are expressly provided in the violated section**.^6 In other words, when Congress writes “a civil penalty” into a particular provision, an aggrieved employee can pursue it within his own lawsuit because Congress has already linked such sanctions expressly to private litigation.^7  

*Under § 181.​101*, however, although it mentions “a penalty,” it couples that reference exclusively with *the commissioner's* power following his own demand.) This coupling indicates Congress intended *only* administratively imposed sanctions—not private recovery—under this subsection.^8 Consequently, despite Cohen’s reliance on § 181​.171’s language allowing recovery of “civil penalties … provided … in the section violated,” he cannot invoke those provisions when his injury stems from a violation identified solely in § 181.​101 because *that* subsection does not allocate any private right to impose its own penalty.^9  

The Eighth Circuit treated these two avenues as mutually exclusive statues rather than overlapping ones—a distinction mandated by their textual differences.



## Legislative History Showing Intentional Delegation to the Commissioner  

Statutory construction often looks beyond isolated language when similar provisions illuminate legislative intent.^10 The Court turned to Minnesota statutes adjacent to §§ 180–182 which demonstrate parliament’s **deliberate choice** to vest penalty‐imposing power in the labor commissioner rather than individual employees:

* **§ 181​.​13(a)** authorizes employees who have been discharged to file either a wage claim or “charge [and] collect a *penalty*” themselves after giving notice—but crucially this sentence contains its own demand mechanism directed at the employer before permitting private recovery.\  
* **§ 199​.​14**, subd.​2 likewise permits employees who quit or resign “to recover unpaid wages … **and may charge and collect a penalty**,” again providing its own internal procedure distinct from §§ 180.​101.*  

These provisions illustrate that when Minnesota legislatures wished private actors capable of seeking statutory damages without administrative intermediation they ***explicitly wrote* those powers into their texts**, creating stand‑alone remedial schemes (§ 13(A), § 14). In contrast, §§ 180.​101 /*(the provision at issue)* does nothing beyond assigning such powers exclusively to “the Commissioner.”\^11  

The Court cited *Lagasse v.* Horton—a Minnesota Supreme Court decision emphasizing that legislative silence or omission is itself evidence of intent ("Legislature knows how… if it wishes…")—to argue convincingly that Congress deliberately omitted private enforcement language from § 180.​101.* By comparing these related sections across the same Act Parliament made it unmistakably clear where discretion lay.^12



## Precedent Supporting a Narrow Construction: Allan v.“Offutt Co.” and Hull v.“ConvergeOne”  

Two controlling Minnesota decisions guided the Eighth Circuit’s approach:

1️⃣ **Allan v. R.D.\ Offutt Co.,** 869 N.W.\2d 31 (Minn.\ 2015). The Minnesota Supreme Court declared that *“When the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous… it is assumed…to manifest legislative intent…[and] must be given effect.”*^^13   This pronouncement obliges courts first—and foremost—to honor ordinary grammatical meaning before invoking extrinsic tools such as legislative history or purpose.*  
  
2️⃣ **Hull v.<i>ConvergeOne</i>,** 570 F.Supp.\!3d 681 (D.Minn.\ 2021), aff’d n.r.e..   At trial level Hull emphasized precisely what Section § 180.​101 demands—a written demand served by •the• labor commissioner—and rejected plaintiffs’ arguments asserting private standing based solely upon phraseology suggesting “penalties.”^^14  

Both cases reinforce two principles vital here:  
*If statutory text plainly identifies *who* performs each step,* courts treat it as conclusive.<sup>15</sup>  
*Interpretations expanding private rights beyond plainly granted authority run afoul of precedent.*  

The Eighth Circuit aligned its decision squarely with these precedents:\_ there was no ambiguity regarding who could assess/collect MPWA penalties under § 180.​101(a); ergo any contrary inference would contravene established Minnesota jurisprudence.



## Applying the Plain‑Meaning Rule Versus a Purposive Approach  

In many contexts litigants argue substantive goals—deterrence against wage theft or equitable compensation—should inform interpretation even when textual phrasing seems narrow (“purposive” reading). Yet Minnesota courts apply the well‑settled hierarchy articulated in *Allan*: start with literal meaning; turn only if language is ambiguous (§ 57 C.R.S.).^16   

Here textual analysis revealed unambiguous diction:

*“When an employer… [fails]… , **the Commissioner shall serve…**, If payment… [is] not made within ten days … **the Commissioner may charge … and a penalty**.”*

Because each operative term (*Commissioner*, *shall serve*, *may charge*, *penalty*) has ordinary meanings leaving little room for alternate readings,^17 there was no motive—or need—to employ extratextual purpose arguments such as discouragement rhetoric crooned elsewhere in wage‐law literature.^18  
  
Moreover contemporaneous statutes seeking comparable deterrence (*§ 13(A)*/*§ 14*) expressly codify private rights where lawmakers intended them,^19 implying deliberate limitation elsewhere rather than oversight.^20 Thus even pursuing national policy rationales would conflict with plenary congressional design evident across statutes.*   Accordingly MAUCC used dissenting opinions applying “purposivism’’ alone without considering plaintext would represent misreading rather than nuanced adjudication.



## Conclusion: No Genuine Issue Exists Regarding Cohen’s Penalty Claim  

Having parsed ‑both syntactically and historically‑ §§ 180.​101(a), distinguished it from ‑the parallel remedial avenue contained in ‑§ 171(1), examined related statutes[k] confirming legislative delegation likewise set aside private suability,,&& supported its view via controlling precedent(*Allan*, *Hull*)​ whyic & finally grounded their method firmly upon enunciated rules&#x2014;a plaintiff cannot displace expressly drafted constraints merely by reciting broader policy purposes.—­ 

Consequently,
1️⃣ there was **no factual dispute** over which body holds authority over MPs average‑daily‐wage penalties;
2️⃣ there was ***no legal dispute*** over whether field `average-daily-wage` adjudicated herein lies exclusively within Commission / administrative enforcement;
3️⃣ thus rubric teleological aims aside – summary judgement filed by Consilio stood solidly upon undisputed statutory text alone::(*Eighth Cir.* de novo standard (§ Karlen))^[21]⁠†—
4️⃣ Accordingly there was “no genuine issue of material fact,” satisfying Plaza precedent requiring demonstrable contestation before overturning summary judgment.[22].

Affirming these conclusions preserved uniform administration—a single governmental entity enforces equal punitive standards throughout Minnesota—and discouraged piecemeal private litigation seeking what Congress expressly reserved for agency enforcement.

# Chapter 4 – The MFLSA Penalty Claim: Standing, Mootness, and Jurisdictional Concerns  

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit confronted a question that arises often in wage‑and‑hour litigation: can an employee sue for a civil‑penalty provision of a state labor statute when the penalty is payable not to the employee but to a state agency?  The court’s analysis brings together statutory interpretation, the modern “citizen‑plaintiff” standing doctrine articulated in *Friends of the Earth Inc.* v. *Laidlaw Env’t Servs.* (528 U.S. 167 (2000)), and mootness jurisprudence such as *United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp’n Ass’n* (393 U.S. 199 (1968)) and *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle* (455 U.S. 283 (1982)).  This chapter walks you through each element of the appellate court’s reasoning so you can see how a judge moves from the text of a penalty provision to the constitutional prerequisites for judicial review.

---

## The MFLSA’s Civil‑Penalty Scheme  

### The Statutory Text  

The Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (“MFLSA”) parallels the federal Fair Labor Standards Act but adds a separate civil‑penalty mechanism.  Under § 177.27, subd. 8, an employer who willfully fails to pay overtime wages may be assessed a civil penalty *by the Commissioner* of Labor and Human Rights.  The statute specifies that:

1. **Overtime wages** are “payable to the aggrieved employee” (§ 177.25).  
2. **Liquidated damages**—the additional amount equal to unpaid overtime—are likewise “payable … to the employee.”  
3. **Civil penalties** “shall be assessed … by the Commissioner” and “collected by the state” (the statute does not expressly grant an employee a private right of action for those penalties).

The Eighth Circuit therefore had to determine two things:

* Who may bring a suit for MFLSA penalties?  
* Whether Cohen’s alleged injury satisfies standing requirements when any recovery would flow directly into state coffers rather than into his pocket.

### Distinguishing Money Owed Directly to Employees from State‑Collected Penalties  

The district court correctly treated Cohen’s overtime wage claim and liquidated‑damages claim as **monetary damages**—compensation owed directly to Cohen under § 177.25 and § 177.27(b).  Those claims are classic wage‑and‑hour causes of action: an employer’s breach creates an immediate personal financial loss that the employee can recover through a private suit.

By contrast, **civil penalties** under § 177.27(8) serve a different purpose:

* **Deterrence:** They punish an employer’s unlawful conduct beyond mere compensation.
* **State enforcement:** The penalty amount is deposited with the Commissioner—not with the employee who suffered the wage loss.

Because the penalty is not intended as remedial compensation for Cohen himself, the statute does not automatically confer on him a private cause of action for it.  This distinction matters because constitutional standing hinges on whether an alleged plaintiff has suffered—or is at risk of suffering—a **personal** injury that can be redressed by his own lawsuit.

---

## The *Friends of the Earth* Test for Citizen‑Plaintiff Standing  

In *Friends of the Earth*, the Supreme Court articulated a four‑part inquiry that lower courts use when a private party seeks enforcement of a civil‑penalty provision:

1️⃣ **Statutory Intent:** Did Congress (or—in Minnesota’s case—the legislature) intend to create a private right of action?  
2️⃣ **Personal Injury:** Does the plaintiff suffer an injury “of the type” that Congress intended to prevent?  
3️⃣ **Causation:** Is there a causal connection between plaintiff’s injury and defendant’s conduct?  
4️⃣ **Redressability:** Will granting relief (including any civil penalty) likely remedy that injury?

If any prong fails, standing is absent and the court must dismiss—or at least refrain from deciding—the claim.

Although *Friends of the Earth* involved an environmental enforcement statute whose penalties flow directly to a federal fund, its analytical framework functions as a “template” for any citizen‑plaintiff penalty suit—including wage‑and‑hour statutes like Minnesota’s MFLSA.

---

## Applying *Town of Chester* & *Davis v. FEC* Standards  

The Eighth Circuit further refines standing analysis through cases such as *Town of Chester v. Laroe Estates* (581 U.S. 433) and *Davis v. Fed. Election Comm’n* (554 U.S. 724).  Both decisions stress three core elements:

* **Injury in fact** – concrete, particularized harm.
* **Causal traceability** – a direct link between wrongful conduct and injury.
* **Redressability** – any relief granted must actually alleviate that injury.

These Supreme Court cases dovetail with *Friends of the Earth*, reinforcing that “a citizen plaintiff cannot vindicate a merely abstract grievance.”  In practice, this means that when penalties are payable solely to a government agency, courts scrutinize whether an individual employee can point to an injury distinct from what any other aggrieved worker could assert.

---

## Does Cohen Suffer a Concrete Injury Sufficient for Standing?  

### The Plaintiff’s Argument  

Cohen contended that because Consilio’s premium‑overtime policy eliminated overtime premium pay—contrary to Minnesota law—he suffered an ongoing violation that justified both remedial damages (already paid) **and** civil penalties intended to deter future misconduct.  He argued that he has “a concrete interest” in seeing Consilio penalized because:

1. He remains subject to an unlawful employment rule.
2. Penalties would discourage Consilio from reinstating its illegal policy.
3. Enforcement reflects his personal stake in ensuring compliance with state labor law.

### The Court’s Counter­analysis  

The Eighth Circuit noted two obstacles:

1️⃣ **Statutory limitation on who may seek penalties** – § 177.27 makes clear that only *the Commissioner* may assess and collect penalties; it does not expressly grant employees any private right “to prosecute” those penalties.
2️⃣ **Lack of individualized harm beyond what is remedied by wage payments** – Cohen had already been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages he claimed under § 177.*(b)*–§ 177.*c*.  What remained was a possible additional payment—not to him—but into state coffers, which does not compensate his personal loss or prevent future loss unless he can show ongoing exposure.

Because Cohen could not point to an injury “of the type” contemplated by § 177.27—i.e., he was not personally deprived of money by any further failure—it was uncertain whether he satisfied prong 2 (personal injury) of both *Friends·of·the·Earth* and *Town·of·Chester*.  Moreover, without sovereign enforcement action by the Commissioner there was no clear mechanism by which Cohen could obtain those penalties; thus his ability to obtain redress was doubtful—a redressability concern under prong 4.

---

## Mootness Considerations Under *Concentrated Phosphate* and *City of Mesquite*  

Even if standing were potentially satisfied, courts must ask whether there remains “a live controversy.”  A claim becomes moot when:

* The underlying conduct has ceased;
* No reasonable expectation exists that it will recur;
* No longer presents any concrete dispute capable of resolution (see *United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Exp’n Ass’n*, 393 U.S.~199).

Conversely, *City of Mesquite* reminds us that voluntary cessation does **not** automatically render a case moot if there remains danger that unlawful conduct could resume or if rights remain violated in perpetuity.

### Facts Relevant To Mootness  

The opinion highlighted two factual knots:

1️⃣   Consilio had already paid Cohen all overtime wages & liquidated damages stemming from its premium‑overtime policy.
2️⃣   The parties could not agree on whether Consilio had since abandoned—or simply suspended—the illegal policy after settlement payments were made.

If Consilio had completely abandoned its illegal overtime regime—evidenced perhaps by new written policies or training—that would suggest no further risk of non‑payment; consequently Cohen’s insistence on civil penalties might become moot (*Concentrated Phosphate*) because nothing left unfinished could ever recur.

If, however, there remained uncertainty about Consilio's ongoing practices—a plausible scenario given only settlement payments were made but no formal rescission agreed upon—the dispute over future violations would persist (“absolutely clear” yet unanswered). In such circumstances courts retain jurisdiction (*City of Mesquite*) because plaintiffs retain a concrete interest in ensuring compliance moving forward.

Given these unresolved facts at summary‐judgment stage, the Eighth Circuit concluded it could not safely pronounce on either standing or mootness without further development on which facts are true—a classic situation where summary judgment is inappropriate because genuine issues of material fact exist concerning jurisdictional prerequisites themselves.

---

## Vacatur of Summary Judgment and Remand for Jurisdictional Determination  

Because statutory interpretation alone did not resolve whether Cohen possessed standing nor whether his penalty claim was moot, the appellate panel declared:

> “We vacate the district court’s grant of summary judgment as to Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand for further proceedings.”  

In appellate practice this language serves two purposes:

1️⃣ **Vacatur** erases—or neutralizes—the lower court’s judgment insofar as it rests on assumptions about jurisdiction or justiciability.
2️⃣ **Remand** instructs the trial court to address those assumptions afresh: first determine whether there's personal stake sufficient for standing under *Friends·of·the·Earth*, then decide if any continuing violation keeps his claim alive under mootness doctrines (*Concentrated Phosphate*, *City·of·Mesquite*).

On remand, the district court will likely hold separate hearings on (a) whether § 177.27 grants any private right at all; (b) whether Cohen can demonstrate individualized fear of recurring unlawful overtime practices; and (c) whether evidence shows Consilio has formally rescinded its premium‑overtime policy or merely paused it pending litigation settlement.

---

## Putting It All Together: How an Appellate Court Analyzes Penalty Claims  

When you read an appellate opinion tackling penalty provisions like those in *Cohen v.*​ Consilio, keep these analytical anchors in mind:

| Step | Question | Source |
|------|----------|--------|
| 1️⃣   | Does statute confer a private right? | Statutory text & legislative intent |
| 2️⃣   | Does plaintiff have personal injury? | *Friends·of·the·Earth*, *Town·of·Chester*, *Davis v.FEC* |
| 3️⃣   | Is injury caused by defendant’s conduct? | Causation principle |
| 4️⃣   | Is relief redressable? | Whether award would benefit plaintiff directly |
| 5️⃣   | Is there still a live controversy? | Mootness standards from *Concentrated Phosphate*, *City·of·Mesquite* |
| 6️⃣   | If any prong fails → No jurisdiction → Vacate/Remand | Procedural consequence |

If at any stage you encounter uncertainty—especially about factual matters like whether an employer continues illegal behavior—the appropriate remedy is typically **denial** or **vacatur** of summary judgment rather than affirmation so that those factual placeholders can be fleshed out at trial level.


---

### Takeaways for Law Students

1. **Statutes often draw sharp lines between compensatory relief (for employees) and punitive/penalty relief (for agencies).** Don’t assume every dollar ordered by law can be recovered by an aggrieved worker.
2.<br>**Citizen‑plaintiff standing demands more than being “injured in abstract.”** Courts look first at who Congress intended could sue.
3.<br>**Mootness isn’t just about payment being made—it’s about ongoing legal risk or continued violation**, especially where statutes aim at deterrence.
4.<br>When appellate courts **vacate**, they’re putting back into play questions about jurisdiction—not necessarily merits—that were either omitted or decided without sufficient factual development.
5.<br>Finally, always match your analysis file-by-file with what appears—both textual language and procedural posture—in the opinion you’re dissecting.



--- 

By grasping how §§ 181.*X*’s Louisiana counterpart illustrates similar reasoning—and how this Eighth Circuit panel applied constitutional doctrine while respecting state statutory nuances—you’ll be prepared both to parse similar appellate opinions quickly and to predict where litigation will likely stall on topographical grounds such as standing or mootness rather than on substantive wage‑law facts alone.

# Chapter 5 – The MWTA Claim and Injunctive Relief: Procedural Deficiencies and Dismissal Rationale  

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s dismissal of Cohen’s Minnesota Wage Theft Act (“MWTA”) claim and declined to address his request for injunctive relief.  Understanding why the appellate court dismissed these portions requires a close reading of three intersecting elements: the statutory notice provisions at issue, the effect of Cohen’s stipulations on any remaining monetary remedy, the Court’s reliance on precedent that bars claims unsupported by statutory authority, and the procedural rules governing when a plaintiff may raise new forms of relief such as injunctions.  This chapter walks you through each element, explains the underlying legal concepts, and shows how they fit together in the Court’s reasoning.

---  

## Statutory Notice Requirements under § 181.032(f) & (d)(4)  

The MWTA governs a limited set of employer‑employee wage violations in Minnesota.  Two subsections—§ 181.032(f) and § 181.032(d)(4)—create affirmative duties for employers to give employees written notice when they change certain terms of employment that affect compensation.  In *Cohen*, Consilio’s August 2019 email announcing that overtime would be paid at “base pay rate” instead of a premium was alleged to violate those notice provisions because it purportedly changed overtime pay without providing the statutorily required written notification.

To succeed on an MWTA claim based on a notice failure, a plaintiff must demonstrate three things:

1. **Statutory Duty** – The employer had an express duty under § 181.032(f) or (d)(4) to furnish written notice before implementing the change.  
2. **Violation** – The employer failed to provide such notice.  
3. **Remedy** – The statute supplies a remedy—typically either monetary damages or a civil penalty—*if* the violation is proven.

In *Cohen* there was no dispute that Consilio changed its overtime policy without giving written notice conforming precisely to those subsections; instead, the dispute centered on whether § 181.032(f) or (d)(4) actually provide a civil‑penalty remedy that Cohen could recover directly from Consilio.

---  

## Lack of Statutory Language Authorizing Civil Penalties for Failure to Give Notice  

The key textual inquiry is whether §§ 181.032(f) or (d)(4) contain language permitting a private employee to sue for civil penalties when an employer neglects its notice obligations.  The Eighth Circuit applied Minnesota’s ordinary rule of statutory construction: **plain meaning controls** when a statute’s language is clear and unambiguous【1】.  

Both subsections describe precisely what an employer “shall” do regarding notice but stop short of stating that any civil penalty is “provided for” in those sections.  Unlike § 181.101(a), which explicitly authorizes the Commissioner to assess penalties after issuing a demand letter, §§ 181.032(f) and (d)(4) contain no comparable penalty language nor do they refer the matter to the Commissioner for enforcement.

The Court therefore concluded that **the statute does not create a private right to civil penalties** for failure to provide required notice:

> “Cohen … points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute.” (*Cohen*, 2025).  

The absence of penalty language meant that even if Consilio had violated its notice duty, Cohen could not recover “average daily wage” or any other statutory penalty directly from Consilio under §§ 181.032(f)/(d)(4).  

This analysis mirrors earlier Minnesota decisions emphasizing that **legislatures must expressly grant penalty provisions**; if they do not, courts cannot read them into the statute【2】.

---  

## Effect of Cohen’s Stipulation That He Received All Wages Owed  

Even assuming that an MWTA violation existed, a plaintiff still needs an enforceable **damage theory**—that is, proof he suffered loss that can be compensated or remedied by statute.  At trial preparation stage both parties entered into a stipulation stating:

> “Cohen has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,”  
> “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages …”  

Because Cohen expressly acknowledged receipt of all wages attributable to his overtime claim, there was **no remaining monetary basis** upon which he could recover anything else under the MWTA—even if such a remedy existed statutorily.  The appellate court noted this fact but emphasized it as part‑of‑the‑reasoning rather than as an independent ground: without damages there is nothing for an MWTA claim seeking money damages—or penalties—to attach to.

Thus two independent bars stood before Cohen:

1. **Statutory barrier:** §§ 181.032(f)/(d)(4) contain no penalty provision.
2. **Factual barrier:** The stipulation eliminated any unpaid wages or liquidated damages that could serve as a compensatory base.

When both hurdles are present, courts typically dismiss the claim outright because it cannot succeed on either *law* or *facts* grounds【3】.

---  

## The Court’s Reliance on *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*  

When faced with arguments lacking statutory support, appellate courts often look to precedent governing **dismissals “for lack of legal authority.”**  In *Cohen*, Judge Kelly invoked *Milligan v.*—a decision holding that when a party offers “no meaningful argument supported by legal authority,” the court may dismiss the claim without extended discussion:

> “Inasmuch as [defendant’s] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority… we do not address it.” (*Milligan*, 2000).  

Applying *Milligan*, the Eighth Circuit observed:

- Cohen offered no citation establishing that §§ 181.032(f)/(d)(4) authorized civil penalties.
- He did not demonstrate how his injury fit within any remedy supplied by those sections.
- Consequently there was nothing for even an appellate review panel “to discuss.”

By integrating *Milligan*, the Court underscored two procedural doctrines:

1️⃣ **Requirement of Legal Authority:** A party must invoke specific statutory text or controlling case law when pleading—or defending—a claim seeking novel relief.
2️⃣ **Judicial Efficiency:** When such authority is absent, courts may summarily dismiss without elaborating on every logical step because further analysis would be moot【4】.

For law students this illustrates how appellate courts balance thoroughness with efficiency: they will engage in substantive analysis only when there is at least one plausible avenue—in text or case law—through which relief might be granted.

---  

## Injunctive Relief Doctrine: Need for an Amended Complaint Under FRCP 15(a)  

Cohen’s original complaint requested an injunction ordering Consilio “to rescind its illegal overtime policy.”  However, after filing summary‑judgment briefs he altered his request by merely demanding “the withdrawal” of the 2019 policy—a narrower form of injunctive relief not evident in his pleadings at first glance.

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a) provides that **an amendment to pleadings is required when new claims or new forms of relief are introduced after filing**, unless they arise out completely from information already contained in either party’s pleading【5】:

> “The court should freely permit amendment when justice so requires.” —*Fed.R.Civ.P.* 15(a).

Because Cohen introduced a substantially different injunctive remedy after filing his complaint—without amending his pleading—the district court correctly concluded it lacked jurisdiction over this nascent request and declined adjudication【6】.

Two points are crucial:

- **Timing matters** – Injunctions must be articulated clearly in the complaint so defendants can respond appropriately during discovery.
- **Formality matters** – Courts enforce Rule 15(a)’s amendment requirement even if parties later agree informally what should be ordered; formal amendment preserves procedural fairness and ensures proper docket entry.【7】

Hence on appeal there was nothing left for review; without a properly filed claim seeking injunctions, there can be no appellate decision on their merits.

---  

## InfoDeli Precedent on Premature Briefing of Unasserted Claims  

The district court also invoked *InfoDeli, LLC* where counsel attempted to argue new theories in briefing rather than through updated pleadings:

> “[N]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs.” (*InfoDeli*, 2025).  

By citing *InfoDeli*, Judge Kelly reinforced that **parties cannot circumvent Rule 15(a)** by raising fresh causes—or fresh versions thereof—in summary‑judgment replies or other post‑pleading motions *without* first amending their pleadings appropriately【8】.

In practice this means:

1️⃣ A plaintiff who decides after filing that she wants an injunction must file a motion to amend under Rule 15(a), giving both parties notice and opportunity to oppose.
2️⃣ Courts will reject attempts by either side (including defendants seeking clarification about an unpleaded remedy) unless pleading requirements have been satisfied.
3️⃣ This mechanism preserves fairness by ensuring all parties know exactly what relief is being sought before costly discovery proceeds toward resolution【9】.

*Cohen* thus serves as an illustration: despite later arguments about withdrawing Consilio’s policy, Cohen never formally amended his complaint; consequently his injunction request fell outside the scope of any adjudicated issue and was left untouched by both trial and appellate courts.

---  

### Synthesis: How Procedural Deficiencies Led to Dismissal  

Putting together these strands clarifies why *Cohen*’s MWTA claim and injunctive request were dismissed:

| Issue | Legal Basis | Effect |
|-------|------------|--------|
| **MWTA penalties** | No explicit penalty provision in §§ 181.032(f)/(d)(4); plain‑meaning interpretation disallows reading one in | No statutory avenue for monetary penalties |
| **Monetary damages** | Plaintiff stipulated receipt of all wages owed | No factual basis for compensation |
| **Lack of supporting authority** | Plaintiff offered no cases/statutes authorizing penalties | Dismissal per *Milligan* |
| **Injunctive relief** | New form raised after filing with no amendment | Dismissal per Rule 15(a); affirmed by *InfoDeli* |
| **Procedural posture** | Both issues were addressed at summary‑judgment stage where pleadings dictate what may be decided | Courts limited their review accordingly |

For students learning how appellate courts scrutinize wage‑law suits—or any employment‑law suit—*Cohen* offers three take‑away lessons:

1️⃣ **Read statutes carefully**; absence speaks louder than presence when it comes to remedies such as civil penalties.
2️⃣ **Stipulations can extinguish damage claims**, even if liability remains alleged.
3️⃣ **Procedural compliance matters as much as substantive arguments**—adequate pleading (Rule 15), timely amendment, and solid supporting authority (as required by *Milligan*) are prerequisites before any merits may be considered.

Understanding these procedural gatekeepers equips future litigators both to craft defensible wage claims and avoid costly dismissals rooted not in factual defeats but in missed procedural marks.

# Chapter 6 – Holding, Disposition, and Implications for Minnesota Wage Litigation  

In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* the Eighth Circuit was asked to decide whether an hourly‑lawyer who had already been paid all wages owed under three Minnesota wage statutes could still recover the civil‑penalty provisions those statutes contain.  The appellate court’s rulings illustrate how courts parse statutory text, apply the de novo standard of review for summary‑judgment decisions, and balance private‑rights enforcement against agency‑only remedies.  This chapter unpacks the disposition of each claim and extracts practical lessons for practitioners who handle Minnesota wage disputes.



## MPWA Penalty Claim – Summary Judgment Affirms Dismissal  

The district court entered summary judgment in favor of Consilio on Cohen’s Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (“MPWA”) claim, which sought “average daily‑wage” civil penalties under §§ 181.101 and 181.171.  On appeal the Eighth Circuit affirmed that judgment because the statutory scheme does **not** give a private employee a direct cause of action for those penalties.

The Court began its analysis by applying its de novo standard for reviewing summary‑judgment rulings: it examined “the evidence and the inferences that may be reasonably drawn … in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party” (see *Karlen v.* Jones Lang LaSalle Americas, Inc., 766 F.3d 863, 866 (8th Cir. 2014)).  The moving party—Consilio—had shown that no genuine issue existed as to whether § 181.101 permits a private plaintiff to collect average‑daily‑wage damages without first involving the Commissioner of Labor and Industry.

Relying on Minnesota precedent controlling plain‑language statutes (*Allan v.* R.D.\ Offutt Co., 869 N.W.2d 31 (2015); *Milner v.* Farmers Ins.\ Exch., 748 N.W.2d 608 (2008)), the Court held that “the plain language” of § 181.101(a) expressly conditions any penalty on **the Commissioner’s demand** and subsequent collection within ten days after service of that demand.  The statute’s own text limits “the ability … to prosecute a claim for wages” while reserving “the [Commissioner’s] power … to charge and collect … a penalty.”  Because Congress deliberately gave the Commissioner exclusive enforcement authority in this provision—a fact underscored by legislative history showing that other Minnesota wage statutes contain explicit penalty provisions when such authority was intended—the Court concluded there was no ambiguous factual dispute warranting trial.  

Consequently, Cohen’s attempt to rely on “the unambiguous meaning” of § 181.101 failed; his private right terminated at recovery of back wages alone.  The appellate affirmation leaves intact only whatever compensatory damages are otherwise available under § 181.171—but those are likewise limited to amounts recoverable through the commissioner’s action—not through an employee–initiated suit.



## MFLSA Penalty Claim – Vacated & Remanded  

Cohen’s Fair Labor Standards Act claim (“MFLSA”) differed from his MPWA claim principally because the federal statute authorizes **civil penalties** payable directly to the United States rather than exclusively through a state agency.  Nonetheless, Cohen’s ability to recover those penalties hinged on two jurisdictional questions: (1) whether he possessed standing after having been fully compensated for overtime wages and liquidated damages, and (2) whether his claim was moot because Consilio’s allegedly illegal overtime policy might no longer be operating.

The Court first noted that “civil penalties … may afford redress … because such penalties may encourage defendants … to discontinue current violations” (*Friends of the Earth v.* Laidlaw Env’t Servs., Inc., 528 U.S. 167 (2000)).  However, it reaffirmed fundamental constitutional limits: a plaintiff must demonstrate a **personal stake** at both initiation and throughout litigation (*Town of Chester v.* Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S._433_(2017)).  In Cohen’s case, although the parties stipulated he had received all delinquent overtime wages and liquidated damages owed under § 7(k) of the MFLSA, they disagreed about whether Consilio’s premium‑overtime policy remained in effect after settlement payments were made.<sup>1</sup>  

If Consilio had irrevocably abandoned the illegal policy—i.e., if its conduct could be said “absolutely clear[ly]” not to recur—the plaintiff’s injury would have ceased, rendering any added civil‐penalty claim moot (*Friends* at 189).  Conversely, if the policy persisted or could feasibly be reinstated, Cohen retained a live personal interest sufficient for standing because he might suffer future unpaid overtime without recourse other than penal sanctions against Consilio.<sup>2</sup>  

Because these factual determinations were undisputedly material—and because neither party had presented evidence resolving them—the district court erred in granting summary judgment on the MFLSA penalty issue without first addressing jurisdictional adequacy and mootness.^3​  
Accordingly, the Eighth Circuit vacated that portion of the judgment and remanded for further proceedings solely focused on whether Cohen possesses standing and whether his claim remains ripe or moot under prevailing facts.



## MWTA Claim – Dismissed for Lack of Statutory Authority  

Cohen’s third statutory theory invoked the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (“MWTA”), alleging that Consilio failed to provide required notice when it altered its overtime compensation scheme—a violation he said should trigger civil penalties under §§ 181.032(f) & (d)(4).  The district court dismissed this claim outright because Cohen offered no statutory language authorizing a private plaintiff “to recover civil penalties” where an employer neglects notice requirements; he merely alleged nonpayment which had already been satisfied by settlement stipulations.



The appellate panel affirmed that dismissal without extensive analysis was appropriate given the absence of any supporting authority.^4​  In *Milligan v.* City of Red Oak*, 230 F​.3d 355 (8th Cir.<em>2000</em>) ends quoted by this Court—“when [the] brief does not support [the] assertion with any argument or legal authority…the issue is waived”—provides direct guidance: Cohen neither cited expressive text granting MWTA civil‐penalty relief nor proffered persuasive authority indicating such an extension was permissible.^5​   Thus there was no genuine dispute capable of reaching trial.



## Injunctive Relief – Not Decided Because It Was Not Properly Before the Court  

Cohen also sought an affirmative injunction ordering Consilio permanently rescind its premium‑overtime policy—a request he originally framed in his complaint but later altered during briefing without filing an amended pleading.^6​  The Eighth Circuit declined adjudication because procedural rules require any new or materially altered claim be presented via an amendment filed within prescribed time limits (§ 15(a), Fed.R.Civ.P.).  

*InfoDeli LLC* illustrated this principle when noting “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints…not opposition briefs.”^7​   Since Cohen did not satisfy those pleading requirements before briefing his appeal, there was simply no “case” concerning injunctive relief before this panel—the issue remained outside their jurisdiction until—or unless—we see a properly filed motion before a trial court.



## Practical Takeaways  

**First**, practitioners must recognize **who** is statutorily authorized to enforce civil‐penalty provisions.  In *Cohen*, Minnesota law confined average–daily‐wage penalties under § 181.101(a) exclusively to **the Commissioner**, whereas federal law permits private plaintiffs—but only conditional upon meeting standing requirements—to pursue MFLSA civil penalties payable to United States Treasury funds.^8​   Misreading these grantor distinctions can lead counsel either to overstate available remedies or miss opportunities where an agency‐only scheme renders private recovery impossible.



**Second**, settlement stipulations—especially agreements confirming “all overtime wages … have been paid”—do **not automatically extinguish** ancillary claims such as statutory penalties when those claims are rooted in distinct statutory rights rather than mere back‑pay calculations.^9​   In *Cohen*, even though Cohen stipulated receipt of all overtime wages under MFLSA, his request for additional civil‑penalty damages survived because those damages arise from a different legislative purpose (deterrence).  However, when settlements explicitly waive *all* claims—including any contemplated statutory sanctions—the waiver forecloses subsequent actions unless parties reserve otherwise.^10​   Counsel should therefore draft settlement agreements with precise language regarding which remedies are waived versus which remain intact.



**Third**, filing **summary‑judgment motions before discovery** can be strategically powerful in wage disputes where parties possess unequivocal payment records or have already negotiated comprehensive settlement stipulations.^11​   Both parties in *Cohen* agreed that reviewing liability via summary judgment would be “most expedient,” allowing them to avoid protracted fact‑finding while preserving narrowly defined issues (e.g., penalty enforceability).   Still, reliance on summary judgment does not preclude later challenges concerning jurisdictional barriers (standing/mootness), which are matters of law rather than fact and consequently survive pre‑discovery dispositive motions—as illustrated by the MFLSA portion being vacated despite an earlier dispositive ruling.



**Fourth**, even though state wage statutes often operate independently from federal constitutional doctrines, **standing and mootness principles govern whether private plaintiffs may pursue penal remedies** against state employers.\*12​   *Cohen* demonstrates how courts apply national standards (*Friends*, *Town of Chester*) intersecting with specific statutory frameworks: without proof that Condor’s offending policy persists or could recur—i.e., without an ongoing injury—the plaintiff cannot sustain a live claim despite an underlying state provision permitting private enforcement.<sup>13</sup>   Thus attorneys must constantly assess both statutory language **and** constitutional viability when framing claims for civil penalties or injunctive relief.



In sum, *Bruce Cohen v.* Consilio teaches future litigants that successful wage‑law advocacy requires meticulous reading of each statute's enforcement scheme, careful drafting around settlement language so as not inadvertently surrender valuable remedial avenues, tactical timing surrounding dispositive motions when evidence supports factual clarity yet jurisdictional questions remain unresolved, and vigilant appraisal of whether any proposed remedy satisfies satisfaction both statutorily **and** constitutionally.
