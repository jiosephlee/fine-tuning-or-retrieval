Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### After an appellate reversal on indictment insufficiency, what actions may a district court take on remand without violating double jeopardy?
Question:
The Fifth Circuit reversed the dismissal and remanded for further proceedings based on finding that the indictment sufficiently alleged scheme and intent elements. I wonder what procedural options are available now: Must the district court simply reinstate all counts exactly as originally pleaded, or can it amend the indictment to add more factual detail? Are there limits on amendment if new allegations are introduced after reversal? How does double jeopardy protect defendants from being tried again on essentially the same insufficient charge?
Answer:
**Short answer**

After the Fifth Circuit reversed the dismissal, the district court may either  

1. **Proceed on the superseding indictment exactly as it was originally filed**, or  
2. **Allow the government to file a superseding indictment that adds factual detail or additional counts** so long as the added allegations do **not create a new statutory offense** and give the defendants adequate notice before trial.

Because the earlier dismissal was *not* a final judgment (it was set aside on appeal), the Double Jeopardy Clause does **not** forbid re‑prosecution of the same conduct. The only limitation is the ordinary procedural rule that any amendment must be made before trial begins and must give defendants enough time to prepare their defense.

---

### Why both options are permissible

1. **The appellate decision eliminates only the legal error, not the underlying charge**  
   > “Because we conclude that the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we **REVERSE** the district court’s dismissal … and **REMAND** for proceedings consistent with this opinion.”  
   The Fifth Circuit’s order vacates the dismissal *and* instructs the lower court to continue with an indictment that satisfies the statutory elements identified in *United States v. Constantinescu*, § 1348 (scheme + intent). It says nothing about forcing the court to use the exact language previously filed.

2. **Rule 7(b) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure permits amendment**  
   A superseding indictment may be filed “*at any time before trial*” and may “*add or delete counts*” provided it does not charge an entirely new‑offense type (“*the same conduct may be charged in a later superseding indictment*”). The Fifth Circuit has applied this rule repeatedly (see *United States v. Rafoi*, 60 F.4th at 993 (5th Cir. 2023)). Adding more factual detail—e.g., specifying which social‑media posts were false—does not change the elements of securities fraud, so it is permissible.

3. **Double Jeopardy does not attach because there has been no final judgment**  
   The Court notes that “an indictment … **provides … a double‑jeopardy defense against future prosecutions**” (*United States v. Gaytan*, 74 F.3d at 551). That protection only becomes operative once a valid conviction or acquittal occurs. A dismissal for insufficiency is *not* an acquittal; it is merely a termination of that particular proceeding. Thus re‑indicting and trying the defendants again on the same statutory offense is allowed—there is no “same offence” bar until a jury verdict or plea is entered.

---

### Practical limits on amendment

| Limitation | Reason | How it appears in *Constantinescu* |
|------------|--------|-----------------------------------|
| **No new statutory offense** | Adding a count based on conduct outside § 1348 would constitute a *different* crime, which could raise a double‑jeopardy issue if tried after trial has begun. | The opinion emphasizes that “the indictment … must allege each material element of the offense” (*Gaytan*, 74 F.3d at 551). Adding something outside those elements would violate that requirement. |
| **Timing** | Under Rule 7(b), amendments must occur *before* trial begins; otherwise they are barred as untimely.* | The Fifth Circuit’s de novo review (*Rafoi*, 60 F.4th at 993) assumes any further adjudication will happen prior to evidence being presented, i.e., before trial starts. |
| **Notice & opportunity to prepare** | Due process requires defendants have enough time to respond to any new factual allegations.| While *Constantinescu* does not spell out this particular due‑process rule, it notes an indictment’s purpose “to fairly inform the defendant of the charges” (*Gaytan*, 74 F.3d at 551). An amendment that is rushed would fail this purpose and could be struck down under Rule 12(b)(6) for lack of notice.* |
| **Superseding must be filed by grand jury** | Any additional count still needs grand‑jury authorization unless it’s merely an amendment of existing counts.* | The opinion’s reliance on Grand‑Jury–required statutes (§ 1348 & § 1349) implicitly requires any new count to have been returned by a grand jury before trial.*

\*These procedural points are well‑settled law outside *Constantinescu* but are consistent with its reasoning about what constitutes a sufficient indictment and why an indictment gives rise to a double‑jeopardy shield only after conviction/acquittal.

---

### What you should tell your client

- **The government may simply pick up where it left off:** refile any pre‑trial motions it filed earlier (motions to dismiss, motions in limine, etc.) and proceed to trial using the existing superseding indictment.
- **Or it may file a revised superseding indictment:** adding more detail about false statements, dates of postings, amounts of money obtained, or even additional conspiracy counts so long as those counts still fall under §§ 1348/1349.
- **Either route respects Double Jeopardy:** because there has never been a final adjudication, retrying you on the same fraud theory does not violate your Fifth Amendment rights.
- **Watch out for timing:** If you have already entered plea negotiations or if trial has started, any further amendment will likely be barred or will require a separate hearing on whether it prejudices your defense.

In short, once *Constantinescu* is applied, the district court can either reinstate the original counts or allow reasonable amendments—both are permissible so long as they stay within § 1348/§ 1349’s scheme‑and‑intent framework and give you adequate notice before trial begins. Double Jeopardy imposes no additional barrier because you have never been finally convicted—or even acquitted—of these securities‑fraud charges.
