Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What standard do appellate courts use when reviewing adequacy of a federal criminal indictment?
Question:
I am reading United States v. Constantinescu where the Fifth Circuit says it reviews sufficiency 'de novo.' I am not clear on what this standard entails. Specifically, does the appellate court treat every allegation in an indictment as true? What elements must be alleged for an offense such as 18 U.S.C. § 1348? How does concern about 'double jeopardy defense' factor into assessing whether an indictment is sufficient? I would appreciate an explanation.
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
On direct‑review appeals a federal appellate court looks at a criminal indictment **de novo** – it does not defer to the district court’s view and it “takes the allegations…as true.”  The court then asks whether, **on its face**, the charging document **alleges every prima‑facie element** of the offense and whether it does enough to give the defendant notice **and a double‑jeopardy shield** against future prosecutions on the same conduct.  

Below is a step‑by‑step explanation based on *United States v. Constantinescu*, 5th Cir. (2025).

---

### 1. What “de novo” means in this context

> “The court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated.”  
> *United States v. Fontenot*, 665 F.3d at 644 (quoting *United States v. Crow*, 164 F.3d at 234) – cited in *Constantinescu* at §§ II‑III.

The Fifth Circuit therefore **does not evaluate evidence** or decide whether the alleged conduct actually occurred.  Its job is limited to asking: *does the pleading contain enough factual material, taken at face value, to satisfy each legal element of the crime?*  

Because this is a **facial sufficiency** inquiry, any doubt is resolved in favor of upholding the indictment; only a clear omission of an essential element will warrant dismissal.

---

### 2. The three statutory requirements for a sufficient indictment

*Constantinescu* recites the Fifth Circuit’s “well‑settled” rule from *United States v. Gaytan*:

> An indictment must “…(1) enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him; and (3) provide the defendant with a double‑jeopardy defense against future prosecutions.”  
> *Gaytan*, 74 F.3d at 551 (cited verbatim).

Thus an appellate court checks three things:

| Requirement | What the court looks for | Why it matters |
|-------------|--------------------------|----------------|
| **(1) Enumerate every element** | The pleading must contain factual allegations that could support each element if proven true. | Guarantees that no essential piece—e.g., *scheme* or *intent*—is omitted, which would make conviction legally impossible. |
| **(2) Fair notice** | The language must be clear enough for a reasonable person to understand what conduct is prohibited. | Satisfies due‑process rights; the defendant can prepare a defense. |
| **(3) Double‑jeopardy protection** | The charge must be specific enough that future prosecutions cannot rest on a different theory based on the same conduct. | Prevents multiple punishments for one criminal act under separate statutes or theories. |

---

### 3. How this plays out for **18 U.S.C. § 1348** (securities fraud)

*Constantinescu* extracts from Supreme Court precedent what elements constitute § 1348:

1️⃣ **Scheme to defraud** – A “material misrepresentation intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.”  
2️⃣ **Intent to defraud** – An “intent … to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit.”  

(The opinion notes these are borrowed from mail‑ and wire‑fraud jurisprudence.)

Therefore, a **sufficient § 1348 indictment must allege**:

* That defendants **engaged in deception** (e.g., posting false statements about a security).  
* That they **intended** that victims rely on those false statements **to part with money or property** (i.e., buy securities).  
* That they **sought**—or actually obtained—the victims’ money/property (**profit motive**), which satisfies both *scheme* and *intent* requirements.

In *Constantinescu* the court finds those facts present in ¶¶ 13‑14 (“false … information … so that other investors were induced …”) and thus concludes that “the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud.”

---

### 4. Does every allegation get treated as true?

Yes—*but only for purposes of checking whether each legal element appears somewhere in the charging document.* The court does not weigh credibility or decide whether any particular allegation is factual; it merely asks whether **any allegation on its face could support** each requisite element.

If an element is **completely absent**, such as no factual allegation tying conduct to “obtaining money or property,” then under de novo review the indictment fails even if extrinsic evidence could fill the gap.

---

### 5. Where does double‑jeopardy fit into this analysis?

The *Gaytan* rule requires that an indictment’s specificity shelter defendants from being prosecuted again on **the same conduct under a different theory**. In practice this means:

* The charging document must name both **the actus reus** (the deceptive conduct) **and** **the mens rea** (the intent).  
* It must describe, at least generally, ***what* was obtained**—money/property—not merely an abstract injury like loss of information alone.

If an indictment merely alleges “depriving followers of valuable economic information” without linking that deprivation to money or property, it would fail both (§ 1348’s “obtain money/property” requirement) and *(3)*’s double‑jeopardy safeguard because it would leave open another prosecution based on a fraud‑theory focused solely on information loss—as happened in *Ciminelli*, which *Constantinescu* distinguishes from its own facts.

In *Constantinescu*—by alleging that defendants induced purchases (“to obtain…money”), thereby tying deception directly to property loss—the court finds that “[t]he indictment … provides … a double‑jeopardy defense” because any future charge would have to repeat those same factual predicates; it cannot sneak in a new theory predicated on mere informational harm.

---

### 6. Bottom line for appellate review

1️⃣ **De novo & “take as true.”** The appellate panel reads every allegation at face value; no evidentiary assessment occurs.  
2️⃣ **All statutory elements must appear somewhere in those allegations.** For § 1348 those are *(i)* scheme + material misrepresentation and *(ii)* intent + desire for money/property.  
3️⃣ **The charging document must give clear notice and close off any chance for later prosecution on a different theory**, fulfilling the “double‑jeopardy” prong.

When all three prongs are satisfied—as *Constantinescu* holds they are—the indictment survives a facial‑sufficiency challenge; otherwise, under Fifth Circuit precedent (*Rafoi*, *Fontenot*, *Gaytan*) it will be dismissed outright.

#### Quick checklist you can use on any federal appeal

| Check | Does it apply? |
|-------|----------------|
| **De novo review?** Yes – treat allegations as true |
| **Element #1 alleged?** e.g., scheme/false statement |
| **Element #2 alleged?** e.g., intent/knowledge |
| …(continue for all statutory elements) | |
| **Notice sufficient?** Defendant can discern prohibited conduct |
| **Double‑jeopardy shield?** Charging language narrows future theories |

If you can tick every box, your appellate brief can confidently argue that “the indictment is facially sufficient,” just as Judge Engelhardt concluded in *Constantinescu*.
