Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Do social-media followers constitute 'persons' with protected property interests under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, and how is their property interest evaluated?
Question:
The Fifth Circuit treats defendants' followers as victims whose money or property was obtained through deceit pursuant to § 1348's reference to 'any person.' I am uncertain about several aspects: Does 'any person' extend to anonymous online followers who might never be individually identified? What constitutes a protected property interest for those followers—does merely holding cash used to buy securities qualify? How have courts handled victim identification in large internet-based pump-and-dump schemes? Understanding victim standing would clarify why this indictment was deemed sufficient.
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes. Under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 the phrase *“any person”* includes social‑media followers even when they are anonymous, and the protected property interest is simply the **money (or other tangible assets)** they part with when they are fraudulently induced to buy a security. The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* applies the Supreme Court’s *Ciminelli*/*Kousisis* framework and makes clear that an indictment need only allege that “other investors” were induced to surrender money or property – it does not have to name each follower individually.

---

### 1. “Any person” reaches anonymous online followers  

§ 1348 proscribes a scheme “to defraud **any person** … in connection with … any security.”  The Fifth Circuit treats this language exactly as the mail‑ and wire‑fraud statutes do: it is **broadly inclusive** and does not require that each victim be positively identified at the pleading stage.  

> *“The indictment … alleges … that defendants … posted false ... so that **other investors were induced** to purchase the security.”* (Indictment ¶ 13 – *Constantinescu*, ¶ III)

The court therefore concluded that it was sufficient that the government allege a class of victims (“followers,” “other investors”) rather than list every follower by name.  This follows the de novo sufficiency review set out in *United States v. Rafoi*, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023) – a court takes the charge’s factual allegations as true and asks whether all elements of § 1348 are alleged, not whether each victim has been identified.

### 2. What counts as a protected property interest?  

The fraud statutes protect **“money or other property”** (see § 1348(a)(1)).  The Fifth Circuit relies on two Supreme Court decisions:

| Supreme Court case | Holding relevant here |
|--------------------|-----------------------|
| *Ciminelli v.* (2023) | Rejects the “right‑to‑control” theory – mere deprivation of valuable information is **not** a protected property interest. |
| *Kousisis v.* (2025) | Confirms the **fraudulent‑inducement theory**: a defendant violates § 1348 when he uses a material misstatement to obtain **the victim’s money or property**, even if he gives back something of equal value or causes no net pecuniary loss (*Id.* at 1388‑92). |

Applying those rulings, *Constantinescu* explains that what matters is whether defendants sought **to obtain** their followers’ **money** (the cash used to buy shares) or another form of **property** – e.g., the shares themselves once purchased:

> *“[A] defendant commits securities fraud when he ‘engaged in deception’ and ‘obtaining the victim’s money or property’ was ‘an object’ of his fraud.”* (*Kousisis*, 145 S.Ct.at 1389–90)

Thus:
* **Cash used to purchase securities** = money → protected.
* **The securities purchased** = personal property → also protected.
* It is immaterial that some followers might later receive market‑value shares; the crime lies in obtaining their money through deception (*Kousisis*, supra).

### 3. How do courts handle victim identification in large internet pump‑and‑dump schemes?  

*Constantinescu* shows how a federal indictment can proceed without enumerating every follower:

1. **Class‑wide allegation** – The indictment refers generically to “followers” or “other investors,” satisfying § 1348’s requirement that each element be alleged (*III*).  
2. **Sufficiency focus, not specificity** – Under *Rafoi* and *Fontenot*, appellate review looks at whether an alleged scheme “to obtain … any money or property” has been pleaded, not at the precise count of victims (*II*).  
3. **Later fact‑finding** – Once the case survives dismissal, discovery can uncover how many followers actually transferred money, which may affect sentencing enhancements or restitution calculations, but those details are irrelevant to *whether* an indictment is facially sufficient (*IV A–B*).

In practice this means prosecutors do not have to identify each anonymous follower at filing time; they must only show that defendants carried out a deceptive plan intended to induce some class of persons—including unidentified social‑media followers—to part with money or other property.

---

### Bottom line

* **Followers = “persons.”** The statutory term is expansive enough to cover anyone who could be induced by false statements, even if they remain anonymous.
* **Protected interest = money/property transferred** (cash used for purchases or the securities themselves).  
* **Indictment sufficiency = class allegation + proof of fraudulent inducement**, not individualized naming of every follower.

Consequently, an indictment such as the one at issue in *Constantinescu* meets all statutory requirements for charging a pump‑and‑dump scheme under § 1348, and courts will treat social‑media followers as proper victims so long as they were deceived into surrendering money or other tangible assets.
