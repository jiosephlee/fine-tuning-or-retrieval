Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### How do courts resolve conflicts when different states govern different aspects of a contract claim (e.g., Illinois for TOS vs. New York for implied contract)?
Question:
In this case, Illinois law was applied to evaluate whether Santos could rely on Cameo’s Terms of Service, while New York law governed the alleged breach of implied contract claim. What choice‑of‑law principles guide courts in such mixed jurisdictional scenarios, and can one state’s doctrine on third‑party beneficiaries override another state’s substantive contract analysis?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Second Circuit solves a “mixed‑jurisdiction” dispute by **applying each state’s substantive law only to the claim that it governs**.  In *Santos v. Kimmel* the court treated the two contract theories as separate causes of action and gave each its own choice‑of‑law analysis:

| Claim | Governing Law | Reason for applying that law (as explained in the opinion) |
|-------|---------------|------------------------------------------------------------|
| Direct breach of Came​o’s Terms of Service | **Illinois** | The Terms of Service contain an express choice‑of‑law provision that makes Illinois law applicable. The court therefore looked to Illinois precedent on third‑party beneficiary rights (“a strong presumption against conferring benefits to non‑contracting third parties”). *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur*, 68 N.E.3d at 976. |
| Breach of implied contract & fraudulent inducement | **New York** | No contractual choice‑of‑law clause covers these claims, so the court applied New York’s ordinary conflict‑of‑laws rule – i.e., the forum state’s substantive law governs a claim arising out of conduct in New York. Under New York law “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” determines whether an implied‑in‑fact contract exists (*Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear‑Brown Grp.*, 744 N.Y.S.2d at 271). |

### Why two different states can govern different parts of a single lawsuit

1. **Choice‑of‑law provisions are controlling when they exist**  
   When a party’s standard agreement (here, Cameo’s Terms of Service) contains an explicit selection of one state’s law, that clause “governs” any dispute **directly grounded in the agreement**. The court therefore applied Illinois doctrines to decide whether Santos could assert any rights under those terms.

2. **Absent a clause, default conflict rules apply**  
   For claims that are *outside* the scope of a contractual choice‐of‐law clause—such as an alleged implied contract between Santos and Kimmel—the Second Circuit follows New York’s ordinary approach: the forum state’s substantive law controls unless a strong public‑policy reason demands otherwise (*see* *Rocky Point Props.*). Because the suit was filed in the Southern District of New York, New York law was used.

3. **Separate analyses prevent one state’s doctrine from overriding another**  
   The court treats each cause of action independently.  An Illinois rule about third‑party beneficiaries is used only to evaluate whether Santos could rely on the Terms of Service; it does **not** replace New York’s test for an implied‑in‑fact contract because those are distinct legal questions.  Consequently, even if one jurisdiction would permit a particular theory, it cannot “override” a different jurisdiction’s substantive analysis for a separate claim.

### Can a third‑party‐beneficiary rule from one state trump another state’s contract analysis?

No—*Santos v. Kimmel* illustrates that each jurisdiction’s rule applies only within its own claim:

* Under **Illinois** law, Santos would have needed an “express declaration” that the Terms of Service extended benefits to him as a non‑signatory (“presumption against conferring benefits”). The court found none, so his TOS claim failed.
* Under **New York** law, Santos had to plead enough facts showing an “indication of a meeting of minds” creating an implied contract between him and Kimmel. The complaint lacked those essential terms, so his breach‑of‑implied‑contract claim also failed.

Because each claim fell under a different governing regime, neither state’s doctrine could supersede the other; each was evaluated according to its own standards and both were dismissed.

### Takeaway for similar fact patterns

1. **Identify whether any contractual choice‑of‑law clause covers each cause of action** – if yes, apply that clause’s designated state.
2. **For claims outside any clause**, apply the forum’s default conflict rule (often the forum state’s substantive law).
3. **Analyze each claim separately** using its designated state's doctrines; do not attempt to import another state’s substantive rule into a claim governed elsewhere.
4. **Beware preemption** – even when state contract theories survive choice‐of‐law analysis, they may still be barred by federal statutes (as happened with Santos’ copyright claims).

In *Santos v. Kimmel* the Second Circuit followed exactly this two‑track approach, leading to dismissal on both grounds without allowing one state's third‑party doctrine to dominate another state's contract analysis.
