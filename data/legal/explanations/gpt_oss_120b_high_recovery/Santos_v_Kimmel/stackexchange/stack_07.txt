Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### What constitutes ‘out‑of‑pocket loss’ for a fraudulent inducement claim in New York, and can intangible harms like reputational damage satisfy this element?
Question:
The appellate court affirmed dismissal of Santos’s fraudulent inducement claim because he did not allege any out‑of‑pocket loss. Under New York law, what types of losses qualify as out‑of‑pocket? If a plaintiff suffers damage to reputation or lost future revenue opportunities due to fraud, can those be pleaded as sufficient losses, or must they be strictly monetary expenditures incurred at the time of fraud?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Under New York law a fraudulent‑inducement claim requires the plaintiff to plead **“out‑of‑pocket” loss**—that is, a *concrete monetary* loss that he actually spent or was forced to spend because he relied on the fraud.  Purely intangible harms—damage to reputation, hurt feelings, or speculative future lost revenue—do **not** satisfy this element at the pleading stage.  The Second Circuit’s decision in **Santos v. Kimmel**, 2025 WL … (2d Cir. Oct. 6 2025), follows this rule by dismissing Santos’s claim for failure to allege any such loss, citing **Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.,** 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996) and **Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.,** 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142‑43 (2017).

---

### The legal standard

New York courts have long held that a fraudulent‑inducement action is essentially a contract claim and therefore requires proof of **actual economic damage**—the “out‑of‑pocket” loss requirement.

* **Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.,** 88 N.Y.2d at 421 – “A plaintiff must allege an actual out‑of‑pocket loss … a loss that can be measured in dollars.”  
* **Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.,** 29 N.Y.3d at 142‑43 – reiterates that “reliance damages must be quantifiable monetary losses; mental anguish or injury to reputation is insufficient.”

The Second Circuit applied those precedents verbatim:

> “We agree with the District Court’s conclusion that Santos failed to allege that he suffered any actual out‑of‑pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud.”  
> —*Santos v. Kimmel*, 2025 WL … at *9 (2d Cir.*)

### What counts as “out‑of‑pocket” loss?

In practice New York courts treat the following as permissible:

| Type of loss | Example | Why it qualifies |
|---|---|---|
| **Payments made pursuant to the fraud** | The plaintiff paid $10,000 for a product that turned out never to exist. | The money left the plaintiff’s pocket and cannot be recovered except by rescission or damages. |
| **Expenses incurred in reliance on the fraudulent representation** | Costs of shipping goods ordered under false pretenses; professional fees expended because of reliance on a bogus contract.| These are expenditures triggered by reliance and are objectively measurable in dollars. |
| **Loss of value already transferred** | A borrower who advanced funds under a fraudulent promise that the loan would be repaid and now cannot recoup principal.| The lender’s capital has been reduced by a specific amount. |
| **Definite financial obligations undertaken because of the fraud** | Paying interest or penalties on debt assumed under misrepresentations.| Again a concrete dollar amount that can be tallied.|

### What does *not* satisfy “out‑of‑pocket” loss?

* **Reputational injury** – Damage to one’s public image is an intangible harm; it may give rise to separate tort claims (e.g., defamation) but does not count as reliance damages in fraudulent inducement.
* **Speculative future lost revenue or profit** – Unless an actual loss has already been incurred (e.g., money spent on marketing based on a false promise), forecasts of missed opportunities are too conjectural at pleadings.
* **Emotional distress / mental anguish** – Even though these injuries may be real and compensable under other theories (e.g., intentional infliction of emotional distress), they do not meet the “out‑of‑pocket” threshold.

### Why Santos’s claim fails

Santos’s complaint alleged that Kimmel used his videos without permission and that this conduct constituted fraud. However:

1. He did not assert any dollar amount he paid or spent because of Kimmel’s alleged misrepresentations.
2. He only complained about being “mocked” on television and about damage to his reputation as a public figure.
3. The court therefore found no factual allegation satisfying **Lama Holding**’s out‑of‑pocket requirement and dismissed the claim per *Connaughton*’s guidance.

### Practical takeaways for pleading

1️⃣ **Quantify every dollar spent** – If you paid for a service/product based on false statements, state exactly how much you paid and when.

2️⃣ **Identify ancillary expenses** – Shipping costs, legal fees incurred *immediately* because you relied on the fraud can be included.

3️⃣ **Separate intangible harms** – If your reputation suffered you may need an additional cause of action (defamation, IIED). Do not rely on it to satisfy fraudulent inducement’s damage element.

4️⃣ **Future losses must be shown as “actual” payments already made** – Prospective lost profits are not enough unless you can demonstrate money you already expended trying to capture those profits (e.g., marketing campaigns funded because of the misrepresentation).

---

**Bottom line:** In New York fraudulent‐inducement suits the plaintiff must plead a concrete monetary loss—that is, money actually taken from his pocket because he relied on the fraud.  Damage to reputation or speculative future earnings are *not* sufficient to meet this statutory element; they require separate legal theories if recovery is desired. The Second Circuit’s decision in **Santos v. Kimmel** illustrates how strictly courts enforce this pleading requirement.
