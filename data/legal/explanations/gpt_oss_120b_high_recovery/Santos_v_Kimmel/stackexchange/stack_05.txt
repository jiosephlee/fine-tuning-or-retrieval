Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Can a non‑signatory enforce provisions of an online Terms of Service as a third‑party beneficiary under Illinois law?
Question:
Santos was not a party to Cameo’s Terms of Service but sued based on alleged breaches of those terms. The opinion cites Illinois’s strong presumption against granting benefits to non‑contracting third parties unless there is an express declaration. What criteria must be satisfied for a non‑signatory like Santos to be considered an intended third‑party beneficiary, and how do courts interpret ‘express declaration’ in website terms?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Under Illinois law a non‑signatory can enforce a contract – including an online Terms of Service (TOS) – **only if the agreement contains a clear, “express” declaration that the parties intended to confer enforceable rights on that third party**.  The Second Circuit applied that rule in *Santos v. Kimmel* and found no such declaration in Cameo’s TOS, so Santos cannot sue on its provisions as a third‑party beneficiary.

---

### How Illinois treats third‑party beneficiaries

1. **Strong presumption against third‑party benefits**  
   Illinois cases hold that contracts are presumed *not* to create rights for anyone who did not sign them.  The Court quoted *Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp.*, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. Ct. App. 2016):

   > “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.”

2. **Two elements must be satisfied**  

   | Element | What it means | How the court looks for it |
   |---|---|---|
   | **(a) Intent to benefit a specific person or class** | The contracting parties must have **intended** the benefit to vest in the non‑signatory. | Courts read the language of the agreement itself; they will not infer intent from extrinsic evidence unless the text is ambiguous. |
   | **(b) “Express” declaration of that intent** | The intent must be expressed in words that are **clear and unambiguous**—so clear that it is treated as though the parties had literally named the beneficiary. | Phrases such as “this Agreement shall be enforceable by any user of the Site” or “the parties intend to confer rights on …” satisfy this requirement. A vague reference (“the Agreement may affect others”) does **not**.*

3. **What is *not* sufficient**  
   * A mere **third‑party‑beneficiary clause elsewhere in the contract** does not automatically extend every provision to all outsiders; each provision whose benefit is claimed must itself contain an express statement.
   * An implied‑in‑fact “meeting of minds” discovered through conduct alone is insufficient under Illinois law—there must be express textual language.

---

### Application in *Santos v. Kimmel*

- **Governing law:** The Court held that Illinois law governs the Cameo TOS.
- **Finding:** The plaintiff “identifies nothing close to an ‘express declaration’ that the relevant provisions of the Terms of Service apply to him or other noncontracting parties.”  
  - The opinion notes that some provisions *do* contain beneficiary language, which illustrates how **when parties intend a provision to be enforceable by a third party they say so expressly**.
- **Result:** Because Santos could not point to any provision that met both elements above, his breach‑of‑contract claim failed on this ground alone.

---

### How courts interpret “express declaration” in website terms

1. **The language must be plain and specific**  
   Courts treat any clause that plainly states “this Agreement benefits …” (or names a class such as “any user”, “any seller”, “any person who purchases through our platform”) as an express declaration.

2. **Context matters but only secondarily**  
   Even if a site’s layout or clickwrap notice suggests an intention to protect users, those cues are irrelevant unless backed by unambiguous text inside the agreement itself.

3. **Absence = no enforceability**  
   If the TOS merely imposes obligations on registered users (e.g., payment terms, content standards) without saying those obligations also bind or protect non‑users, courts will refuse to extend those duties to outsiders.

---

### Bottom line for a non‑signatory like Santos

To succeed as an intended third‑party beneficiary under Illinois law, Santos would have needed:

1️⃣ A provision in Cameo’s TOS that *explicitly* says something like “the obligations set forth herein are enforceable by any individual who creates content for Cameo,” or  
2️⃣ A similarly clear clause naming “any person who supplies videos pursuant to this Agreement” as a beneficiary.

Because no such clause existed—and because other provisions with explicit beneficiary language were limited to specific classes—Santos could not invoke those terms against Kimmel or ABC.

---

**Takeaway for law students:**  

When evaluating whether a person who never signed an online contract can sue on its terms, first ask:

- Does Illinois (or the controlling state’s) law presume against third‑party benefits?  
- Does the contract contain unmistakable text showing both (a) intention to benefit and (b) an express statement effecting that intention?

If either prong is missing—especially the “express declaration”—the claim will be dismissed, exactly as *Santos v. Kimmel* demonstrates.
