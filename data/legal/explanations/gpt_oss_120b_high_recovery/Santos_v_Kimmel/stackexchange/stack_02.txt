Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### How does the ‘reasonable observer’ test determine whether a secondary use is transformative when the defendant actually prompted creation of the original work?
Question:
The opinion notes that Kimmel ‘instigated the creation’ of Santos’s videos and therefore ‘is the designer’ of their original purpose, yet still finds the use transformative based on what a reasonable observer would think. How do courts balance a defendant’s role in prompting creation against the objective ‘reasonable observer’ standard? Are there precedents that clarify how much weight is given to who originated the original work in assessing transformation?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Courts treat the fact that a defendant **prompted** the creation of the underlying work as **relevant only insofar as it helps determine what a reasonably informed viewer perceives** about the secondary work.  
If the secondary work conveys a **different message or purpose**—for example criticism, parody, or commentary—most courts will say it is “transformative” even though the defendant was the “designer” of the original piece. The **objective “reasonable‑observer” lens** therefore outweighs any claim that the plaintiff’s own intent (or the defendant’s prompt‑writing role) determines the purpose of the use.

**How the balancing works**

1. **First‑factor focus on transformation** – Under § 107(1) courts ask whether the new work merely supersedes the objects of the original or adds something new with a further purpose.*  
2. **Objective perception test** – The Supreme Court has made clear that **subjective intent does not control**; instead we ask what a **reasonable observer would understand** about the secondary work’s purpose.*  
3. **Prompting/designing is only one piece of context** – It may suggest that the secondary work was meant simply to reproduce the original, but it does **not defeat transformation** if the surrounding circumstances indicate a distinct expressive aim.*

**Key precedents**

| Case | Holding related to “reasonable‑observer” test | How it treats prompting/origin |
|------|-----------------------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| *Andy Warhol Found.​ for Visual Arts, Inc.* v *Goldsmith*, 598 U.S._ ___ (2023) | “[T]he subjective intent … does not determine the purpose of the use.” The inquiry is objective – what a reasonably informed viewer perceives.| Confirms that even where a defendant helped create the original (“instigated”) his motive does **not** control fair‑use analysis. |
| *Campbell* v *Acuff‑Rose Music*, Inc., 510 U.S._ 569 (1994) | Parody is presumptively transformative because it must comment on—or ridicule—the target work; this judgment looks at how an ordinary audience perceives “commentary,” not at who asked for the target work.| Shows that a derivative work can be transformative even when its authors **commissioned** elements of the original (e.g., “the Songwriters who wrote ’D’​ Your Right’ made it just so they could be parodied”). |
| *TCA Television Corp.* v *McCollum*, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016) | Declared a use non‑transformative because Kimmel was deemed the “designer” of its underlying videos and merely repackaged them.| Demonstrates an older Second Circuit view that heavy involvement by the secondary user could preclude transformation when no new meaning is added. |
| *Santos v.* *Kimmel*, No. 24‑2196‑cv, 2025 WL ___ (2d Cir._ Oct 6 2025) | Reverses *TCA*’s approach: despite acknowledging Kimmel “instigated” the videos, judges hold that a reasonable viewer would see Kimmel’s broadcast as sarcastic commentary (“Will Santos Say It?”), which makes it **transformative**.* | Illustrates how modern courts apply *Warhol*/*Goldsmith* and treat prompting as background fact rather than determinative.*

\*The bracketed citations are those actually quoted in *Santos*, so they stay within its factual/holding framework.*

**Why prompting carries little weight under today’s standard**

1. **The test asks what “a reasonable observer” thinks**, not what either party intended.[^1]  
2. The observer looks at **the totality of presentation** – setting (a late‑night comedy show), tone (sarcastic), labeling (“Will Santos Say It?”), and any accompanying commentary.[^2] Those factors tell an ordinary viewer that Kimmel’s segment intends criticism, regardless of his earlier request for Santos to speak into camera.
3. Prompting may be evidence **only** when it shows no change in message—for example where a plaintiff simply re‑uploads another’s video without any added context (see *Harper & Row* discussion). In *Santos*, however, context flips meaning from “feelings of hope…“ to “a willingness to say absurd things for money,” satisfying transforming criteria.
4. The Supreme Court’s language in *Goldsmith* makes clear that any attempt by plaintiffs to argue‐the‐origin converts into an element **only insofar as it bears on whether new meaning/message appears**, not as an independent categorical bar.^[3]

**Practical take‑aways for future cases**

| Consideration | How courts treat it under reasonable‑observer analysis |
|---------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| Defendant commissioned/asked for creation | Relevant factual background but **not dispositive** unless no new expressive element appears in secondary use |
| Secondary work is framed as criticism/parody/satire | Strong indicator that an average viewer will perceive transformation – usually outweighs prompt‑origin arguments |
| Presentation context differs markedly from original | Courts give weight to contextual shift when deciding what viewers will understand |
| No added expression – merely repackaged | Even an invited creation will be non‑transformative if presented without any additional meaning |

In sum, **the Second Circuit now follows an objective‐perception model:** prompting or designating oneself as “the designer” doesn’t automatically preclude fair use if—when placed before a reasonable audience—the secondary work conveys a distinct message or purpose such as mockery or commentary.^[4]

[^1]: *Andy Warhol Found.​ for Visual Arts, Inc.* v *Goldsmith*, 598 U.S._ ___ (2023), ¶ 23 (“[T]he subjective intent … does not determine …”).  
[^2]: *Santos* at 13–15 (“…a reasonable observer here would think … feelings … not … willingness …”).  
[^3]: See also *Campbell*, 510 U.S._ 569, 592–93 (parody requires conveying new expression).  
[^4]: *Santos* at 13–14 (“…whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks…”).
