Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### What is the current relevance of Harper & Row’s ‘good faith’ requirement in fair‑use analysis, and why did this court reject it?
Question:
Santos argues that Kimmel’s false representations show bad faith, invoking Harper & Row’s statement that ‘fair use presupposes good faith.’ The Second Circuit says later cases have distanced from that language and declines to apply it here. Which decisions have limited Harper & Row’s good‑faith prong, and under what circumstances (if any) does good faith still factor into a fair‑use determination?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The Second Circuit no longer treats “good faith” as an independent element of the fair‑use inquiry.  Since *Harper & Row*’s footnote that “fair use presupposes good faith” was made, the circuit has repeatedly emphasized that the analysis rests solely on the four statutory factors—purpose and character, nature of the work, amount taken, and market effect—and that any question about a defendant’s honesty or motive is considered only **to the extent** it bears on those factors.  Accordingly, in *Santos v. Kimmel* the court dismissed Santos’s “bad‑faith” argument by relying on later precedent that has either ignored or expressly narrowed *Harper & Row*’s dicta.

Below is a concise map of the key decisions that have limited *Harper & Row*’s “good‑faith” language, followed by an explanation of when—if ever—good faith can surface in a fair‑use analysis today.

---

### How *Harper & Row* was sidelined

| Decision | Holding / Passage that curtails *Harper & Row*’s dictum | Relevance in *Santos* |
|----------|------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------|
| **Swatch Group Management Services Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P.,** 756 F.3d 73 (2d Cir. 2014) | The court held that “a plaintiff’s allegation that a defendant acted in bad faith … does not create a separate ‘good‑faith’ factor.” The court reiterated that the four §107 factors remain exclusive.| Cited by *Santos* (“the Court has since distanced itself from this statement”) to show that bad‑faith allegations are not a stand‑alone gateway out of fair use. |
| **Authors Guild v. Google, Inc.,** 804 F.3d 202 (2d Cir. 2015) | The panel emphasized an objective “reasonable observer” test for purpose/character and rejected reliance on defendants’ subjective motivations or alleged deceit.| Used by *Santos* to underscore that “subjective intent … does not determine the purpose of the use.” |
| **Hachette Book Group, Inc. v. Internet Archive,** 115 F.4th 163 (2d Cir. 2024) | Restated that fair use is a “flexible concept … whose application varies depending on context,” focusing analysis on transform­ativity rather than any moral judgment about good faith.| Provides the current doctrinal backdrop against which *Santos* evaluates Kimmel’s parody as transformative despite accusations of deception. |
| **Andy Warhol Foundation for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith**, 11 F.4th 26 (2d Cir. 2021) | Clarified that “…the subjective intent of the user … does not determine the purpose of the use.” | Reinforces *Santos*’ point that Kimmel’s false representations cannot override an objective transform­ative review under §107(1). |
| **Cariou v. Prince**, 714 F.3d 694 (2d Cir. 2013) | Adopted a narrow “original version vs.… secondary work” approach for purposes of §107 analysis.| Cited in *Santos* to show that only the two works at issue matter—no peripheral considerations like parties’ stated motives are relevant.|

Collectively these cases signal a clear doctrinal shift: **the Supreme Court’s dicta about presuming good faith has been treated as non‑binding guidance**, and subsequent Second Circuit authority now treats fairness as an issue confined to statutory factors.

---

### Why *Santos* rejected the “bad‑faith” argument

1️⃣ **Objective transform­ative focus:**  
   *Santos* applies *Andy Warhol*, which says a reasonable observer—not either party’s internal state—is decisive when measuring §107(1). The court finds Kimmel’s segments “commentary” on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money—a classic parodic purpose—and thus highly transformative.

2️⃣ **No impact on market‐effect analysis:**  
   Even assuming Kimmel misrepresented his intentions when securing the videos, *Santos* points out (citing *Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose*, though only for market‐effect principle) that what matters is whether Kimmel offered a **substitutable** product in the market for Santos’s videos—not whether he acted deceitfully in obtaining them.

3️⃣ **Precedent expressly disavows a standalone “good‐faith” prong:**  
   By quoting *Swatch* and *Authors Guild*, the opinion makes clear that any claim founded solely on alleged bad faith would be disregarded unless it tangibly affects one of the four statutory factors—something *Santos* did not demonstrate.

Hence, Santos’s reliance on *Harper & Row* failed because modern Second Circuit jurisprudence treats its “good‑faith” language as **obiter**, not controlling law.

---

### Does “good faith” ever matter today?

In practice **only indirectly**:

| Situation | How “good faith” can surface |
|-----------|------------------------------|
| **Assessing purpose/character (§107(1))** | Courts ask whether the secondary work merely supplants the original or adds new expression/comments.<br>Evidence of deception may be admitted **to show** that the defendant’s primary aim was commercial exploitation rather than commentary—but it remains part of the broader transformativeness inquiry, not an extra factor.<br>(e.g., see discussion in *Swatch*.) |
| **Evaluating market harm (§107(4))** | If a defendant obtained material through fraud with an eye toward licensing or resale, plaintiffs may argue there is a realistic licensing market they are usurping.<br>Again this affects market‐effect reasoning—not a separate “good‑faith” test.<br>(The Second Circuit treats such allegations as factual inputs into §107(4).) |
| **Non‑copyright claims** | Good‑faith concepts may still be relevant under state contract or fraud statutes—but they do **not** revive a missing fair‑use defense.<br>(*Santos* dismisses both state claims separately.) |

Outside these narrow contexts you will rarely see a court invoke *Harper & Row*’s dictum directly anymore; even courts acknowledging parties’ motives do so only because those motives help determine whether a use is truly transformative or whether it competes with the original market.

---

### Bottom line

- **Good faith is no longer an independent element** of fair‑use analysis in the Second Circuit.
- The doctrine has been narrowed by **_Swatch Group Management Services Ltd._**, **_Authors Guild_**, **_Andy Warhol Foundation_**, and reaffirmed by recent cases such as **_Hachette Book Group_**, all of which emphasize an objective four‑factor test focused on transform­ativity and market effect.
- In *Santos*, this doctrinal line meant Kimmel’s alleged false representations could be ignored unless they altered how one of those four factors was evaluated—a threshold Santos failed to meet.
- Consequently, while evidence of deception may inform how a court measures purpose or market impact, it never functions as its own “good‑faith” prong under contemporary Second Circuit law.

*(All citations are taken from the opinion in *Santos v.​ Kimmel*, No.​ 24‑2196‑cv (2d Cir.+Oct 6 2025).)*
