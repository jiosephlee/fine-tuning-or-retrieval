Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Contract Claims vs. Copyright Preemption: Why Santos’s Breach‑of‑Contract Theory Was Doomed  

## Why This Case Matters  

When a celebrity’s likeness is harvested by a late‑night host for a parody segment, the dispute seems like a straight‐forward copyright battle. Yet former Congressman George Santos’s suit against Jimmy Kimmel and ABC tried to sidestep the federal Copyright Act entirely by framing the alleged misuse as a breach of contract and fraudulent inducement under Illinois and New York law. The Second Circuit’s decision—affirming dismissal on both pre‑emptive and pleading grounds—offers a clear warning: in the digital age, state‑law contract theories rarely rescue plaintiffs from federal preemption when the underlying conduct is essentially a copyright exercise. Content creators, platforms, and would‑be litigants should take note, because the ruling tightens the bridge between contract claims and the exclusive federal scheme that governs creative works.

## The Facts in a Nutshell  

Jimmy Kimmel, using fictitious names on the Cameo platform, asked Santos to record short video messages for fans. Santos obliged each request, delivering videos that projected “hope, strength…encouragement.” Kimmel then spliced those clips into his TV series “Will Santos Say It?”—a recurring parody that lampooned Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. In response, Santos sued for copyright infringement and for violations of his contractual rights under Cameo’s Terms of Service (ToS), asserting breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement.

The Southern District of New York granted summary judgment in favor of Kimmel and ABC on all counts. On appeal, Judge Lohier of the Second Circuit reviewed the dismissal de novo under Rule 12(b)(6). The appellate panel affirmed both the copyright dismissal—on fair‑use grounds—and the contract dismissals—on preemption and pleading deficiencies.

## Fair Use at the Pleading Stage  

Before diving into preemption, it helps to understand why the court never needed to parse any alleged damages from Kimmel’s use. The district court applied the four‑factor fair‑use test at the pleading level—a practice sustained by Second Circuit precedent (e.g., *TCA Television Corp.*).  

1. **Purpose and Character** – The court focused on transformation rather than subjective intent, noting that a reasonable viewer would see Kimmel’s clips as commentary about Santos’s “willingness…to say absurd things.” That purpose diverges sharply from Santos’s original intent—to spread positivity—making the use highly transformative.  

2. **Nature of the Work** – The videos were unpublished creations posted on Cameo shortly after recording; their expressive nature is typical of works protected by copyright but does not diminish transformative value in a parody context.  

3. **Amount Used** – Though Kimmel aired entire videos rather than excerpts, precedent recognizes that full reproduction can be justified when necessary for effective parody (*Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose*).  

4. **Market Effect** – The court found no plausible market for “critical reviews or lampoons” of Santos’s own messages; thus no substitutionary harm existed.

The panel concluded that even assuming all facts alleged were true, fair use would bar an infringement claim—so much so that dismissal could be proper before discovery.

## The Preemption Analysis  

Santos’ remaining theories hinged on whether suing under state contract law would circumvent § 301’s preemption clause (“all exclusive rights…are governed by this title”). The district court dismissed breach‑of‑contract and implied‑contract claims as preempted because they were essentially attempts to enforce exclusive rights—namely reproduction and public performance—that already belong to copyright owners.

#### “Qualitatively Different” Test

The Second Circuit has articulated that a state claim is *not* preempted only if it is “qualitatively different” from any exclusive right in § 106—a high bar established in *Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio* (2022). In *Melendez*, the court held that contractual restrictions on unauthorized streaming were indistinguishable from an infringement claim and therefore preempted.

In *Santos*, however, Judge Lohier did not need to reach a detailed “qualitative difference” analysis because Santos failed to allege an enforceable contractual relationship at all:

* **No Party to the Terms** – Under Illinois law (which governs Cameo’s ToS), third parties enjoy a strong presumption against receiving benefits from contracts they never signed. The plaintiff must show an explicit third‑party beneficiary clause—a requirement plainly unmet here.
* **Absence of Essential Terms** – New York courts require pleadings to detail essential contractual terms when alleging implied contracts (*Caniglia*). Santos’ complaint merely pointed to generic ToS language without indicating which provisions he claimed Kimmel violated or how those provisions bound non‑signatories.
* **Failure to Plead Fraudulent Inducement** – New York also demands proof of out‑of‑pocket loss for fraud claims—a factual element missing from Santos’ allegations.

Because these procedural deficiencies rendered his state-law theories untenable on their face, the court declined to analyze whether any residual claim could be “qualitatively different” enough to survive preemption.

## Why Preemption Won’t Be Evaded By Contract Claims  

*Santos* reinforces two intertwined doctrines:

1. **Statutory Exclusivity Is Broadly Protective** – Even when defendants argue they merely breached an online platform agreement rather than infringed a copyright, courts will look through contractual language to see if plaintiffs are pursuing rights already occupied by § 106(1)–(4).  
2. **Third-Party Beneficiary Doctrine Remains Rigid** – Digital platforms routinely embed user agreements within massive ToS scrolls; unless they expressly name an outside party as beneficiary or grant them enforceable rights, those outsiders cannot invoke contractual liability against platform users or affiliates.

Thus a star who creates personalized video content on Cameo cannot later claim breach-of-contract when another user repurposes those clips for satire unless there is an unmistakable clause extending contractual protections beyond signatories—something most platforms deliberately avoid.

## Doctrinal Implications  

### Narrowing State Law Avenues

Plaintiffs often turn to contract or unjust enrichment theories when federal defenses like fair use appear likely to succeed (as they did here). After *Santos*, courts in New York and Illinois are likely to scrutinize whether such suits truly target an exclusive right or merely attempt a parallel remedy beneath another statutory canopy. Expect stricter motions to dismiss where:

- The alleged violation involves copying or public performance.
- The plaintiff is not an actual party to any governing agreement.
- The complaint lacks concrete terms or arguably redundant elements.

### Signal for Platform Drafting

For platforms like Cameo—or newer services built around short-form personalized content—a practical lesson emerges: if you want immunity from third‑party breach complaints based on users’ subsequent exploitation of content, draft your ToS without ambiguous third‑party beneficiary language while ensuring you have clear licensing terms governing reuse. Conversely, if you wish your users (including celebrities) to retain enforceable rights against each other’s derivative uses, you must embed explicit carve-outs spelling out who may—or may not—reproduce content outside the platform’s scope.

### Impact on Parody Litigation

While *Santos* does not reshape fair‑use jurisprudence per se—it simply applies existing Second Circuit standards—it underscores how convenient it is for defendants like Kimmel to rely on parody defenses early on. Plaintiffs considering litigation over satirical uses must confront two formidable hurdles: establishing non‑transformative usage at pleading stage (unlikely when criticism is evident) and overshooting any contractual arguments without precise benefit language.

### Potential Split With Other Circuits?

Some jurisdictions have been more willing than others to recognize “non‑preemptive” state claims when plaintiffs allege fraud or misrepresentation unrelated directly to copying—for example, Fifth Circuit decisions allowing fraud claims when plaintiffs allege deception about ownership rather than mere licensing violations (*Alaska Oil & Gas Corp.*). *Santos* aligns with Second Circuit doctrine that such claims still overlap with exclusive rights unless they clearly target distinct conduct (e.g., fraudulent inducement regarding price terms). If other circuits adopt more lenient standards, we could see divergent outcomes ripe for Supreme Court review—particularly as digital platforms proliferate globally and give rise to novel licensing structures.

## Looking Ahead  

The *Santos* decision may seem like another routine affirmation of existing precedent—but its real significance lies in reinforcing how hard it will be for plaintiffs—celebrity or otherwise—to escape federal preemption through creative contract theories after digital platforms mediate their content creation process. As user-generated media continues its migration onto services ranging from Cameo-like personalization tools up through TikTok duets and AI‑generated deepfakes, litigants will likely confront identical factual patterns: a creator signs off on an online Terms of Service; another user repurposes that material for commentary; now one side wants monetary relief beyond what copyright offers.

Practically speaking:

- Celebrities looking past parity between transactional licensing agreements may need **explicit contractual clauses granting them enforceable control over downstream uses**, perhaps through separate endorsement contracts rather than relying solely on platform ToS.
- Platforms must balance **protective indemnities** against potential liability with enough transparency so users can clearly discern what rights survive beyond initial posting.
- Practitioners should assess early whether “contract” complaints genuinely allege separate legal obligations—or merely shadow intrusion into exclusive copyright territory—in order not to waste resources on doomed pleadings.

In sum, *Santos v. Kimmel* fortifies the gatekeeping role of § 301 across both direct infringement actions and ancillary state-law suits grounded in contracts or fraud allegations related thereto. For those hoping discord between federal copyright law and state-based remedies can be exploited in our increasingly remix-oriented culture—the gate remains firmly shut unless you possess an unmistakable contractual invitation into the arena you wish to sue over.
