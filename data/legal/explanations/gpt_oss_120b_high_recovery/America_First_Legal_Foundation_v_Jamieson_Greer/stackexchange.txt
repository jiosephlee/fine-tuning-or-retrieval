Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Does 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) impose a mandatory duty on the Office of Special Counsel to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings?
Question:
In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* the D.C. Circuit discusses whether OSC must investigate an alleged FOIA violation under § 1216(a)(3) even when the triggers in FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) are not met. I’m confused about how these statutes interact. Does § 1216 create an absolute obligation for OSC to launch an investigation, or is it still discretionary? And if it is mandatory, why did the district court find that OSC’s refusal was only an error of law rather than a deprivation of a statutory right?
Answer:
**Short answer**

5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) gives the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) *authority* to open an investigation when someone alleges an “arbitrary or capricious withholding” of information prohibited by FOIA, but it does **not** create an absolute, enforceable duty that compels OSC to investigate in every case. The D.C. Circuit reads the word “shall” in § 1216(a)(3) together with the permissive language of § 1216(c)—which says OSC “may investigate … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” That combination makes the investigatory power discretionary. Because the statute provides no private right of action for a plaintiff whose request is declined, OSC’s refusal can be judged only as an **error of law** (a misinterpretation of its own authority), not as a deprivation of a statutory right that courts can enforce.

---

### Why the duty is not mandatory

1. **Statutory text** – The opinion quotes the two relevant subsections:

   > “OSC **shall** … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding …” — § 1216(a)(3).  
   > “… OSC **may** investigate and seek corrective action … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” — § 1216(c).

   The D.C. Circuit treats these as complementary provisions. While “shall” ordinarily imposes an obligation, courts interpret statutes so that later language qualifies earlier commands when there is an express grant of discretion. Here, **§ 1216(c)** expressly tells us that OSC *may* undertake such investigations *in the same way* it would handle a prohibited‑personnel‑practice case—i.e., at its own discretion whether to proceed at all.

2. **Court’s reading** – In paragraph B of its opinion the Court says:

   > “the court declined to order OSC to investigate any FOIA policy because, in its view, **section 1216(c) committed that decision to OSC’s discretion**.”  
   
   This captures the Court’s conclusion that even though OSC has authority under § 1216(a)(3), it retains agency‑wide judgment about whether opening an investigation is warranted.

3. **No private right of action** – The decision makes clear that § 1216 does not create a plaintiff‑enforceable procedural right. The Court notes that “a plaintiff claiming informational standing suffers a concrete injury if he is denied information that a ‘statute entitled him to receive.’” But section 1216 never says a requester *is entitled* to receive notice or information from OSC; it merely grants OSC the power **if it chooses** to act.

### Why the district court called it “an error of law” rather than a deprivation of rights

- **Misinterpretation, not violation** – The district court correctly identified that OSC’s reliance on FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) triggers was mistaken; those triggers do *not* limit OSC’s broader investigatory authority under § 1216. Hence, as the appellate panel says, “OSC’s decision not to investigate was based on a legal error.”

- **No enforceable entitlement** – Because § 1216(c) makes the investigatory decision discretionary and because Congress gave no private cause‑of‑action for denial of an investigation, AFL could not demonstrate that it possessed a protected statutory right whose denial caused a concrete injury. Consequently, even though OSC’s refusal was legally erroneous, it did not rise to “deprivation of a statutory right” actionable under Article III.

- **Standing ties it together** – The Court ultimately dismissed on lack of standing: without an enforceable personal right under § 1216 and without concrete injury from OSC’s decision, AFL could not bring an Article III claim. This reinforces why an error in applying the statute does not translate into a justiciable violation.

---

### Bottom line

- **Authority vs. Obligation:** § 1216(a)(3) grants OSC authority *to* investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings; § 1216(c) turns that authority into *discretion*.
- **No mandatory investigative duty:** OSC may decline to open an investigation after weighing its own enforcement priorities.
- **District court’s characterization:** OSC’s refusal was legally mistaken (it misapplied FOIA’s trigger rule), but because there is no private right compelling investigation and because AFL lacks standing, the refusal does not constitute deprivation of a statutory right subject to judicial relief.

Thus, while Congress intended for OSC *to have* investigative power over FOIA misconduct, it did **not** intend for plaintiffs like AFL to sue for enforcement when OSC elects not to act.

### How do courts apply the ‘relaxed’ standing test for procedural‑injury claims under Article III?
Question:
The opinion cites *Center for Law & Education v. Dep’t of Educ.* and says that plaintiffs alleging violation of a procedural right get ‘relaxed’ standing requirements, but they still must show injury in fact and causation. What exactly changes under this relaxed standard? Which elements are still required, and how have courts interpreted "concrete interest" and "redressability" in similar procedural‑injury cases?
Answer:
**Short answer**

When a plaintiff’s claim turns on the denial of a *procedural* right—e.g., the right to an agency‑initiated investigation—courts still apply the three‑prong Lujan standing test (*injury‑in‑fact*, *causation*, *redressability*).  What “relaxes” is **how strictly they enforce the *imminence* and *redressability* sub‑requirements** that normally prevent speculative claims.  The plaintiff must still show:

1. **A concrete, particularized injury** (the “concrete interest”);  
2. **A causal link** between the government’s action and that injury;  
3. **That a favorable judgment will likely cure the injury**, though courts will deem “likely” more loosely when the injury is rooted in a statutory process designed to protect the plaintiff’s concrete interest.

The D.C. Circuit’s discussion in *Center for Law & Education v. Dep’t of Educ.*, 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005) encapsulates this approach and is applied verbatim in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, 2025 WL ……, slip op. at 8‑9.

---

### 1. What changes under the “relaxed” test?

| Traditional Lujan element | Standard Lujan analysis | “Relaxed” procedural‑injury analysis |
|----------------------------|------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Injury‑in‑fact** – concrete, particularized, actual or imminent | Rigid – plaintiff cannot rely on abstract grievances | Unchanged – plaintiffs **must still demonstrate** a concrete interest (“injury‑in‑fact”). The only concession is that *imminence* is interpreted loosely because the injury may be “threatened” rather than already realized when a procedural wrong occurs (*Center*, 396 F.3d at 1157). |
| **Causation** – injury must be fairly traceable to defendant’s conduct | Strict – no speculation about independent actors | Unchanged – plaintiffs cannot rely on “wildly speculative” chains of causation (*America First*, 2025 WL at ¶ 31‑33). The court will strike any claim that requires guessing how an agency *might* act after it is ordered to investigate. |
| **Redressability** – favorable decision must likely remedy injury | Must be virtually certain that relief will end the harm | Relaxed – it is enough if the court’s order **could** set in motion the protective statutory process (e.g., compel an investigation). Courts do not demand proof that subsequent discretionary steps (like disciplinary action) will occur (*Center*, 396 F.3d at 1157). However, if relief leaves the plaintiff dependent on further discretionary choices, redressability may still fail—as it did in *America First*, where OSC’s discretion rendered any judgment insufficiently redressive (*America First*, ¶ 34‑35). |

In short, **the relaxation concerns only two sub‑elements—imminence and how far into the future relief need be assured—but *injury‑in‑fact* and *causation* remain fully required**.

---

### 2. Concrete interest

A “concrete interest” is **not merely an abstract desire for enforcement**; it must be an actual stake protected by statute.

*Key points from case law*

| Case | Holding on “concrete interest” |
|------|--------------------------------|
| **Center for Law & Education**, 396 F.3d at 1157 | A plaintiff who asserts a statutory right to an investigation has a concrete interest **only if** the statute confers a *benefit* (the investigation itself) that is “designed to protect their threatened concrete interest.” |
| **Fla. Audubon Society v. Bentsen**, 94 F.3d at 664–65 (D.C. Cir. 1996) | A plaintiff’s enrollment in a program created a concrete stake sufficient for standing when the statute promised individualized benefits (e.g., eligibility determinations). |
| **Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau**, 115 F.4th at 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024) | No standing where plaintiffs claimed only a generalized taxpayer grievance—no specific statutory benefit was identified → no concrete interest found. |
| **America First Legal Foundation**, ¶ 23‑26 | The court rejected AFL’s claim because its alleged “informational injuries” were not tied to any *statutory entitlement*—the FOIA request itself was directed at DOJ, not OSC; thus AFL lacked a concrete interest in OSC’s investigative process itself.|

**Takeaway:** A plaintiff must point to a specific statutory provision that grants them an individual right—often an informational or notice right—and show how its denial deprives them of something they are legally entitled to receive.

---

### 3. Redirection of “redressability”

In ordinary Article III analysis, *redressability* asks whether the Court’s decree will **directly eliminate** the injury without depending on external actions.

For procedural rights:

* The court may treat an order compelling an agency **to perform its statutorily mandated process** as sufficient redress—even though later discretionary steps remain uncertain.
* If compliance with the court’s order would merely create a *possibility* that another agency might act later, many courts deem redressability lacking.

**Illustrations**

- ***Center* (2005)** held that because §1216(​a​)(​3​) explicitly obligates OSC to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings, ordering OSC to comply would satisfy redressability despite OSC’s later discretion over enforcement actions.
- ***America First* (2025)** found redressability lacking because even if OSC were forced to investigate, there was no guarantee it would produce any further information or discipline DOJ employees—so any judgment would leave AFL’s informational injuries unmitigated.
- ***Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC*, 126 F.​4th at 667 (D.C. Cir.\*2025)** emphasized that speculation about how independent agencies will react after an order defeats redressability.

Thus, **the “relaxed” rule means courts are willing to treat “compelling performance of the statutory process” as adequate remediation**, but they stop short when ultimate relief depends on further discretionary acts outside the court’s reach.

---

### 4. Putting it together – checklist for procedural‑injury standing

1️⃣ **Identify the statutory procedural right** – e.g., right to an agency investigation or notice provision (§1216).  
2️⃣ **Show you are within the class entitled** – you possess a personal stake (“concrete interest”) by virtue of being named or directly affected by the statute (§1216(a)(3) applies to anyone alleging arbitrary FOIA withholding).  
3️⃣ **Demonstrate actual/in‑flight injury** – e.g., denial of those notices or information you are statutorily entitled to receive now or imminently; do *not* rely on speculative downstream harms.  
4️⃣ **Link defendant’s conduct directly** – prove denial of the procedural right caused your lack of information/notice; avoid chains that require guessing how third parties will respond (`speculative causation`).  
5️⃣ **Argue redressability based on compulsory statutory duties** – show that ordering compliance with §1216 would itself cure your injury because you would receive whatever information/notice statutorily belongs to you; if relief merely triggers further discretionary steps, be prepared for probable failure on this prong.

---

### TL;DR

The “relaxed” standing test for procedural‑injury claims does **not waive any of Lujan’s three core elements**; it merely eases *imminence* and permits judges to treat an order compelling performance of a statutorily mandated process as sufficient redress even when later discretionary actions remain uncertain.  

To succeed, you still need:
- A **concrete, particularized statutory entitlement**,
- A clear causal link between government refusal and your injury,
- Proof that judicial compulsion of the agency’s duty will likely erase that injury—no more than a mere hope that downstream actors will act.

If any of these pieces is missing—as was fatal to AFL in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer*—the suit lacks Article III standing regardless of how “relaxed” the test may sound.*

### Why can’t AFL claim Article III standing based on its failure to receive FOIA records from DOJ because OSC refused to investigate?
Question:
*America First Legal* argues that AFL’s informational injury from DOJ’s non‑production is too attenuated from OSC’s decision not to investigate. I’m trying to understand how courts evaluate causation and redressability across multiple agencies. What level of speculation is permissible before a causal chain is considered too remote? Are there precedents that explain why an agency’s refusal to enforce its own statutory duties cannot give rise to standing against another agency’s unrelated action?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
AFL cannot rely on the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) refusal to investigate to obtain Article III standing because it lacks a *concrete, particularized injury* that is *fairly traceable* to OSC’s conduct and that a favorable judgment would *redress*. The D.C. Circuit rejected both of AFL’s theories—(1) that OSC’s inaction caused its FOIA‑related informational loss and (2) that AFL has an informational interest in the OSC investigation itself—by applying the ordinary standing requirements (Lujan) and the “relaxed” procedural‑injury test. In each case the required causal connection was too speculative and the redressability requirement was unmet.

---

### 1. The standing elements at issue  

The Court reiterated the three prongs from *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*:

1. **Injury‑in‑fact** – a concrete and particularized harm;
2. **Causation** – the injury must be “fairly traceable” to the defendant’s conduct;
3. **Redressability** – a favorable decision must be likely to remedy that injury.  

(See *Steel Co.* at 523 U.S. at 101‑02; D.C. Cir. opinion, ¶ II.)

It also noted that even where a plaintiff alleges a procedural right violation, the relaxed standards still require a concrete interest and a showing that the procedural breach caused a real injury (*Center for Law & Education* at 396 F.3d at 1157).

---

### 2. Why AFL’s *informational injury* fails

#### a) No concrete injury from OSC’s refusal  

AFL claimed two injuries:

1. **Failure to obtain FOIA records** from the DOJ.
2. **Denial of notices/information** that would accompany an OSC investigation under § 1214.

The Court held that an *informational* right “in vacuo” – i.e., a procedural guarantee without any statutory entitlement to receive information – does not satisfy injury‑in‑fact (*Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. at 496). Because § 1216 does **not** create a right for AFL to receive investigative notices, its asserted injury is abstract rather than concrete.

#### b) Causation is too attenuated  

Even assuming an injury existed, the Court found the alleged causal chain speculative:

> “If OSC had conducted … investigation, it might have … pursued corrective action … then MSPB might have sanctioned … Finally DOJ might have produced the records.”  
> —D.C. Cir., ¶ II‑A

The Court emphasized that causation “cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” (*FDA v. All.* 2024). The chain requires **two** independent agencies (MSPB and DOJ) to act in ways that are not compelled by law or by any court order. This fails the “fairly traceable” requirement articulated in *Lujan* and applied in *FDA*.

#### c) Redressability does not flow

Standing requires that relief actually *bind* the party whose conduct caused the injury. A judgment ordering OSC to investigate would **not** obligate either MSPB or DOJ to produce records, nor would it compel them to take any corrective action (*Haaland v. Brackeen*, 599 U.S. at 293‑94). The Court therefore concluded:

> “Redressability … generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale ‘might persuade’ actors who are not before the court.”  
> —D.C. Cir., ¶ II‑A

Because a favorable ruling would leave DOJ’s production decisions entirely untouched, AFL’s claim fails this prong as well.

---

### 3. The “relaxed” procedural‑injury test does not help

AFL hoped to invoke the more lenient standard applied when plaintiffs assert a statutory procedural right (*Center for Law & Education*). That test still demands:

* A **concrete interest** affected by the procedural breach;
* A **causal link** between the breach and that interest;
* Likelihood that judicial relief will **redress** it.

The Court found AFL satisfied none of those requirements: there is no statutory entitlement to receive OSC notices (§ 1216 is discretionary), causation again hinges on speculation, and any order compelling investigation would not force DOJ compliance.

---

### 4. How far can speculation go before the chain is “too remote”?

The D.C. Circuit’s analysis makes clear that speculation becomes fatal when:

1. The plaintiff must rely on **independent discretionary decisions** of multiple agencies (here OSC → MSPB → DOJ);
2. The plaintiff cannot show a **“but‑for”** relationship—i.e., would the injury have occurred *but for* OSC’s conduct? The Court concluded it could not;
3. The remedy would **not directly bind** the offending agency but merely hope to influence it indirectly.

These limits echo Supreme Court guidance that “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about… independent actors” (*FDA* at 383) and that “redressability … turns on how a judgment binds the parties” (*Haaland*).

---

### 5. Bottom line

Because AFL’s alleged informational loss stems directly from DOJ’s FOIA withholding—not from OSC’s discretionary refusal—and because any court order directed at OSC would not compel DOJ or any other agency to act, AFL cannot meet any of the Article III standing requirements. The speculative nature of its proposed causal chain and the lack of a concrete entitlement to information render its claim non‑justiciable, prompting the D.C. Circuit to vacate and remand for dismissal on jurisdictional grounds.

--- 

**Key takeaways for future cases**

| Standing prong | Requirement | Why AFL failed |
|---|---|---|
| Injury‑in‑fact | Concrete, particularized harm | No statutory right to receive OSC notices; FOIA loss traces to DOJ |
| Causation | Fairly traceable & not speculative | Requires conjecture about MSPB & DOJ actions after an optional OSC investigation |
| Redressability | Judgment must directly remedy harm | Order against OSC would not bind DOJ; no guarantee records will be produced |

Understanding these limits helps avoid “remote” causation arguments when challenging one agency’s inaction as a basis for suing another agency under Article III.

### What is ‘informational standing’ and does a statutory entitlement automatically confer it?
Question:
The opinion discusses ‘informational injury’ and references cases like *Zivotofsky v. Sec’y of State* and *AFC v. Feld*. If a statute (e.g., § 552 or § 1216) grants a party a right to receive certain information, does that automatically give rise to Article III standing when that information is withheld? Or must there be an additional concrete injury beyond the mere denial of the statutory right?
Answer:
**Informational standing – what it is and when a statutory right to information creates it**

---

### 1.  The basic rule

“Informational standing” is just another name for the *injury‑in‑fact* requirement of Article III when the plaintiff’s injury consists of being **denied information that a statute gives him a legal right to receive**.  
The Supreme Court has phrased the doctrine repeatedly:

* “A plaintiff claiming informational standing suffers a concrete injury if he is denied information that a ‘statute entitled him to receive.’” – *American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld*, 659 F.3d at 23 (quoting *Zivotofsky* (2006)).  
* The injury must be “concrete and particularized” and “actual or imminent.” – *Lujan* (1992); *Akers v. FEC*, 524 U.S. at 21 (the “informational‑injury” formulation).

Thus, two things are required:

1. **Statutory entitlement** – the law must expressly give the plaintiff a right **to obtain** the particular information.
2. **Denial or threat of denial** – the plaintiff must show he has been—or will be—prevented from receiving that information, creating a concrete personal harm.

If both prongs are satisfied, the plaintiff may proceed under ordinary Article III standing; no additional “substantive” harm is needed.

---

### 2.  A statutory grant is **not** sufficient by itself

The DC Circuit makes clear that *the existence of a statutory grant alone does not automatically confer standing*. The courts still require an actual or imminent denial that translates into a concrete injury.

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, 2025 WL …, Judge Katsas wrote:

> “A plaintiff claiming informational standing suffers a concrete injury if he is denied information that a ‘statute entitled him to receive.’ … But here … § 1216 … creates no entitlement to receive information.”  

The court therefore rejected AFL’s claim that § 1216 gave it standing simply because the statute tells OSC it “shall conduct an investigation.” The provision merely authorizes OSC’s **investigative power**, leaving discretion as to whether any notice or data are provided to the complainant. Because no statutory right **to receive** information was created, there was no informational injury at all.

---

### 3.  How the court applied the rule

| Theory advanced by AFL | Why it failed |
|---|---|
| **(a) Injured by DOJ’s refusal under FOIA (§ 552)** | The alleged injury traces back to DOJ, not OSC; there is no causal link between OSC’s discretionary decision not to investigate and DOE’s withholding (speculative chain). Hence no redressability and no personal stake in OSC’s conduct. |
| **(b) Injured by lack of notices/info from OSC under § 1216** | § 1216 does **not** grant AFL any right “to receive” OSC‑generated notices or investigative reports. The statute only empowers OSC to *investigate*, with discretion over how much information it gives parties (see *Carson v. USC*). Without an affirmative entitlement, there can be no “informational injury.” |

Both theories collapse because they either lack *concrete* causation/redressability (*a*) or lack an underlying *statutory entitlement* (*b*).

---

### 4.  Contrast with statutes that do confer informational standing

- **FOIA (§ 552)** – directly provides every person “the right to… request access” to agency records. A denial here **does** create an informational injury because Congress gave each requester an enforceable right to obtain those records.
- **Other statutes** – many grant agencies authority (*e.g.*, § 1216) but stop short of promising any particular individual receipt of documents or notices; those statutes do **not** by themselves generate informational standing for requesters.

The distinction matters even when a statute appears “rights‑bearing.” If the text merely says an agency *may* disclose or *shall* investigate without saying “the requester shall receive X,” courts treat any alleged denial as lacking an enforceable entitlement and therefore lacking an informational injury.

---

### 5.  Procedural‑right claims are treated similarly

The opinion also stresses that merely losing a procedural guarantee—“a procedural right in vacuo”—doesn’t give Article III jurisdiction unless the procedural right protects some underlying concrete interest (*Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. at 496). AFL’s claim that OSC violated its procedural due‑process rights under § 1216 therefore required, first, a demonstration that those procedures protected an independent substantive interest (the receipt of notices). Because none existed, the procedural claim could not supply standing either.

---

### 6.  Bottom line for your question

> **Does a statutory entitlement automatically confer informational standing when that information is withheld?**

No. A statute must actually **grant** the plaintiff a right **to receive** particular information, and the plaintiff must show he has been—or will be—denied that information in a way that harms him concretely and personally. If the statute merely authorizes an agency’s investigation or creates discretion (as § 1216 does), then denial of any agency‑generated material does not satisfy either prong of informational standing.

Consequently:

* A FOIA request denied by an agency triggers informational standing because § 552 creates an enforceable right to those records.
* An allegation that OSC failed to investigate under § 1216 does **not** generate informational standing because § 1216 confers no such receipt right; it only creates investigative authority subject to discretion.

The DC Circuit’s decision in *America First Legal* therefore illustrates precisely why courts look beyond mere statutory language— they require both an enforceable entitlement and an actual/imminent denial before recognizing an informational injury for Article III purposes.

### What does ‘de novo’ review mean when an appellate court vacates a district court judgment on jurisdictional grounds?
Question:
*America First Legal* notes that its review is de novo after the district court dismissed the case for failure to state a claim but also addressed standing later. I’m unclear on what de novo review entails here: Does it mean the appellate panel re‑examines every factual and legal issue afresh, or is its scope limited because the dismissal was based on subject‑matter jurisdiction/standing? How does de novo review differ from an appellate standard like abuse of discretion in similar contexts?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* the D.C. Circuit said plainly that “our review is **de novo**” (¶ I‑A).  That statement tells us how the Court treats an appeal that turns on *subject–matter jurisdiction*—in this case, Article III standing—rather than on a merit‑based dismissal.

Below is a step‑by‑step explanation of what “de novo” means here, why it applies, and how it differs from the more deferential “abuse‑of‑discretion” standard.

---

### 1.  What “de novo” review is

* **Definition** – A de novo appeal means the appellate panel considers the issue *fresh*, as if it were being decided for the first time.  The Court looks at the record anew and applies the governing law without giving any presumption that the district court’s conclusion was correct.  (The opinion cites *N. Am. Butterfly Ass’n v. Wolf*, 977 F.3d at 1249 for this rule.)

* **Scope** – The review covers *legal conclusions* and any *factual findings that are part of those legal conclusions*.  For jurisdictional questions, the relevant “facts” are usually those alleged by the parties to satisfy elements of standing (injury‑in‑fact, causation, redressability).  The panel may re‑evaluate those allegations under the applicable doctrinal standards quoted throughout the opinion—e.g., *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. at 560–61; *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. at 496.

* **Result in this case** – The circuit re‑read AFL’s pleading and supplemental briefing on standing and applied Supreme Court and D.C. Cir precedents (*Steel Co.*, *Allen*, *Falkner*, etc.) to determine whether AFL satisfied each element of Article III standing.  Because it found AFL’s alleged injuries too speculative and lacking concrete injury, it concluded AFC “lacks Article III standing” and therefore “the district court had no subject‑matter jurisdiction.”  Consequently it **vacated** the district court’s judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction (¶ III).

---

### 2.  Why de novo is required for a jurisdictional determination

1️⃣ **Jurisdiction cannot be waived or created by choice** – A court’s power to hear a case derives from Article III; if that power is absent, the decision is void regardless of any later factual findings (*Steel*).  

2️⃣ **The law treats jurisdiction as a pure question of law** – The D.C. Circuit treats standing as “a question bearing on whether the district court had subject‑matter jurisdiction” (§ II).  Because it is a legal threshold rather than a discretionary exercise, appellate courts must decide it anew rather than by deferring to the trial judge’s view.

3️⃣ **Statutory interpretation is involved** – The panel had to interpret §§ 1214–1216 of title 5 and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) to decide whether OSC’s refusal triggered a statutory right that could confer standing—a matter of statutory construction governed by established legal principles rather than trial‐court discretion.

---

### 3.  How this differs from an “abuse‑of‑discretion” standard

|                     | **De novo review** | **Abuse‑of‑discretion review** |
|---------------------|--------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Presumption about lower‑court decision** | None – appellate court makes its own independent determination | Presumption that lower‐court decision was proper; reversal only if decision was arbitrary, unreasonable, or outside authority |
| **Typical use** | Jurisdictional questions (standing, sovereign immunity), pure questions of law, statutory construction | Discretionary rulings such as evidentiary determinations, preliminary injunctions, sentencing decisions |
| **Factual findings** | Re‑examined if they are part of the legal analysis (e.g., injury facts needed for standing) | Generally upheld unless clearly erroneous |
| **Effect in America First case** | Circuit dissected AFL’s alleged injuries and redressability afresh under *Lujan* & *Summers* | Not applicable – there was no discretionary judgment being reviewed |

In other words, if this appeal had involved an ordinary motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim (a nonjurisdictional rule-of-law question), the circuit might have applied an “abuse‐of‐discretion” standard when reviewing how the district judge weighed plaintiff’s allegation against Rule 12(b)(6) criteria.  Instead, because the pivotal issue was whether AFL even had Article III standing—a prerequisite for *any* judicial power—the correct approach was de novo.

---

### 4.  Bottom line for you

When an appellate court says its review is **de novo**, it will:

1. Ignore any presumption that the district court’s legal conclusion was correct.
2. Apply controlling precedent directly to the record.
3. Reassess any factual foundations needed for those legal conclusions.
4. Vacate or reverse without regard to how carefully—or carelessly—the trial judge reached its decision.

That contrasts with an “abuse‐of‐discretion” approach where the appellate panel would usually defer to the trial judge’s judgment unless it was manifestly unreasonable.

Thus in *America First Legal Foundation*, “de novo” meant that the D.C. Circuit independently evaluated AFL’s Article III standing under Supreme Court doctrine and concluded there was no subject‑matter jurisdiction—prompting vacatur and remand—rather than merely checking whether the district court had abused its discretion in dismissing on other grounds.

### Can a federal court compel OSC to follow § 1214 notice requirements during a § 1216 investigation?
Question:
The opinion suggests that whether OSC must provide notices and information required by § 1214 in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary, citing *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*. If so, does Congress allow courts to order OSC to adopt those procedural safeguards, or are they outside judicial control? Under what circumstances have courts imposed § 1214‑type procedures on investigations that are technically governed by § 1216?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
No. Under the D.C. Circuit’s decision *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* the Court held that **§ 5 U.S.C. § 1216 does not create a mandatory duty for the Office of Special Counsel (“OSC”) to follow the notice‑and‑information requirements of § 5 U.S.C. § 1214** when it conducts an investigation under § 1216. The statute gives OSC *discretion* over whether—and how—it applies those procedural safeguards, and therefore a federal court lacks Article III authority to compel compliance unless Congress has supplied an independent statutory hook that creates an enforceable right.

Below is a step‑by‑step explanation grounded in the opinion.

---

### 1.  The statutory scheme

| Provision | What it says | Effect on procedural duties |
|-----------|--------------|-----------------------------|
| **§ 1214(a)** | Enumerates *notice* requirements for “corrective actions” in prohibited‑personnel‑practice cases (e.g., notifying the complaining party of investigative progress). | Creates **mandatory** procedural safeguards *only* in **§ 1214** investigations. |
| **§ 1216(a)(3)** | Requires OSC “to conduct an investigation … concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” | Imposes a **duty** to investigate FOIA withholdings but does **not** specify the procedures to be used. |
| **§ 1216(c)** | Allows OSC “to investigate and seek corrective action **under section 1214**…in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” | Uses permissive language (“may”) and ties the *substantive* remedy (“corrective action”) to **§ 1214**, but stops short of making every **§ 1214** procedure obligatory in every **§ 1216** probe. |

The Court emphasized this reading at several points:

> “the district court declined … because **section 1216(c) committed that decision … to OSC’s discretion**.” (*Jamieson Greer*, ¶ 13)

Thus, Congress deliberately left open how far OSC must mimic **§ 1214** formalities when it acts under **§ 1216**.

---

### 2.  Discretionary‑function doctrine & *Carson*

The opinion treats *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel* as controlling:

> “OSC’s ‘decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements’ in a section 1216 investigation is ‘discretionary.’” (*Jamieson Greer*, ¶ 15)

*Carson* held that an agency’s choice whether to apply **§ 1512**’s notice scheme in a whistleblower context was left to its judgment and therefore unreviewable absent clear statutory direction or arbitrary‑and‑capricious conduct under the Administrative Procedure Act (“APA”).

By invoking *Carson*, the D.C. Circuit signals two things:

1️⃣ The statute itself supplies **no mandatory procedural right** whose violation would give rise to judicial review;  

2️⃣ Even if an agency’s *exercise* of discretion were arguably improper, any challenge would have to succeed on an APA “arbitrary‑and‑capricious” ground—a standard rarely met where Congress says the agency *may* act “in the same way”.

The Court also cites *Heckler v. Chaney* (“an agency’s decision **whether** or **not** to undertake enforcement is presumptively unreviewable”), reinforcing that courts cannot compel an agency simply because they think notice would be helpful.

---

### 3.  Standing – why the Court never got past Article III

Even assuming plaintiffs could point to an alleged injury caused by lack of notice, *Jamieson Greer* explains why they did not have standing:

> “[A] procedural right … without some concrete interest … is insufficient,” and “AFL … fails … both those relaxed standards.” (*Jamieson Greer*, §§ II–III)

Because plaintiffs could not show a concrete injury traceable solely to OSC’s refusal—and because any redress would depend on speculation about actions by third parties (MSPB, DOJ)—the Court dismissed the case before examining merits. This underscores another reason why forcing **§ 1214** formalities would be impossible: without an enforceable right there can be no injury-in-fact, hence no jurisdiction.

---

### 4.  When have courts forced **§ 1214‑type** procedures onto investigations?

The opinion notes that *Carson* is essentially *the only precedent* discussing this question in the context of OSC:

> “…cases holding thatOSC’s ‘decision … is discretionary.’” (*Jamieson Greer*, ¶ 15)

No other appellate decision cited in *Jamieson Greer* compels an OSC inquiry governed by **§ 1216** to follow every **§ 12014** requirement.  

Generally, courts will impose statutory notice schemes only when Congress makes them *mandatory*. Examples outside this case (though not mentioned in the opinion) illustrate the principle:

| Situation | What triggers judicially enforceable notice |
|-----------|---------------------------------------------|
| A MSPB appeal filed after an OSC‐initiated corrective action under **§ 1214(b)** | The APA requires formal notice because statutes expressly grant parties a right “to be notified.” |
| A FOIA court order invoking **5 U.S.C.​ § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)** | That provision creates a *direct duty* for OSC (“shall promptly initiate a proceeding”), which carries its own procedural timetable separate from **§ 1216**.|

In each scenario the *notice* obligation stems from an independent statutory command—*not* from reading **§ 12216(c)** into imposing **§ 12214** verbatim.

Consequently, there is currently **no reported case** wherein a court has mandated full-fledged **§ 12214** notice duties for a purely **§ 12216** investigation without another governing provision doing so.

---

### 5. Practical takeaways

| Issue | Governing principle derived from *Jamieson Greer* |
|-------|---------------------------------------------------|
| Can a plaintiff sue for failure to receive §§ 12214 notices during a § 12216 probe? | No – statutes give OSC discretionary authority; plaintiffs lack an enforceable right and therefore lack standing. |
| Does any congressional text empower courts otherwise? | Only if another provision creates a mandatory duty (e.g., FOIA trigger at **§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i)**). In those narrow circumstances courts can order compliance with whatever procedure Congress prescribed for that duty—but not automatically import §§ 12214 notices into every § 12216 inquiry. |
| When might courts intervene? | When an agency has already taken an appealable step (e.g., filing a corrective‑action complaint under **§ 12214**) or when Congress explicitly commands certain procedural steps (as FOIA does). Absent such command, agencies retain unfettered discretion.* |

---

### Bottom line

The D.C. Circuit makes clear that Congress gave OSC broad leeway under **§ 5 U.S.C. § 6216**, expressly allowing it “to investigate … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved,” but did not obligate it to invoke every procedural safeguard found in **§ 5 U.S.C. § 6214**. Because there is no statutory entitlement for affected parties to receive those notices, courts lack Article III jurisdiction—and even assuming jurisdiction they are bound by the discretionary‑function doctrine (**Carson**, **Heckler**) and cannot compel compliance with §§ 6214 notices during ordinary §§ 6216 investigations.
