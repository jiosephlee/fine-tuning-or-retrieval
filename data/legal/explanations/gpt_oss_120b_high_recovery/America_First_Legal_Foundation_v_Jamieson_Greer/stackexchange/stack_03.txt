Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Why can’t AFL claim Article III standing based on its failure to receive FOIA records from DOJ because OSC refused to investigate?
Question:
*America First Legal* argues that AFL’s informational injury from DOJ’s non‑production is too attenuated from OSC’s decision not to investigate. I’m trying to understand how courts evaluate causation and redressability across multiple agencies. What level of speculation is permissible before a causal chain is considered too remote? Are there precedents that explain why an agency’s refusal to enforce its own statutory duties cannot give rise to standing against another agency’s unrelated action?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
AFL cannot rely on the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) refusal to investigate to obtain Article III standing because it lacks a *concrete, particularized injury* that is *fairly traceable* to OSC’s conduct and that a favorable judgment would *redress*. The D.C. Circuit rejected both of AFL’s theories—(1) that OSC’s inaction caused its FOIA‑related informational loss and (2) that AFL has an informational interest in the OSC investigation itself—by applying the ordinary standing requirements (Lujan) and the “relaxed” procedural‑injury test. In each case the required causal connection was too speculative and the redressability requirement was unmet.

---

### 1. The standing elements at issue  

The Court reiterated the three prongs from *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*:

1. **Injury‑in‑fact** – a concrete and particularized harm;
2. **Causation** – the injury must be “fairly traceable” to the defendant’s conduct;
3. **Redressability** – a favorable decision must be likely to remedy that injury.  

(See *Steel Co.* at 523 U.S. at 101‑02; D.C. Cir. opinion, ¶ II.)

It also noted that even where a plaintiff alleges a procedural right violation, the relaxed standards still require a concrete interest and a showing that the procedural breach caused a real injury (*Center for Law & Education* at 396 F.3d at 1157).

---

### 2. Why AFL’s *informational injury* fails

#### a) No concrete injury from OSC’s refusal  

AFL claimed two injuries:

1. **Failure to obtain FOIA records** from the DOJ.
2. **Denial of notices/information** that would accompany an OSC investigation under § 1214.

The Court held that an *informational* right “in vacuo” – i.e., a procedural guarantee without any statutory entitlement to receive information – does not satisfy injury‑in‑fact (*Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. at 496). Because § 1216 does **not** create a right for AFL to receive investigative notices, its asserted injury is abstract rather than concrete.

#### b) Causation is too attenuated  

Even assuming an injury existed, the Court found the alleged causal chain speculative:

> “If OSC had conducted … investigation, it might have … pursued corrective action … then MSPB might have sanctioned … Finally DOJ might have produced the records.”  
> —D.C. Cir., ¶ II‑A

The Court emphasized that causation “cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” (*FDA v. All.* 2024). The chain requires **two** independent agencies (MSPB and DOJ) to act in ways that are not compelled by law or by any court order. This fails the “fairly traceable” requirement articulated in *Lujan* and applied in *FDA*.

#### c) Redressability does not flow

Standing requires that relief actually *bind* the party whose conduct caused the injury. A judgment ordering OSC to investigate would **not** obligate either MSPB or DOJ to produce records, nor would it compel them to take any corrective action (*Haaland v. Brackeen*, 599 U.S. at 293‑94). The Court therefore concluded:

> “Redressability … generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale ‘might persuade’ actors who are not before the court.”  
> —D.C. Cir., ¶ II‑A

Because a favorable ruling would leave DOJ’s production decisions entirely untouched, AFL’s claim fails this prong as well.

---

### 3. The “relaxed” procedural‑injury test does not help

AFL hoped to invoke the more lenient standard applied when plaintiffs assert a statutory procedural right (*Center for Law & Education*). That test still demands:

* A **concrete interest** affected by the procedural breach;
* A **causal link** between the breach and that interest;
* Likelihood that judicial relief will **redress** it.

The Court found AFL satisfied none of those requirements: there is no statutory entitlement to receive OSC notices (§ 1216 is discretionary), causation again hinges on speculation, and any order compelling investigation would not force DOJ compliance.

---

### 4. How far can speculation go before the chain is “too remote”?

The D.C. Circuit’s analysis makes clear that speculation becomes fatal when:

1. The plaintiff must rely on **independent discretionary decisions** of multiple agencies (here OSC → MSPB → DOJ);
2. The plaintiff cannot show a **“but‑for”** relationship—i.e., would the injury have occurred *but for* OSC’s conduct? The Court concluded it could not;
3. The remedy would **not directly bind** the offending agency but merely hope to influence it indirectly.

These limits echo Supreme Court guidance that “causation generally cannot rely on speculation about… independent actors” (*FDA* at 383) and that “redressability … turns on how a judgment binds the parties” (*Haaland*).

---

### 5. Bottom line

Because AFL’s alleged informational loss stems directly from DOJ’s FOIA withholding—not from OSC’s discretionary refusal—and because any court order directed at OSC would not compel DOJ or any other agency to act, AFL cannot meet any of the Article III standing requirements. The speculative nature of its proposed causal chain and the lack of a concrete entitlement to information render its claim non‑justiciable, prompting the D.C. Circuit to vacate and remand for dismissal on jurisdictional grounds.

--- 

**Key takeaways for future cases**

| Standing prong | Requirement | Why AFL failed |
|---|---|---|
| Injury‑in‑fact | Concrete, particularized harm | No statutory right to receive OSC notices; FOIA loss traces to DOJ |
| Causation | Fairly traceable & not speculative | Requires conjecture about MSPB & DOJ actions after an optional OSC investigation |
| Redressability | Judgment must directly remedy harm | Order against OSC would not bind DOJ; no guarantee records will be produced |

Understanding these limits helps avoid “remote” causation arguments when challenging one agency’s inaction as a basis for suing another agency under Article III.
