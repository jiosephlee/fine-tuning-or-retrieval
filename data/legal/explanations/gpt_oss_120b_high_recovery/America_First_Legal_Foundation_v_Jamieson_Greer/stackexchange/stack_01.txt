Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Does 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) impose a mandatory duty on the Office of Special Counsel to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings?
Question:
In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* the D.C. Circuit discusses whether OSC must investigate an alleged FOIA violation under § 1216(a)(3) even when the triggers in FOIA § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) are not met. I’m confused about how these statutes interact. Does § 1216 create an absolute obligation for OSC to launch an investigation, or is it still discretionary? And if it is mandatory, why did the district court find that OSC’s refusal was only an error of law rather than a deprivation of a statutory right?
Answer:
**Short answer**

5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) gives the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) *authority* to open an investigation when someone alleges an “arbitrary or capricious withholding” of information prohibited by FOIA, but it does **not** create an absolute, enforceable duty that compels OSC to investigate in every case. The D.C. Circuit reads the word “shall” in § 1216(a)(3) together with the permissive language of § 1216(c)—which says OSC “may investigate … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” That combination makes the investigatory power discretionary. Because the statute provides no private right of action for a plaintiff whose request is declined, OSC’s refusal can be judged only as an **error of law** (a misinterpretation of its own authority), not as a deprivation of a statutory right that courts can enforce.

---

### Why the duty is not mandatory

1. **Statutory text** – The opinion quotes the two relevant subsections:

   > “OSC **shall** … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding …” — § 1216(a)(3).  
   > “… OSC **may** investigate and seek corrective action … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” — § 1216(c).

   The D.C. Circuit treats these as complementary provisions. While “shall” ordinarily imposes an obligation, courts interpret statutes so that later language qualifies earlier commands when there is an express grant of discretion. Here, **§ 1216(c)** expressly tells us that OSC *may* undertake such investigations *in the same way* it would handle a prohibited‑personnel‑practice case—i.e., at its own discretion whether to proceed at all.

2. **Court’s reading** – In paragraph B of its opinion the Court says:

   > “the court declined to order OSC to investigate any FOIA policy because, in its view, **section 1216(c) committed that decision to OSC’s discretion**.”  
   
   This captures the Court’s conclusion that even though OSC has authority under § 1216(a)(3), it retains agency‑wide judgment about whether opening an investigation is warranted.

3. **No private right of action** – The decision makes clear that § 1216 does not create a plaintiff‑enforceable procedural right. The Court notes that “a plaintiff claiming informational standing suffers a concrete injury if he is denied information that a ‘statute entitled him to receive.’” But section 1216 never says a requester *is entitled* to receive notice or information from OSC; it merely grants OSC the power **if it chooses** to act.

### Why the district court called it “an error of law” rather than a deprivation of rights

- **Misinterpretation, not violation** – The district court correctly identified that OSC’s reliance on FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) triggers was mistaken; those triggers do *not* limit OSC’s broader investigatory authority under § 1216. Hence, as the appellate panel says, “OSC’s decision not to investigate was based on a legal error.”

- **No enforceable entitlement** – Because § 1216(c) makes the investigatory decision discretionary and because Congress gave no private cause‑of‑action for denial of an investigation, AFL could not demonstrate that it possessed a protected statutory right whose denial caused a concrete injury. Consequently, even though OSC’s refusal was legally erroneous, it did not rise to “deprivation of a statutory right” actionable under Article III.

- **Standing ties it together** – The Court ultimately dismissed on lack of standing: without an enforceable personal right under § 1216 and without concrete injury from OSC’s decision, AFL could not bring an Article III claim. This reinforces why an error in applying the statute does not translate into a justiciable violation.

---

### Bottom line

- **Authority vs. Obligation:** § 1216(a)(3) grants OSC authority *to* investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings; § 1216(c) turns that authority into *discretion*.
- **No mandatory investigative duty:** OSC may decline to open an investigation after weighing its own enforcement priorities.
- **District court’s characterization:** OSC’s refusal was legally mistaken (it misapplied FOIA’s trigger rule), but because there is no private right compelling investigation and because AFL lacks standing, the refusal does not constitute deprivation of a statutory right subject to judicial relief.

Thus, while Congress intended for OSC *to have* investigative power over FOIA misconduct, it did **not** intend for plaintiffs like AFL to sue for enforcement when OSC elects not to act.
