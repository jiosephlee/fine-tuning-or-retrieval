Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Can a federal court compel OSC to follow § 1214 notice requirements during a § 1216 investigation?
Question:
The opinion suggests that whether OSC must provide notices and information required by § 1214 in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary, citing *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*. If so, does Congress allow courts to order OSC to adopt those procedural safeguards, or are they outside judicial control? Under what circumstances have courts imposed § 1214‑type procedures on investigations that are technically governed by § 1216?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
No. Under the D.C. Circuit’s decision *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* the Court held that **§ 5 U.S.C. § 1216 does not create a mandatory duty for the Office of Special Counsel (“OSC”) to follow the notice‑and‑information requirements of § 5 U.S.C. § 1214** when it conducts an investigation under § 1216. The statute gives OSC *discretion* over whether—and how—it applies those procedural safeguards, and therefore a federal court lacks Article III authority to compel compliance unless Congress has supplied an independent statutory hook that creates an enforceable right.

Below is a step‑by‑step explanation grounded in the opinion.

---

### 1.  The statutory scheme

| Provision | What it says | Effect on procedural duties |
|-----------|--------------|-----------------------------|
| **§ 1214(a)** | Enumerates *notice* requirements for “corrective actions” in prohibited‑personnel‑practice cases (e.g., notifying the complaining party of investigative progress). | Creates **mandatory** procedural safeguards *only* in **§ 1214** investigations. |
| **§ 1216(a)(3)** | Requires OSC “to conduct an investigation … concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552.” | Imposes a **duty** to investigate FOIA withholdings but does **not** specify the procedures to be used. |
| **§ 1216(c)** | Allows OSC “to investigate and seek corrective action **under section 1214**…in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” | Uses permissive language (“may”) and ties the *substantive* remedy (“corrective action”) to **§ 1214**, but stops short of making every **§ 1214** procedure obligatory in every **§ 1216** probe. |

The Court emphasized this reading at several points:

> “the district court declined … because **section 1216(c) committed that decision … to OSC’s discretion**.” (*Jamieson Greer*, ¶ 13)

Thus, Congress deliberately left open how far OSC must mimic **§ 1214** formalities when it acts under **§ 1216**.

---

### 2.  Discretionary‑function doctrine & *Carson*

The opinion treats *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel* as controlling:

> “OSC’s ‘decision whether to follow section 1214’s procedural requirements’ in a section 1216 investigation is ‘discretionary.’” (*Jamieson Greer*, ¶ 15)

*Carson* held that an agency’s choice whether to apply **§ 1512**’s notice scheme in a whistleblower context was left to its judgment and therefore unreviewable absent clear statutory direction or arbitrary‑and‑capricious conduct under the Administrative Procedure Act (“APA”).

By invoking *Carson*, the D.C. Circuit signals two things:

1️⃣ The statute itself supplies **no mandatory procedural right** whose violation would give rise to judicial review;  

2️⃣ Even if an agency’s *exercise* of discretion were arguably improper, any challenge would have to succeed on an APA “arbitrary‑and‑capricious” ground—a standard rarely met where Congress says the agency *may* act “in the same way”.

The Court also cites *Heckler v. Chaney* (“an agency’s decision **whether** or **not** to undertake enforcement is presumptively unreviewable”), reinforcing that courts cannot compel an agency simply because they think notice would be helpful.

---

### 3.  Standing – why the Court never got past Article III

Even assuming plaintiffs could point to an alleged injury caused by lack of notice, *Jamieson Greer* explains why they did not have standing:

> “[A] procedural right … without some concrete interest … is insufficient,” and “AFL … fails … both those relaxed standards.” (*Jamieson Greer*, §§ II–III)

Because plaintiffs could not show a concrete injury traceable solely to OSC’s refusal—and because any redress would depend on speculation about actions by third parties (MSPB, DOJ)—the Court dismissed the case before examining merits. This underscores another reason why forcing **§ 1214** formalities would be impossible: without an enforceable right there can be no injury-in-fact, hence no jurisdiction.

---

### 4.  When have courts forced **§ 1214‑type** procedures onto investigations?

The opinion notes that *Carson* is essentially *the only precedent* discussing this question in the context of OSC:

> “…cases holding thatOSC’s ‘decision … is discretionary.’” (*Jamieson Greer*, ¶ 15)

No other appellate decision cited in *Jamieson Greer* compels an OSC inquiry governed by **§ 1216** to follow every **§ 12014** requirement.  

Generally, courts will impose statutory notice schemes only when Congress makes them *mandatory*. Examples outside this case (though not mentioned in the opinion) illustrate the principle:

| Situation | What triggers judicially enforceable notice |
|-----------|---------------------------------------------|
| A MSPB appeal filed after an OSC‐initiated corrective action under **§ 1214(b)** | The APA requires formal notice because statutes expressly grant parties a right “to be notified.” |
| A FOIA court order invoking **5 U.S.C.​ § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)** | That provision creates a *direct duty* for OSC (“shall promptly initiate a proceeding”), which carries its own procedural timetable separate from **§ 1216**.|

In each scenario the *notice* obligation stems from an independent statutory command—*not* from reading **§ 12216(c)** into imposing **§ 12214** verbatim.

Consequently, there is currently **no reported case** wherein a court has mandated full-fledged **§ 12214** notice duties for a purely **§ 12216** investigation without another governing provision doing so.

---

### 5. Practical takeaways

| Issue | Governing principle derived from *Jamieson Greer* |
|-------|---------------------------------------------------|
| Can a plaintiff sue for failure to receive §§ 12214 notices during a § 12216 probe? | No – statutes give OSC discretionary authority; plaintiffs lack an enforceable right and therefore lack standing. |
| Does any congressional text empower courts otherwise? | Only if another provision creates a mandatory duty (e.g., FOIA trigger at **§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i)**). In those narrow circumstances courts can order compliance with whatever procedure Congress prescribed for that duty—but not automatically import §§ 12214 notices into every § 12216 inquiry. |
| When might courts intervene? | When an agency has already taken an appealable step (e.g., filing a corrective‑action complaint under **§ 12214**) or when Congress explicitly commands certain procedural steps (as FOIA does). Absent such command, agencies retain unfettered discretion.* |

---

### Bottom line

The D.C. Circuit makes clear that Congress gave OSC broad leeway under **§ 5 U.S.C. § 6216**, expressly allowing it “to investigate … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved,” but did not obligate it to invoke every procedural safeguard found in **§ 5 U.S.C. § 6214**. Because there is no statutory entitlement for affected parties to receive those notices, courts lack Article III jurisdiction—and even assuming jurisdiction they are bound by the discretionary‑function doctrine (**Carson**, **Heckler**) and cannot compel compliance with §§ 6214 notices during ordinary §§ 6216 investigations.
