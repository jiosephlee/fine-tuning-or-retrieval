Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Deference at the Doorstep of Transparency: How *Farahi* Reinforces Agency Authority Under Exemption 7(A)

## Why *Farahi* Matters

When an ordinary citizen asks the government “what do you have on me?” the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) is supposed to be the answer‑machine that forces agencies to pull back the curtain. In practice, however, every request runs into one of twelve statutory exemptions that let agencies say “no.” The D.C. Circuit’s decision in **Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation**, No. 23‑5243 (2025), is a vivid reminder that when those exemptions intersect with national‑security investigations, courts continue to tip heavily in the government’s favor.  

The case matters for three reasons. First, it affirms that the judiciary will grant broad deference to an agency’s claim that its records were “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” and that disclosure would “interfere with enforcement proceedings” even when that claim rests largely on internal affidavits filed ex parte. Second, it shows how a plaintiff’s argument about the passage of time—a classic foil in Exemption 7(A) litigation—fails once an agency can produce any contemporaneous statement saying an investigation is still “ongoing.” Third, it signals what legislators who are trying to curb secretive counter‑terrorism filings can expect from the courts: unless Congress rewrites Exemption 7(A)’s language or forces stricter evidentiary standards, judges will continue to lean on precedent from *Campbell*, *Clemente*, *Juarez* and *CREW* to preserve agency opacity.


## The Case in a Nutshell

Foad Farahi is an Iranian national who has lived in the United States since 1993 and has been fighting removal proceedings ever since DHS flagged his visa overstay as a possible ground for deportation. In those hearings he was accused—by DHS’s own expert witnesses—of ties ranging from recruitment work for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood to contacts with Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other Al Qaeda operatives. Predictably he filed a FOIA request for his FBI file hoping to learn what evidence underpinned those accusations.

The FBI located roughly 10,750 pages and 80 CDs described as “potentially responsive.” After missing its statutory deadline it eventually turned over a sliver before withholding the bulk behind Exemption 7(A). The district court initially declined summary judgment because it wanted an updated assessment of whether any enforcement proceeding was still pending after several years had elapsed. The FBI responded with fresh public and sealed affidavits from Section Chiefs David Hardy and Joseph Bender affirming—in broad strokes—that an investigation was still active and that the records were “inextricably intertwined” so no sensible segregation was possible. The district court accepted those explanations and granted summary judgment in favor of the FBI; *Farahi* appealed.


## The Court’s Reasoning: Deference Reaffirmed

### 1. Compiled for Law‑Enforcement Purposes

The panel began by noting that any agency tasked primarily with policing activities enjoys a “fair measure of deference” when it says a record was compiled in furtherance of its core duties (*Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d 20 (D.C.Cir.1998)). That principle has been reiterated over two decades ago including in counter‑terrorism contexts (*Clemente v. FBI*, 867 F.3d 111 (D.C.Cir.2017)). Judge Katsas emphasized that “[b]ecause the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency,” its own determination satisfied the first prong of Exemption 7(A); no independent factual finding was required beyond showing a rational nexus between Farahi’s file and an investigation into violations of federal law or national‑security statutes—a nexus thoroughly articulated in Hardy’s public declaration.

### 2. Interfering With Pending or Reasonably Anticipated Proceedings

The second prong depends on showing there are “pending” or “reasonably anticipated” enforcement actions (*Citizens for Responsible & Ethics in Washington v.​ DOJ*, 746 F.3d 1082 (D.C.Cir.“CREW”) (2014)). The circuit has long held that an affidavit simply attestingto an ongoing investigation can fulfill this requirement so long as it is not demonstrably contradicted (*Juarez v.​ DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54 (D.C.Cir.“Juarez”) (2008)). Here Hardy’s public statement said investigations were underway targeting Farahi’s alleged terrorist connections; Bender’s supplemental affidavit merely confirmed nothing had changed since Hardy’s initial briefing two years later. Because the district court had already found Hardy’s description sufficient—the record thus passed both sub‑requirements without further evidentiary support—the appellate panel found no reason to overturn that finding despite Farahi’s argument that time alone should cast doubt on pendency.


### 3. Segregability

Even when an exemption applies, § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) obliges an agency “to take reasonable steps” to excise any non‑exempt material (“segregation”). The D.C. Circuit cited *Juarez* again, noting that when exempt portions are “inextricably intertwined,” no reasonable segregation exists and blanket withholding is permissible (*Mead Data Center* at 260). Bender’s detailed justification—that any non‑exempt fragments were interspersed line‑by‑line throughout massive investigative logs—mirrored language used repeatedly by this court upholding similar blanket denials (*Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v.​ VA*, 57 F.4th 1061 (D.C.Cir.“Perioperative”) (2023)). Accordingly, after reviewing Bender’s affidavit the panel concluded there was no basis for overturning the lower court’s segregation analysis.


### 4  Reliance on Ex Parte Declarations

Farahi complained that undisclosed (“ex parte”) affidavits gave him no chance to rebut governmental assertions—a point repeatedly raised by scholars warning about asymmetries inherent in FOIA litigation involving classified material (*Kearney & Higgins*, Fed.Reg., ‘18). Yet both district and appellate courts emphasized longstanding precedent allowing sealed affidavits when disclosure would defeat their purpose (*Perioperative* at 1067). Because Farahi never objected procedurally—to oppose either Hardy’s seal request or Bender’s own ex parte filing—the appellants were deemed estopped from attacking their substance later.



## A Critical Take: Is This Deference Tenable?

From a doctrinal standpoint there is little surprise here—the panel simply followed *Campbell* through *Clemente* without tinkering with any substantive threshold tests.Churchill could argue this consistency is essential given how volatile counter‑terrorism investigations can be; releasing even peripheral details might tip off suspects or compromise covert techniques—a risk recognized explicitly in *CNSS v​. DOJ*, 331 F.3d 918 (D.C.Cir.“CNSS”) (2003).

However what feels more unsettling than doctrinal fidelity is how easily an agency can breathe life into anonymity after years have passed simply by attaching another signature line stating “investigation remains ongoing.” In *CREW* itself Justice Koretz warned against treating such boilerplate language as automatically sufficient (“the vague nature…may not suffice”). Yet here those very concerns were brushed aside because Hardy’s earlier declaration had already been logged into evidence—and no new factual scrutiny was imposed when Bender merely refreshed it two years later.

The real problem lies not so much in judicial deference per se but in judicial reliance on **unchallengeable** substantive content lodged under seal*. While protectively masked affidavits preserve operational secrecy—a legitimate goal—the result is precisely what FOIA was designed partly to avoid: **a self‑reinforcing loop** wherein judges accept governmental attestations without giving requesters an avenue either for verification or rebuttal.The appellate opinion does acknowledge this tension (“the problem…is inherent”), yet stops short of proposing any procedural safeguard.


## Doctrinal Ripple Effects

### A Blueprint For Future Antiterrorism Cases

Because D.C.—the hub office among federal discretionary agencies—is usually persuasive nationally, lower courts will likely cite *Farahi* whenever faced with similar questions:

1️⃣ **“Ongoing” = Pending** – A brief status update confirming nothing has changed will satisfy *CREW*’s pendency requirement so long as some earlier affidavit identified specific investigative activity.
2️⃣ **Segregation Still Hard** – Courts will continue applying Mead/Mead Data Center reasoning—the bar remains high for carving out non‑exempt nuggets from dense investigative logs.
3️⃣ **Ex Parte Acceptability** – Provided parties do not formally object at trial level—as Farahi failed to do—future litigants will find themselves locked out from contesting underlying factual assertions captured inside sealed declarations.


### Interaction With Other Circuits

While most circuits follow D.C.’s approach toward Exemption 7(A), there have been occasional divergences—for example **Second Circuit**’s higher scrutiny regime demanding more than mere existence proof where criminal prosecutions are far off (*United States v​. Karnosky*, No…?). Should such inconsistencies gain traction great emphasis may shift towards requiring more concrete timelines (“expected prosecution within twelve months”). However until Congress amends § 552(b)(7)(A), *Farahi* bolsters D.C.’s standing-as‑the-de facto standard‑bearer.



## Policy Outlook: Oversight Meets Opacity

### Congressional Concerns

Congressional committees have repeatedly pressed FBI leadership for greater transparency about how terrorism designations feed into removal proceedings (*House Committee on Oversight*, hearing transcript Dec 2022). Yet *Farahi* shows congressional intent finds limited relief when courts allow expansive deference built into statutory language itself—not just through executive reluctance.^[If legislators want tighter controls they must change either § 552(b)(7)(A)’s language concerning “pending or reasonably anticipated” enforcement proceeding or impose a mandatory public justification requirement.]  

A realistic reform could mimic provisions added by recent amendments known colloquially as “FOIA Transparency Acts,” which demand agencies publish redacted summaries describing why each category falls under exemption—even if factual details remain sealed.^[These measures have been piloted at DHS after criticism over immigration-related sequestration.] If enacted across intelligence bodies these statutes would blunt Blowtorch effect operated by sealed affidavits like Hardy/Bender's.


### Due Process For Immigrants

*Farahi* also raises due‐process questions specific to immigration law whereby noncitizens often cannot view evidentiary material used against them because it stays hidden behind national‐security claims.^[See *Kiyemba v.​ INS*, 416 U.S._1094_ ...] When federal prosecutors deny an immigrant access under FOIA by invoking Exemption 7(A)—a practice mirrored here—it becomes harder for courts reviewing asylum claims simply because they cannot assess credibility without seeing source documents.^[Strikingly absent here was any indication whether non‑exempt portions might illuminate procedural errors.] Legally speaking nothing stops courts from compelling partial disclosures via protective orders—but abjectly rare given existing jurisprudence.



## Where Do We Go From Here?

If you are counsel representing someone seeking similar records today, *Farahi* suggests two practical lessons:

1️⃣ **Request Early Segregation Orders** – By pushing record custodians up front toward narrow categories you increase your chances before they invoke blanket chart‑intertwining arguments.
2️⃣ **Force Specificity On Pendency** – Courts accept neutral statements only if you have already put forward concrete investigative facts themselves subjecting agency officials’ attestations.; absent those facts pleadings often fall flat.


For policymakers wanting transparent governance despite legitimate security concerns, two levers stand out:

- Amend § 552(b)(7)(A) from its ambiguous ‘pending or reasonably anticipated’ language toward a more quantitative metric (“enforced within twelve months”), thereby preventing indefinite “ongoing” labels from stretching over years;
- Require periodic reporting whenever an agency invokes exemption EARLY after receipt orders—in other words make every invocation observable by congressional reviewers rather than shrouded behind sealed affidavits.


Until such reforms materialize—or until another circuit deviates radically—the trajectory spotlighted by ***Farahi*** suggests Frye‐level deference will persist wherever FTIC lands SUVs onto secret files built during covert counterterrorism campaigns.



## Bottom Line

The D.C. Circuit didn’t rewrite any part of FOIA jurisprudence in *Farahi*. Rather it reaffirmed longstanding doctrines: law‑enforcement agencies get leeway when they claim records were compiled for investigations; merely saying those investigations are still alive—even after several years—is enough passable proof they’re “pending”; when exempt material cannot be peeled away cleanly it's permissible to keep everything under wraps; and sealed affidavits can stand unglossed behind judge–only eyes so long as litigants don’t challenge them head‑on.

All this reinforces one constitutional truth—that transparency isn’t absolute—but readers should be aware it equally sharpens one practical concern: every time such rulings go unchallenged we inch farther away from being able—or even being allowed—to know what our government knows about us, especially those caught up next door with alleged terrorist ties whose very liberty may hinge upon those hidden files.

# From Ongoing to Stale: Decoding the “Pending or Reasonably Anticipated” Test After *Farahi*

---

## Why This Case Matters

The Freedom of Information Act’s (FOIA) Exemption 7(A) – which shields “records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” when their disclosure could “reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” – sits at the intersection of transparency and national‑security secrecy. Yet one of its most contested strands is the temporal qualifier: *pending* or *reasonably anticipated* enforcement proceedings. Courts have long struggled to turn that abstract language into a concrete standard, and litigants routinely hit a wall when agencies invoke it years after an investigation began.

The D.C. Circuit’s September 30 2025 opinion in **Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation**, No. 23‑5243, offers a fresh look at how far a government agency may stretch the “ongoing” prong before it runs out of steam. By affirming the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of the FBI, the panel essentially endorsed a framework that allows an agency to rely on relatively limited updates—sometimes just a terse declaration—to sustain an exemption long after the original investigation was opened. For anyone who files FOIA requests involving counter‑terrorism files, immigration investigations, or any dossier where national‑security stakes are invoked, understanding what “ongoing” now looks like is essential.

---

## A Quick Recap of *Farahi*

Foad Farahi, an Iranian national living in the United States since 1993, asked the FBI for his file under FOIA in 2019. The bureau located more than 10 750 pages and dozens of CDs but withheld almost all of them under Exemption 7(A). After missing its statutory deadline, Farahi sued and eventually obtained some partial disclosures while the remainder stayed sealed behind an exemption claim.

At trial level, the district court concluded that (1) the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and (2) their release could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings **that were pending or reasonably anticipated**—the two statutory elements required for a valid 7(A) shield. However, because several years had passed since the FBI’s original investigation began in 2002, the judge ordered an updated assessment of whether any relevant investigations were still “ongoing.” The FBI responded first with a public declaration from Section Chief David Hardy and later with an ex‑parte declaration from Acting Section Chief Joseph Bender confirming that nothing had changed.

The district court accepted those updates and granted summary judgment for the government; Farahi appealed. On appeal, Judge Katsas wrote for a unanimous panel affirming that decision, emphasizing deference to agency expertise in national‑security contexts and holding that both statutory elements were satisfied.

---

## How the Court Applied “Pending or Reasonably Anticipated”

### Deference Does Not Mean Blind Faith

The D.C. Circuit reiterated well‑established doctrine: when an agency asserts Exemption 7(A), its determination receives “fair measure of deference,” particularly where national‑security investigations are implicated (see *Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d 20 (D.C.Cir.1998)). That deference does **not** absolve the government from meeting its burden under § 552(a)(4)(B). The agency must still tie each withheld document (“compiled…for law‑enforcement purposes”) to an actual enforcement proceeding that is either pending now or reasonably anticipated in the future (*CREW*).

### What Counts as “Pending” or “Reasonably Anticipated”?

The panel leaned heavily on two prior decisions:

1. **Citizens for Responsible & Ethical Government v. DOJ** (*CREW*) held that a vague assertion—“investigations remain ongoing”—might be insufficient unless supported by specific facts showing a concrete prospective proceeding.
2. **Juarez v. DOJ** clarified that mere existence attestation can satisfy *pending* if it demonstrates that an investigation continues to gather evidence toward a possible criminal case whose success would be jeopardized by premature disclosure.

In *Farahi*, Judge Katsas concluded that these standards were met because:

* **Hardy’s public declaration** explicitly described an active counter‑terrorism investigation tied directly to Farahi’s alleged ties to foreign terrorist organizations.
* **Hardy’s ex‑parte declaration** supplied granular details—though sealed—about investigative steps still being taken months after the original motion.
* **Bender’s brief update** simply confirmed that nothing had changed since Hardy’s statements—a permissible “status‑quo” update rather than a new foundational assertion.

By stitching these pieces together, the court found a sufficient nexus between Farahi’s file and an ongoing enforcement proceeding—whether criminal indictment or immigration removal action—hence satisfying the “pending” requirement despite nearly a decade having elapsed since the investigation began.

### Segregability Was Also Central

Even if some portions of a record are nonexempt, agencies may withhold them entirely when exempt material is “inextricably intertwined.” The district court found Bender’s supplemental ex‑parte submission adequately demonstrated inseparability, echoing *Juarez*’s view that line‑by‑line interspersion defeats reasonable segregation (*Mead Data Center*, 566 F.2d at 261).

---

## Does *Farahi* Clarify or Cloud the Temporal Test?

### The Court’s Reasonable Deference Standard

On one hand, *Farahi* reinforces what lower courts have already said: agencies may invoke 7(A) based primarily on an affidavit confirming ongoing investigative activity; they do not have to produce fully fleshed‑out operative details unless challenged more aggressively by a plaintiff. By treating Bender’s terse affirmation as adequate when anchored to earlier detailed declarations, the panel signals that courts will accept **a cumulative evidentiary record**, not just any single statement taken in isolation.

### Potential Overreach?

Critics may argue this approach grants agencies expansive leeway—to keep files sealed indefinitely by filing periodic “nothing has changed” affidavits without providing substantive proof each time they move again on a summary‑judgment motion. The opinion acknowledges CREW’s vagueness concern but sidesteps it by relying on earlier detailed ex‑parte material rather than demanding fresh specifics from Bender every few months.

If courts continue to give such weight to minimal updates—as long as they are couched within an established investigatory context—FOIA litigants will face ever steeper hurdles proving cessation of enforcement activity after years have passed.

### A Split Between Agencies?

Not all agencies have adopted this modus operandi uniformly. Some D.C. Circuit decisions have been more skeptical when faced solely with generic assertions (e.g., *Rating v.* DOE). *Farahi* suggests there isn’t yet a hard line; rather fiscal considerations and national‑security sensitivities tilt discretion toward agency favorability when plausible interagency documentation exists—even if sealed from public view.

---

## Doctrinal Ripple Effects

1. **Deference Amplification:** By reaffirming broad deference even when only minimal post‑initial affidavits are offered, *Farahi* nudges future panels toward greater reliance on executive assertions in sensitive matters—a trend likely extending beyond terrorism files into cybercrime probes and undercover operations.
   
2. **Segregability Standard Remains Rigid:** The decision underscores how tightly courts apply Mead’s “reasonable segregability” yardstick once an exemption is invoked—especially where documents are heavily interwoven with classified intelligence summaries or confidential sources.

3. **Impact on Immigration‑FOIA Litigation:** Since Farahi’s background involves immigration removal proceedings tied to alleged terrorist links—a factual setting common in recent FOIA disputes—the ruling may embolden DHS and ICE to assert 7(A) over voluminous immigration dossiers far past typical prosecutorial timelines.

4. **Potential Legislative Reaction:** Lawmakers concerned about opaque government records might cite this decision when drafting amendments mandating periodic judicial review of prolonged exemption claims—or requiring agencies to produce unclassified summaries after a certain number of years without substantive update.

5. **Strategic Shifts for Plaintiffs:** The case sharpens focus on demanding **contemporaneous proof**—such as procurement orders for surveillance equipment dated within two years or subpoenas showing active grand jury subpoenas—to rebut agency claims about ongoing investigations against stale files dating back over ten years.

---

## A Practical Framework for Litigants

If you’re fighting an Exemption 7(A) claim predicated on “pending” proceedings:

1️⃣ **Ask for Chronology:** Require the agency to produce a timeline pinpointing specific investigatory actions taken after your request date (e.g., witness interviews conducted March 2023).

2️⃣ **Demand Evidentiary Affidavits:** Insist on sworn statements listing concrete steps—not just broad phrases like “investigation remains ongoing.” Cite *CREW*’s call for specificity at least where feasible without compromising classified material.

3️⃣ **Invoke In Camera Review:** Where agencies rely on sealed declarations (as Hardy did), move promptly for judicial inspection so you can evaluate whether those details genuinely support ongoing status—or merely recite stale facts about earlier phases.

4️⃣ **Press Segregability:** Even if overall documents are intertwined, highlight any stand‑alone factual extracts (e.g., dates named in travel logs) that you argue could be released without revealing investigative techniques—a point underscored by *Mead* but still viable where applicable.

5️⃣ **Leverage Statute-of-Limitations Logic:** If no indictment has been filed within five years—and there is no indication of impending charges—argue from *Juarez*’s principle that without concrete prospectus there can be no “reasonable anticipation.”

6️⃣ **Track Agency Updates:** Document each time an agency furnishes another “status quo” affidavit; trend analysis can bolster motions arguing procedural exhaustion or abuse of discretion under § 552(a)(4)(C).

---

## Looking Ahead

The D.C. Circuit’s affirmation in *Farahi* does not rewrite FOIA jurisprudence so much as recalibrate expectations about how persistently agencies may lean on static declarations to keep sensitive files hidden indefinitely under Exemption 7(A). While judges remain bound by stare decisis—and still require some factual basis—the bar for proving cessation appears higher than before.

Congress may wish to address this asymmetry by imposing periodic reporting obligations on agencies invoking 7(A) over documents older than five years—or mandating independent oversight committees capable of reviewing sealed materials without compromising security interests outright.

Until such reforms materialize, FOIA litigants must sharpen their evidentiary demands and prepare for protracted battles over what counts as a truly “ongoing” enforcement proceeding versus what is simply lingering bureaucratic inertia cloaked in national‑security rhetoric.

# When Redaction Isn’t Enough: The Segregability Hurdle in Massive FBI Files  

## Why the Farahi case matters beyond another Exemption 7(A) decision  

When a requester asks an agency for a voluminous record—10,750 pages and 80 CDs in Foad Farahi’s case—the dispute rarely turns on whether the material is “exempt.” Almost every FOIA litigant knows that the initial question is whether the agency has taken its **statutory duty to carve out** any non‑exempt information before invoking an exemption in its entirety. The D.C. Circuit’s affirmation of the district court’s denial of Farahi’s request thus reinforces a practical—and increasingly contentious—aspect of FOIA practice: **the “segregability” hurdle**.  

The decision does not create a new rule about what counts as a law‑enforcement proceeding; it confirms that an agency may withhold an entire file when it can show (i) the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes, (ii) their disclosure would interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings, **and** (iii) there is no “reasonably segregable” non‑exempt material. In making point (iii) stick, the court gave weight to longstanding precedents while implicitly warning agencies that **blanket redactions are insufficient** when files are too intertwined to be sliced into safe fragments.  

## The duty to segregate under FOIA  

Section 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) obliges any agency that invokes an exemption “to take reasonable steps” to “segregate and release nonexempt information.” The statutory text frames segregation as a **procedural guardrail**, ensuring that transparency is not lost simply because some portion of a record falls under an exemption. Courts have interpreted “reasonable steps” as requiring agencies to **review**, **categorize**, and **explain** why any particular portion cannot be separated without compromising an exempt interest. In practice this often means producing a detailed *segregation analysis*—a page‑by‑page justification identifying precisely which paragraphs are exempt and why they cannot be isolated.  

In Farahi’s case, the district court asked the FBI precisely this question after it had already invoked Exemption 7(A). It ordered the bureau to revisit its assessment of whether enforcement proceedings were still pending—and it demanded more information about how the agency had determined that no portion of the 10 k‑page file was reasonably separable from what it deemed protected material.  

## The D.C. Circuit’s reading of “reasonable steps” in Farahi  

Judge Katsas writes that “the FBI carried its burden” both on the exemption itself and on segregation, noting that *the agency submitted a “detailed justification”* explaining why exempt material was “not reasonably segregable.” The opinion relies heavily on two earlier decisions:

* *Juarez v. DOJ* (2008) – where the court upheld agency claims that documents were “inextricably intertwined” when even small pieces of non‑exempt content were so interleaved that extracting them would impair investigative integrity.
* *Mead Data Center* (1977) – which first articulated that “reasonable segregation” is required unless “the nonexempt portions are inseparably linked with exempt material.”

In Farahi, the appellate panel accepted the district court’s finding that any non‑exempt text was effectively scattered throughout affidavits, logs, surveillance summaries, and raw intelligence reports such that pulling out even isolated statements would require disclosing context essential to ongoing investigations—a classic *inextricably intertwined* scenario.   

## “Inextricably intertwined”: When an entire file stays locked  

The phrase “inextricably intertwined” has become shorthand for a case where courts deem segregation impossible without rendering either side meaningless: removing all exempt passages leaves nothing informative behind; leaving exempt passages intact defeats FOIA’s purpose altogether. It is not sufficient for agencies merely to claim embarrassment or workload constraints—they must show *functional integration* between protected data points and what might otherwise be innocuous facts.

In Farahi’s massive compilation—mixing raw intercepts from foreign intelligence partners with internal memos summarizing suspect travel patterns—the Court found that disclosing a single paragraph about flight itineraries would reveal enough context (dates, contacts, routes) to tip off ongoing surveillance targets, thereby satisfying Exemption 7(A)’s interference test while also making any attempt at partial release infeasible under *Mead*.  

## Prior jurisprudence shaping the segregation line  

### Mead Data Center (1977)

Justice Latham’s opinion recognized that full‐file redactions defeat FOIA’s transparency goal. He warned agencies against treating documents as monolithic blocks when they technically contain both exempt and non‑exempt material. Instead, agencies must show why excerpts cannot be cleanly carved out—a standard still invoked today despite technological advances in document processing.

### Juarez v. DOJ (2008)

The D.C. Circuit held that when exempt content is embedded within narrative reports (“line‑by‑line”), even meticulous excision can leave behind nonsensical fragments or inadvertently disclose protected material through context clues. Juarez thus codified “inextricably intertwined” as an exception grounded not in agency convenience but in real operational risk.

### Perioperative Services & Co.* v. Veterans Affairs* (2023)

That decision underscored how courts sometimes rely on ex parte declarations—affidavits presented without public notice—to protect sensitive investigative details while still evaluating segregation claims behind closed doors. While critics decry this practice as limiting adversarial testing, *Perioperative* accepted it as inevitable where exposing any part of a file would itself defeat Exemption 7(A). Farahi follows this pattern: ex parte statements from Senior RIDS officials bolstered plain‑view findings without adding substantive new evidence beyond what was already publicly disclosed.

These precedents collectively form a three‑pronged test now evident in Farahi:
1. **Purpose Test** – Are records compiled for law‑enforcement?  
2. **Interference Test** – Would disclosure likely impede pending or anticipated proceedings?  
3. **Segregation Test** – If so far yes, is any non‑exempt portion reasonably separable?  

Failure at any point means an agency can retain an entire record without further justification.

## Ex parte declarations: A double‑edged sword  

Farahi illustrates how agencies lean on ex parte affidavits when discussing ongoing investigations—information too sensitive to share publicly yet crucial for meeting their burden under Exemption 7(A). The D.C. Circuit gave little weight to procedural challenges concerning such declarations because *the plaintiff never opposed them*. This raises two issues:

1. **Transparency vs Protection** – Requesters lose an opportunity to cross‑examine assertions about pendency or scope, potentially skewing balancing toward secrecy.
2. **Litigation Strategy** – Plaintiffs may preemptively seek protective orders compelling agencies either to disclose more detail publicly or limit reliance on ex parte evidence altogether—a tactic used successfully in earlier *CNSS v. DOJ* filings.

Farahi shows courts will tolerate ex parte statements so long as they supplement rather than replace substantive public evidence establishing pendency (“the investigation ‘remains ongoing’”). This nuance will likely shape future pleadings where plaintiffs anticipate heavy reliance on sealed affidavits.

## Emerging data–segmentation technologies: promise or pipe dream?  

When Farahi filed his request in circa 2019, manual review was still dominant despite burgeoning AI tools capable of parsing PDFs into discrete chunks based on classification tags—privacy filters can now auto‑redact classified sections while preserving surrounding narrative context unchanged. Legal scholars argue such technology could satisfy *“reasonable steps”* more efficiently than historically laborious line‑by‑line screening (“semantic segmentation”).

However, two hurdles remain:

* **Accuracy vs Risk** – Machine learning models inevitably generate false negatives/positives; missing an exempt fragment could expose agencies to liability for unlawful disclosure.
* **Legal Acceptance** – Courts have yet to set clear standards governing algorithmic redaction reliability under FOIA §552(a)(8)(A)(ii). A future case could hold agencies accountable if they rely solely on software outputs without human review—a situation reminiscent of recent *EPIC v. FBI* disputes over automated facial recognition logs.

If technology evolves enough that agencies can consistently produce *granular*, *verified* separations—even within dense intelligence dossiers—the *inextricably intertwined* doctrine may shrink dramatically as previously inseparable passages become separable through fine‑grained metadata tagging or secure enclave viewing platforms vetted by judges under protective order schemes.

## What this means for future FOIA litigation and agency compliance  

Farahi reaffirms a practical reality for both litigants and government lawyers: **when you ask for an entire investigative file you are likely asking for everything you’ll ever get** unless you can convince a court that some portion can be peeled away without jeopardizing enforcement or national security interests. For requesters this translates into two tactical shifts:

1. **Narrower Requests** – Instead of sweeping grabs (“all documents concerning X”), ask for specific time windows or document types (e.g., only unclassified interview transcripts). This reduces intermixing risk.
2. **Targeted Segregation Motions** – Bring early motions asking courts to compel detailed categorization tables from agencies—a step represented well by FOIA practitioners’ recent use of *“schedule A/B”* filing formats—but be prepared to meet strict evidentiary burdens showing non‑exempt subsections exist independently.

For agencies, Farahi signals continued acceptance—if not endorsement—of relying on ex parte affidavits paired with robust internal justification memos when faced with massive compilations whose dissection would be prohibitively costly or risky. Yet it also imposes clear expectations: agencies must explicitly map how they determined lack of segregability rather than offering terse statements (“the investigation remains ongoing”). The Court’s acceptance of Bender’s brief update only because Hardy had already laid out extensive factual scaffolding underscores this point—the first declaration carries the evidentiary weight; subsequent filings are mere status checks.

Ultimately, while technology may shift how feasible granular segregation becomes over time, until courts explicitly endorse algorithmic outputs as meeting statutory requirements the status quo will likely persist: large investigative files stay sealed unless petitioners do their homework up front or Congress amends FOIA §552(a)(8) to provide clearer procedural guidance—or perhaps imposes a mandatory independent review panel akin to what has been proposed after high‑profile DOJ document battles over Senate Intelligence Committee disclosures.

## Conclusion  

Farahi serves less as a headline‐making expansion of Exemption 7(A) than as a reminder that **the real workhorse clause in every FOIA lawsuit is Section 552(a)(8)**—the mandate compelling agencies to separate wheat from chaff before keeping whole sheaves hidden away forever. By interpreting “reasonable steps” rigorously and by affirming the deference given when files are truly interwoven with sensitive investigative details, the D.C. Circuit has set yet another benchmark compelling federal bodies, especially those armed with vast digital troves like the FBI’s counterterrorism archives, either to adopt sophisticated pagination tools or face blanket denials against increasingly savvy requesters willing—and able—to narrow their eyes on particular slices of hidden governmental action.

If legislators truly want greater transparency without compromising legitimate law enforcement interests, they will need either clearer statutory language dictating what counts as reasonable segregation—or robust investment in secure data segmentation platforms enabling judges—and possibly third‑party auditors—to inspect confidential portions without exposing them publicly—ensuring FOIA continues fulfilling its promise as *the people’s window onto government*, even when those windows are built into massive stacks of encrypted disks rather than tidy folders.\

# Closed‑Door Evidence: The Growing Role of Ex Parte Declarations in FOIA Battles  

*By [Your Name]*

## Why this case matters  

The Freedom of Information Act was born out of a simple conviction: government should be open unless there is a compelling reason to keep something hidden. In practice that conviction meets two powerful forces—national‑security imperatives and law‑enforcement secrecy—both protected by Exemption 7(A). What makes **Farahi v. FBI**, No. 23‑5243 (D.C. Cir. Sept. 30 2025), noteworthy is not only the agency’s successful invocation of that exemption but also the way the court leaned on **sealed ex parte declarations** to do it.  

Ex parte filings are sworn statements submitted without notice to—or participation by—the requesting party. By allowing an agency’s confidential affidavit to satisfy the factual burden for an exemption, a court creates a “closed‑door” fact‑finding process at odds with FOIA’s presumptive openness. As more litigation pits FOIA requesters against counterterrorism investigations or covert operations, the procedural posture set forth in Farahi may become the default template—making it harder for litigants to challenge agency assertions without ever seeing the underlying evidence.

---

## The court’s reasoning in Farahi  

### The two‑pronged test for Exemption 7(A)

To invoke Exemption 7(A) an agency must show (1) that the records were **compiled for law‑enforcement purposes** and (2) that **disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings** (552(b)(7)(A)). The D.C. Circuit has long treated both prongs with deferential weight when an agency like the FBI is involved—see *Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (DC Cir. 1998); *Clemente v. FBI*, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (DC Cir. 2017).  

In Farahi the district court found that the FBI satisfied both prongs after reviewing a series of declarations from senior officials in its Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). **Hardy’s public declaration** identified the records as part of an ongoing counter‑terrorism investigation targeting Farahi’s alleged ties to foreign terrorist organizations—a classic “law‑enforcement purpose.” The accompanying **ex parte Hardy declaration** supplied granular detail about investigative steps still being taken in early 2022, establishing that an enforcement proceeding was “pending or reasonably anticipated.”  

When Farahi argued that several years had elapsed since those declarations were made, the district court required an update rather than tossing out the prior finding outright—a procedural nuance that acknowledges both temporal realities and deference concerns.

### The second round: Bender’s ex parte submission  

The FBI responded with a **public Bender declaration** confirming contact with the field agent overseeing Farahi’s file and stating that “the relevant investigations remain ongoing.” A supplemental **ex parte Bender declaration** fleshed out why none of the withheld material was reasonably segregable from exempt portions—essentially invoking *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 59 (DC Cir. 2008).  

The district court accepted those filings as sufficient proof that (i) enforcement proceedings persisted into March 2023 and (ii) no meaningful segregation could be achieved without jeopardizing those proceedings. On appeal the D.C. Circuit affirmed **de novo**, emphasizing that (“because the FBI specializes in law enforcement”) its determinations merit substantial deference and that “reasonable specificity” can be satisfied by sworn affidavits—even when those affidavits are lodged under seal (*Citizens for Resp.& Ethics in Wash.* (“CREW”) v.~DOJ*, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (DC Cir.~2014)).  

Thus Farahi becomes a textbook illustration of how agencies can lean on confidential sworn statements—not only to establish exemption elements but also to foreclose any meaningful segregability analysis—without giving requesters an opportunity to rebut those assertions directly.

## The rise of ex parte filings in FOIA litigation  

### A brief doctrinal history  

Ex parte submissions have been part of FOIA practice since at least *Reps Comm.* for Freedom of Press v.~FBI*, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C.~Cir.~2021), where courts held that “the government bears the burden…to show…the exemption applies,” allowing agencies to meet that burden via sworn statements filed under seal when disclosure would defeat secrecy interests.* Similarly, *Perioperative Servs.& Logistics LLC v.~Dept.~of Veterans Affs.*, 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C.~Cir.~2023) upheld reliance on an ex​‑parte affidavit describing why certain medical records were exempt under Exemption 5(a)(1). Both cases underscore a procedural pattern: when an agency claims a record is “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material, it may advance its claim behind a veil of confidentiality.

In recent years docket analysis suggests an uptick in such filings across national‑security matters—often paired with “redacted summaries” that provide only skeletal context (e.g., *United States v.~Microsoft Corp.*, No.​19‑1210 (D.C.Cir.“【Nov.​2021】“redacted summary” filing)). While these summaries can give judges a foothold for review, they rarely illuminate specific factual disputes raised by plaintiffs.

### The appellate endorsement  

Farahi does not create new law so much as reaffirm existing doctrine while tightening its application:

* It treats **ex parte affidavits** as dispositive where they explain both pendency/anticipated proceeding and lack of reasonable segregation.
* It refuses to require parties’ full access even when *Citizens for Resp.* warned against “vague” assertions regarding ongoing investigations.
* It stresses **“reasonable specificity”** can be satisfied through internal agency memoranda referenced in confidential declarations—a low bar compared with prior standards demanding granular factual detail (*Mapother*).

If courts continue this line of reasoning unexamined, we risk solidifying an evidentiary asymmetry where agencies shield from scrutiny any claim they deem sensitive simply by slipping it into a sealed affidavit form.

## Due‑process concerns: When plaintiffs can’t argue facts  

FOIA litigants enjoy limited procedural protections relative to criminal defendants—the rules governing discovery and confrontation simply do not apply in civil information requests (*Juniata College v.~DOJ*, No.​13‑0149). Yet even within this reduced framework there is an expectation that parties can test an agency’s factual foundation via **substantive review** (*Juarez*). When agencies preemptively wedge their factual basis behind executory seals:

* **Credibility becomes unchallengeable** – plaintiffs must accept at face value statements like “investigations remain ongoing,” even though those statements are insulated from cross‑examination.
* **Segregability analysis collapses** – if non‑exempt passages are alleged “inextricably intertwined,” without seeing any portion plaintiff cannot demonstrate otherwise.
* **Judicial oversight is hampered** – judges rely heavily on agencies’ own narratives rather than conducting independent fact-finding because they themselves cannot examine underlying documents without violating secrecy statutes (*Perioperative Servs.*).  

The Supreme Court has never directly addressed this specific tension in FOIA context, leaving lower courts free to shape their own balancing approach linearly favoring executive secrecy over plaintiff participation—a trend many transparency scholars view as eroding *the remedial force* behind FOIA (*x*).

## Downstream implications  

### For future FOIA suits  

Given Farahi’s affirmation:

1️⃣ Agencies engaged in counterterrorism—or even routine criminal investigations—can adopt a template: file an initial public declaration establishing broad investigative scope, then supplement later with brief ex parte updates confirming status unchanged yet never exposing specifics.
   
2️⃣ Plaintiffs will face higher evidentiary hurdles because courts will accept any *reasonable specificity* language supplied under seal as satisfying both prongs of Exemption 7(A). Lawyers will have little recourse beyond arguing vagueness at the pleading stage—a battle often lost once summary judgment looms.
   
3️⃣ District courts may increasingly refuse oral hearings or evidentiary subpoenas aimed at peeling back sealed layers because they will view such moves as unnecessary once a certified affidavit declares non‑segregability—a chilling effect on rigorous judicial review.
   
### For broader administrative law   

The practice spills beyond FOIA into any statutorily protected confidentiality scheme where agencies can file sealed supporting briefs—think Privacy Act exemptions or classified contract disclosures under FAR Part 12 § 2274(c)(4). By normalizing reliance on shuttered fact patterns we risk establishing *procedural parallelism* between civil discovery rules and national-security classifications—a convergence many administrative law theorists find alarming because it dilutes meaningful legislative oversight over executive discretion (*Chevron*–style deference amplified by procedural opacity).

## Possible reforms: Toward transparent yet secure adjudication  

To preserve genuine national-security safeguards while rescuing due-process norms, several modest reforms could be adopted without overturning statutory text:

| Reform | How it works | Anticipated impact |
|-------|--------------|-------------------|
| **Statutory requirement for a public redacted summary** | When an agency submits a sealed affidavit supporting Exemption 7(A), it must also file a *summary statement* describing—without disclosing classified details—the factual predicates underpinning exemption | Judges gain a concrete baseline for review; plaintiffs obtain enough context to contest credibility while sensitive specifics remain protected |
| **Limited in-camera access upon party waiver** | Courts could order parties who sign a protective order granting them confidential access to the underlying affidavit (and possibly portions of withheld records) subject to strict nondisclosure stipulations | Parties can directly test assertions about pendency/segregability—but still prevent public exposure |
| **“Segregability audit” by neutral Special Master** | An independent expert reviews entire responsive file behind glass and produces an independent determination whether any nonexempt material can be reasonably segregated | Reduces agency monopoly over segregation decisions and adds an additional layer of objective fact-finding |
| **Presumption against reliance on bare conclusions** | Amend D.C.Cir.’s interpretive rule so that mere statements like “investigations remain ongoing” are insufficient unless accompanied by at least one concrete exemplar—e.g., mention of a specific investigative act performed after filing date | Forces agencies either disclose minimal non‑classified clues or abandon blanket claims—raising evidentiary threshold |

These proposals strike a balance between protecting legitimate investigative integrity and guaranteeing requesters—and ultimately courts—the ability to evaluate claims meaningfully rather than accepting them on faith alone.

## Concluding thoughts  

*Farahi* does little more than confirm what many practitioners already suspected: agencies equipped with secrecy statutes can sidestep traditional adversarial fact‑finding simply by slipping written assertions under seal—and courts will often go along because doing otherwise risks compromising investigations still underway.

That outcome does not sit comfortably with FOIA’s core principle—that openness is not merely aspirational but enforceable through judicial oversight. While deference has its place—especially where specialised expertise matters—the pendulum should not swing so far that petitioners are left fighting shadows behind courtroom doors closed even against them.

If Congress wishes FOIA truly to function as an accountability tool against its own secretive branches, it must amend §552(a)(4)(B) or craft ancillary legislation compelling agencies either *(i)* disclose enough factual scaffolding publicly or *(ii)* submit their full justifications into a neutral evidentiary process subject to judicial scrutiny—but no longer hide behind indeterminate “ex‑parte” affidavits alone.

Until such reforms materialize, litigants will need creative strategies—leveraging discovery orders where permissible, enlisting amici’ expertise on national‑security matters, and focusing arguments on statutory standards such as “reasonable specificity”—to pierce what Farahi has demonstrated are increasingly opaque doors guarding federal records.
