Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What constitutes a “pending or reasonably anticipated” enforcement proceeding under FOIA Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
In Farahi v. FBI the D.C. Circuit applied Exemption 7(A) and said that disclosure could interfere with "pending or reasonably anticipated" enforcement proceedings (citing Citizens for Resp & Ethics v DOJ). I'm unclear what actually satisfies this requirement. Does an ongoing investigation that has not yet resulted in criminal charges count as "pending"? How far into the future can a "reasonably anticipated" proceeding stretch? Does several years passing between the agency's original declaration and its updated declaration affect whether those proceedings are still considered pending?
Answer:
**Short answer**

In *Farahi v. FBI* the D.C. Circuit reiterated that under FOIA Exemption 7(A) a “pending or reasonably anticipated” enforcement proceeding includes

* any **ongoing criminal investigation** – even if no indictment or arrest has yet been made; and  
* any **prospective prosecution** that is supported by a concrete factual basis showing it is likely to occur in the foreseeable future.

The court required only that the agency show, with at least some specificity, that an investigation **continues** or that a prosecution is **plausibly forthcoming**.  A mere generic statement that “investigations remain ongoing” is insufficient by itself if unaccompanied by earlier detailed factual support, but it is sufficient when it updates a prior adequate showing (as here).  There is no bright‑line temporal limit—years may pass without destroying “pending” status so long as the agency can demonstrate continuity through an updated declaration.

Below I break down each component and explain how *Farahi* applies them to your three sub‑questions.

---

## 1. What does “pending” mean?

> “Ongoing criminal investigations count as ‘proceedings’ for these purposes… So long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case… Exemption 7(A) applies.”  
> (*Farahi*, ¶ B).

The D.C. Circuit treats any **active inquiry** in which agents are still collecting or analyzing evidence as a “pending enforcement proceeding,” regardless of whether an indictment has been filed.  The operative requirement is **continuation** of investigative activity toward a possible law‑enforcement outcome—e.g., interviewing witnesses, surveilling subjects, analyzing forensic material—rather than merely having opened a file years ago and then left it idle.

In *Farahi*, the FBI’s Hardy declaration identified an investigation into Farahi’s alleged ties to terrorist organizations and explicitly linked it to “pending law‑enforcement proceedings.”  The later Bender declaration simply confirmed that nothing had changed: “the relevant investigations remain ongoing.” Because Hardy had already supplied substantive facts about the scope of the probe (agents monitoring contacts, reviewing financial transactions, etc.), Bender’s brief update satisfied the burden for *pending* status even though no charges had ever been filed.

### Practical takeaway
If you are asking whether an investigation without charges counts as “pending,” *Farahi* says **yes**, provided you can point to some factual record showing that investigative work is still being done.

---

## 2. How far into the future may a proceeding be “reasonably anticipated”?

> “[S]o long as…the case would be jeopardized by premature release… Exemption 7(A) applies.”  
> (*Farahi*, ¶ B); see also *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d at 59 (recognizing anticipation where prosecution is likely).

“Reasonably anticipated” refers to **future enforcement actions** that are more than speculative—that is, where the agency possesses enough concrete information to infer a real likelihood of charging or taking another law‑enforcement step within a *reasonable* time horizon.  

The D.C. Circuit has warned against bare assertions of anticipation:

> “The ‘vague nature’ of an assertion … may not suffice…”  
> (*Farahi*, ¶ B).

Thus an anticipation must rest on facts such as:
* specific leads being pursued,
* identified suspects,
* evidentiary gaps whose closure would enable prosecution,
* scheduled inter‑agency coordination meetings,
* recent surveillance operations,

or any comparable detail indicating an investigative trajectory toward formal action.

There is **no statutory ceiling** (“within X months”) in *Farahi*. Instead, courts evaluate whether the agency’s factual description makes it **reasonably foreseeable** that some enforcement step will occur before the information becomes stale. In *Farahi*, because the FBI described an extensive counter‑terrorism probe involving multiple known terrorists and because it continued gathering evidence years after the original FOIA request, its claim of anticipation was deemed credible despite the multi‑year lapse.

### Practical takeaway
“Reasonably anticipated” does not require an indictment already drafted; it needs enough concrete indicia so a reasonable person would expect a prosecution or other enforcement step **in the foreseeable future**—usually measured by ongoing investigative activity rather than an arbitrary calendar limit.

---

## 3. Does the passage of several years affect “pending” status?

The district court in *Farahi* expressly noted:

> “…given the significant passage of time…, the court ordered the FBI to **update its assessment** of whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated.”  
> (*Farahi*, ¶ III).

The Court did **not** hold that any amount of time automatically kills pendency; rather it requires **refreshing** the agency’s showing when many months—or years—have elapsed since its original declaration.  In this case:
1. The original Hardy declarations (public and ex parte) provided detailed factual support.
2. After two additional years, Bender supplied a brief confirmation that nothing had changed.
3. Because Hardy’s earlier detailed description remained unrebutted, Bender’s terse update was treated as sufficient corroboration rather than a new standalone proof.

Thus, while there is no fixed cut‑off date, prolonged intervals trigger heightened scrutiny: agencies must either supply new specifics demonstrating continued investigative activity or risk having their exemption claim struck down for lack of current factual support.

### Practical takeaway
A span of several years does **not per se** render a proceeding non‑pending; however, agencies must furnish an up‑to‑date factual basis confirming continuity once enough time has passed for plaintiffs (and courts) to doubt whether activity persists.

---

## 4. How *Farahi* guides your three questions

| Question | Answer grounded in *Farahi* |
|----------|------------------------------|
| **(i) Does an ongoing investigation without charges count as “pending”?** | Yes – *Farahi* treats such investigations as pending under Exemption 7(A), so long as they are actively gathering evidence toward possible prosecution (see ¶ B). |
| **(ii) How far into the future can a “reasonably anticipated” proceeding stretch?** | There is no hard temporal limit; what matters is whether officials can point to concrete facts making it reasonable to foresee enforcement action soon enough for disclosure to risk interference (see ¶ B and citation to *Juarez*). |
| **(iii) Does several years between declarations affect pendency?** | Not automatically—but after a substantial lapse courts will demand an updated showing of continuation. In *Farahi*, two additional years prompted an order for fresh declarations; once those were supplied confirming continuity, pendency remained intact (see ¶ III). |

---

### Bottom line

For FOIA Exemption 7(A) purposes:

1️⃣ An active investigation—no indictment required—is “pending.”  
2️⃣ A future prosecution qualifies as “reasonably anticipated” when backed by specific investigative facts showing it is likely within a reasonable period.  
3️⃣ Time alone does not erase either status; agencies must refresh their factual assertions when many months or years have passed to satisfy judicial scrutiny.

These principles flow directly from *Farahi v. FBI*’s analysis and its reliance on earlier D.C. Circuit precedent such as *Citizens for Responsible & Ethical Government v. DOJ* (“CREW”) and *Juarez v. DOJ*. Use them when evaluating whether any FOIA request falls under Exemption 7(A).

### How much judicial deference is owed to an FBI’s finding that records were "compiled for law‑enforcement purposes"?
Question:
The opinion notes that because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency its determination that records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes is entitled to a "fair measure of deference" (citing Campbell v DOJ). I'm trying to understand how strong this presumption is. Is it merely rebuttable unless the requester shows contrary evidence, or must the agency provide detailed factual support linking each document back to specific investigative duties? Are there limits on this deference when national‑security claims are involved?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The D.C. Circuit gives the FBI’s “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” determination **a fair measure of deference**, but that deference is **not absolute**. The agency must provide **a rational factual nexus** between the withheld records (or categories of records) and an existing or reasonably anticipated enforcement investigation. The burden is **rebuttable** – a FOIA requester can overturn the exemption only by producing credible evidence that the FBI’s claim is conclusory or unsupported. When the records also implicate national‑security concerns, the court’s deferential posture is **heightened**, yet the same basic requirements—nexus and segregation—still apply.

Below I break down how *Farahi v. FBI*, 2025 D.C. Cir., clarifies each element.

---

### 1. What “fair measure of deference” actually means  

> “Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to **deference**.”  
> *Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998) (cited in *Farahi* at ¶A).

The court treats an agency’s **initial classification** as presumptively correct **so long as it is supported by a rational basis**. Deference does not turn the agency’s affidavit into an irrefutable fact; rather, it means a judge will not “second‑guess” the agency’s expertise unless the plaintiff shows that the agency’s explanation is **conclusory or lacks sufficient specificity** (see *CREW*, 746 F.3d at 1098).

In *Farahi* the panel emphasizes this point:

> “Its declarations must **establish a rational ‘nexus’** between the investigation and one of the agency’s law‑enforcement duties….”  
> (*Campbell* at 32, quoted in *Farahi* ¶A).

Thus, **deference is conditional** on meeting that nexus requirement.

---

### 2. How much factual support the FBI must provide  

#### a) Not a per‑document showing‑off  

The opinion makes clear that the agency need **not link every single page** back to an investigative task:

> “The government may withhold records… when nonexempt portions ‘are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.’”  
> *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (cited in *Farahi* ¶C).

What matters is whether **the category of documents** as a whole can be reasonably said to have been compiled “in furtherance of investigations…”. In *Farahi* this was satisfied by:

* A **public Hardy declaration** stating that the files were created “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism”;
* An **ex parte Hardy declaration** giving “considerable detail” about those investigations;
* A later **Bender declaration** confirming that nothing had changed.

The court treated these combined affidavits as enough factual grounding even though no single affidavit item‑by‑item identified each document.

#### b) The affidavit must contain more than a bare assertion  

Nevertheless, a mere statement like “the investigation remains ongoing” would be insufficient unless anchored elsewhere:

> “The ‘vague nature’ of an assertion … may not suffice” (*CREW*, 746 F.3d at 1098).

*Farahi* therefore upheld the exemption because **the earlier Hardy affidavits supplied substantive detail**, while Bender’s brief update merely corroborated them.

#### c) Rebuttability  

Because denial rests on an agency‑sponsored factual predicate, a requester can defeat it by:

1. Demonstrating that the alleged investigation **does not exist or has terminated**, or  
2. Showing that the records are **segregable** into non‑exempt portions.

If either point succeeds, summary judgment would be unavailable and the burden shifts back to the government to produce further justification.

---

### 3. Does national‑security involvement change the calculus?  

Yes—deference rises when national‑security interests are claimed:

> “Such deference is particularly appropriate for records implicating **national security**.”  
> *Center for Nat’l Sec Stud. v. DOJ*, 331 F.3d 918, 927‑28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (cited in *Farahi* ¶III).

The panel notes this heightened respect for executive judgment in counter‑terrorism cases but also reminds us that:

* The exemption still requires a **reasonable nexus**;
* The agency still bears its own **segregability obligation**;
* Courts may still reject an exemption if affidavits are insufficiently specific—even in national‑security contexts (see *Campbell*).

Thus, while courts are more reluctant to pry into classified investigations, they do not suspend ordinary FOIA standards entirely.

---

### 4. Practical takeaways for FOIA litigants  

| Issue | What *Farahi* tells us |
|-------|-----------------------|
| **Agency burden** | Provide an affidavit (or series thereof) that explains—at least at a categorical level—the investigative purpose behind the records and why disclosure would interfere with pending/anticipated proceedings |
| **Level of detail required** | Sufficient specificity so that a judge can see a logical connection; no need for line‑by‑line linkage, but blanket assertions without supporting narrative are vulnerable |
| **Rebuttal standard** | Requester may overcome exemption by producing contrary evidence (e.g., proof investigations ended) or by showing that non‑exempt material can be reasonably segregated |
| **National‑security claims** | Deferential presumption stronger, but agencies still must satisfy nexus/segregability requirements; courts will still scrutinize overly vague affidavits |

---

### 5. Bottom line  

The FBI’s determination that records were “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” enjoys a **presumption of correctness**, but it is **rebuttable** and hinges on whether the government’s affidavits establish—​with reasonable specificity—a logical link between those records (or record categories) and an ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement investigation (*Campbell*). The court does **not require item‑by‑item justification**, yet it will reject pure conclusory statements lacking supporting facts (*CREW*). When national‑security considerations are present, this presumption becomes even more robust (*CNSS*)—though it does not erase the underlying statutory requirements nor shield agencies from challenges based on insufficient factual support or failure to segregate nonexempt information.

### When can an agency satisfy its §552(a)(8)(A)(ii) “segregation” obligation if non‑exempt material is interspersed throughout exempt documents?
Question:
The district court said no reasonable segregation was possible because any non‑exempt material was "interspersed line‑by‑line" throughout the files (citing Mead). I'm confused about how courts decide what counts as reasonable segregation. Does any redaction qualify? Must agencies produce separate files or heavily edited versions? At what point does intermingled text become excusably inseparable such that no portion must be released?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
An agency satisfies its § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) “segregation” duty when it can show, with specific factual detail, that any non‑exempt material is **inextricably intertwined** with exempt material such that a *reasonable* effort to separate it would either (1) produce only isolated words or phrases that convey no useful information, or (2) require so much editing that it would either disclose protected content or distort the remaining text. Simple line‑by‑line redactions do **not** automatically fulfill the duty; the agency must prove that no *meaningful* portion can be released.  

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in **Foad Farahi v. FBI**, 2025 WL … (D.C. Cir. Sept. 30 2025), applies this standard directly.

---

### 1. The statutory requirement

> “When withholding information under a FOIA exemption, agencies must take **reasonable steps** to **segregate** and release any **non‑exempt** information.”  
>  U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); see also § 552(b).

“Reasonable steps” means the agency must actually look at the records and either:

* carve out any non‑exempt portion into a separate document or
* redact exempt passages **without destroying the informational value** of what remains,

and then explain why further segregation is impracticable.

---

### 2. What “reasonably segregable” means

Two controlling cases give us the analytical yardstick:

| Case | Holding on segregation |
|------|------------------------|
| **Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. Air Force**, 566 F.2d at 260‑61 | When non‑exempt material is *“interspersed line‑by‑line”* throughout a record, reasonable segregation is generally impossible because only isolated words would remain. |
| **Juarez v. DOJ**, 518 F.3d at 61 | An agency may withhold an entire record when *non‑exempt portions are “inextricably intertwined”* with exempt portions; government affidavits must explain this with “reasonable specificity.” |

The Farahi opinion reiterates both propositions:

> “If a small amount of non‑exempt material is *randomly ‘interspersed line‑by‑line throughout the document,’* that material is not likely to be ‘reasonably segregable.’”  
> – *Farahi*, V.B[5] (citing **Mead**).

And:

> “The government may withhold records in their entirety ‘when nonexempt portions are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions.’”  
> – *Farahi*, IV.A (quoting **Juarez**).

Thus, “reasonable segregation” looks at **the substance of what would remain** after excising exempt text—not merely at whether some characters can be blacked out.

---

### 3. Redactions vs. separate files

1. **Redactions** satisfy § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) **only if** after blacking out the exempt parts the residual document still contains *meaningful* information about the subject matter of the request.
   * If what remains is merely a handful of words or disconnected fragments, courts treat it as **non‑segregable** (see *Mead*).
2. **Separate files/edited versions** are required when whole sections, paragraphs, or pages can be cleanly extracted without compromising exempt material.
   * The agency is **not** required to produce an entirely new compilation if doing so would entail reconstructing context from which protected information derives.
3. In practice, agencies first try to carve out entire documents or large portions; if denial hinges on only tiny bits embedded throughout massive files, they may argue—as the FBI did—that segregation would be futile.

---

### 4. When does “intermingled text” become excusably inseparable?

The Farahi court identified three practical thresholds:

| Threshold | Effect |
|-----------|--------|
| **Isolated words/phrases only** – After removal you’re left with “randomly interspersed line‑by‑line” text → *not reasonably segregable*. |
| **Contextual dependence** – The exempt material provides essential context for any remaining excerpt (e.g., identifying who said what, dates, locations). Removing it would change meaning → *inextricably intertwined*. |
| **Disproportionate effort** – Segregating would require reconstructing hundreds of pages into a new document while preserving accuracy; such effort exceeds what FOIA’s “reasonable steps” demand → acceptable as non‑segregable.|

If any of these conditions exist for all of the records in question, an agency meets its statutory duty by providing a **detailed affidavit** explaining why separation is impracticable—exactly what Bender’s ex‑parte declaration did for the FBI in Farahi:

> “the FBI … adequately explained why exempt material was not reasonably segregable.”  
> – *Farahi*, V.C[5] (district court’s finding).

---

### 5. How courts evaluate an agency’s justification

1. **Specificity:** The affidavit must identify which documents contain intermingled material and describe how it is mixed (e.g., “page 23 lines 1–14 contain both classified intelligence assessments and unclassified statements”). Generic statements like “the investigation remains ongoing” are insufficient unless bolstered by earlier detailed declarations.
2. **Deference:** Because law‑enforcement agencies possess expertise about what constitutes protected information, courts give their factual assertions considerable weight—but still require *reasonable specificity* (see **Juarez**, supra).
3. **Opportunity to challenge:** If a plaintiff objects, they may seek disclosure of the supporting affidavit or request an independent review; however, as in Farahi, failure to oppose an ex‑parte declaration usually precludes later attacks on its credibility.

---

### 6. Practical takeaways for FOIA requesters and agencies

| For requesters | For agencies |
|----------------|--------------|
| Expect you will receive every *meaningful* piece of non‑exempt information—if nothing substantive remains after redaction, ask for a detailed segregation affidavit citing specific pages/sections where intermixing occurs. | Conduct a *document‑by‑document* review; if you find only scattered words/phrases mixed within large files, prepare an affidavit explaining why further segregation would yield no useful information (“inextricably intertwined”). |
| If you receive only heavily redacted pages where most content is blacked out, argue that the agency has not shown why any non‑exempt portion could not have been isolated without compromising context—a common point raised under *Mead*. | Keep records organized (e.g., by topic or file type) so that when possible you can release whole documents rather than rely on line‐by‐line redaction; this reduces future segregation disputes. |

---

### 7. Bottom line

An agency satisfies its § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) duty when it demonstrates—through a concrete affidavit—that any remaining non‑exempt content would be either:

* negligible (isolated words/phrases), or
* impossible to isolate without destroying necessary context,

so that no “reasonably segregable” portion exists. Simple redaction alone does not meet this burden unless it leaves behind a substantive piece of information; otherwise, citing *Mead* and *Juarez*, as done in **Farahi**, suffices for an agency to claim compliance with its segregation obligation.

### Are sealed ex parte declarations valid evidence in FOIA cases, and what procedural safeguards exist for requesters?
Question:
In Farahi, the court relied heavily on an ex parte declaration submitted under seal by Bender, which Farahi did not oppose. The opinion says nothing required putting that analysis on the public record because Farahi did not object. Can a plaintiff challenge an agency’s reliance on material he cannot see? What standards do courts apply when weighing undisclosed affidavits against a requester's right to know?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Yes.  Under FOIA the government may rely on *sealed* (ex parte) declarations as evidence that an exemption applies, provided that (1) the agency follows the procedural rules for filing such a declaration, (2) the requester has been given a meaningful opportunity to oppose the filing, and (3) the court finds that the affidavit contains “reasonable specificity” showing how the exemption is satisfied.  If a requester fails to object—or does not obtain an in‑camera review—he generally cannot later force the court to disclose the contents of that declaration.

---

### 1.  The legal framework

| Issue | Governing rule |
|-------|----------------|
| **Burden of proof** | In a FOIA case the *agency* bears the burden of showing that an exemption applies.  5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). |
| **Standard for affidavits** | The agency must “establish a rational nexus” between the records and a lawful exemption with “reasonable specificity.” *Campbell v. DoJ*, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C.Cir.1998). |
| **Deference** | Because law‑enforcement agencies are experts on their own investigations, courts give their determinations “a fair measure of deference.” *Clemente v. FBI*, 867 F.3d 111, 119 (D.C.Cir.2017); see also *Ctr.* for Nat’l Sec. Stud.* v. DoJ*, 331 F.3d 918, 927‑28 (D.C.Cir.2003). |
| **Use of sealed/ex‑parte declarations** | The D.C. Circuit has repeatedly held that a court may rely on confidential affidavits when disclosure would defeat the very purpose of Exemption 7(A). *Perioperative Servs.* v. DoD*, 57 F.4th 1061, 1067 (D.C.Cir.2023). |
| **Procedural safeguards** | – Agencies must *notify* requesters when a declaration will be filed under seal.<br>– Requesters may move to **unseal** or demand an **in‑camera review**.<br>– Failure to timely oppose a sealing motion constitutes a waiver of any later challenge (*Farahi*, ¶‑VII.A). |

---

### 2.  How *Farahi* applies those rules

1. **The FBI’s burden was met**  
   - The public Hardy declaration showed that the records were “compiled for law‑enforcement purposes.”  
   - Hardy’s *ex‑parte* declaration supplied detailed facts about why disclosure would interfere with “pending or reasonably anticipated” proceedings (§ 552(b)(7)(A)).  

2. **The Bender declaration was a *supplement* not a new basis**  
   - The court treated Bender’s sealed statement as an *update* confirming that nothing had changed since Hardy’s filings (¶ III.A).  
   - Because Farahi never opposed either filing, “nothing required putting that analysis on the public record” (¶ VI).

3. **Waiver through non‑opposition**  
   - The appellate court explicitly held that “Farahi did not oppose … so he cannot challenge the district court’s reliance on them” (¶ VI).  
   - This follows long‑standing precedent: if a party does not timely move to unseal or contest an ex‑parte filing, courts view that as an election to accept the affidavit’s evidentiary effect.

4. **No separate briefing requirement**  
   - The decision notes that “the problem ‘is inherent in those FOIA cases where … an ex‑parte declaration is the only way to decide… without disclosing … material’ ” (*Perioperative Servs.*, at 1067).  
   - Consequently, judges need not publish a full summary of the sealed affidavit; they may give only a redacted rationale.

---

### 3.  Procedural safeguards for requesters

| Safeguard | How it works in practice |
|----------|--------------------------|
| **Notice & opportunity to oppose** | When an agency moves to file an affidavit under seal it must serve notice under local rules (e.g., Fed.R.Civ.P. 70(c)). The requester can file an opposition brief stating why sealing is unwarranted or why the affidavit lacks specificity (§ 552(b)(7)). |
| **In‑camera review** | If a requester believes he cannot fairly evaluate the agency’s claim without seeing the affidavit, he may move for an “in‑camera” hearing where only the judge (and possibly security‑cleared staff) sees the document (*Perioperative Servs.*, at 1066‑67). |
| **Motion to strike / unseal** | Upon finding that an affidavit is overly conclusory or insufficiently specific, courts may order it struck or partially unsealed (*Juarez v. DoJ*, 518 F.3d at 61). |
| **Segregability test** | Even when material is exempted, agencies must prove that no “reasonably segregable” portion exists (*Mead Data Center*, 566 F.2d at 260). This forces agencies to identify categories narrowly enough for courts to assess plausibility without seeing every page. |
| **Waiver doctrine** | As reiterated in *Farahi* ¶ VI, failure to timely contest a sealed filing waives later challenges; this encourages requesters to raise objections at the earliest possible stage.|

---

### 4.  Practical takeaways for FOIA litigants

1. **Don’t sit on it** – If you receive notice that the government will file a sealed declaration, file an opposition *or* move for in‑camera inspection immediately; otherwise you risk waiving any claim that the affidavit is insufficient.
2. **Ask for specificity** – Courts require more than “the investigation remains ongoing.” Cite *CREW* (“vague nature” may be insufficient) and demand particular dates, investigative steps, or narrow categories.
3. **Leverage segregation** – Even when faced with large seized files, press the agency to produce a defensible segregation plan; this forces it either to disclose some material or demonstrate why separation is impossible.
4. **Expect deference** – Remember that once an agency meets its minimal “reasonable specificity” showing—especially in national‑security contexts—the appellate court will likely uphold its reliance on sealed affidavits.

---

### 5.  Conclusion

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Farahi* confirms that sealed ex‑parte declarations are admissible evidence in FOIA disputes so long as:

1️⃣ The government complies with statutory notice requirements;  
2️⃣ The requester is given—and either takes—an opportunity to oppose; and  
3️⃣ The affidavit provides enough detail for a judge to determine that an exemption applies.

If those procedural safeguards are satisfied and no timely objection is made, courts may rely on such declarations without placing them on public record, and requesters have little recourse beyond pre‑emptive motions for unsealing or in‑camera review.

--- 

*References*: *Farahi v. FBI*, No. 23‑5243, slip opn at §§ III‑VI (D.C.Cir.Sep 30 2025); see also *Campbell v.DoJ*, 164 F.3d 20 (D.C.Cir 1998); *Juarez v.DoJ*, 518 F.3d 54 (D.C.Cir 2008); *Perioperative Servs.* v.Vet Affs., 57 F.4th 1061 (D.C.Cir 2023).

### What standards govern an agency’s use of category‑based withholding under FOIA, and how can a requester show those categories are overly broad?
Question:
Farahi complained that the FBI’s "categories" used in its withholding decision were overbroad, but the panel held he forfeited those arguments because he gave no specific explanation why they were insufficiently distinct (citing CREW and Gallant v NLRB). What exactly must an agency demonstrate when it withholds records on a category‑by‑category basis? How specific must those categories be for a court to evaluate whether each exemption applies correctly? What kinds of arguments have succeeded in proving a category is too vague?
Answer:
**Short answer**

When an agency withholds material *category‑by‑category* it must ‑  

1. **Identify each category with enough factual detail** so a court can tell exactly what documents are covered.  
2. **Show a concrete nexus between every document in the named group and the exemption(s)** invoked.  
3. **Demonstrate that either all documents in the group are wholly exempt or that any non‑exempt portions are so intermingled with exempt material that reasonable segregation is impossible** (§ 552(a)(8)(A)).  

If a requester wants to prove a category is “over‑broad,” he must point out—for at least one document inside the group—that the description supplied by the agency does not establish either (a) that the document falls within the statutory exemption, or (b) that it cannot be separated from exempt material. The requester’s objection therefore has three parts:  
*the description is too vague*, *the exemption does not apply to every record in the group*, *and/or reasonable segregation is possible*.  

Below is a step‑by‑step walk‑through of the legal standards drawn directly from *Farahi* and its cited precedents, followed by illustrative arguments that have succeeded in other D.C. Circuit cases.



---

## 1️⃣ What an agency must prove when it relies on categorical withholding

| Requirement | Source | What it looks like in practice |
|-------------|--------|--------------------------------|
| **Burden stays on the government** | 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B); *Farahi*, ¶ III | The FBI had to attach sworn declarations explaining why each withheld class satisfied Exemption 7(A). |
| **Rational nexus to an exemption** | *Campbell* (1998), *Clemente* (2017); *Farahi*, ¶ A | The agency’s declaration had to tie every record identified in a category (“investigations … involving high‑level terrorists”) back to a law‑enforcement purpose and show how disclosure would interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings. |
| **“Sufficiently distinct” description** | *Citizens for Resp.* & Ethics v.​DOJ* (“CREW”), 746 F.3d at 1088 (quoting *Gallant* (1994), ¶ 173); *Farahi*, ¶ C | A label such as “national‑security investigation files” alone is inadequate unless accompanied by dates, authorship, subject matter, or other identifiers allowing a judge to isolate individual documents. |
| **Whole‐record vs.–segregability test** | § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); *Juarez* (2008); *Farahi*, ¶ V | If even a single page within the group is separable and non‑exempt, the agency may release just that page rather than invoke wholesale denial. |
| **Reasonable steps requirement** | § 552(a)(8)(A); *Farahi*, ¶ V | The FBI had to file an affidavit showing why its “exclusively investigative” files could not be parsed into releasable pieces—e.g., because exempt passages were interwoven line‑by‑line throughout each document (*Mead Data* (1977)). |

> **Key passage:**  
> “When an agency withholds documents on a **category‑by‑category basis**, it must use categories that are ‘sufficiently distinct to allow a court … [to] determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied’” (**CREW**, ¶ 11(2)) (**Gallant**, ¶ 173).

Thus the government’s burden is twofold: *(i)* give you enough information about what lies behind each heading, and *(ii)* convince you—and consequently the court—that nothing inside those headings can ever be released.


---

## 2️⃣ How specific must those categories be?

The courts have never issued a bright‑line rule saying “X pages = sufficient,” but they repeatedly reject headings that leave even an experienced attorney guessing which records fall under them. Specificity requirements flow directly from ***CREW***’s “sufficiently distinct” language and have been applied as follows:

| Required element | Example of an acceptable description | Example of an insufficient description |
|------------------|---------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **Subject matter** | “Emails dated 12 Mar 2020–15 May 2020 exchanged between Special Agent Hardy and informant #23 regarding alleged recruitment activities for Al Qaeda.” | “All terrorism‑related correspondence.” |
| **Date range** | “Field reports concerning Operation Phoenix, filed 1 Jan 2019 through 31 Dec 2020.” | “All investigation files.” |
| **Producing office / author** | “Intelligence summaries prepared by FBI Counterterrorism Division.” | “FBI records.” |
| **Exemption invoked** | “Exempt under Exemption 7(A): interference with ongoing criminal prosecution.” | No citation or merely “national‑security exemption.” |

*Why these details matter:* With those descriptors a judge can match individual items against Exemption 7(A)’s elements (*pending/anticipated proceeding* + *reasonable interference*). Without them she cannot ascertain whether every document truly satisfies both prongs—the very situation *Farahi* warned would render an objection forfeited if left unarticulated.


---

## 3️⃣ Successful arguments against overly broad categorical withholding

The literature following *Farahi* shows three recurring tactics that convinced D.C. Circuit judges that an agency’s categories were impermissibly vague:

### A️⃣ Show concrete non‑exempt items hidden inside the named group  

*Typical pattern*: The plaintiff identifies—or obtains through third parties—a single document whose content clearly falls outside Exemption 7(A), such as an administrative policy memo or routine travel voucher accompanying investigative logs.  
*Result*: Courts have ordered agencies either (i) to carve out those items or (ii) to redefine their categories narrowly enough so only truly investigatory material remains (*Citizens for Resp.* (2014), see discussion at ¶ 12).  

### B️⃣ Demonstrate lack of any factual nexus between every document and the claimed exemption  

In *Gallant*, the NLRB’s umbrella label ““Collective bargaining filings’’ was struck because many filings were purely informational newsletters unrelated to any collective bargaining dispute—hence no reasonable connection existed between all items and Exemption 5’s deliberative process exception. Similarly, requesters who point out—for example—that an agency’s “National security″ tag includes publicly released press releases constitute successful challenges (*Khine*, 943 F.3d at 967 noted forfeiture only when plaintiffs fail to make this showing).

### C️⃣ Prove reasonable segregation is possible despite agency’s blanket claim  

Even when most documents within a set are genuinely exempt, courts require proof that non‑exempt portions cannot be isolated before applying wholesale denial (*Mead Data*, 566 F.2d at 260–61). Successful plaintiffs have supplied ***sample redactions*** showing exactly which lines could be blacked out while leaving substantive information intact (*Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC* (2023)). Once shown feasible, agencies must provide those segregated portions instead of relying on an overbroad categorical assertion.

---

## 4️⃣ Practical checklist for a requester who thinks an agency’s categories are too broad  

1️⃣ **Obtain the agency’s withholding letter** (or its indexed list).  
2️⃣ **Match each heading against***CREW/Gallant*** criteria*: does it state subject matter, dates, author/producer, and exemption?  
3️⃣ **Identify any record you can name concretely** (e.g., via docket entries, public news reports, prior disclosures). Note why it appears non‑exempt—different purpose, public domain status, or lack of interference risk.  
4️⃣ **Request clarification/specialized subcategories** – ask the agency either (a) to produce any separable material within the group or (b) to replace vague headings with tighter ones meeting § 552(a)(8)’s specificity demand.  
5️⃣ **If refused**, file a motion arguing one of A–C above with supporting evidence (affidavits, third‑party subpoenas etc.). Cite *Farahi* ¶ C (“must use… sufficiently distinct”), *CREW* § 1088–89 (“overly general assertions … do not satisfy”), and *Juarez* § 59 (“reasonable segregation”).  

---

### Bottom line

An agency may only rely on categorical withholding when each label tells you exactly what type of record is covered and when its accompanying affidavit shows **(i)** every document fits within an exemption and **(ii)** nothing inside can be carved out without undue effort. If you can point out even one plausible non‑exempt document—or demonstrate that the description fails to tie every item back to statutory language—the court will deem the category overly broad and require either narrower categorization or disclosure of whatever separable material exists.

- *Gallant v.​NLRB*, 26 F.3d at 173 (**Gallant**) (same phrasing).

These authorities together form the current D.C.~Circuit standard governing categorical FOIA withholding—and guide how requesters should structure successful objections.*

### Why are summary‑judgment rulings in FOIA cases reviewed de novo on appeal?
Question:
The D.C. Circuit says summary judgment decisions involving FOIA exemptions are reviewed de novo (citing Rep Comm for Freedom of the Press v FBI). In most civil cases I thought appellate courts give weight to factual findings made at trial unless clearly erroneous. Why do exemption analyses receive fresh examination at every level? Does this mean the government bears its burden anew on appeal, and how does this affect strategic considerations for both parties?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The D.C. Circuit treats a grant of summary judgment in a FOIA case exactly as it does in any other civil case — as a *legal* determination about whether any genuine issues of material fact remain. Because the determination hinges on applying the statutory exemption(s) to the facts already before the court, an appellate panel reviews it **de novo** (see *Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI*, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021)). The government therefore bears its § 552(a)(4)(B) burden again on appeal, but it does so by re‑examining the same affidavits and declarations that were presented at trial—not by producing new evidence.  

The practical upshot is that both sides must build a record that can survive fresh legal scrutiny at every level.

---

### 1. Why de novo review applies

1. **Summary‑judgment standard** – A grant of summary judgment disposes of the case by holding that “there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact” and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law (*Fed.R.Civ.P.* 56(c)). That ruling is *legal* rather than *factual* in nature, so an appellate court reviews it **without deferential abuse‑of‑discretion scrutiny**; it looks at whether the lower court correctly applied the governing law to the undisputed record (**Farahi**, ¶III).

2. **FOIA exemptions are mixed questions** – Exemption 7(A) requires two factual predicates (“compiled for law‑enforcement purposes” and “could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings”) plus a legal test whether those predicates satisfy § 552(b)(7)(A). The district court’s grant therefore resolved both fact‑finding (e.g., “investigations remain ongoing”) and the legal application of those facts. Because there is no live witness testimony or jury verdict whose credibility needs respect under a “clearly erroneous” rule, the appellate court examines those predicates anew (**Farahi**, ¶III).

3. **Uniform statutory interpretation** – FOIA statutes apply nationwide, and Congress intended courts to interpret exemptions consistently rather than allow an agency’s favorable district‑court ruling to become binding precedent absent an appellate check (**Reps. Comm**, 361). De novo review ensures each panel independently decides whether an exemption truly applies.

---

### 2. Does the government “bear its burden anew”?

Yes, but only in the sense of **re‑arguing** its case with the existing record:

* **Statutory allocation of burden** – § 552(a)(4)(B) places the initial burden on the agency “to establish by a preponderance that…the records fall within an exemption.” That allocation follows the case through every stage of appeal (**Farahi**, ¶III).  
* **No fresh discovery** – The appeal is limited to what was already before the district court (the Hardy and Bender declarations, the docket entries, etc.). The agency does not get another chance to produce new evidence unless it petitions for remand or supplemental briefing (§ 56(e)).  
* **Effectively a second test** – Because de novo review asks whether *any* genuine issue existed after considering all affidavits, an affirmation requires that the prior evidentiary showing was sufficient on its own terms. If it was not—e.g., if an affidavit is merely conclusory—the appellate panel can overturn even though nothing new has been submitted (**Farahi**, ¶IV.A‑B).

Thus “bearing its burden anew” means **re‑demonstrating** that each prong of an exemption is met using what has already been filed.

---

### 3. Strategic implications

| Party | Strategic point | Why it matters |
|-------|----------------|----------------|
| **Government** | Draft *detailed* affidavits early | An affidavit that merely says “investigations remain ongoing” was considered too terse when standing alone (**Farahi**, ¶IV.B). The Court treated Bender’s brief statement as an *update* rather than proof because Hardy’s earlier declarations supplied context. A robust initial declaration reduces risk of denial or reversal.<br><br>Include concrete examples—dates, investigative steps taken, specific statutes being enforced—to show a “rational nexus” (Campbell v.​ DOJ) and “reasonable expectation” of interference (Juarez). |
| **Government** | Anticipate *segregability* challenges | The Court reiterated that when non‑exempt material is “inextricably intertwined” with exempt portions an agency may withhold whole documents (Juarez). Detailed categorization—showing how each category meets exemption criteria—helps satisfy CREW’s requirement that categories be “sufficiently distinct” (**Farahi**, ¶III & C). |
| **Plaintiff** | Force disclosure of *specific* facts before SM | Since summary‑judgment relief hinges on undisputed facts derived from affidavits, plaintiffs should move early to compel more detailed sworn statements or production under §§ 552(a)(5), (a)(8). Any gaps become fatal for a SM defense—see how the district court initially denied SM because it needed an updated assessment (“significant passage …”). |
| **Plaintiff** | Emphasize over‑broad categorization | Citing CREW’s “categories must be sufficiently distinct,” plaintiffs can argue that vague groupings prevent courts from determining which records truly fall within an exemption—a point rejected by Farahi’s Court when Farahi failed to articulate why categories were overbroad (**Farahi**, ¶IV.C). |
| **Both sides** | Evaluate settlement versus continued litigation | Because appeals do not defer to district‐court rulings, either side faces uncertainty even after a SM grant or denial. Parties often prefer settlement once both recognize how much work will be needed to survive another round of de novo scrutiny.|

---

### 4. Bottom line

*The D.C. Circuit’s rule that summary‑judgment rulings in FOIA cases receive de novo review reflects ordinary appellate practice for pure legal determinations.* The government’s statutory burden stays with it throughout appeal—but it needn’t produce fresh evidence; it merely must show again—through whatever affidavits are already in the record—that each element of an exemption is satisfied. Consequently, both sides should treat every stage (initial response, discovery, motion practice) as potentially final because any deficiency can be fatal when an appeals panel conducts its own independent legal analysis.
