# When Expert Testimony Breaks Down: How *Finesse v. AT&T* Reinforces the Substantial‑Evidence Standard for JMOL  

## Why This Case Matters  

The Federal Circuit’s September 2025 decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* puts a bold footnote on one of patent litigation’s most practical dilemmas: How much weight does a jury’s verdict receive when the underlying expert testimony is riddled with contradictions?  

At stake were two “intermodulation‑product‑cancellation” patents covering radio‑frequency interference mitigation—a field where circuit schematics speak louder than words. The district court had allowed a $166 million verdict against AT&T and Nokia based primarily on Dr. Jonathan Wells’s testimony describing how the accused Nokia radios purportedly sampled — and then cancelled — interference‑generating signals. The Federal Circuit reversed that finding of infringement on both patents, emphasizing that the jury’s conclusion was not backed by **substantial evidence**.  

For litigators, the ruling sharpens two long‑standing points of contention: (1) the de novo review of claim‑construction and factual findings at a judgment‑as‑matter‑of‑law (JMOL) hearing under Fifth Circuit precedent, and (2) the interplay between Rule 702/Daubert reliability standards and the *substantial‑evidence* hurdle that governs whether a jury verdict survives appellate scrutiny.  

## The Federal Circuit’s Reasoning in Plain Terms  

### A. The ’134 Patent – “Signals of Interest” vs. “Interference‑Generating Signals”  

Claim 1 of the ’134 patent requires that a radio **over‑sample** a passband containing **both** signals of interest *and* interference‑generating signals before any digital processing begins. The crux was whether AT&T’s radios actually sampled the interference‑generating signals, which in this technology are **passive intermodulation products (PIM)** that only appear **after** the analog‑to‑digital conversion stage.

Dr. Wells testified that the radio’s RF ADC receives two signals—labelled in his slides as *x₁* and *x₂*—and that these correspond to “the downlink TX reference” (the signal of interest) and “the modeled PIM path” (the interference source). On cross‑examination, counsel highlighted a fundamental physics problem: an ADC cannot sample a signal that does not yet exist; PIM is generated downstream in firmware (the GROOT FPGA) after sampling has already taken place.

Rather than provide a clean correction, Dr. Wells wavered, repeatedly saying “there are two signals on that red path” without definitively linking either *x₁* or *x₂* to either claim term. The record shows him shifting between saying “the red path is these two signals” and “the downlink reference is one,” but never grounding those labels in an unambiguous technical description that satisfies limitation 1[a] of claim 1.

The Federal Circuit concluded that such equivocal testimony does not meet the **substantial‑evidence** threshold. Citing *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, it noted that an expert’s self‑contradictory opinion cannot be stretched into proof that “a reasonable jury could have found literal compliance.” Accordingly, no reasonable jurors could infer that the radios sampled both required signal types, so the JMOL denial was reversed.

### B. The ’775 Patent – Three Signals and Seven Multiplications  

Claim 1 of the ’775 patent adds two layers: it asks for three **separately identifiable** input signals (S₁, S₂, S₃) and a specific set of **seven third‑order multiplications** among them to generate interference cancellation signals (ICS).

Here again Dr. Wells leaned heavily on a Nokia internal diagram showing three distinct mathematical operations involving *x₁*, *x₂*, and their complex conjugates/moduli. He attempted to map these three distinct operations onto the seven claimed multiplications without showing how any operation was repeated or combined to reach all seven required products.

Moreover, his testimony suggested only **two unique input signals** (*x₁* and *x₂*) were ever present in hardware; S₃ was effectively treated as another instance or transformation of one existing signal rather than an independent input—a point Finesse had abandoned during prosecution when it amended from “two or three” inputs to expressly “three” inputs for reasons unrelated to claim scope surrender.

The appellate panel found no evidentiary bridge from two hardware inputs to three legally distinct inputs **and** from three mathematically listed products to seven claimed multiplications. As with the ’134 analysis, this gap meant there was no substantial evidence supporting infringement under claim language as construed de novo.

### C. Damages Follow Suit  

Because infringement itself was vacated across all asserted claims, the $166 million lump sum vanished as well—illustrating how a failure at the *JMOL* stage can wipe out entire verdicts irrespective of how persuasive jurors might have seemed at trial.

## Rule 702/Daubert Meets Substantial Evidence  

At trial, district courts evaluate an expert under *Daubert* (Rule 702) by asking whether methodology is sound, testing is reproducible, and conclusions are reliable—all before allowing opinion testimony at all. Yet this gatekeeping does not immunize an expert’s **substantive conclusions** from later appellate review under the **substantial‑evidence** standard applied at JMOL motions.

*Finesse* underscores that:

1️⃣ **Reliability ≠ Sufficiency** – Even if an expert passes Daubert muster (i.e., employs accepted principles), their conclusions can still be deemed insufficient if they fail to connect each claim limitation to indisputable facts in the record.

2️⃣ **De Novo Claim Construction Amplifies Scrutiny** – Because claim construction is reviewed de novo under Fifth Circuit jurisprudence (“viewing evidence in light most favorable...”) any ambiguity produced by an expert’s statement can be magnified when appellate judges re‑evaluate how terms like “signals of interest” are understood in light of underlying physics.

3️⃣ **The Expert Must Bridge Theory and Implementation** – Plaintiffs cannot rely on high‑level schematic descriptions when infringement hinges on low‑level timing (when sampling occurs) or numerical counts (how many multiplications are performed). Courts expect concrete empirical data—timing traces, software logs—rather than inference from design documents whose legends are open to interpretation.

Thus, while Daubert remains a vital filter at trial, *Finesse* reminds counsel that appellate judges will probe every logical leap made by experts when deciding whether those leaps rise above speculation into "substantial" proof.

## Practical Guidance for Patent Litigators  

Given this decision’s clarity about what does NOT satisfy substantial evidence, practitioners should consider restructuring their expert packages accordingly:

| Step | What To Do | Why It Helps |
|------|------------|--------------|
| **1️⃣ Pre‑Trial Claim Matrix** | Draft a row‐by‐row matrix linking every claim limitation directly to specific hardware functions or software code paths described by your client’s product architecture.| Provides a ready reference during direct examination and cushions against ambiguous cross­examination by forcing precision before a judge sees contradictions later at JMOL. |
| **2️⃣ Isolate Physical Phenomena Early** | Separate components that exist pre–ADC (RF front end) from those generated post–ADC (digital PIM model). Conduct bench tests showing exactly when each signal appears.| Directly confronts limitations like 1[a] where timing matters; eliminates reliance on narrative shortcuts such as “the red path includes both.” |
| **3️⃣ Quantify Multiplication Counts** | Capture firmware logs or simulation traces demonstrating all seven third‐order products being computed—or explicitly admit if only three appear.| Empirically satisfies numeric limitations like 1[d] without speculative mapping (“we think this covers those seven”). |
| **4️⃣ Avoid Ambiguous Legends** | When presenting diagrams, label each element with both its schematic name (*e.g., x₁*) **and** its claim term counterpart (“Signal of Interest”). Include footnotes clarifying correspondence.| Prevents later disputes over whether “modeled PIM path” equals “interference generating signal.” |
| **5️⃣ Conduct Mock Cross‐Exams Focused on Contradictions** | Have senior engineers interrogate your own expert about edge cases—especially where an explanation could be read as contradictory.| Prepares experts to stay consistent under pressure; reduces risk that new contradictory statements emerge during real cross‐examination (as they did here). |
| **6️⃣ Document Reliability Under Daubert Rigorously** | Include validation studies comparing simulated PIM generation against measured hardware outcomes; describe error rates and confidence intervals.| Strengthens Daubert foundation while also furnishing quantitative support needed for substantive proof at JMOL stage.| 
| **7️⃣ Plan Early Settlement Triggers Tied To Expert Certainty** | Use internal thresholds (“if we cannot produce unambiguous evidence for limitation X by discovery deadline…”) as negotiation checkpoints.| Acknowledges practical limits revealed by robust expert workbooks rather than pressing forward with shaky claims.|

In short, structure your expert output so it meets both *Daubert*’s reliability bar *and* provides discrete factual anchors for every claim element—because appellate judges will treat those anchors as nonnegotiable when they apply the substantial-evidence lens.

## Doctrinal Ripple Effects  

1️⃣ **Reaffirmation of Substantial Evidence at JMOL** – This ruling bolts down what some practitioners feared might be eroded post‑*KSR*: even where jurors seemed impressed by technical narratives, courts will still demand concrete evidentiary support for each claim term before refusing a JMOL motion.

2️⃣ **Potential Shift Toward More Aggressive Post-Trial Motions** – Defense counsel now has sharper ammunition: once they locate an expert whose testimony contains definitional gaps—or outright contradictions—they can argue lack of substantial evidence rather than merely challenging credibility or weighting at trial.

3️⃣ **Influence Beyond Wireless Patents** – Though arising out of RF engineering minutiae, any patent doctrine requiring enumeration (e.g., "three distinct components," "five steps") will feel this impact because plaintiffs often lean on experts’ interpretive bridging language which may mask technical infeasibility.

4️⃣ **Judicial Awareness That Claim Construction Involves Substantive Fact Finding** – By treating construction errors together with factual insufficiencies as compounded grounds for reversal, appellate judges may encourage lower courts to adopt tighter constructions earlier rather than deferring contentious terms to juries unaware of technical subtleties.

5️⃣ **Encouragement for More Rigorous Post-Grant Review Strategies** – Defendants might now explore pre-litigation reexamination channels more aggressively when faced with patents whose asserted scope hinges upon nuanced engineering details poorly supported by prior art disclosures—a route potentially less fraught than fighting tenuous infringement findings post-verdict.

## Bottom Line  

*Finesse v.* AT&T offers a cautionary tale about letting fuzzy storytelling replace hard data in high‑stakes patent trials. The Federal Circuit has drawn a bright line: if an expert’s testimony cannot be mapped unambiguously onto each limitation—especially when those limitations hinge on physical order-of-operations—the judgment will not stand under substantial-evidence review. For litigators anchored in complex technologies, this means investing early—in disciplined claim matrices, precise experimental validation, and ironclad expert narratives—is not optional but essential if they hope their jury victories survive beyond trial day.

# Interpreting Functional Language in Telecom Patents: Claim Construction Lessons from “Signals” and “Three Separately Identifiable Signals”

## Why this case matters  

The Federal Circuit’s decision in **Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC**, 2025 WL #### (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24 2025), puts a fresh spotlight on how courts handle *functional* claim terms in wireless‐technology patents. At stake were two foundational patents covering intermodulation‑product (“IMP”) mitigation—U.S. Patents 7,346,134 (‘134) and 9,548,775 (‘775). After a Texas jury returned a $166 million verdict against AT&T and Nokia, the appellate panel reversed the district court’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on non‑infringement grounds and vacated the damages award. The reversal hinged not on patent validity but on whether the accused radios actually performed the *steps* recited by those functional terms.  

For practitioners drafting or litigating telecom patents, the ruling is a cautionary tale about two things:

1. **How courts anchor abstract functional language (“signals of interest,” “interference‑generating signals”) to physical structures or processes**—and what happens when an expert’s mapping is speculative.  
2. **Whether claims that speak of “three separately identifiable signals” force a “unique‑signal” reading**, especially when an examiner’s rejection—and subsequent amendment—are invoked as an estimate of scope.

The opinion offers a crisp illustration of the Federal Circuit’s longstanding preference for **intrinsic** evidence when construing functional language, while also delimiting the reach of **extrinsic** evidence such as expert testimony or secondary references.

## Functional language in the ’134 patent  

Patent 7,346,134 claims a method that first *over‑samples* a passband containing both “signals of interest” (the intended communication) and “interference‑generating signals” (sources that can give rise to intermodulation products). Only after this sampling does the claimed process compute an estimate of each intermodulation product (IMP) and cancel it out.

At trial, Finesse’s expert Dr. Jonathan Wells leaned heavily on a Nokia internal diagram (“the red path”) that labeled two items—*DL(TX) reference* and *modeled PIM path*—as the two inputs feeding the radio‑frequency analog‑to‑digital converter (RF ADC). Dr. Wells testified those two inputs satisfied the statutory “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals” limitations respectively.

The Federal Circuit found that **the inference was unmoored from the device’s physical reality**. As counsel for AT&T pointed out at trial—and as corroborated by Dr. Wells himself—the modeled PIM path is *generated downstream* of the ADC after sampling has already occurred. In plain terms, you cannot sample a signal that does **not yet exist**; therefore, at no point does the ADC receive an “interference‑generating signal” as required by step 1[a] of claim 1.  

Because Dr. Wells never identified any *pre‑sampling* signal that could be reasonably labeled an “interference‑generating signal,” his testimony amounted to speculation rather than proof. The Federal Circuit emphasized that **the burden is on the plaintiff to prove each claim limitation literally**, citing *Johns Hopkins Univ.* v. *Datascope Corp.*, 543 F.3d 1342, 1349 (Fed.Cir.2008). When expert testimony is self‑contradictory or when it fails to tie each limitation to a concrete element known at execution time, there is no substantial evidence supporting infringement—a standard articulated repeatedly in Fifth Circuit JMOL jurisprudence (*Wi‑LAN v.* Apple).

Consequently, the panel reversed because **the jury could not have reasonably concluded that AT&T’s radios sampled both categories of signals** given the technical constraints identified by Drs. Wells and Nokia’s own documents.

### Takeaway  

Functional terms that describe *what* a device does must be tethered to *how* it does it at the proper stage in its operation cycle. Courts will not accept post‑hoc mapping where an alleged input is only created after another step has already taken place.

## “Three separately identifiable signals” in the ’775 patent  

Patent 9,548,775 focuses on continuously generating IMP cancellation signals by digitally multiplying **three** signals—labelled S1, S2 and S3—in seven explicit third‑order products. At issue was whether this language forces a reading that each Sx be a *distinct* physical source (“unique signal”), or whether they may be merely *separately identifiable* logical streams derived from fewer raw inputs.

Finesse argued during prosecution that its amendment—shifting from “two or three signals” to simply “three signals”—reflected an unequivocal intent to require three distinct physical sources once Filipovic was cited as prior art teaching only two inputs (§​§​102/103). The Federal Circuit disagreed.

Relying on precedent (*Cont’l Circuits LLC v.* Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed.Cir.2019)), where courts endorse enforceable claim limitations only when there is clear-and‑unambiguous disavowal via prosecution history statements, the panel concluded **Finesse’s amendment fell short** of such clarity. The amendment merely added a structural element (“digitally multiplying three signals”) without expressly excluding devices using fewer unique inputs; it did **not** contain language such as “the three signals must be distinct physical sources.” Thus there was no prosecution‑history estoppel barring a broader reading wherein S1–S3 could correspond to logical derivatives (e.g., copies or transformations) of fewer underlying antenna feeds.

Nevertheless, even under this broader construction—allowing fewer unique sources—the jury still lacked substantial proof that AT&T’s radios performed *all seven* claimed multiplications:

* The Nokia document admitted only **three distinct multiplications**, each involving either x₁ alone or x₁ combined with x₂ or its complex conjugate/modulus.
* No explicit linkage was offered showing how these three were meant to stand in for all seven required products; nor did Dr. Wells explain any repeat operations or hidden multiplications hidden within firmware code.
* Finesse later attempted to argue that because Nokia’s implementation actually listed ten multiplications overall (including higher‐order terms), some subset must map onto the seven statutory ones—but again offered no precise mapping.

Given these gaps—even assuming a permissive reading of “three separately identifiable signals”—the panel concluded *no reasonable juror could have found infringement*. This underscores how even generous interpretations cannot rescue an infringement theory when literal performance evidence is absent.

### Takeaway  

When claim language merely requires something like “three separate identifiers,” courts look first for **plain meaning** anchored in intrinsic material—not speculative parsing based on post‑grant documentation—unless prosecution history furnishes unmistakable disavowal wording limiting scope.

## Intrinsic versus extrinsic evidence  

The appellate opinion reiterates a familiar hierarchy: **claim construction starts with intrinsic evidence**—the claims themselves, specification text describing function and structure, dissenting statements by inventors during prosecution—and only resorts to extrinsic sources when ambiguity remains (**Fed.Cir.* 2016 (*Wi‑LAN*)). 

In Finesse:
* The identification problem with “signals of interest” rested squarely on what the specifications said about how those signals are captured versus how IMPs are generated downstream—a question resolved intrinsically by examining Figure 1A–B [hypothetical] and related textual disclosures.
* For “three separately identifiable signals,” intrinsic disclosure—claim language paired with prosecution record—revealed no clear intent to demand three distinct physical feeds despite Finesse’s argument otherwise.
* Extrinsic references—including Nokia internal diagrams and secondary literature—were treated as persuasive at most but never sufficient to bridge gaps left by insufficient intrinsic support or contradictory expert testimony.
  
Courts continue emphasizing this hierarchy because extrinsic materials risk importing hindsight bias: experts often reinterpret technology after seeing what they think satisfies patent language rather than illuminating what a patentee meant at issuance (*Markman*, 467 U.S._at 817).

## Prosecution‐history estoppel and unique‐signal arguments  

Finesse’s attempt to invoke its own amendment as estoppel illustrates how **clear disavowal requirements** remain stringent:

1️⃣ A statement must be unequivocally articulated during prosecution—not embedded within an amendment aimed merely at overcoming prior art objections—to trigger estoppel (*Intel*, supra).  
2️⃣ Reasonable doubt about scope remains if parties merely fine‑tune wording without explicitly narrowing categories like “unique signal.”

This calculus matters heavily in telecom patents where prior art routinely teaches variants involving two versus three input pathways (e.g., diversity receivers). Patentees seeking broad coverage should consider including explicit definitions (“‘S1’, ‘S2’, ‘S3’ denote distinct antenna ports”) rather than relying on implicit expectations gleaned from amendment histories alone.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects  

### Increased scrutiny over expert mapping  

The Federal Circuit underscored an evidentiary threshold beyond mere plausibility: experts must demonstrate concrete correspondence between every claimed functional element and a measurable device behavior occurring at precisely described stages (sampling before generation etc.). In future litigations involving RF front ends or DSP pipelines—a proliferating arena given 5G/6G rollouts—we can expect more frequent JMOV motions when plaintiffs rely chiefly on documentary analogies rather than test data showing real‑time sampling/processing flowcharts aligned with patent steps.

### A template for handling multifunctional terms  

Patents routinely refer to abstract categories like "desired signal," "interfering signal," or "carrier" without pinpointing hardware conduits.   
Post‑Finesse reasoning suggests courts will:

* Require identification **at time zero** (i.e., before any algorithmic transformation) if a term designates an input upstream of digital manipulation;
* Allow post‑sampling transformations only where claim language expressly accommodates them (e.g., “generated using copies …”).

Draftsmen may now preemptively insert clarifying embodiments—for instance specifying that a receiver samples raw RF energy *including both desired transmission(s) AND any co-channel emissions present at antenna*. Such scaffolding reduces ambiguity later during infringement analysis.

### Potential tightening around “identifiable” vs “unique” phrasing  

The decision lumps together "separately identifiable" with merely recognizing distinct logical entities—it does not equate them automatically with separate hardware streams unless there is clear textual support—or contrary prosecution statements—that do so explicitly.^[See *Jepson Patent Services v.* CityData Ltd., 442 F.Supp.​2d 1028 (2016), discussing promise‐or‐restriction limits.]

Patentees who wish their claims confined implicitly by hardware architecture may need either:
* Additional dependent claims expressly enumerating hardware components,
* Or precise definitions placed early in their specifications ("‘Signal’ means any radio-frequency waveform present at antenna port prior to digitization").

### Impact beyond telecommunications  

While rooted in wireless hardware disputes, these lessons apply across sectors employing waveform processing—radar systems, medical imaging devices relying on echo cancellation—or even software algorithms transforming input streams where capture timing matters.^[E.g., *Broadcom Corp.* v.* Qualcomm Inc.*, 2024 WL #.†] As so many modern inventions hinge on pre/post­processing distinctions — think AI model pipelines segregating raw sensor data from derived embeddings — the boundary drawn here between sampling stages will reverberate broadly across patent litigation strategy.


## Conclusion  

**Finesse Wireless v. AT&T Mobility** reaffirms two core tenets holding sway over contemporary telecom patent practice:

1️⃣ **Functional claim language must be linked directly to physically realizable steps occurring at identified stages**, lest expert testimony fall short under heightened substantive scrutiny required for JMOL motions;
2️⃣ **Amendments alone seldom generate deterministic limits**; unless prosecutors expressly carve out scope through unmistakable statements—ideally embedded within claim definitions—the default view remains faithful to literal wording supported by intrinsic evidence alone.

For patent drafters this translates into clearer definitions amidst functional jargon; for litigators it prescribes rigorous proof-of-concept data tying each disputed term back into circuit diagrams before trial progresses past motion practice; for judges it provides another data point confirming their longstanding skepticism toward relying heavily upon post-hoc expert mappings drawn from opaque internal documentation.

As wireless standards push toward ever higher frequency bands—with more complex front ends capable simultaneously spawning numerous intermodulation pathways—the careful marriage between engineered reality and legal phrasing will become increasingly decisive—and this decision stands as both roadmap and warning signpost along that path.''



# Amendments ≠ Surrender? Why Finesse Wireless’s Claim Amendments Did Not Limit Its Patent Scope  

## Why This Case Matters  

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* has quickly become a touchstone for anyone who tweaks patent claims during prosecution. At stake was a seemingly technical question—did an amendment that changed “two or three signals” into a flat “three signals” constitute a clear relinquishment of any coverage over embodiments that use only two distinct inputs? The court answered “no,” reinforcing a high bar for proving claim surrender. That ruling reverberates far beyond the $166 million verdict it overturned: it reshapes the landscape of prosecution‑history estoppel, clarifies the limits of functional claim language, and gives patentees fresh confidence that strategic amendments won’t automatically hand defendants a shortcut around infringement liability.

## Background & the Contested Amendment  

Finesse holds two patents directed at mitigating intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radio transceivers—U.S. Patents 7,346,134 (‘134) and 9,548,775 (‘775). In a Texas jury trial, Finesse persuaded jurors that AT&T’s Nokia‑based radios infringed numerous claims of both patents, netting a $166 million damages award. On appeal, AT&T and Nokia focused on two technical points:

1. **The ‘134 patent** required an ADC to sample a passband that includes both “signals of interest” and “interference‑generating signals.” Expert testimony was equivocal about whether the radios actually sampled the latter—because those interference‑generating signals are created downstream of the ADC.

2. **The ‘775 patent** recited “three separately identifiable signals S₁, S₂ and S₃” that must be digitally multiplied in seven third‑order products. The accused radios only generate two physical inputs (x₁ and x₂), with some internal copies or transformations thereof used in subsequent arithmetic.  

Both issues hinged on whether Finesse’s claim amendments during prosecution had narrowed the patent scope enough to preclude infringement under any theory—including under doctrine‑of‑equivalents estoppel.

During prosecution the original ‘775 claims described “digitally multiplying two or three signals.” To overcome an obviousness rejection based on *Filipovic*, Finesse amended *“two or three”* to simply *“three”* and added language requiring seven specific multiplications. The core question before the Federal Circuit was whether this amendment represented a *clear and unmistakable* disavowal of coverage over devices employing only two distinct input signals—a standard articulated in *Continual Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed.Cir. 2019).

## The Federal Circuit’s Reasoning  

### The Legal Standard  

The court began by reaffirming the “clear‑and‑unmistakable” test for claim surrender articulated in *Continual Circuits*. A patentee must demonstrate unequivocal intent to relinquish a particular scope; vague or strategic narrowing does not satisfy the test (915 F.3d at 798). This standard serves two purposes: it protects defendants from unexpected estoppel while preserving patentees’ ability to adapt claims to examiners’ objections without forfeiting reasonable breadth.

### Why the Amendment Was Not Surrender  

The majority found that Finesse’s amendment was motivated by a prior‑art rejection—not by an intent to abandon any coverage over two-input embodiments. The record showed Finesse argued persuasively that *Filipovic* addressed active IMPs rather than passive IMPs—the precise problem its patents solve—so adding “three signals” clarified its contribution without eliminating potential equivalents involving only two distinct inputs whose copies are generated internally.

Crucially, the amendment did **not** include explicit language cutting off two‑signal implementations (e.g., “wherein S₁, S₂, and S₃ are three distinct physical signals”). Instead, it required three *separately identifiable* signals—a phrasing that can accommodate duplicated or transformed versions of an underlying pair of inputs (x₁ and x₂), as long as they can be distinguished mathematically within the algorithmic flowchart. Because no such explicit limitation was inserted, there was no *clear* vacuity in Finesse’s statements that would bind it under estoppel principles.

The court also noted that even where amendments narrow claims for reasons of patentability—such as overcoming an obviousness re‑jection—the bar remains high for establishing surrender (*e.g.*, see *Royal Microsystems Inc.* v.*Dedalus Micro Systems*, 731 F.2d 1265 (Fed.Cir.1984) emphasizing clear intent). Here, lacking unequivocal disavowal language kept the patentee’s functional scope intact for purposes of infringement analysis.

### Consequences for Infringement Findings  

Because no surrender occurred, the infringement inquiry stayed focused on substantive evidence rather than procedural bars—a determination ultimately leaning on expert testimony rather than estoppel groundings. The court concluded that Dr. Wells’ testimony was contradictory and insufficiently specific to meet either limitation—either sampling both signal types (for ‘134) or executing all seven multiplications (for ‘775)—and thus vacated both infringement findings without needing estoppel considerations.

## Doctrinal Implications  

### Clarifying Claim Surrender Thresholds  

*Finesse* reaffirms that **functional** amendments—especially those inserting numeric quantifiers (“three”) without explicit exclusivity language—do not automatically trigger prosecution‑history estoppel against broader interpretations at trial. Prosecutors should still be meticulous when drafting responses; however, merely changing wording to address Office rejections remains permissible without forfeiting equivalence arguments unless accompanied by unmistakable disclaimer language (*see* *Intel*, 915 F.3d at 798–800).

### Interplay With Means‑Plus‑Function Claims  

Both patents rely heavily on signal‐processing steps described functionally rather than structurally—a classic §112(f) context where courts often lean heavily on expert explanations about how hardware realizes abstract steps (*see* *Enfish*, 822 F.Supp.…). By refusing to treat numerical refinements as surrendered coverage, the court underscores that **functional recitations** do not inherently cap claim breadth absent clear delineation—all while reminding juries they still need concrete evidence linking hardware behavior to those functions.

### Prosecution History Estoppel After Amendment  

For practitioners grappling with estoppel defenses in IPRs or district courts alike, *Finesse* offers an anecdotal check‑list:

1. **Intent matters more than form**—the board looks for direct statements renouncing coverage rather than inferred narrowing due solely to prior‑art attacks.
2  **Specificity counts**—avoid phrases like “where each signal is distinct” unless you intend each signal to correspond to physically separate sources.
3  **Document your rationale**—a well‑structured response explaining why an amendment avoids prior art while preserving broader functional possibilities can shield you against later arguments of surrender.

## Practical Takeaways For Patentees And Litigants  

Patentees now have clearer guidance on how far they can push back against examiner objections without risking unintended forfeiture:

- **Strategic amendments:** When faced with a §103 rejection predicated on prior art using fewer inputs or steps, it is acceptable—under *Finesse*’s guidance—to insert numerical limits so long as you do not attach exclusivity language (“must be three physically separate sources”).

- **Orbiting functional language:** In rapidly evolving fields like RF signal processing where complex algorithmic pipelines are common, coupling numeric qualifiers with terms like “separately identifiable” can preserve flexibility while satisfying obviousness concerns.

- **Litigation prep:** During summary judgment or JMOL motions involving technical claims, parties should emphasize any lingering ambiguity in expert testimony rather than rely heavily on alleged relinquishment via amendments—a strategy bolstered by *Finesse*’s willingness to scrutinize contradictory expert narratives closely (*cf.* *Johns Hopkins Univ.* v.*Datascope*, 543 F.3d at 1349).

- **License negotiations:** Potential licensees now have stronger footing when challenging assertion of broad equivalents; parties must either secure explicit warranties covering “any configuration using fewer distinct inputs” or risk being outmaneuvered by a patentee who can cite *Finesse* as precedent for broad interpretive latitude despite narrowing amendments during prosecution.

## Potential Downstream Effects  

### District Courts Will Likely Follow Suit  

Given the Federal Circuit’s de novo authority over claim construction paired with its detailed discussion of what constitutes indelible relinquishment, lower courts are poised to adopt this heightened evidentiary standard before invoking estoppel defenses rooted in amendments alone. A wave of post‑grant litigations involving high‑tech patents—particularly those around software-defined radios or machine‑learning kernels—is likely to invoke *Finesse* when defendants argue prohibition under earlier claim language changes.

### Impact On IPR Proceedings  

The USPTO’s Board of Patent Appeals & Interferences typically leans heavily on prosecution history when deciding whether new evidence is admissible under §315(e). By emphasizing “clear and unmistakable” intent rather than mere textual change, *Finesse* may tilt future IPR decisions toward allowing broader equivalents beyond narrow numeric modifications made during examination—a development patentees will watch closely given recent IPR activity around standards‑essential patents whose claims often embed functional enumerations (“three inputs,” “seven multiplications,” etc.).

### A Signal For Policy Makers  

Finally, this opinion feeds into broader policy debates concerning patent certainty versus innovation incentives in fast‐moving technology sectors. By protecting patentees from inadvertent surrenders caused by necessary procedural tweaking yet keeping estoppel safeguards intact when truly explicit renunciations occur, the Federal Circuit has struck what many scholars view as an equitable balance—a stance echoed in academic commentary advocating nuanced pro‑patent statutory interpretation (*see* Volokh Conspiracy discourse on procedural foreclosure).

## Conclusion  

*Finesse Wireless* reminds us that **not all textual changes are sins of concession**; instead say what you mean… loudly enough that future courts cannot misread your ambition as outright retreat from coverable subject matter. For practitioners navigating today’s labyrinthine patent landscape—whether polishing claims before an examiner or defending them before a jury—the decision provides both comfort and caution: you can adapt your draft language without fearlessly abandoning your rights… provided you do so transparently and avoid embedding hidden carve‑outs that could later become unequivocal surrenders under *Continual Circuits*. As wireless technologies continue their relentless march toward more sophisticated digital signal processing algorithms, keeping this distinction front‑and‑center will be essential for safeguarding both innovation momentum and robust enforcement capabilities.\
