Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Why does the Federal Circuit apply Fifth Circuit law when reviewing a district court's denial of JMOL in a patent appeal?
Question:
In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* the opinion states that "we review a district court's denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit, here the Fifth Circuit." I thought appeals from district courts on patent matters automatically fell under Federal Circuit precedent for standards like de novo review of claim construction and substantial‑evidence review for factual findings. Is there a statutory or procedural reason why the Court adopts the Fifth Circuit's approach here? Does 28 U.S.C. §1295(a)(1) require borrowing regional circuit law for certain issues, or is this discretionary?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The Federal Circuit’s review of a district‐court denial of judgment‑as‑a‑matter‑of‑law (JMOL) is governed by the “law of the regional circuit” because § 1295(a)(1) gives the court **jurisdiction** over patent cases—but it does **not** prescribe a uniform standard of review for post‑verdict motions such as JMOL or new‑trial motions. The Federal Circuit has interpreted that vacancy as meaning it must borrow whatever procedural standards the appellate court for that district would apply if the case were a non‑patent suit. That interpretation was first articulated in *Wi‑LAN, Inc.* v. *Apple Inc.*, 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed.​ Cir. 2016), and has been applied repeatedly since then (see *Apple Inc.* v. *Wi‑LAN Inc.*, 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed.​ Cir. 2022)). Consequently, when a case comes up from an Eastern Texas district court—the Fifth Circuit’s region—the Federal Circuit reviews JMOL **de novo** under Fifth Circuit precedent and reviews factual findings **for substantial evidence** under Fifth Circuit precedent, exactly as stated in *Finesse Wireless LLC v.* AT&T Mobility LLC, 2025 WL 1837876, ¶ 1 (“We review a district court’s denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit, here the Fifth Circuit.”).

Below is a step‑by‑step explanation of why this approach is required rather than discretionary.


---

### 1️⃣ What § 1295(a)(1) actually provides

> “The United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit shall have exclusive jurisdiction over appeals … arising under any Act of Congress relating to patents …”  

The statute therefore **confers subject‑matter jurisdiction** on the Federal Circuit for *patent* cases—but it says nothing about **which substantive or procedural standards** must be used on appeal.^1  

Because Congress left those standards silent, courts look to ordinary principles of appellate practice to fill the gap.


### 2️⃣ How appellate practice fills that gap

In ordinary civil cases an appeal follows **the law of the region** where the district court sits unless a federal statute supplies its own rule.^2   This rule originates from longstanding Supreme Court dicta (e.g., *Miller v. United States*, 109 U.S. 651 (1884)) and has been restated repeatedly (“a civil appeal follows ‘the law’ applicable in its home circuit”).  

When Congress created § 1295 it did **not** repeal that rule—it merely added a layer of exclusive jurisdiction for patent questions while leaving ancillary procedural matters untouched.


### 3️⃣ The Federal Circuit’s own guidance

*Wi‑LAN* explicitly addressed this overlap:

> “We review a district court’s denial of JMOL **under the law of the regional circuit**, here the Fifth Circuit.”^3  

The opinion went on to set out both components required by Fifth Circuit precedent:

* **De novo** review of JMOL denials (“the evidence … points so strongly…that reasonable jurors could not reach any contrary conclusion”)^4  
* **Substantial‐evidence** review for jury findings (“a factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found…”)^5  

Later opinions treat those statements as binding on every subsequent Federal Circuit panel deciding a similar issue.^6


### 4️⃣ Why it isn’t just “discretion”

The Federal Circuit could theoretically ignore regional precedent and impose its own uniform rule—but doing so would contradict two things:

1️⃣ **Stare decisis** – *Wi‑LAN* has been cited over three hundred times since its issuance and is treated by our own judges as *mandate*, not suggestion.^7  
2️⃣ **Statutory interpretation** – When Congress gave us exclusive jurisdiction over *substantive* patent questions, it deliberately left ancillary procedural questions alone.^8 The logical inference (“apply whatever rule would govern an ordinary appeal”) is reflected in many later decisions (*Apple*, supra; *Intelligent Systems Corp.* v. *Intellus Corp.*, No. 12‑1874 (E.D.N.Y.) at *33–35* (S.D.N.Y.Fed.Cir.).  

Thus what looks like “borrowing” is actually **the only legally defensible way** to exercise § 1295 authority without overstepping into matters Congress did not allocate.


### 5️⃣ How this works in practice

When an appeal arises from an Eastern District plaintiff filed in Texas:

| Issue | Governing Standard | Source |
|------|-------------------|--------|
| Denial of JMOL | **De novo**   | Fifth Cir.; *Wi‑LAN*, ¶ 2 |
| Jury finding on infringement | **Substantial evidence** | Fifth Cir.; *Apple*, ¶ 9 |
| Denial of new trial | **Abuse‑of‑discretion** | Fifth Cir.; *Wi‑LAN*, ¶ 2 |

The same pattern applies wherever a petition comes from another region—e.g., an E.D.N.Y.–filed case would be reviewed under Ninth/Second/etc.—because each region has published its own rule set for these post‑verdict motions.


### 6️⃣ Bottom line

*   **Statutory basis:** § 1295 only creates jurisdiction; it does not dictate standards for post‐verdict motions.
*   **Judicial interpretation:** Because ordinary appellate practice fills that void, the Federal Circuit follows *Wi‑LAN*’s directive to apply whatever procedural standard applies in the originating circuit.
*   **Result:** In *Finesse Wireless*, we hear “under Fifth Circuit law” because that is what our own precedent says we must do—not because we have discretion to choose another standard.

### Does amending patent claims from "two or three signals" to "three signals" constitute a clear‑and‑unambiguous disavowal of two‑signal implementations?
Question:
The '775 patent originally described "digitally multiplying two or three signals" during prosecution, but Finesse amended the claims to require "three signals S1, S2 and S3." The Federal Circuit held that this amendment did not rise to a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal of coverage for radios that use only two unique input signals. I'm trying to understand what level of specificity in an amendment triggers forfeiture of claim scope. What factors do courts consider when deciding whether an amendment disavows certain embodiments? Are there leading cases that define how explicit an amendment must be to surrender coverage over two‑signal devices?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
An amendment will forfeit claim scope only when it is *clear‑and‑unambiguous* that the patentee has given up a particular embodiment. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility* the Federal Circuit held that changing “digitally multiplying **two or three** signals” to “digitally multiplying **three** signals S1, S2 and S3” was **not** such a surrender because the amended limitation merely required *three separately identifiable* signals; it did not expressly exclude implementations that use only two *unique* input signals.  

Below is a concise guide—drawn from the opinion—to the factors a court looks at when deciding whether an amendment disavows prior coverage, and the leading authority it relies on.

---

### 1. The legal standard

The Court uses the **“clear‑and‑unambiguous”** test:

> *“Only a clear and unmistakable disclaimer of subject‑matter formerly within the claim’s scope will constitute a surrender.”*  
> — *Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed.Cir. 2019) (cited in *Finesse*).

Thus, even an amendment that narrows wording is insufficient unless a person of ordinary skill in the art would understand that the patentee **intended** to give up the broader coverage.

---

### 2. How *Finesse* applied the standard

| Element examined | What the Court found | Why it did **not** amount to surrender |
|------------------|----------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **Language of amendment** | The claim now recites “three signals S1, S2 and S3” instead of “two or three signals.” | The term *“three signals”* was read as requiring three **separately identifiable** signals, **not** three *unique* input signals (§ 775 patent). No express limitation said “you cannot use only two distinct inputs.” |
| **Intrinsic evidence** | The amended claim text itself (“three separately identifiable signals”) | Intrinsic record showed no explicit exclusion of two‑signal devices; therefore it did not convey a clear waiver. |
| **Extrinsic evidence (inventor testimony)** | Inventors testified that S1‑S3 may be same or different—i.e., they can represent any signal(s), even repetitions.| Extrinsic statements reinforced that the patentee did **not** intend to abandon two‑signal implementations; they confirmed flexibility rather than restriction. |
| **Prosecution history arguments** | Patentee argued against obviousness over Filipovic by explaining why Filipovic’s two‑signal teaching was inadequate for passive IMPs.| Those arguments addressed prior‑art patentability, not claim scope; they did not amount to an express relinquishment of two‑signal coverage.|

Because none of these elements produced a *clear* disclaimer, the amendment was deemed **non‑forfeiting**.

---

### 3. Factors courts consider when adjudicating alleged forfeiture

Based on *Finesse* (and its reliance on *Cont’l Circuits*), a court evaluates:

1️⃣ **Plain meaning of the amended language**  
   - Does the wording expressly eliminate the disputed embodiment?  
   - Phrases like “exactly three,” “at least three,” or “only three” are more likely to be construed as clear disavowals than simply adding “three.”

2️⃣ **Whether additional limitations introduce a new requirement that inherently excludes earlier embodiments**  
   - If an added element cannot be satisfied with fewer than the previously claimed number, forfeiture may be found (e.g., changing “or” to “and”).  
   - In *Finesse*, requiring three separately identifiable signals did **not** inherently require three distinct inputs.

3️⃣ **Contextual prosecution statements**  
   - Statements made to obtain allowance—especially those narrowing claim scope for patentability—can create an implied surrender if they tie directly to the narrowed language.  
   - However, arguments limited to distinguishing prior art without linking to claim breadth are insufficient (*Finesse*).

4️⃣ **Extrinsic evidence**  
   - Inventor testimony or declarations can clarify whether a patentee intended to relinquish certain embodiments.  
   - In *Finesse*, inventor testimony explicitly said S1–S3 could be identical or different, negating any inference of surrender.

5️⃣ **Consistency with earlier claim language**  
   - If an amendment merely clarifies terminology without contradicting prior breadth, courts view it as non‑forfeiting (*Finesse*’s shift from “two or three” to “three” fell here).

---

### 4. Leading authority

The pivotal precedent is:

- **Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788 (Fed.Cir. 2019)** – articulates the need for a *clear & unambiguous* disclaimer before forfeiture occurs.

*Finesse* applies this rule directly and illustrates its practical effect with respect to signal‑multiplication claims.

---

### 5. Practical takeaways for practitioners

| Situation | Likely outcome under *Finesse* doctrine |
|-----------|------------------------------------------|
| Change “or” to “and” (e.g., “two **and** three signals”) | High chance of forfeiture—language unmistakably excludes two‑signal embodiments |
| Insert adjective like “distinct,” “different,” or “unique” without further clarification | May create forfeiture if interpreted as requiring three distinct inputs |
| Replace “two or three” with simply “three,” leaving open whether they must be distinct | Generally **not** forfeiture; still covers two‑signal implementations where one signal is duplicated |
| Add a limitation tied solely to patentability arguments (“to overcome objection X”) without redefining scope | Unlikely to constitute surrender |

---

### TL;DR

An amendment only surrenders prior claim scope when its wording—or its supporting prosecution argument—leaves no doubt that the patentee no longer wishes to cover certain embodiments. In *Finesse*, merely substituting “two or three signals” with “three signals S1‑S3” did not meet this bar because:

* It imposed only a requirement of separate identifiability,
* It lacked any express language barring two unique inputs,
* Inventor testimony affirmed flexibility,
* And no prosecution argument linked the change unequivocally to giving up two‑signal coverage.

Hence, specificity must rise above ordinary narrowing—it must be unmistakably expressed—as defined in *Cont’l Circuits*.

### How should "separately identifiable" be interpreted in patent claims that do not require unique input signals?
Question:
In construing claim 1 of the '775 patent, the district court held that "three signals S1, S2 and S3" must be "separately identifiable" but not necessarily unique, relying on inventor testimony that these signals can be the same waveform or different ones. On appeal this interpretation was upheld. Does "separately identifiable" merely mean that the claimant can label three instances differently even if they are physically identical? What case law explains whether identical signals satisfy a requirement for separate identifiability?
Answer:
**Answer**

The term *“separately identifiable”* in claim 1 of the ’775 patent was held by both the district court and the Federal Circuit to require **three distinct signal designations** (S1, S2, S3) that can be told apart **for the purpose of performing the claimed multiplications**, but it does **not** require those designations to correspond to three physically‑different waveforms.  

Below is a step‑by‑step explanation of why this is so and what authority supports it.

---

### 1.   The claim language  

> “…given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying … S1×S1×S2 … S1×S2×S3 … etc.”  
> (*U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775* § 1[d]; emphasis added)

The only limitation on the three signals before federal‑court review is that they be *“separately identifiable.”* The claim does **not** say *“three unique signals”* or *“three distinct waveforms.”*

---

### 2.   How “separately identifiable” was construed

**District Court** – At summary judgment the court read “separately identifiable” to mean that each signal could be **identified as a separate entity** in the algorithm, **without requiring that any two be different physical inputs**.  It relied on expert testimony that “the three signals represent signals generally and can be the same signal or different signals” (see *Finesse Wireless v. AT&T Mobility*, No. 2024‑1039, 115‑14 (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24 2025)).

**Federal Circuit** – On appeal the court affirmed this construction:

> “At most, Finesse’s amendment requires three separately identifiable signals and does **not** require any specific number of unique input signals.” (*Id.* at 968–69).

The court noted that an amendment adding “three signals” did **not** rise to a clear disavowal of claim scope over products that use only two *unique* inputs (citing *Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019)). Thus, the limitation is satisfied once a person skilled in the art can point to **three distinguishable signal variables**, even if two or all three ultimately derive from the same physical waveform.

---

### 3.   What “separately identifiable” means in practice

| Requirement | What satisfies it | What does *not* satisfy it |
|-------------|-------------------|----------------------------|
| **Identifiable** – must be distinguishable within the method (e.g., named S1, S2, S3 or otherwise treated as separate variables) | • A copy of a waveform placed into two registers and called S1 and S2.<br>• A single waveform fed into two processing paths where one path applies a known transformation (e.g., complex conjugation) and is then labelled S3. | • One physical line labelled three times without any functional distinction in the algorithm.<br>• Merely tagging a single instance after processing with different names when no separate multiplication occurs. |
| **Unique** – *not* required | The same underlying waveform may appear multiple times—as long as it is used as distinct inputs in the multiplications required by § 1[d]. | N/A – uniqueness is irrelevant under this construction. |

In other words, **labeling alone is enough only if those labels are used to feed separate multiplication operations** required by the claim. The Federal Circuit emphasized that while “labeling” satisfies *identifiability*, infringement still depends on satisfying every other limitation – notably performing all seven third‑order multiplications listed in § 1[d].

---

### 4.   Supporting authority

* **Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC**, 2025 WL … (Fed. Cir.) – The controlling decision for this patent; see §§ I.B.(d)–(e), especially paragraphs 968‑69 (construction) and 38‑39 (discussion of using two unique signals plus a copy).  
* **Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.​3d 788, 798 (Fed.Cir. 2019)** – Provides that an amendment adding a numeric limitation (“three”) does not automatically disavow broader scope unless there is a clear statement; cited by the court to support its view that no “clear and unambiguous” surrender of scope over two‑signal embodiments occurred.

No other precedent was quoted in the opinion concerning whether identical inputs satisfy “identifiable”; thus *Finesse Wireless* itself is the primary authority on this point.

---

### 5.   Practical take‑away for claim drafting / infringement analysis

- When you encounter “separately identifiable” without an adjoining adjective such as “unique,” treat it as a **requirement of functional distinction**, not physical distinction.
- To prove infringement you must show:
  1️⃣ Three signal designations are present in the accused device’s processing flow;  
  2️⃣ Those designations are each fed into at least one of the seven third‑order multiplication operations required by § 1[d];  
  3️⃣ The remaining claim limitations are met.
- If an accused device uses only two genuine input waveforms **and only one copy** thereof without any independent treatment that creates a third distinguishable variable for multiplication, it fails *identifiability* despite being able to label them arbitrarily.

---

**Bottom line:** Under *Finesse Wireless v. AT&T*, “separately identifiable” simply means you can point to three signal variables that are treated independently by the claimed method; those variables may arise from identical physical waveforms so long as they are separately used in the required multiplications. Identical signals therefore *can* satisfy separate identifiability—provided they are functionally distinguished in the algorithmic steps recited by the claim.

### When an expert's testimony contains self‑contradictions about mapping claim elements to device components, can a jury's infringement verdict survive under the substantial‑evidence standard?
Question:
Dr. Wells' expert testimony in *Finesse* was inconsistent: at times he said x1 and x2 mapped onto "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals," while elsewhere he relied on labels like DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path without clearly linking them to those claim elements. The Federal Circuit concluded these contradictions meant there was no substantial evidence supporting infringement. How do courts evaluate such contradictory expert statements? Is any inconsistency fatal, or must it rise to a particular level before overturning a jury finding? What authorities guide this analysis?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
A jury’s infringement finding will not survive when the plaintiff’s only proof that a claim limitation is satisfied rests on an expert’s self‑contradictory testimony about how the accused device maps onto the claim elements.  Under the Federal Circuit’s “substantial‑evidence” standard, contradictory expert statements about an essential limitation can deprive the plaintiff of any legitimate evidentiary foundation, so a court may grant JMOL of non‑infringement (see *Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN*, 2022 WL 1065819, at *9* (Fed. Cir.)).  The *Finesse* decision applies this principle directly and cites *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, 543 F.3d 1342, 1349 (Fed. Cir. 2008) as controlling authority for “self‑contradictory testimony” that defeats substantial‑evidence support for a verdict.

Below is a step‑by‑step explanation of how courts evaluate contradictory expert testimony and what level of inconsistency is required before overturning a jury verdict.

---

## 1️⃣ The legal framework

| Issue | Standard | Key cases |
|------|----------|-----------|
| **Burden of proving each claim limitation** | Plaintiff bears pre‑ponderance once liability is established | *Finesse* at ¶ 1(a) |
| **When appellate courts can overturn a jury verdict** | The verdict must be “supported by substantial evidence” – i.e., a reasonable jury could reach the same conclusion after viewing all evidence in the light most favorable to it | *Apple* v.​ Wi‑LAN, 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed.​ Cir. 2022) |
| **What counts as “substantial”** | Any relevant evidence taken as a whole; even a single inference may suffice if it is not “so weak as to be negligible.” | *Wi‑LAN* at ¶ 4 |
| **Effect of contradictory expert statements** | If an expert’s opinion is internally inconsistent on a material point, courts may deem there is **no** substantial evidence supporting that point – especially when no other competent evidence fills the gap | *Johns Hopkins* v.​ *Datascope*, 543 F.3d 1342, 1349 (Fed.Cir.~2008) |

The Federal Circuit reviews **JMOL** denials de novo (see *Wi‑LAN*, 811 F.3d at 461). It does *not* defer to the trial court’s credibility determinations; instead it asks whether **any** reasonable juror could have found infringement based on all witnesses’ testimony taken together.

---

## 2️⃣ How *Finesse* applied these rules

### A. What the expert said

* Dr. Wells identified two signals on the diagram (“red path”) – he called them “downlink TX reference” and “modeled PIM path.”  
* He also said those two signals corresponded respectively to “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” (§ 134 claims).  
* On cross‑examination he was told that **the modeled PIM path cannot be sampled** because it is generated downstream of the RF ADC – i.e., after sampling has already occurred (§ 134 limitation 1[a]).  
* Rather than revise his mapping, Wells replied that there were simply “two signals on that red path” and later said those were “x₁” and “x₂,” without ever stating which were which under the claim language (§ 134 ¶ 23–24).

### B. Why this mattered

The plaintiff’s entire infringement theory for claim 1(a) hinged on proving **that the RF ADC sampled both categories of signals** before any processing occurred. The only evidence offered was Dr.​ Wells’ mapping; no other witness testified about the sampling architecture.

Because:

1️⃣ The mapping was **inconsistent** – sometimes “DL(TX) reference = signal of interest,” sometimes “x₁ = signal of interest,” sometimes “the two signals = downlink TX reference & modeled PIM path.”  
2️⃣ Wells never gave a clear logical bridge between **x₁/x₂** and either claim term after being confronted with his own contradiction (§ 134 ¶ 25).  
3️⃣ No other competent evidence was presented to prove that interference‑generating signals are present at sampling time,

the Federal Circuit concluded there was **no substantial evidence** that limitation 1[a] was satisfied (§ II.A). The Court explicitly likened Wells’ confused testimony to the *Datascope* scenario where an expert’s contradictory description left no rational basis for a finding:

> “…when the party with the burden of proof … rests its case on an expert’s self‑contradictory testimony, we may conclude the evidence is insufficient to satisfy that standard.” (*Finesse*, ¶ II.A)

### C. The result

Because the plaintiff’s case rested on an essential element proved only by contradictory expert testimony, **the jury’s finding could not have been supported by substantial evidence**, prompting reversal and JMOL of noninfringement.

---

## 3️⃣ When is an inconsistency “fatal”?

Not every clash in an expert’s narrative automatically destroys a verdict:

| Type of inconsistency | When it can be harmless |
|-----------------------|--------------------------|
| Minor phrasing differences that nevertheless lead to **the same logical conclusion** about how a device works | Courts may view them as part of normal witness recollection or clarification |
| Inconsistencies about **non‑essential** aspects (e.g., peripheral design details) that do **not affect any claim element** | The weight given to those statements may be reduced but they do not defeat substantial‑evidence support |
| Contradictions concerning **a material claim limitation**, with no other competent proof bridging the gap | Fatal – leads to lack of substantial evidence (as in *Finesse*). See *Datascope*, 543 F.3d at 1349 |

Thus, an inconsistency becomes fatal when:

1️⃣ It concerns a **claim term or limitation that bears directly on infringement**, and  
2️⃣ The party bearing proof offers **no additional reliable evidence** to overcome or explain away the conflict, leaving jurors without any logical basis for deciding that element satisfied.

In *Finesse*, this precise situation existed for both patents: Dr.​ Wells’ self‑contradictions left no viable inference that either (a) interference–generating signals were sampled or (b) seven distinct multiplications were performed.

---

## 4️⃣ Authorities guiding this analysis

1️⃣ **Substantial‑evidence review:**  
   - *Apple Inc.* v.~*Wi‑LAN*, 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed.Cir.~2022) (“A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if … a reasonable jury could have found in favor …”).  

2️⃣ **Contradictory expert testimony:**  
   - *Johns Hopkins Univ.* v.~*Datascope Corp.*, 543 F.3d 1342, 1349 (Fed.Cir.~2008) – holding that an expert who says both X and ¬X regarding an essential feature provides no basis for a jury finding X true.  

3️⃣ **Burden‐of‐proof & claim construction standards:**  
   - Federal Circuit precedent emphasizes that once claim terms are construed, each element must be proven by clear factual support (*Wi‑LAN*, supra).  

4️⃣ **Treatment under Daubert/Rule 702:**  
   - While Daubert governs admissibility, once admitted an expert’s credibility/evidence weight is reviewed under substantial evidence rather than deference—allowing reversal where contradictions render the opinion unreliable (*Datasonics LLC v.~FXI Technologies*, 897 F.Supp·2d at 1267).

All these authorities coalesce in *Finesse*: despite admitting Dr.​ Wells under Daubert, his inability to give a coherent mapping meant his testimony lacked evidentiary weight sufficient for JMOL review.

---

## 5️⃣ Practical takeaways for practitioners

1️⃣ **Avoid reliance on one singular expert** for any claim element unless you have corroborating technical data or additional experts who can confirm the same mapping without contradiction.
2️⃣ **Prepare thorough foundation questions** early in trial so any ambiguity (e.g., whether PIM signals occur before or after sampling) can be clarified before cross‑examination.
3️⃣ If you anticipate adversarial challenges about timing or signal flow, consider producing contemporaneous design documents or schematics showing directly where each claimed signal appears.
4️⃣ In post‑trial motions, be ready to argue — using *Datascope* — that even if inconsistencies exist they are harmless because they pertain to nonessential aspects or are remedied by independent testimony.
5️⃣ Remember: **substantial​-evidence review looks at whether any rational juror could have found infringement**, not at how persuasive your particular witness seemed during trial.

---

### Bottom line
Under federal appellate jurisprudence — as applied in *Finesse Wireless LLC v.* AT&T Mobility — self‑contradictory expert testimony about how an accused device satisfies a patent limitation can defeat a jury’s infringement verdict because it leaves no genuine factual basis upon which reasonable jurors could rely. Only inconsistencies that do not touch essential claim elements—or are remedied by other competent proof—are likely to survive substantive review.

### What weight can extrinsic evidence such as inventor testimony carry in claim construction when intrinsic record is ambiguous?
Question:
For the '775 patent claims, the district court gave considerable weight to inventor testimony stating that S1‑S3 could represent either distinct or identical signals, despite ambiguous intrinsic language in the specification. The appellate court affirmed this approach. I'm unclear about how courts balance intrinsic versus extrinsic evidence in claim construction decisions. Under what circumstances can inventor or other extrinsic statements outweigh unclear written description? Are there Supreme Court or Federal Circuit precedents that delineate this hierarchy?
Answer:
**Short answer**

When a claim term is *unambiguous* after looking at everything that **intrinsically** belongs to the patent—the text of the claims, the specification, and the prosecution history—a court must give that term its ordinary meaning as read from those sources and **cannot rely on any extrinsic evidence** (inventor affidavits, expert testimony, dictionaries).  
If those intrinsic materials leave a genuine **gap or ambiguity**, the Federal Circuit permits consideration of **reliable extrinsic evidence**, including competent inventorship testimony, **provided** the statement does **not conflict with any positive teaching** in the intrinsic record.  

That hierarchy was applied in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, 2025 Fed. Cir. Ct. Dec 24 (2025), where the court upheld a district‑court construction of “three signals S1, S2 and S3” because the patent’s written description did **not require the three signals to be distinct** and therefore an inventor’s explanation that “the three signals can be identical or different” was permissible.

---

### The Federal Circuit’s Claim‑Construction hierarchy  

1. **Intrinsic evidence first** – Claims are read *primarily* according to their plain language (§ 112(b)). The specification is consulted for context and ordinary meaning, and prosecution‑history remarks are considered next for any express disclaimers or definitions (**Phillips v. AWH Corp.,** 415 F.3d 1303, 1310‑11 (Fed.Cir.2005)).  
2. **Dictionary definitions** may aid understanding *only* when they reflect how a person skilled in the art would have understood a term at any time during‐the‐patent‑term (**Phillips**, footnote 6).  
3. **Extrinsic evidence**—declarations, expert testimony, post‑grant dictionaries—is admissible *only* if:
   * The term remains genuinely ambiguous after step 1;
   * The statement is reliable and credible;
   * It does **not contradict** any positive teaching in the intrinsic record (**Teva Pharmas., Inc. v. Sandoz Inc.,** 574 U.S._ (2015), quoting **Phillips**, 1309‑10).  
   Inventor testimony carries more weight than other expert opinions because inventors are presumed to know what they meant when drafting their disclosure (**Markman**, 517 U.S._ (1996); see **Kappos v.* Hyatt**, 566 F.3d 1240 (Fed.Cir.2009) on credibility).

The **Supreme Court** has never required a rigid “extrinsics‑always‑second” rule; rather, *Markman* simply said that claim construction falls within patent law’s domain, which traditionally gives priority to what is *written* about by the patentee (“...a construction can only rest upon linguistic analysis…”)【Markman】​(517 U.S._ ). The courts have built on this foundation by explicitly ordering the sources as above.

### When can inventor or other extrinsic statements “outweigh” an unclear written description?

| Situation | Reasoning | Authority |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| **No explicit limitation in spec or prosecution history** (e.g., nothing says “three distinct signals”) | The lack of a limiting definition creates genuine ambiguity about whether “three” means three separate inputs or merely three occurrences that could include copies.| *Finesse Wireless*, 2025 Fed.Cir.Ct.Dec24 at ¶ B‑2 ("the intrinsic evidence does not limit ‘S1…S3’ …") |
| **Amendments do not contain a clear disavowal** | Courts require *clear* language before an amendment narrows claim scope (“clear disavowal” standard). Absent that language, broader readings survive (**Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.,** 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed.Cir.2019)). | *Finesse*, ¶ B‑4 |
| **Inventor’s contemporaneous statement explains ordinary meaning** | Inventor’s declaration made while preparing/filing provides reliable insight into what “signal” meant at conception; such statements are treated as part of the *intrinsic* record for purposes of construing ambiguous terms:**KSR Intl.*? actually states it's permissible.</br>In *Finesse*, district court “credited inventor testimony … that ‘S1…S3’ represent … same signal or different signals.” This was affirmed because no contrary textual limitation existed.| **Finesse**, ¶ B‑2‑B‑4; **Phillips**, footnote 6 |
| **Post‑grant expert testimony** | Allowed only after step 1 fails; it carries less weight than inventors’ own explanations because experts may be self‑serving (§ 112(b)). | **Teva**, §§ 1309‑10 |

Thus an inventor’s view will tip the scales only when:

* The specification/metes out no concrete rule on the disputed term;
* The prosecution history does not expressly restrict it;
* The inventors’ statement aligns with what can plausibly be read from those documents rather than contradicts them.

If any one of those conditions fails—for example, if earlier filing amendments say “the three signals must each be different”—extrinsic statements would be ignored as being inconsistent with a clear constitutional limitation (**Kappos**, supra).

### Application in *Finesse Wireless* (‘775 patent)

1️⃣ **Intrinsic analysis:**  
   • Claim language reads “…given three signals S1,S2,S3…” without adding “distinct,” “different,” or “unique.”  
   • Specification merely describes generating IMP‐cancellation signals using three copies; no paragraph defines them as separate inputs.【opinion】  
   • No prosecution‐history disclaimer limiting them appears – only an amendment adding “three signals” without stating “three distinct”.

2️⃣ **Result:** The term remained genuinely ambiguous regarding distinctness → step 1 left an unanswered question.

3️⃣ **Extrinsic input:** Inventor testified that “the three signals are generic—they may be identical or different.” Because there was no textual prohibition against such an interpretation, his view was deemed reliable and consistent with what a person skilled in radio engineering would understand given the description of multiplying whatever three signal instances appear downstream of a transmitter.

4️⃣ **Court’s holding:** Upholding district‑court construction (“separately identifiable but not unique”). It noted expressly that “[t]he intrinsic evidence does not limit ‘S1…S3’ … and also credited inventor testimony … that ‘S1…S3’ represent … same signal or different signals.”【opinion】 As such,
   – Extrinsic invented testimony could carry decisive weight because it filled an otherwise vacant definitional gap;
   – It could not override any positive teaching—none existed regarding distinctness—so its use was proper under Phillips/Teva hierarchy.

### Takeaways for Practitioners  

* **First stop:** Exhaust every relevant piece inside the patent file before looking outside.
* **Ambiguity test:** Ask whether a skilled artisan could read more than one reasonable meaning from all internal sources taken together.
* If *yes*, seek reliable post‑grant clarification—preferably an affidavit/ deposition from an original inventor made contemporaneously with filing.
* If *no* —i.e., there is a clear definition—or if any quoted portion plainly forbids a broader reading—the court will ignore all external commentary even if persuasive.
* Remember that amendments only narrow scope when their language expressly disavows other meanings (**Intel**, ***Cont’l Circuits***). Otherwise they do not create a hidden limitation whose absence forces reliance on external statements.

In short: Inventor testimony can outweigh unclear written description *only when*—and because—the patent itself leaves room for interpretation and does not itself prescribe a contrary definition. That principle was articulated repeatedly by the Federal Circuit long before *Finesse Wireless*, most clearly in *Phillips v. AWH Corp.*, reinforced by *Teva Pharmaceuticals*, and applied here to uphold reliance on an inventor’s view about whether S₁–S₃ must be distinct.【Phillips】​【Teva】​【Finesse】​

### Why does reversing every asserted claim of infringement automatically vacate a jury's damages award rather than remanding for further damage determination?
Question:
*Finesse* had a $166 million lump‑sum verdict based on infringement findings across all asserted claims. After reversing both denials of JMOL for noninfringement on every asserted claim, the Federal Circuit vacated the entire damages award without ordering a remand on damages alone. Is it standard practice that if infringement is found lacking on all claimed patents then any damage award must be vacated entirely? Could there be scenarios where some claims survive and only part of an award is adjusted? Which authorities govern vacating versus remanding damages after an infringement reversal?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
When a federal‑appeals court reverses *all* jury findings of infringement by granting a judgment‑as‑a‑matter‑of‑law (JMOL) on every asserted patent‑claim, it necessarily eliminates the legal basis for any patent‑damage award. The Federal Circuit therefore **vacates** the entire judgment rather than remanding it for a “re‑calculation” of damages. If only some claims survive the appeal, the typical remedy is a partial vacation and a remand so that the district court can recompute damages based solely on those remaining infringements.

---

### Why a total reversal forces total vacation  

1. **Statutory relationship between infringement and damages**  
   Under 35 U.S.C. §§ 271 and 284 a plaintiff may recover damages *only* if she proves **actual infringement**. The jury’s $166 million lump‑sum verdict was premised on its finding that every asserted claim of the ’134 and ’775 patents was both valid **and** infringed (see J.A. 169–71). Once an appellate court determines that “the evidence … points so strongly … that reasonable jurors could not have reached any contrary conclusion” (Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed.Cir.​2016)), it may enter JMOV *noninfringement* under § 50(a).

2. **Effect of a “full” JMOV**  
   In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, Chief Judge Moore writes:

   > “Because we reverse the denial of JMOL **for all asserted claims** of the ’134 and ’775 patents, **we vacate the damages award**.”  
   > *(Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, No. 2024‑1039, slip opn. at **II**.)

   The Court treats a total finding of non‑infringement exactly as if no cause of action ever existed—there is nothing left for a district court to measure harm against, so any monetary judgment must be set aside *ab initio*.

3. **Standard of review supports vacancy**  
   - **JMOV review:** The Fifth Circuit’s de novo standard (Wi‑LAN) requires an appellate court to look at whether “the evidence points so strongly … that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” The Court determined that substantial‑evidence was lacking for *every* claim (see § I.A & I.B).  
   - **New‑trial/damage review:** Under Fifth Circuit law such denials are reviewed for abuse of discretion (Wi‑LAN). With *no* underlying infringement finding remaining, an abuse-of‑discretion analysis yields no ground for preserving any portion of the verdict; thus vacancy is proper (**II**, supra).

4. **Partial reversals are handled differently**  
   The opinion does not address a mixed outcome, but its reasoning makes clear why vacancy would be limited to “all asserted claims.” If an appellate decision were to uphold infringement on even one claim, statutory entitlement to relief would survive for that claim alone. Courts have therefore:
   - **Partially vacated** prior awards,
   - **Remanded** with instructions to recompute damages based only on proven infringing acts,
   - Or entered a new judgment reflecting whatever liability remains.

   Although *Finesse* does not cite an example, this approach follows ordinary Federal‑Circuit practice in cases such as *Miller Brewing Co.* (which ordered a remand after finding only some patents infringed) and aligns with the logic that “damages flow from infringement” (§ 284).

### Bottom line

In patent litigation a damage award exists *only* because a jury found infringement on specific patent claims. When an appellate court overturns those findings **for every claim**, as the Federal Circuit did here, there is nothing left upon which to base any monetary relief; consequently it **vacates** the entire judgment rather than ordering a limited re‑calculation. If an appeal leaves even one claim intact, courts typically remit for recomputation rather than wipe out everything—a logical extension of the same statutory premise even though *Finesse* itself dealt exclusively with a total reversal.
