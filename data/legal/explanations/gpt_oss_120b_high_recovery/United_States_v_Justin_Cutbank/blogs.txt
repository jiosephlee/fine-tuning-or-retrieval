# When Prior Possession Becomes Evidence: Rule 404(b) and Felon‑in‑Possession Cases in the Eighth Circuit  

## Why this decision matters  

The Eighth Circuit’s affirmation of *United States v.* Cutbank revives a doctrinal flashpoint that has haunted felon‑in‑possession prosecutions for years: when, if ever, a defendant’s earlier contact with a gun may be introduced to prove ownership at the moment of the charged offense. At issue was whether testimony that witnesses had “seen” Cutbank handling a sawed‑off .22 rifle before the night of the crime was barred as impermissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b) or was “intrinsic” evidence directly bearing on the core element of continuous possession required by §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e).  

The panel’s reliance on *United States v.* Battle (774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014)) signals that the Eighth Circuit continues to treat prior‑possession observations as admissible when they help establish control over the weapon at issue—not merely as character attacks. Because felon‑in‑possession statutes are often paired with mandatory‑minimum enhancements under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), any expansion—or even reaffirmation—of evidential latitude can directly increase terms of confinement for already‑incarcerated felons. It also raises a broader question for criminal practitioners nationwide: Will more courts follow this “intrinsic” logic, making past sightings a routine weapon‑ownership probe?  

In what follows I break down the appellate court’s rule‑404 analysis, place Battle in context, compare how other circuits handle similar facts, and assess whether Cutbank nudges the Eighth Circuit toward a more permissive evidentiary regime that could reshape both trial strategy and sentencing outcomes in felon‑in‑possession cases.  

## The factual backdrop  

On the night of November 19–20 2020, Justin Cutbank—an experienced felon—allegedly threatened his girlfriend, D.F., at gunpoint while under the influence of methamphetamine. After detaining her in a closet he fled with her cell phone and a Marlin .22 rifle whose barrel and stock had been sawed off. Police later recovered that very rifle—DNA matched Cutbank—and found additional knives and ammunition nearby. At trial D.F. testified she had seen Cutbank handling the sawed‑off rifle “a handful of times” before the incident; housemate C.F., testifying for the government, recalled seeing him possess it “once or twice” previously as well.  

The jury convicted Cutbank both of felon‑in‑possession and of having three prior violent felonies, thereby triggering ACCA’s enhanced penalty regime (§ 924(e)). On appeal he raised several evidentiary objections—including his contention that D.F.’s pre‑incident sightings should have been excluded as impermissible propensity evidence—and complained that the district court misapplied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which adds four levels when a firearm is used “in connection with another felony.” The appellate panel rejected every claim, finding no reversible error either at trial or at sentencing.  

## The eighth circuit’s Rule 404(b) analysis  

### Battle’s holding resurrected  

The crux of Cutbank’s evidentiary challenge rested on Rule 404(b)’s bar against introducing character evidence—or “propensity” evidence—to show conduct in conformity therewith.^[Fed.R.Evid 608(c); see *United States v.* Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir.* 2014)]. In *Battle*, Judge Loken wrote that “[p]rior possession…is directly relevant…to proving later possession…because it helps establish ownership or control,” thereby classifying such testimony as “intrinsic” rather than merely character proof.^[Id.]  

In *Cutbank* Judge Loken expressly reiterated *Battle*’s language, concluding that D.F.’s observations were admissible because they bore directly on whether Cutbank owned or controlled the Marlin rifle at the time he allegedly possessed it illegally.^[Cutbank, No.​ 24‑1225, ¶ A]. The district court therefore correctly permitted both D.F.’s and C.F.’s sightings without a limiting instruction because they were non‑hearsay statements offered to prove an essential element—continuous ownership—not to paint Cutbank as a “bad person.”  

The panel rejected Cutbank’s claim that these testimonies were improper propensity evidence because it found no indication that their purpose was solely character impeachment.^[Cutbank, ¶ C]. Moreover, any alleged prejudice was deemed harmless given the weight of physical DNA matching and other corroborating witness testimony.^[Id.]  

### Intrinsic versus propensity  

Rule 404(b)’s ban applies only when past conduct is offered solely to suggest an individual has a disposition toward committing similar wrongdoing.^[*Hudson* v.​ United States, 510 U.S._426_, 432 (1994)]. Courts therefore ask whether the proffered act bears sufficient logical relevance (“intrinsic”) to an essential element such that its probative value outweighs any prejudicial effect.^[*United States* v.​ Gillespie,,​ 454 F·3d 766 (9th Cir.​2006)](). In *Cutbank*, owning or having recently handled a particular firearm is logically part and parcel of establishing continuous possession—a statutory element requiring proof that the defendant possessed *that* weapon at adult‑prohibited moment.^[18 U.S.C.§ 922(g)(1)]. Accordingly, earlier sightings directly inform factual inference about who exercised dominion over this specific sawed-off rifle throughout any relevant period leading up to its recovery by police.^[Id.]  

The panel thus applied an “intrinsic‐evidence” test rather than an automatic propensity label—a distinction echoed throughout *Battle*’s reasoning yet rarely articulated outside firearms contexts until now.[^1]

# When Prior Possession Becomes Evidence: Rule 404(b) and Felon‑in‑Possession Cases in the Eighth Circuit  

## Why this decision matters  

The Eighth Circuit’s affirmation of *United States v.* Cutbank revives an evergreen tension in federal criminal trials: when does testimony about a defendant’s earlier contact with a gun cross from impermissible character attack into permissible proof of continuous ownership? The answer determines whether prosecutors can lean on “I’ve seen you with this weapon before” to satisfy one of the core elements of §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e)—that a felon possessed **that specific firearm** at the moment Congress criminalized its possession. Because ACCA enhancements hinge on proving multiple violent felonies together with illegal firearm use, any easing of evidentiary restraints potentially adds years—or decades—to sentences already subject to mandatory‐minimum treatment under § 924(e).  

Cutbank therefore offers more than a routine affirmance; it reactivates *United States v.* Battle (774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir.“2014”)), confirming that prior sightings are “intrinsic” evidence rather than prohibited propensity material under Rule 404(b). If other circuits follow suit—or if district judges feel empowered enough to routinely admit such testimony—the empirical landscape for felon‐in‐possession prosecutions could shift dramatically toward convictions hinged less on seized weapons themselves and more on testimonial recollections from months or even years earlier.

# When Prior Possession Becomes Evidence: Rule 404(b) and Felon‑in‑Possession Cases in the Eighth Circuit  

## Why this decision matters  

Federal statutes criminalizing illegal firearm possession hinge on one factual predicate: did the defendant actually own or control **the weapon** he is charged with possessing? In practice prosecutors often rely on physical recovery—DNA matches, serial numbers, ballistics—to satisfy that requirement. When those forensic anchors are weak or missing—as they were after Cutbank fled his girlfriend’s house—the government turns instead to eyewitness recollections about prior encounters between defendant and gun. Whether those recollections count as permissible proof of continuous ownership—or impermissibly paint the defendant as “a gun guy” for character purposes—affects every felon‑in‑possession prosecution across the country. Moreover, ACCA mandates mandatory minimums once three violent felonies combine with illegal firearm use (§ 924(e)); thus any ease with which prosecutors can demonstrate continuous possession translates into longer sentences under mandatory statutes designed precisely to keep guns away from repeat offenders.\[1\] By reaffirming *United States v.* Battle’s intrinsic/evidence standard, the Eighth Circuit clearly signals willingness to admit pre‑incident sighting testimony so long as it bears directly on proven elements rather than merely bolstering bad‐character impressions.\[2\] This pronouncement therefore deserves close scrutiny both for trial lawyers evaluating what witnesses they can call and for judges calibrating sentencing enhancements tied tightly to proof-of-possession facts.\[3\]   

## A brief factual recap  

On November 19–20, 2020 Justin Cutbank allegedly held his girlfriend D.F.~at gunpoint inside her home while intoxicated on methamphetamine before fleeing into his garage—but not before stealing her cell phone and departing with a sawed–off Marlin .22 caliber rifle whose barrel and stock had been lopped off.\[4\] Police later recovered exactly that rifle; DNA swabs matched Cutbank’s profile.\[5\] At trial D.F.~testified she had seen Cutbank handling “that same sawed–off rifle … a handful of times” during visits preceding the night in question; housemate C.F.~ corroborated having witnessed him possess it once or twice earlier.\[6\] Based largely on those recollections together with forensic DNA evidence, a jury convicted him under §§ 922(g)(1) (felon-in-possession) and §\!924(e) (ACC­A three‑strike enhancement). # Victim Statements, Hearsay, and the Confrontation Clause: The Eighth Circuit’s Approach in **United States v. Cutbank**

## Why This Case Matters  

Victim testimony sits at the heart of prosecutions for gun‑related violence, yet it also raises thorny constitutional questions about the Confrontation Clause and the admissibility of out‑of‑court statements.  In *United States v. Cutbank*, 2025 WL 3324361 (8th Cir. Oct. 7 2025), the Eleventh Circuit confronted precisely that tension when a defendant argued that the district court should have given a limiting instruction barring the jury from treating his girlfriend’s pre‑trial statements to police as substantive evidence of his conduct.  The appellate panel rejected that argument, holding that no such instruction was required because D.F.’s own testimony rendered any hearsay concerns moot and because the trial court had not actually admitted her out‑of‑court statements as substantive evidence.  

The decision is noteworthy for three reasons:

1. **It clarifies how the Eighth Circuit applies *Crawford*‑era Confrontation Clause jurisprudence when a witness later testifies in person**, reaffirming that “the Clause is satisfied when…the declarant actually appears in court and testifies.”  

2. **It signals continued deference to district courts’ evidentiary discretion**, especially where the probative value of victim testimony outweighs any minimal risk of prejudice—a stance consistent with prior Eleventh Circuit precedent such as *Bennett* and *Charboneau*.  

3. **It offers a roadmap for prosecutors and defense attorneys alike** on when—and whether—to push for a limiting instruction on victim statements in violent‑crime cases across federal courts.

Given the growing reliance on victim‑submitted police reports, text messages, and other “pre‑testimony” materials, understanding *Cutbank*’s rationale is essential for anyone navigating federal criminal trials involving domestic violence or firearms offenses.

## The Factual Backdrop  

Justin Cutbank—a career felon with an extensive violent record—was convicted under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) after an October 2020 incident in which he allegedly held his girlfriend (identified only as D.F.) at gunpoint, threatened her children, and later fled with her cell phone and a sawed‑off Marlin .22 rifle discovered by police at a nearby garage where he resisted arrest and bit an officer’s face.   At trial, D.F. testified she had “seen Cutbank with [the] sawed‑off rifle a handful of times” before the night in question; she also recounted her own “not familiar with guns” comment during cross‑examination—details originated from earlier statements she made to police officers during their investigation.

Cutbank appealed his conviction on several grounds, but chief among them was his claim that allowing D.F.’s earlier police statements into evidence without a special jury instruction violated his Fifth Amendment rights under *Crawford* v Washington (541 U.S._36_ (2004)). 

## The Procedural Problem: Prior Statements Without a Limiting Instruction  

Cutbank's motion sought an instruction telling jurors they could consider D.F.’s pre‑testimony statements only for limited purposes—namely impeachment—not as substantive proof of his possession of a firearm or intent during the incident. He asserted two intertwined concerns:

1. **Hearsay** – That D.F.’s out‑of‑court remarks about what she saw constituted “new” evidence offered to prove material facts (e.g., continuous possession).  
2 **Confrontation** – That using those remarks without an opportunity for him to cross‑examine their original source denied him “the right to confront witnesses” under the Sixth Amendment incorporated by *Crawford*.

The district court denied both requests, reasoning that D.F.’s testimony at trial superseded any purportedly admittable hearsay—because she herself repeated her observations—thus satisfying both Federal Rules of Evidence § 803(23) (“available witness”) considerations and constitutional confrontation requirements.

On appeal, Cutbank invoked *United States v.* *Bennett*, 765 F.3d 887 (8th Cir._2014), arguing that any error in refusing a limiting instruction warranted reversal under an “abuse of discretion” standard applied to evidentiary rulings.^[**Bennett**, 765 F·3d at 899.] He also leaned heavily on *Charboneau*, 613 F·3d 860 (8th Cir._2010), which holds that “the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when…the declarant actually appears in court and testifies.”^[**Charboneau**, 613 F·3d at 861.]

## The Court’s Analysis  

Judge Loken’s opinion rested squarely on two pillars: (1) procedural deference under *Bennett*, which cautions appellate courts against overturning evidential discretion absent clear abuse; and (2) doctrinal reliance on *Charboneau*’s dictum concerning witness availability.

### No Hearsay Error Because No Hearsay Was Admitted  

The panel noted that there was no indication from the record that D.F.’s prior police remarks were introduced as “substantive evidence.” Rather than admitting those out‑of‑court statements directly, the trial judge permitted D.F.’s live testimony about having previously seen Cutbank with the sawed‑off rifle—a factual recollection now subject to cross‑examination.^[The record confirms “the jury heard … testimony … at trial.”] Under Federal Rule of Evidence § 802, hearsay is inadmissible unless an exception applies; here no exception was needed because there was no hearsay proposition offered—merely eyewitness testimony reiterated by its original source.^[Fed.R.Evid.@803]. 

### Confrontation Satisfied By Live Testimony  

Because D.F.’s statements were not offered through any hearsay exception but instead via her own courtroom presence, *Charboneau* directly applied: her face‑to‑face testimony means any element of confrontation is fulfilled regardless of earlier police interviews.^[Id.] Consequently, cutting off any portion of her live testimony through an additional juror instruction would have been unnecessary—and possibly harmful—by undermining juror comprehension of a key factfinder’s narrative.^[Judge Loken notes “no abuse … because … no admission of hearsay.”]

### Proper Application Of Abuse-of-Discretion Standard  

Following *Bennett*, which emphasizes deference unless trial conduct materially prejudices defendants’ rights or substantially influences verdicts,^ [**Bennett**, 765 F·3d at 898–899] Loken concluded that any error—if present—was harmless given Cutbank’s overwhelming evidentiary landscape (DNA match on gun barrel, corroborating testimonies from multiple witnesses). The appellate panel therefore affirmed without discussing facial reversal authority.^[See also *DeMarce*, 564 F·3d at 997.]

## Is This Reasoning Persuasive?  

From my perspective—shaped by recent federal appellate developments—the Eighth Circuit’s approach aligns well with sound constitutional doctrine yet leaves space for nuanced refinement.

### Strengths  

1️⃣ **Conformance With Crawford Foundations** – By anchoring its reasoning in *Charboneau*’s articulation of confrontation fulfillment through live testimony—as opposed to laboriously parsing whether each prior statement satisfies “testimonial” criteria from *Crawford*/*Davis* – Loken sidesteps unnecessary doctrinal gymnastics.^[*Crawford*, 541 U.S._36_; see also *Davis* 504 U.S._167_]. 

2️⃣ **Preserving Trial Efficiency** – Requiring separate limiting instructions whenever a witness has made preliminary police reports would greatly complicate trial procedures without demonstrable gains in fairness⁠—a position echoed by numerous Ninth Circuit decisions allowing unencumbered admission of such “available witness” evidence.^[E.g., *United States v.* *Katz*, 980 F·2d 438 (9th Cir._1993)]. 

3️⃣ **Adhering To Harmless Error Doctrine** – In violent felony cases where physical evidence markedly outweighs any questionable testimonial admission,^ [e.g., DNA matching weapons], treating marginal evidentiary missteps as harmless preserves judicial resources while maintaining defendants’ due process rights.^[cf.*Kuntzman*, 574 F·2d at 34].

### Potential Weaknesses  

🔍 **Overlooking Cumulative Prejudice** – While Loken correctly finds no direct admission of hearsay, he arguably glosses over whether mentioning D.F.’s previous sightings could unfairly bias jurors toward believing she possesses perfect recall—a subtle form of impermissible “storytelling”. Some circuits—most notably the Fourth Circuit in *United States v.* *Kreitzman*, 371 F·3d 694 (4th Cir._2004)—have emphasized heightened scrutiny where victim recollections are reiterated multiple times through varying narrative lenses.^[Kreitzman] The lack of explicit acknowledgment here may signal missed opportunity for richer analysis at least under Rule 403 balancing considerations.* 

🛠️ **Uniform Application Across Circuits** – As noted below, there remains nonuniformity regarding whether jury instructions are routinely employed where victims offer prior incriminating remarks before their own live testimony arrives on the stand.^[In contrast see *United States v.* *Copley*, 780 F·3dwhile relevant discussions surface.] An Eighth Circuit pronouncement explicitly setting standards could help harmonize practice nationwide—an omission near this decision's edges paves way for continued circuit splits.[^footnote] 

Overall though I find Loken’s rationale largely sound—especially given how often prosecutors rely upon victims’ early investigative interactions—and appreciate its faithful adherence to established precedents while avoiding needless procedural bloat.[^footnote]

[^footnote]: See discussion below concerning divergent treatment among circuits regarding imperative limiting instructions after victim preliminary statements are introduced indirectly through witness recollection or documentary forms such as text messages or audio recordings experience elsewhere.*

## Doctrinal Context: Hearsay Exceptions vs Confrontation  

At first blush,*Hearsay* doctrine disallows out–of–court assertions presented to prove truth unless tossed into narrow exceptions—including “available witness,” “present sense impression,” or “statement against interest.” However signal value lies precisely within Confrontation Clause analysis after ***Crawford*.*** Roughly speaking—per *Crawford*-*Davis*-*Melendez-Diaz* lineage—the focus shifts from formal hearsay rules toward assessing whether an out–of–court statement is testimonial (*i.e.*, whether its primary purpose was creating evidence suitable for prosecution), its reliability indicators (*e.g.*, corroborated reliability), and importantly whether it affords defendants an opportunity "to cross-examine."^[(*Crawford*, 541 U.S._36_; *Davis*, 504 U.S._167_; *Melendez-Diaz*, 529 U.S._293_)] 

In Cutbank's instance:

- **Hearsay Point:** If D.F.’s earlier descriptions had been read straight into evidence absent her presence—a classic scenario where "statement against interest" would be invoked—they would be deemed testimonial because they aim principally at establishing criminal conduct.^[*Carter v.* US,*†(same citation hierarchy)*]

- **Confrontation Point:** Yet those same comments became non‐testimonial once D.F.’s live demeanor entered courtroom—the declaration became part of intra‐trial exchange subject to cross-examination guaranteeing confrontation compliance.^[*Charboneau*]

Thus what mattered guided more by "availability" than by pure reliance upon technical inframodal exceptions; this nuance fits comfortably within contemporary jurisprudence barringing expansion toward procedural redundancy except where fairness becomes compromised—for example where defamation claims involve high stakes documentation occasionally best evaluated via private affidavits.

# Stacking Sentencing Enhancements Under the ACCA and USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B): Does Cutbank’s Sentence Meet the “Sufficient but Not Greater Than Necessary” Standard?

## Why Cutbank Matters

When Justin Cutbank was sentenced this spring to 292 months—a term essentially at the bottom of his advisory guideline range—the Eighth Circuit seized upon two questions that reverberate far beyond any single domestic‑violence case. First, does it make sense to pile multiple United States Sentencing Guidelines (USSG) enhancements—saw‑off firearms, altered serial numbers, reckless flight, and “possession in connection with another felony”—on top of the Armed Career Criminal Act’s (ACCA) mandatory‑minimum regime without running afoul of the core sentencing principle that a punishment must be “sufficient, but not greater than necessary” § 3553(a)? Second, what does this decision say about appellate deference to district courts when they aggregate enhancements under both statutory and guideline regimes?

The answers will shape how prosecutors stack “stackable” enhancements across the nation and could force courts/legislators to revisit whether such accumulations run counter to congressional intent behind both the ACCA and the Guideline system post‑Booker.

## The Facts (Brief)

In November 2020 Cutbank—an experienced felon—entered his girlfriend’s home while heavily intoxicated on methamphetamine, threatened her at gunpoint with a saw‑ed‑off .22 rifle, assaulted her, then fled with her phone and firearm. Police later intercepted him after he barricaded himself in a nearby garage where he bit an officer’s neck before surrendering. A search recovered D.F.’s cellphone, a butcher‑style knife, and the saw‑ed‑off Marlin rifle whose DNA matched Cutbank. At trial a jury convicted him under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) (felon in possession) and found he had at least three prior violent felonies—triggering the ACCA’s enhanced penalty provisions.

The district court calculated an offense level of 35 based on four separate guideline enhancements:

1. **Saw‑off firearm** (U.S.S.G.§ 2K2.1(b)(5)(C)) – +2;
2. **Altered/obliterated serial number** (§ 2K2.1(b)(5)(D)) – +2;
3. **Recklessly creating a risk of death while fleeing police** (§ 2K2.1(b)(4)) – +2;
4. **Possession in connection with another felony** (§ 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)) – +4.

The base offense level was 26 for a “firearm possessed in furtherance of unlawful activity,” plus those eight levels yields an overall offense level of 35 and (with a Criminal History Category VI) an advisory range of 292–365 months—the sentence imposed sat at the low end.

## How the Eighth Circuit Applied Existing Law

Judge Loken wrote that each enhancement was properly supported by the record:

* The saw‑off designation was undisputed because District Court findings confirmed both barrel length (< 16 in.) and stock reduction (< 26 in.).
* Serial‑number alteration was established by forensic experts who testified that the rifle’s original markings had been obliterated.
* Reckless flight risk stemmed from officers’ testimony that Cutbank intentionally fled while brandishing a weapon that could have caused serious bodily injury.
* Possession “in connection with another felony” relied on the fact pattern where Cutbank’s possession overlapped with his violent assault—a felony offense—even though no separate charge was lodged for that specific assault during sentencing.

Crucially, the court emphasized its reliance on precedent such as *United States v.* Dunn, 723 F.3d 919 (8th Cir. 2013), which holds that an enhancement may be applied when the firearm is used “in connection with any other federal, state or local felony,” irrespective of whether another prosecution ensues.

On appeal Cutbank argued these accumulations violated § 3553(a)’s “no greater than necessary” clause because they effectively made his sentence longer than required by statutory minima plus a reasonable guideline range once you factor in his hefty criminal history already mandated by ACCA’s three‑strike rule.

The Eighth Circuit rejected this contention on two fronts:

1. **Procedural deference:** The court reiterated that because there were no reversible procedural errors—no abuse of discretion in evidentiary rulings—the conviction stands under the harmless‑error standard articulated in *United States v.* DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989 (8th Cir. 2009). 

2. **Substantive reasonableness:** Invoking *Gall v.* United States (552 U.S.—2007) and *Rosales‑Mireles v.* United States (585 U.S.—2018), Judge Loken held that once the proper guideline range is derived—here using all four listed enhancements—the district court need only show it genuinely considered all § 3553(a) factors before imposing its within‑range sentence. No explicit “mechanical recitation” is required so long as there is a clear record indicating consideration—something they affirmed existed through sentencing memoranda and oral argument transcripts.

Accordingly, Cutbank’s appeal failed on both procedural merits and substantive grounds; his sentence remained intact.

## The Core Question: Is “Stacking” Consistent With § 3553(a)?

### Statutory Text versus Practical Effect

At first glance § 3553(a)’s “no greater than necessary” language sounds like a bright line against stacking penalties indiscriminately—if you can achieve statutory goals with fewer sentencing points, why add more? Yet Supreme Court precedent treats this clause as **a guidance principle**, not an independent constraint overriding clear statutory mandates like those found in ACCA or USSG §§ specifically authorizing those very enhancements.

*In* *United States v.* Roberge, 728 F.3d 1174 (9th Cir., 2014), *the Ninth Circuit* noted that “enhancements are part…an intentional design...” intended “to reflect specific aggravating conduct.” Thus when Congress creates separate enhancement categories—each addressing distinct harms—courts are expected to apply them cumulatively unless statutory language expressly bars overlap (*e.g.*, double counting for same conduct). 

The federal anti‐stacking doctrine exists only where Congress explicitly says so (*e.g.*, prohibiting multiple counts for same conduct under e.g., *Miller v.*). Absent such limitation here, each statutory trigger—saw‐off property (*§ 5845(a)*), altered serial number (*§ 5845(c)*), reckless flight (*§ 922(g)*(?), *etc.*)—has its own policy rationale:

* **Saw‐offs** increase lethality,
* **Serial-number alteration** burden law enforcement,
* **Reckless flight** evidences conscious disregard for public safety,
* **Possession tied to another felony** underscores weapon use as part of broader criminal conduct.

Each contributes uniquely to assessing culpability—and collectively lifts offense level well above what would result from any single factor alone.

### Guideline Theory Post‑Booker

Following *United States v.* Booker (543 U.S., 2005), Guidelines are advisory rather than mandatory—but they are still ***prescribed*** components building towards statutory goals via round‑up calculations blended into §§3110–3117 considerations for reasonableness review (§​3553(a)). The Eighth Circuit has consistently held post‑Booker that even advisory ranges are subject to judicial scrutiny only when they produce sentences outside permissible ranges or ignore relevant §​3553(a) factors (*see* *United States v.* Goodhouse*, 81 F.​4th 786 (8th Cir.,2023)). So long as district courts establish a factual basis satisfying each enhancement element,\*the accumulation remains within their discretion.

### Overlap Concerns

Critics might argue there is conceptual overlap between #3 (reckless flight risk) and #4 (possession used while committing another felony). Both stem from Cutbank’s use of a firearm during an ongoing unlawful encounter—and some scholars worry such “stacking” inflates penalties beyond what Congress envisioned when it set out each factor separately.\*

Yet Federal Sentencing Commission policy statements explicitly note they intend cumulative application unless doing so would result in **“double counting”** identical conduct (\*USSCG §​47–34(A)\*) . In Cutbank’s case each labeled conduct satisfies distinct statutory predicates:

* He possessed an already-modified saw‑off rifle (**Category A**); 
* He deliberately destroyed its serial number (**Category B**);
* He fled from police while brandishing it (**Category C**, specifically reckless creation of risk);
* He possessed it while committing assault—a distinct felony separate from flight (\**Category D)**,

so double counting isn’t triggered per Commission guidance \[U.S.S.G.§ 47–34(A)]\]. 

Thus his sentence arguably satisfies rather than exceeds every relevant condemnation Congress encoded into both ACCA legislative history (“eliminate dangerous repeat offenders”) and USSG designations (“provide gradated penalty increments reflecting incremental culpability”).

### Balancing Act: Removing Excess vs Undermining Statutory Intent

If we applied an overly stringent reading—telling courts they cannot stack any more than one firearm enhancement per case—we risk eroding deterring effect embedded within statutes confronting high-risk weaponry traits._Transactions_ like saw‐offs were singled out precisely because their junction with other illicit behavior signals amplified danger.\*

Conversely, unbridled stacking could create disproportionately lengthy sentences once you add up upward adjustments born out wanly related facts.\—raising concerns about proportionality pleaded by defense counsel across circuits since *Williams v.* \[9th Cir.] which flagged random supercharging.\  

The correct equilibrium lies pending further guidance either from higher appellate authority or legislative refinement—that seems unlikely given Congress’ longstanding propensity toward hardening penalties.]

# Harmless Error Doctrine After Eighth Circuit Trial Rulings: Lessons from *United States v. Cutbank*

## Why *Cutbank* Matters  

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, 8th Cir. No. 24‑1225 (Oct. 7 2025), offers a concise illustration of how federal appellate courts wield the “harmless‑error” doctrine after a series of disputed trial‑court evidentiary rulings. Defendants routinely argue that improper admission or exclusion of testimony requires reversal, yet appellate courts rarely overturn convictions unless the error is both “non‑harmless” under *Chapman*/*DeMarce* and “substantially prejudicial” under *Keys*. In *Cutbank*, the panel walked through eight distinct objections—ranging from Rule 404(b) propensity challenges to improper impeachment—and concluded none rose to the level of reversible error. For practitioners who hope to rescue a conviction on such grounds, the opinion serves as both a cautionary tale about preservation and a roadmap for what it takes—materiality plus demonstrable prejudice—to persuade an appellate panel to intervene.

## The Court’s Harmless‑Error Framework  

### The *DeMarce* Standard  

The Ninth Band “harmless‑error” rule asks whether an appellate court can conclude beyond a reasonable doubt that “the evidence presented at trial would have been sufficient to support a conviction even without the alleged error.” *United States v. DeMarce*, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009). In practice this means looking at the strength of the untainted record: eyewitness identification, forensic linkage, confession or admission, and any other proof independent of the disputed material. If those sources alone satisfy *Chapman*’s “probability” test—that there is “a probability beyond a reasonable doubt” that the conviction would have occurred—the error is harmless even if it was flagrantly improper.

### The *Keys* Amplifier  

While *DeMarce* focuses on whether an individual error could have changed the verdict, *United States v. Keys* expands scrutiny to cumulative prejudice: “the cumulative effect of multiple trial‐court errors…must be shown to have substantially influenced…the verdict.” 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019). A defendant thus bears a double burden—first to demonstrate each illegal admission or exclusion was material, second to show that together they tipped the scales toward guilt.

### Applying Those Tests in *Cutbank*  

1️⃣ **Propensity Evidence Under Rule 404(b).** Cutbank contended that D.F.’s testimony about seeing him with the sawed‑off rifle prior to the incident was inadmissible character evidence. The district court treated it as intrinsic—relevant to ownership—and allowed it under *Battle*, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014). On appeal there was no indication that any alternative source could have proved possession without this testimony; DNA on the recovered rifle already linked Cutbank directly to the weapon. Thus under *DeMarce* any correct inference about possession could be drawn without D.F.’s propensity narrative—rendering the ruling harmless.

2️⃣ **Confrontation Concerns About D.F.’s Police Statements.** Cutbank argued that admitting D.F.’s statements made to police before she testified violated her Sixth Amendment rights absent a limiting instruction. The court rejected because D.F.’s statements were never offered as substantive hearsay—the officer testimony alone rendered them unnecessary—and because she later testified in person, satisfying *Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010). Even assuming an informal breach occurred, *DeMarce* requires proof that excluding those statements would have produced doubt about guilt—a proposition unsupported given physical evidence and multiple eyewitness accounts.

3️⃣ **Impeachment Through Prior Inconsistent Statements (“Sister Was Killed”).** The district judge allowed D.F.’s mention of her sister’s death despite Cutbank’s objection under Rule 403’s prejudice/confusion inquiry. The trial record shows this side note was merely explanatory background after cross‑examination probed D.F.’s reliability regarding firearms; it did not introduce new factual claims about Cutbank’s conduct. A review under *DeMarce* finds no risk that its removal would have altered jurors’ view of his culpability—the core incriminating facts remain untouched—so again harmlessness follows automatically.

4️⃣ **Authentication Requirements for Police Reports Used in Impeachment Of C.F.’s Testimony**  
Here the district court enforced proper authentication standards consistent with *Cumbie*, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir.​ 2022). Even if Cutter had succeeded in admitting excerpts from an unauthenticated report—a possible violation—the report would only serve impeachment purposes rather than substantive proof of possession or violence; its absence scarcely diminishes proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

Across all eight objections—notably none were “preserved” in traditional form because Cutbank offered little authority or detailed argument—the panel’s reliance on *DeMarce*’s “overwhelming evidence” language made clear why no single mistake could be truly determinative.



## A Critical Take on Deference  

The Eighth Circuit’s opinion underscores two enduring themes:

1️⃣ **Preservation Is Paramount** – Most of Cutbank’s complaints were barely articulated (“no cases,” “almost no analysis”), rendering them technically unpreserved under Rule 52(a). Appellate courts traditionally give little weight where counsel fails to signal an objection clearly enough for an immediate record‑building opportunity—an omission almost as fatal as any merits deficiency.
   
2️⃣ **The “Harmless Beyond Reasonable Doubt” Threshold Remains High** – By anchoring its analysis in concrete fact‑finding rather than abstract legal theory (“the government’s evidence…was substantial”), the panel adhered closely to Supreme Court precedent requiring proof **that without the questioned material there still exists sufficient proof** (*Chapman* 475 U.S.—at 469). Some commentators argue this high bar leaves little room for addressing subtle cumulative biases that may flow from multiple minor infractions—a concern especially relevant when defendants are socio‑economically disadvantaged and lack seasoned counsel capable of raising nuanced objections at trial.

Nevertheless, I find little reason to fault Judge Loken for staying within standard bounds; overturning convictions merely because an attorney failed to cite binding precedent would erode finality without benefiting justice when physical DNA and witness testimony already provide irrefutable proof.


## Doctrinal Ripple Effects  

### Reinforcing Existing Evidentiary Presumptions  

Because *Cutbank* reaffirmed several older precedents—*Battle* for intrinsic firearm‑possession relevance, *Bennett* for discretionary limiting instructions, *Cumbie* for authentication—the decision adds another citation line supporting routine admission of prior possession evidence in §922(g)(1) prosecutions across federal courts in the Midwest and beyond.


### Guiding Future Harmless‑Error Motions  

Defendants hoping for relief on evidentiary grounds now have an explicit reminder:

- **Show Independent Sufficiency:** Identify specific innocuous elements—DNA matches, seized firearm serial numbers modified—but overlooked by defense counsel.
  
- **Quantify Cumulative Prejudice:** Merely cataloguing several minor errors will no longer suffice; plaintiffs must articulate how those together created “substantial prejudice,” ideally by demonstrating an alternative factual scenario peaked toward acquittal (*Keys*, supra).

Lawyers may adapt by obtaining jury verdict forms or pre‑sentencing statements indicating reliance upon disputed testimony—a strategy increasingly common after these decisions.


### Sentencing Enhancements Remain Secure  

The panel also rejected Cutbank’s argument that applying §2K2·1(b)(6)(B)—the four‑level enhancement for firearm possession during another felony—clashed with §3553(a)’s proportionality principle because it nudged his sentence upward within range (§924(e)). By emphasizing correct calculation per United States v.Dunn and noting no misuse of discretionary factors per Gall and Rosales‑Mireles, the ruling signals continued deference toward guideline worksheets so long as districts stay within advisory ranges and address §3553(a) factors qualitatively rather than mechanically enumerating them.


## Looking Ahead  

For criminal litigators operating in the Eighth Circuit—and arguably nation‑wide—the Takeaways are clear:

1️⃣ **Meticulous Preservation Wins:** A flashpoint argument uttered without citation or wavefoot can become dead wood at appeal.
   
2️⃣ **Harmless Error Requires More Than ‘Harmless’:** Courts will ask whether you could prove guilt without every contested piece—not just whether each piece alone is extremly prejudicial.
   
3️⃣ **Cumulative Prejudice Must Be Substantively Demonstrated:** Mere accumulation does not equal causation; you must connect each fragment into an alternative narrative persuasive enough to tip reasonable doubt.
   
4️⃣ **Guideline Enhancements Remain Rugged:** As long as sentencing calculators follow statutory commentary exactly—as here—they are unlikely avenues for reduction once advisory ranges are reached.


In short, *United States v.* Cutbank reiterates once again what practitioners have learned since *Chapman*: federal appeals courts will look past trial‐court slip‑ups when overwhelming proof exists elsewhere on file—and they will demand concrete quantification when defendants claim otherwise.
