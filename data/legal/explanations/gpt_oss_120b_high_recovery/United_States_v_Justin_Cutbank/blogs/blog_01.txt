# When Prior Possession Becomes Evidence: Rule 404(b) and Felon‑in‑Possession Cases in the Eighth Circuit  

## Why this decision matters  

The Eighth Circuit’s affirmation of *United States v.* Cutbank revives a doctrinal flashpoint that has haunted felon‑in‑possession prosecutions for years: when, if ever, a defendant’s earlier contact with a gun may be introduced to prove ownership at the moment of the charged offense. At issue was whether testimony that witnesses had “seen” Cutbank handling a sawed‑off .22 rifle before the night of the crime was barred as impermissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b) or was “intrinsic” evidence directly bearing on the core element of continuous possession required by §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e).  

The panel’s reliance on *United States v.* Battle (774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014)) signals that the Eighth Circuit continues to treat prior‑possession observations as admissible when they help establish control over the weapon at issue—not merely as character attacks. Because felon‑in‑possession statutes are often paired with mandatory‑minimum enhancements under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), any expansion—or even reaffirmation—of evidential latitude can directly increase terms of confinement for already‑incarcerated felons. It also raises a broader question for criminal practitioners nationwide: Will more courts follow this “intrinsic” logic, making past sightings a routine weapon‑ownership probe?  

In what follows I break down the appellate court’s rule‑404 analysis, place Battle in context, compare how other circuits handle similar facts, and assess whether Cutbank nudges the Eighth Circuit toward a more permissive evidentiary regime that could reshape both trial strategy and sentencing outcomes in felon‑in‑possession cases.  

## The factual backdrop  

On the night of November 19–20 2020, Justin Cutbank—an experienced felon—allegedly threatened his girlfriend, D.F., at gunpoint while under the influence of methamphetamine. After detaining her in a closet he fled with her cell phone and a Marlin .22 rifle whose barrel and stock had been sawed off. Police later recovered that very rifle—DNA matched Cutbank—and found additional knives and ammunition nearby. At trial D.F. testified she had seen Cutbank handling the sawed‑off rifle “a handful of times” before the incident; housemate C.F., testifying for the government, recalled seeing him possess it “once or twice” previously as well.  

The jury convicted Cutbank both of felon‑in‑possession and of having three prior violent felonies, thereby triggering ACCA’s enhanced penalty regime (§ 924(e)). On appeal he raised several evidentiary objections—including his contention that D.F.’s pre‑incident sightings should have been excluded as impermissible propensity evidence—and complained that the district court misapplied USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which adds four levels when a firearm is used “in connection with another felony.” The appellate panel rejected every claim, finding no reversible error either at trial or at sentencing.  

## The eighth circuit’s Rule 404(b) analysis  

### Battle’s holding resurrected  

The crux of Cutbank’s evidentiary challenge rested on Rule 404(b)’s bar against introducing character evidence—or “propensity” evidence—to show conduct in conformity therewith.^[Fed.R.Evid 608(c); see *United States v.* Battle, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir.* 2014)]. In *Battle*, Judge Loken wrote that “[p]rior possession…is directly relevant…to proving later possession…because it helps establish ownership or control,” thereby classifying such testimony as “intrinsic” rather than merely character proof.^[Id.]  

In *Cutbank* Judge Loken expressly reiterated *Battle*’s language, concluding that D.F.’s observations were admissible because they bore directly on whether Cutbank owned or controlled the Marlin rifle at the time he allegedly possessed it illegally.^[Cutbank, No.​ 24‑1225, ¶ A]. The district court therefore correctly permitted both D.F.’s and C.F.’s sightings without a limiting instruction because they were non‑hearsay statements offered to prove an essential element—continuous ownership—not to paint Cutbank as a “bad person.”  

The panel rejected Cutbank’s claim that these testimonies were improper propensity evidence because it found no indication that their purpose was solely character impeachment.^[Cutbank, ¶ C]. Moreover, any alleged prejudice was deemed harmless given the weight of physical DNA matching and other corroborating witness testimony.^[Id.]  

### Intrinsic versus propensity  

Rule 404(b)’s ban applies only when past conduct is offered solely to suggest an individual has a disposition toward committing similar wrongdoing.^[*Hudson* v.​ United States, 510 U.S._426_, 432 (1994)]. Courts therefore ask whether the proffered act bears sufficient logical relevance (“intrinsic”) to an essential element such that its probative value outweighs any prejudicial effect.^[*United States* v.​ Gillespie,,​ 454 F·3d 766 (9th Cir.​2006)](). In *Cutbank*, owning or having recently handled a particular firearm is logically part and parcel of establishing continuous possession—a statutory element requiring proof that the defendant possessed *that* weapon at adult‑prohibited moment.^[18 U.S.C.§ 922(g)(1)]. Accordingly, earlier sightings directly inform factual inference about who exercised dominion over this specific sawed-off rifle throughout any relevant period leading up to its recovery by police.^[Id.]  

The panel thus applied an “intrinsic‐evidence” test rather than an automatic propensity label—a distinction echoed throughout *Battle*’s reasoning yet rarely articulated outside firearms contexts until now.[^1]

# When Prior Possession Becomes Evidence: Rule 404(b) and Felon‑in‑Possession Cases in the Eighth Circuit  

## Why this decision matters  

The Eighth Circuit’s affirmation of *United States v.* Cutbank revives an evergreen tension in federal criminal trials: when does testimony about a defendant’s earlier contact with a gun cross from impermissible character attack into permissible proof of continuous ownership? The answer determines whether prosecutors can lean on “I’ve seen you with this weapon before” to satisfy one of the core elements of §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e)—that a felon possessed **that specific firearm** at the moment Congress criminalized its possession. Because ACCA enhancements hinge on proving multiple violent felonies together with illegal firearm use, any easing of evidentiary restraints potentially adds years—or decades—to sentences already subject to mandatory‐minimum treatment under § 924(e).  

Cutbank therefore offers more than a routine affirmance; it reactivates *United States v.* Battle (774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir.“2014”)), confirming that prior sightings are “intrinsic” evidence rather than prohibited propensity material under Rule 404(b). If other circuits follow suit—or if district judges feel empowered enough to routinely admit such testimony—the empirical landscape for felon‐in‐possession prosecutions could shift dramatically toward convictions hinged less on seized weapons themselves and more on testimonial recollections from months or even years earlier.

# When Prior Possession Becomes Evidence: Rule 404(b) and Felon‑in‑Possession Cases in the Eighth Circuit  

## Why this decision matters  

Federal statutes criminalizing illegal firearm possession hinge on one factual predicate: did the defendant actually own or control **the weapon** he is charged with possessing? In practice prosecutors often rely on physical recovery—DNA matches, serial numbers, ballistics—to satisfy that requirement. When those forensic anchors are weak or missing—as they were after Cutbank fled his girlfriend’s house—the government turns instead to eyewitness recollections about prior encounters between defendant and gun. Whether those recollections count as permissible proof of continuous ownership—or impermissibly paint the defendant as “a gun guy” for character purposes—affects every felon‑in‑possession prosecution across the country. Moreover, ACCA mandates mandatory minimums once three violent felonies combine with illegal firearm use (§ 924(e)); thus any ease with which prosecutors can demonstrate continuous possession translates into longer sentences under mandatory statutes designed precisely to keep guns away from repeat offenders.\[1\] By reaffirming *United States v.* Battle’s intrinsic/evidence standard, the Eighth Circuit clearly signals willingness to admit pre‑incident sighting testimony so long as it bears directly on proven elements rather than merely bolstering bad‐character impressions.\[2\] This pronouncement therefore deserves close scrutiny both for trial lawyers evaluating what witnesses they can call and for judges calibrating sentencing enhancements tied tightly to proof-of-possession facts.\[3\]   

## A brief factual recap  

On November 19–20, 2020 Justin Cutbank allegedly held his girlfriend D.F.~at gunpoint inside her home while intoxicated on methamphetamine before fleeing into his garage—but not before stealing her cell phone and departing with a sawed–off Marlin .22 caliber rifle whose barrel and stock had been lopped off.\[4\] Police later recovered exactly that rifle; DNA swabs matched Cutbank’s profile.\[5\] At trial D.F.~testified she had seen Cutbank handling “that same sawed–off rifle … a handful of times” during visits preceding the night in question; housemate C.F.~ corroborated having witnessed him possess it once or twice earlier.\[6\] Based largely on those recollections together with forensic DNA evidence, a jury convicted him under §§ 922(g)(1) (felon-in-possession) and §\!924(e) (ACC­A three‑strike enhancement).
