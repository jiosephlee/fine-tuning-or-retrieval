# Victim Statements, Hearsay, and the Confrontation Clause: The Eighth Circuit’s Approach in **United States v. Cutbank**

## Why This Case Matters  

Victim testimony sits at the heart of prosecutions for gun‑related violence, yet it also raises thorny constitutional questions about the Confrontation Clause and the admissibility of out‑of‑court statements.  In *United States v. Cutbank*, 2025 WL 3324361 (8th Cir. Oct. 7 2025), the Eleventh Circuit confronted precisely that tension when a defendant argued that the district court should have given a limiting instruction barring the jury from treating his girlfriend’s pre‑trial statements to police as substantive evidence of his conduct.  The appellate panel rejected that argument, holding that no such instruction was required because D.F.’s own testimony rendered any hearsay concerns moot and because the trial court had not actually admitted her out‑of‑court statements as substantive evidence.  

The decision is noteworthy for three reasons:

1. **It clarifies how the Eighth Circuit applies *Crawford*‑era Confrontation Clause jurisprudence when a witness later testifies in person**, reaffirming that “the Clause is satisfied when…the declarant actually appears in court and testifies.”  

2. **It signals continued deference to district courts’ evidentiary discretion**, especially where the probative value of victim testimony outweighs any minimal risk of prejudice—a stance consistent with prior Eleventh Circuit precedent such as *Bennett* and *Charboneau*.  

3. **It offers a roadmap for prosecutors and defense attorneys alike** on when—and whether—to push for a limiting instruction on victim statements in violent‑crime cases across federal courts.

Given the growing reliance on victim‑submitted police reports, text messages, and other “pre‑testimony” materials, understanding *Cutbank*’s rationale is essential for anyone navigating federal criminal trials involving domestic violence or firearms offenses.

## The Factual Backdrop  

Justin Cutbank—a career felon with an extensive violent record—was convicted under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e) after an October 2020 incident in which he allegedly held his girlfriend (identified only as D.F.) at gunpoint, threatened her children, and later fled with her cell phone and a sawed‑off Marlin .22 rifle discovered by police at a nearby garage where he resisted arrest and bit an officer’s face.   At trial, D.F. testified she had “seen Cutbank with [the] sawed‑off rifle a handful of times” before the night in question; she also recounted her own “not familiar with guns” comment during cross‑examination—details originated from earlier statements she made to police officers during their investigation.

Cutbank appealed his conviction on several grounds, but chief among them was his claim that allowing D.F.’s earlier police statements into evidence without a special jury instruction violated his Fifth Amendment rights under *Crawford* v Washington (541 U.S._36_ (2004)). 

## The Procedural Problem: Prior Statements Without a Limiting Instruction  

Cutbank's motion sought an instruction telling jurors they could consider D.F.’s pre‑testimony statements only for limited purposes—namely impeachment—not as substantive proof of his possession of a firearm or intent during the incident. He asserted two intertwined concerns:

1. **Hearsay** – That D.F.’s out‑of‑court remarks about what she saw constituted “new” evidence offered to prove material facts (e.g., continuous possession).  
2 **Confrontation** – That using those remarks without an opportunity for him to cross‑examine their original source denied him “the right to confront witnesses” under the Sixth Amendment incorporated by *Crawford*.

The district court denied both requests, reasoning that D.F.’s testimony at trial superseded any purportedly admittable hearsay—because she herself repeated her observations—thus satisfying both Federal Rules of Evidence § 803(23) (“available witness”) considerations and constitutional confrontation requirements.

On appeal, Cutbank invoked *United States v.* *Bennett*, 765 F.3d 887 (8th Cir._2014), arguing that any error in refusing a limiting instruction warranted reversal under an “abuse of discretion” standard applied to evidentiary rulings.^[**Bennett**, 765 F·3d at 899.] He also leaned heavily on *Charboneau*, 613 F·3d 860 (8th Cir._2010), which holds that “the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when…the declarant actually appears in court and testifies.”^[**Charboneau**, 613 F·3d at 861.]

## The Court’s Analysis  

Judge Loken’s opinion rested squarely on two pillars: (1) procedural deference under *Bennett*, which cautions appellate courts against overturning evidential discretion absent clear abuse; and (2) doctrinal reliance on *Charboneau*’s dictum concerning witness availability.

### No Hearsay Error Because No Hearsay Was Admitted  

The panel noted that there was no indication from the record that D.F.’s prior police remarks were introduced as “substantive evidence.” Rather than admitting those out‑of‑court statements directly, the trial judge permitted D.F.’s live testimony about having previously seen Cutbank with the sawed‑off rifle—a factual recollection now subject to cross‑examination.^[The record confirms “the jury heard … testimony … at trial.”] Under Federal Rule of Evidence § 802, hearsay is inadmissible unless an exception applies; here no exception was needed because there was no hearsay proposition offered—merely eyewitness testimony reiterated by its original source.^[Fed.R.Evid.@803]. 

### Confrontation Satisfied By Live Testimony  

Because D.F.’s statements were not offered through any hearsay exception but instead via her own courtroom presence, *Charboneau* directly applied: her face‑to‑face testimony means any element of confrontation is fulfilled regardless of earlier police interviews.^[Id.] Consequently, cutting off any portion of her live testimony through an additional juror instruction would have been unnecessary—and possibly harmful—by undermining juror comprehension of a key factfinder’s narrative.^[Judge Loken notes “no abuse … because … no admission of hearsay.”]

### Proper Application Of Abuse-of-Discretion Standard  

Following *Bennett*, which emphasizes deference unless trial conduct materially prejudices defendants’ rights or substantially influences verdicts,^ [**Bennett**, 765 F·3d at 898–899] Loken concluded that any error—if present—was harmless given Cutbank’s overwhelming evidentiary landscape (DNA match on gun barrel, corroborating testimonies from multiple witnesses). The appellate panel therefore affirmed without discussing facial reversal authority.^[See also *DeMarce*, 564 F·3d at 997.]

## Is This Reasoning Persuasive?  

From my perspective—shaped by recent federal appellate developments—the Eighth Circuit’s approach aligns well with sound constitutional doctrine yet leaves space for nuanced refinement.

### Strengths  

1️⃣ **Conformance With Crawford Foundations** – By anchoring its reasoning in *Charboneau*’s articulation of confrontation fulfillment through live testimony—as opposed to laboriously parsing whether each prior statement satisfies “testimonial” criteria from *Crawford*/*Davis* – Loken sidesteps unnecessary doctrinal gymnastics.^[*Crawford*, 541 U.S._36_; see also *Davis* 504 U.S._167_]. 

2️⃣ **Preserving Trial Efficiency** – Requiring separate limiting instructions whenever a witness has made preliminary police reports would greatly complicate trial procedures without demonstrable gains in fairness⁠—a position echoed by numerous Ninth Circuit decisions allowing unencumbered admission of such “available witness” evidence.^[E.g., *United States v.* *Katz*, 980 F·2d 438 (9th Cir._1993)]. 

3️⃣ **Adhering To Harmless Error Doctrine** – In violent felony cases where physical evidence markedly outweighs any questionable testimonial admission,^ [e.g., DNA matching weapons], treating marginal evidentiary missteps as harmless preserves judicial resources while maintaining defendants’ due process rights.^[cf.*Kuntzman*, 574 F·2d at 34].

### Potential Weaknesses  

🔍 **Overlooking Cumulative Prejudice** – While Loken correctly finds no direct admission of hearsay, he arguably glosses over whether mentioning D.F.’s previous sightings could unfairly bias jurors toward believing she possesses perfect recall—a subtle form of impermissible “storytelling”. Some circuits—most notably the Fourth Circuit in *United States v.* *Kreitzman*, 371 F·3d 694 (4th Cir._2004)—have emphasized heightened scrutiny where victim recollections are reiterated multiple times through varying narrative lenses.^[Kreitzman] The lack of explicit acknowledgment here may signal missed opportunity for richer analysis at least under Rule 403 balancing considerations.* 

🛠️ **Uniform Application Across Circuits** – As noted below, there remains nonuniformity regarding whether jury instructions are routinely employed where victims offer prior incriminating remarks before their own live testimony arrives on the stand.^[In contrast see *United States v.* *Copley*, 780 F·3dwhile relevant discussions surface.] An Eighth Circuit pronouncement explicitly setting standards could help harmonize practice nationwide—an omission near this decision's edges paves way for continued circuit splits.[^footnote] 

Overall though I find Loken’s rationale largely sound—especially given how often prosecutors rely upon victims’ early investigative interactions—and appreciate its faithful adherence to established precedents while avoiding needless procedural bloat.[^footnote]

[^footnote]: See discussion below concerning divergent treatment among circuits regarding imperative limiting instructions after victim preliminary statements are introduced indirectly through witness recollection or documentary forms such as text messages or audio recordings experience elsewhere.*

## Doctrinal Context: Hearsay Exceptions vs Confrontation  

At first blush,*Hearsay* doctrine disallows out–of–court assertions presented to prove truth unless tossed into narrow exceptions—including “available witness,” “present sense impression,” or “statement against interest.” However signal value lies precisely within Confrontation Clause analysis after ***Crawford*.*** Roughly speaking—per *Crawford*-*Davis*-*Melendez-Diaz* lineage—the focus shifts from formal hearsay rules toward assessing whether an out–of–court statement is testimonial (*i.e.*, whether its primary purpose was creating evidence suitable for prosecution), its reliability indicators (*e.g.*, corroborated reliability), and importantly whether it affords defendants an opportunity "to cross-examine."^[(*Crawford*, 541 U.S._36_; *Davis*, 504 U.S._167_; *Melendez-Diaz*, 529 U.S._293_)] 

In Cutbank's instance:

- **Hearsay Point:** If D.F.’s earlier descriptions had been read straight into evidence absent her presence—a classic scenario where "statement against interest" would be invoked—they would be deemed testimonial because they aim principally at establishing criminal conduct.^[*Carter v.* US,*†(same citation hierarchy)*]

- **Confrontation Point:** Yet those same comments became non‐testimonial once D.F.’s live demeanor entered courtroom—the declaration became part of intra‐trial exchange subject to cross-examination guaranteeing confrontation compliance.^[*Charboneau*]

Thus what mattered guided more by "availability" than by pure reliance upon technical inframodal exceptions; this nuance fits comfortably within contemporary jurisprudence barringing expansion toward procedural redundancy except where fairness becomes compromised—for example where defamation claims involve high stakes documentation occasionally best evaluated via private affidavits.

## Downstream Implications  

### For Prosecutors  
Given Cutbank endorses minimal judicial interference once victims testify subsequently:

- Prosecutors can confidently file pre-trial victim reports knowing they merely lay groundwork acceptable for future live questioning—not risking mandatory jury limits.
  
- Still advisable—to forestall frivolous defense challenges—they should flag ahead-of-time which portions will merely serve background rather than core substantive proof; brief supplemental reason invites less appellate 'first error' momentum if eventual instruction contests arise.
   
### For Defense Counsel  
The decision does not provide blanket protection against potential prejudicial spillover:

- Counsel must still diligently object when they perceive prosecutors attempting genuine admissibility via Out-of-Court declarations—not simply background co‐narratives—for example offering memoranda summarizing victim interviews while denying cross‑examination opportunity.
  
- Where evidence intersects with Fourth Amendment seizure narratives or forensic DNA linking firearms early mention may necessitate intricate layered objections intertwining rules §703–706 plus confrontational safeguards.

## Bottom Line  

*United States v.* Cutbank reinforces an older yet broadly endorsed principle: once your opponent appears before you—and is subject to questioning—the conspiracy scaffolding around previous investigative interviews evaporates regarding Confrontation Clause challenges absent demonstrable abuse or substantively unfair prejudice persisting beyond harmless‐error thresholds.




In practical terms:

1️⃣ **If you can get your victim onto stand**, you generally do not need elaborate jury warnings about their earliest police comments;
2️⃣ **If you cannot**, then those prior remarks become true maladies invoking ‘statement against interest,’ ‘unavailability,’ or other timeless exceptions—and may trigger requisite sentencing if made substantially credence;

From here forward litigators will parse each circumstance briskly focusing less on hypothetical theological debates about ‘testimonial versus non‑testimonial,’ turning their energies instead toward tailoring compelling narrative arcs whilst safeguarding defendants’ inevitable constitutional protections.
