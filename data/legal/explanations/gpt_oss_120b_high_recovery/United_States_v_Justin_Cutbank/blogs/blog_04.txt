Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Harmless Error Doctrine After Eighth Circuit Trial Rulings: Lessons from *United States v. Cutbank*

## Why *Cutbank* Matters  

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, 8th Cir. No. 24‑1225 (Oct. 7 2025), offers a concise illustration of how federal appellate courts wield the “harmless‑error” doctrine after a series of disputed trial‑court evidentiary rulings. Defendants routinely argue that improper admission or exclusion of testimony requires reversal, yet appellate courts rarely overturn convictions unless the error is both “non‑harmless” under *Chapman*/*DeMarce* and “substantially prejudicial” under *Keys*. In *Cutbank*, the panel walked through eight distinct objections—ranging from Rule 404(b) propensity challenges to improper impeachment—and concluded none rose to the level of reversible error. For practitioners who hope to rescue a conviction on such grounds, the opinion serves as both a cautionary tale about preservation and a roadmap for what it takes—materiality plus demonstrable prejudice—to persuade an appellate panel to intervene.

## The Court’s Harmless‑Error Framework  

### The *DeMarce* Standard  

The Ninth Band “harmless‑error” rule asks whether an appellate court can conclude beyond a reasonable doubt that “the evidence presented at trial would have been sufficient to support a conviction even without the alleged error.” *United States v. DeMarce*, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009). In practice this means looking at the strength of the untainted record: eyewitness identification, forensic linkage, confession or admission, and any other proof independent of the disputed material. If those sources alone satisfy *Chapman*’s “probability” test—that there is “a probability beyond a reasonable doubt” that the conviction would have occurred—the error is harmless even if it was flagrantly improper.

### The *Keys* Amplifier  

While *DeMarce* focuses on whether an individual error could have changed the verdict, *United States v. Keys* expands scrutiny to cumulative prejudice: “the cumulative effect of multiple trial‐court errors…must be shown to have substantially influenced…the verdict.” 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019). A defendant thus bears a double burden—first to demonstrate each illegal admission or exclusion was material, second to show that together they tipped the scales toward guilt.

### Applying Those Tests in *Cutbank*  

1️⃣ **Propensity Evidence Under Rule 404(b).** Cutbank contended that D.F.’s testimony about seeing him with the sawed‑off rifle prior to the incident was inadmissible character evidence. The district court treated it as intrinsic—relevant to ownership—and allowed it under *Battle*, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014). On appeal there was no indication that any alternative source could have proved possession without this testimony; DNA on the recovered rifle already linked Cutbank directly to the weapon. Thus under *DeMarce* any correct inference about possession could be drawn without D.F.’s propensity narrative—rendering the ruling harmless.

2️⃣ **Confrontation Concerns About D.F.’s Police Statements.** Cutbank argued that admitting D.F.’s statements made to police before she testified violated her Sixth Amendment rights absent a limiting instruction. The court rejected because D.F.’s statements were never offered as substantive hearsay—the officer testimony alone rendered them unnecessary—and because she later testified in person, satisfying *Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010). Even assuming an informal breach occurred, *DeMarce* requires proof that excluding those statements would have produced doubt about guilt—a proposition unsupported given physical evidence and multiple eyewitness accounts.

3️⃣ **Impeachment Through Prior Inconsistent Statements (“Sister Was Killed”).** The district judge allowed D.F.’s mention of her sister’s death despite Cutbank’s objection under Rule 403’s prejudice/confusion inquiry. The trial record shows this side note was merely explanatory background after cross‑examination probed D.F.’s reliability regarding firearms; it did not introduce new factual claims about Cutbank’s conduct. A review under *DeMarce* finds no risk that its removal would have altered jurors’ view of his culpability—the core incriminating facts remain untouched—so again harmlessness follows automatically.

4️⃣ **Authentication Requirements for Police Reports Used in Impeachment Of C.F.’s Testimony**  
Here the district court enforced proper authentication standards consistent with *Cumbie*, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir.​ 2022). Even if Cutter had succeeded in admitting excerpts from an unauthenticated report—a possible violation—the report would only serve impeachment purposes rather than substantive proof of possession or violence; its absence scarcely diminishes proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

Across all eight objections—notably none were “preserved” in traditional form because Cutbank offered little authority or detailed argument—the panel’s reliance on *DeMarce*’s “overwhelming evidence” language made clear why no single mistake could be truly determinative.



## A Critical Take on Deference  

The Eighth Circuit’s opinion underscores two enduring themes:

1️⃣ **Preservation Is Paramount** – Most of Cutbank’s complaints were barely articulated (“no cases,” “almost no analysis”), rendering them technically unpreserved under Rule 52(a). Appellate courts traditionally give little weight where counsel fails to signal an objection clearly enough for an immediate record‑building opportunity—an omission almost as fatal as any merits deficiency.
   
2️⃣ **The “Harmless Beyond Reasonable Doubt” Threshold Remains High** – By anchoring its analysis in concrete fact‑finding rather than abstract legal theory (“the government’s evidence…was substantial”), the panel adhered closely to Supreme Court precedent requiring proof **that without the questioned material there still exists sufficient proof** (*Chapman* 475 U.S.—at 469). Some commentators argue this high bar leaves little room for addressing subtle cumulative biases that may flow from multiple minor infractions—a concern especially relevant when defendants are socio‑economically disadvantaged and lack seasoned counsel capable of raising nuanced objections at trial.

Nevertheless, I find little reason to fault Judge Loken for staying within standard bounds; overturning convictions merely because an attorney failed to cite binding precedent would erode finality without benefiting justice when physical DNA and witness testimony already provide irrefutable proof.


## Doctrinal Ripple Effects  

### Reinforcing Existing Evidentiary Presumptions  

Because *Cutbank* reaffirmed several older precedents—*Battle* for intrinsic firearm‑possession relevance, *Bennett* for discretionary limiting instructions, *Cumbie* for authentication—the decision adds another citation line supporting routine admission of prior possession evidence in §922(g)(1) prosecutions across federal courts in the Midwest and beyond.


### Guiding Future Harmless‑Error Motions  

Defendants hoping for relief on evidentiary grounds now have an explicit reminder:

- **Show Independent Sufficiency:** Identify specific innocuous elements—DNA matches, seized firearm serial numbers modified—but overlooked by defense counsel.
  
- **Quantify Cumulative Prejudice:** Merely cataloguing several minor errors will no longer suffice; plaintiffs must articulate how those together created “substantial prejudice,” ideally by demonstrating an alternative factual scenario peaked toward acquittal (*Keys*, supra).

Lawyers may adapt by obtaining jury verdict forms or pre‑sentencing statements indicating reliance upon disputed testimony—a strategy increasingly common after these decisions.


### Sentencing Enhancements Remain Secure  

The panel also rejected Cutbank’s argument that applying §2K2·1(b)(6)(B)—the four‑level enhancement for firearm possession during another felony—clashed with §3553(a)’s proportionality principle because it nudged his sentence upward within range (§924(e)). By emphasizing correct calculation per United States v.Dunn and noting no misuse of discretionary factors per Gall and Rosales‑Mireles, the ruling signals continued deference toward guideline worksheets so long as districts stay within advisory ranges and address §3553(a) factors qualitatively rather than mechanically enumerating them.


## Looking Ahead  

For criminal litigators operating in the Eighth Circuit—and arguably nation‑wide—the Takeaways are clear:

1️⃣ **Meticulous Preservation Wins:** A flashpoint argument uttered without citation or wavefoot can become dead wood at appeal.
   
2️⃣ **Harmless Error Requires More Than ‘Harmless’:** Courts will ask whether you could prove guilt without every contested piece—not just whether each piece alone is extremly prejudicial.
   
3️⃣ **Cumulative Prejudice Must Be Substantively Demonstrated:** Mere accumulation does not equal causation; you must connect each fragment into an alternative narrative persuasive enough to tip reasonable doubt.
   
4️⃣ **Guideline Enhancements Remain Rugged:** As long as sentencing calculators follow statutory commentary exactly—as here—they are unlikely avenues for reduction once advisory ranges are reached.


In short, *United States v.* Cutbank reiterates once again what practitioners have learned since *Chapman*: federal appeals courts will look past trial‐court slip‑ups when overwhelming proof exists elsewhere on file—and they will demand concrete quantification when defendants claim otherwise.
