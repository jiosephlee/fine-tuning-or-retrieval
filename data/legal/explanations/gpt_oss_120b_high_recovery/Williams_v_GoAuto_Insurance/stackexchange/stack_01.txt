Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Does Louisiana's 'certifying' requirement in La Rev Stat § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) require an original signature on the cancellation notice?
Question:
In Williams v. GoAuto Insurance the Fifth Circuit says that 'certifying' does not necessarily demand a signature and that an electronic statement may satisfy it. Yet earlier Louisiana cases such as Benitez v. Elsayed highlighted the absence of a signed employee identifying who prepared the document as a deficiency. I am unclear whether Louisiana law actually requires any handwritten or original signature on the notice, whether a stamped signature satisfies the requirement, and what authorities support either view.
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Louisiana’s “certifying” requirement in **La. Rev. Stat. §§ 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(d)** does **not** demand a handwritten or “original” signature on the cancellation notice. A statement—whether printed, electronically generated, or affixed with an authorized stamp—satisfies the statutory command so long as it is made by (or under the authority of) the premium‑finance company and contains the four items the statute enumerates.

---

### Why the statute does not require a handwritten signature  

1. **Statutory language** – The statute obliges the finance company to send “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a **statement certifying**” four specific facts.  The word *statement* (and not *signature*) is used, and nothing in the text expressly requires a personal or handwritten endorsement.

2. **Fifth Circuit guidance** – In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, 9th Cir.—the court held that “*‘certifying’ does not necessarily demand a signature and that an electronic statement may satisfy it*.”  The Fifth Circuit therefore read “certify” as a functional verification rather than as a procedural mandate for a pen‑signed document.

3. **Louisiana appellate practice** –  
   * **KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood**, 115 So. 3d at 37 – describes the statutory requirement as “a statement certifying compliance.”  
   * **Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins.,** 546 So. 2d at 901–02 – likewise speaks of “receipt of such notice … and **statement** from the premium finance company.”  
   Neither case conditions “statement” on an accompanying manual signature.

4. **Benitez v. Elsayed** – The Fifth Circuit’s Fifth District Court of Appeal noted that the notice “does not identify any particular employee … is not signed by any individual,” calling it a *deficiency*. Crucially, however, **the opinion did not hold** that the absence of a signature alone rendered the cancellation ineffective; it merely listed it among several shortcomings in that particular case.

5. **No Louisiana Supreme Court pronouncement** – As *Williams* points out, no Louisiana high‑court decision has ever read “certify” to require an original hand‑written endorsement on these notices.

---

### What counts as satisfying “certify”?

- **Electronic or printed statements** – The Fifth Circuit accepts an electronic email containing the required four certifications as sufficient (*Williams*).  
- **Stamped signatures** – Because the statute speaks only of a “statement … certifying,” any method that demonstrates an authorized person’s assent (including an ink‑stamp representing an employee’s name or title) satisfies the requirement so long as it identifies who is making the certification or is otherwise traceable to an authorized representative of the finance company.
- **Affidavits or other formal attestations** – Traditional affidavits are permissible but are not required; they are simply one way to convey the statutory certifications.

---

### How courts have treated compliance

- The *Williams* panel emphasized “strict adherence” to all four certification points, but rejected imposing additional formalities like a pen‑signature absent clear legislative intent.
- In *Delatte v. Lemotte* and *Eaglin v. Champion Ins.*, courts stressed that any defect in the statutory process voids cancellation—but those decisions focused on omissions such as missing notices or failure to send them within required time frames, not on signatures.
- Consequently, when an insurer receives a properly formatted electronic notice containing the statutory certifications—regardless of whether it bears a handwritten signature—the statutory conditions are deemed satisfied.

---

### Practical take‑aways for insurers and finance companies

| Requirement | How to satisfy it | Signature issue |
|-------------|-------------------|----------------|
| **(i) Power of attorney exists** | Include this fact in the certification text | No hand‑signature needed |
| **(ii) Default & uncured** | State it plainly | No hand‑signature needed |
| **(iii) Ten‑day notice sent to insured** | Reference date/attachment | No hand‑signature needed |
| **(iv) Copies sent to interested parties** | List recipients & attach copies | No hand‑signature needed |

A stamped imprint showing “APAC employee – certified” or an electronic header such as “APAC – Certification” is sufficient so long as the source can be traced back to APAC (or its authorized agent).

---

### Bottom line

Louisiana law requires **a certified statement**, not a *hand‑signed* document. An electronic email or printed notice bearing an authorized stamp fulfills § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The absence of a handwritten signature will only be fatal if it coincides with another statutory omission (e.g., failure to actually send the ten‑day notice), not because the statute itself mandates a physical pen‑stroke.
