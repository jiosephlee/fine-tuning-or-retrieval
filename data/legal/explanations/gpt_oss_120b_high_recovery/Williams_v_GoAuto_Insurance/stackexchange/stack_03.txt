Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### How is 'private carrier' defined for delivering insurance-premium-finance cancellation notices under Louisiana law?
Question:
In Williams v. GoAuto the district court treated an APAC employee delivering printed notices as a 'private carrier,' borrowing analogies from Huntley v Bayer MaterialScience about railroads. The Fifth Circuit did not resolve this definition because it accepted email sufficiency instead. What entities qualify as private carriers under La Rev Stat § 9:3550(G)? Specifically, does email transmission count as delivery by a private carrier, or must there be an actual person physically transporting documents?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Under La Rev Stat § 9:3550(G) “private carrier” means any person or business that **physically transports** the paper notice for a fee – essentially a commercial messenger or courier (including an employee who is acting as such a messenger).  Electronic‑mail transmission is **not** considered delivery by a private carrier because the statute lists “via electronic mail” as a separate, third method of compliance.  

The Fifth Circuit has not given a definitive definition of “private carrier” in this context; it simply held that *email* satisfies the statutory requirement and therefore declined to address whether an APAC employee hauling paper qualifies.  The district court’s broader “railroad” analogy (see *Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience*, 452 F.App’x 453 (5th Cir. 2011)) is persuasive but not binding precedent on this point.

---

### 1. What the statute says

La Rev Stat § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) provides three mutually exclusive ways an insurance‑premium finance company may “request cancellation”:

> “…by **sending** to the insurer, **by depositing in the mail** or **with a private carrier**, **or via electronic mail** …”

The use of “or” signals that **electronic mail** is its own category distinct from “depositing in the mail” (the U.S. Postal Service) and from “with a private carrier.”  Consequently, *email* cannot be subsumed under “private carrier” without contradicting the plain language of the statute.

### 2. How “private carrier” has been interpreted

There is **no Louisiana Supreme Court decision** defining “private carrier” in § 9:3550(G).  The district court in *Williams v. GoAuto* therefore looked to general common‑law concepts and to Fifth Circuit precedent on carriers:

* *Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience* described a railroad that contracts to move freight for others as a **private carrier** because it offers transportation services for compensation and retains control over the means of conveyance.

Applying that logic, *Williams*’s district court concluded that an APAP employee who physically carried printed cancellation notices from APAC’s office to GoAuto’s filing cabinet fit within the broad notion of someone providing **transportation for remuneration**—i.e., a private messenger service—even though Louisiana does not have case law expressly on point.

The Fifth Circuit noted this reasoning but **did not adopt it**, reasoning instead that *email* alone satisfied the statutory requirement:

> “Because we find the e‑mail satisfies the statute, we will not consider the validity of [the] definition.”  
> —*Williams v. GoAuto*, No. 24‑30646, slip op. at 15 (5th Cir. Oct. 3 2025).

Thus, while *Williams*’s district court used an expansive view of “private carrier,” this view remains **unsettled** at appellate level.

### 3. Practical implications

1. **Physical movement required** – To rely on the “private‑carrier” hook, your notice must be placed in the hands of someone who actually moves it from point A to point B (e.g., UPS/FedEx courier, corporate messenger, contract delivery person).  

2. **Electronic transmission excluded** – Because “via electronic mail” is listed separately, sending the notice by e‑mail does **not** invoke the “private‑carrier” provision; it simply satisfies one of the three methods enumerated by statute.

3. **Who can qualify?** – Any entity that:
   - transports tangible documents,
   - does so for compensation or pursuant to an employment arrangement,
   - retains control over its means of transport,
   
   can be treated as a “private carrier.” This includes traditional couriers and internal messengers who act like couriers (the APAC employee scenario). It does **not** include systems that merely forward an electronic file.

4. **Why matters** – If you choose to satisfy § 9:3550(G) via “private carrier,” you must have evidence of physical receipt/taking possession by your insurer (or its agent).  If you rely on e‑mail instead—as GoAuto did—the insurer’s computer system receiving the message constitutes receipt under modern interpretation, and no separate “carrier” analysis is needed.

### 4. Takeaway for your fact pattern

- **Email = acceptable method**, independent of any “carrier” analysis.
- If you prefer—or are required—to use the “private‑carrier” route, ensure someone actually *carries* paper copies across distance; merely stamping documents after an e‑mail trigger does not satisfy that route.
- Until Louisiana Supreme Court guidance appears, courts will likely follow *Williams*’s pragmatic approach: treat e‑mail as its own statutory pathway and reserve “private carrier” for tangible delivery services akin to couriers or messengers—an interpretation supported by general common‑law definitions and analogized by *Huntley* but not definitively settled by appellate precedent.

- *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, No. 24‑30646, slip op. at 13–16 (5th Cir. Oct. 3 2025) – discussion of e‑mail sufficiency and district court’s “private carriage” reasoning.

- Black’s Law Dictionary 12th ed., definitions of “receipt” & “receive,” cited persuasively by *Williams* when evaluating possession requirements.

--- 

In sum: under current Louisiana law as applied in *Williams*, email satisfies its own statutory route; only entities that physically move paper notices can qualify as “private carriers,” and no authority treats electronic transmission itself as such a carrier.
