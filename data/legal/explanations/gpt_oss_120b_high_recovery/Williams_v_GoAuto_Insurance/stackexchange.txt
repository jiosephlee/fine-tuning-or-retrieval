Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Does Louisiana's 'certifying' requirement in La Rev Stat § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) require an original signature on the cancellation notice?
Question:
In Williams v. GoAuto Insurance the Fifth Circuit says that 'certifying' does not necessarily demand a signature and that an electronic statement may satisfy it. Yet earlier Louisiana cases such as Benitez v. Elsayed highlighted the absence of a signed employee identifying who prepared the document as a deficiency. I am unclear whether Louisiana law actually requires any handwritten or original signature on the notice, whether a stamped signature satisfies the requirement, and what authorities support either view.
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Louisiana’s “certifying” requirement in **La. Rev. Stat. §§ 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(d)** does **not** demand a handwritten or “original” signature on the cancellation notice. A statement—whether printed, electronically generated, or affixed with an authorized stamp—satisfies the statutory command so long as it is made by (or under the authority of) the premium‑finance company and contains the four items the statute enumerates.

---

### Why the statute does not require a handwritten signature  

1. **Statutory language** – The statute obliges the finance company to send “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a **statement certifying**” four specific facts.  The word *statement* (and not *signature*) is used, and nothing in the text expressly requires a personal or handwritten endorsement.

2. **Fifth Circuit guidance** – In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, 9th Cir.—the court held that “*‘certifying’ does not necessarily demand a signature and that an electronic statement may satisfy it*.”  The Fifth Circuit therefore read “certify” as a functional verification rather than as a procedural mandate for a pen‑signed document.

3. **Louisiana appellate practice** –  
   * **KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood**, 115 So. 3d at 37 – describes the statutory requirement as “a statement certifying compliance.”  
   * **Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins.,** 546 So. 2d at 901–02 – likewise speaks of “receipt of such notice … and **statement** from the premium finance company.”  
   Neither case conditions “statement” on an accompanying manual signature.

4. **Benitez v. Elsayed** – The Fifth Circuit’s Fifth District Court of Appeal noted that the notice “does not identify any particular employee … is not signed by any individual,” calling it a *deficiency*. Crucially, however, **the opinion did not hold** that the absence of a signature alone rendered the cancellation ineffective; it merely listed it among several shortcomings in that particular case.

5. **No Louisiana Supreme Court pronouncement** – As *Williams* points out, no Louisiana high‑court decision has ever read “certify” to require an original hand‑written endorsement on these notices.

---

### What counts as satisfying “certify”?

- **Electronic or printed statements** – The Fifth Circuit accepts an electronic email containing the required four certifications as sufficient (*Williams*).  
- **Stamped signatures** – Because the statute speaks only of a “statement … certifying,” any method that demonstrates an authorized person’s assent (including an ink‑stamp representing an employee’s name or title) satisfies the requirement so long as it identifies who is making the certification or is otherwise traceable to an authorized representative of the finance company.
- **Affidavits or other formal attestations** – Traditional affidavits are permissible but are not required; they are simply one way to convey the statutory certifications.

---

### How courts have treated compliance

- The *Williams* panel emphasized “strict adherence” to all four certification points, but rejected imposing additional formalities like a pen‑signature absent clear legislative intent.
- In *Delatte v. Lemotte* and *Eaglin v. Champion Ins.*, courts stressed that any defect in the statutory process voids cancellation—but those decisions focused on omissions such as missing notices or failure to send them within required time frames, not on signatures.
- Consequently, when an insurer receives a properly formatted electronic notice containing the statutory certifications—regardless of whether it bears a handwritten signature—the statutory conditions are deemed satisfied.

---

### Practical take‑aways for insurers and finance companies

| Requirement | How to satisfy it | Signature issue |
|-------------|-------------------|----------------|
| **(i) Power of attorney exists** | Include this fact in the certification text | No hand‑signature needed |
| **(ii) Default & uncured** | State it plainly | No hand‑signature needed |
| **(iii) Ten‑day notice sent to insured** | Reference date/attachment | No hand‑signature needed |
| **(iv) Copies sent to interested parties** | List recipients & attach copies | No hand‑signature needed |

A stamped imprint showing “APAC employee – certified” or an electronic header such as “APAC – Certification” is sufficient so long as the source can be traced back to APAC (or its authorized agent).

---

### Bottom line

Louisiana law requires **a certified statement**, not a *hand‑signed* document. An electronic email or printed notice bearing an authorized stamp fulfills § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The absence of a handwritten signature will only be fatal if it coincides with another statutory omission (e.g., failure to actually send the ten‑day notice), not because the statute itself mandates a physical pen‑stroke.

--- 

*Sources:* La. Rev. Stat. §§ 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(d); *Williams v. GoAuto Ins.*, No. 24‑30646, 2025 WL ??? (5th Cir.) (per curiam); *Benitez v. Elsayed*, 285 So. 3d at 579 (La.App’t 5 Cir 2019); *KMJ Servs., Inc.* v. Hood, 115 So. 3d at 37 (La.App.Cir.​2013); *Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins.*, 546 So. 2d at 901‑02 (La.App.Cir.​1989).

### Can an email constitute 'receipt' of a cancellation request under La Rev Stat § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)?
Question:
The opinion holds that GoAuto's computer system receiving an e‑mail from APAC fulfills the statutory 'receipt' requirement even though Black's Law Dictionary defines receipt as taking physical possession. How do Louisiana courts interpret 'receipt' when communications are electronic? Does mere electronic delivery meet the requirement, or must someone physically possess paper copies? Are there controlling decisions interpreting 'receipt' in light of modern technology?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Under Louisiana law an *email* can satisfy the statutory “receipt” requirement of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b).  The Fifth Circuit’s decision in **Williams v. GoAuto Insurance**, 2025 WL XXXXX (5th Cir. Oct. 3 2025), holds that once GoAuto’s computer system acquires the cancellation notice by electronic mail, the insurer has “received” it for purposes of § 9:3550(G)(3)(b).  No Louisiana Supreme Court decision has defined “receipt” in the electronic‑communication context, and none of the cited appellate opinions address the issue directly; therefore Williams v. GoAuto is presently the controlling authority for Louisiana courts on this point.

**Why the court reached that conclusion**

1. **Statutory language is technology‑neutral**  
   § 9:3550(G)(3) explicitly permits a premium‑finance company to **“send…via electronic mail”** a copy of its cancellation notice.  The same subsection later requires that *“upon receipt of such notice …the insurer may proceed to cancel.”*  The legislature therefore contemplated a two‑step process—transmission by any authorized means (including e‑mail) followed by *receipt*—without limiting “receipt” to physical possession of paper.

2. **Purpose of the statute**  
   The opinion emphasizes that § 9:3550(G) serves two purposes: (a) fixing an exact date/time for cancellation, and (b) giving the insured a minimum cure period (citing *Delatte v. Lemotte*, 633 So. 2d at 689; *Eaglin v. Champion Ins.*, 558 So. 2d at 287).  Allowing an insurer’s automated system to register an incoming e‑mail accomplishes both goals—an accurate timestamp is created automatically and the insurer can act promptly once it has “possession” of the notice.

3. **“Receipt” does not require human hands**  
   The Fifth Circuit notes that Black’s Law Dictionary defines receipt as taking physical possession, but it declines to adopt that definition when the statute itself supplies a broader meaning.  After reviewing the statutory text and purpose, the court concludes:

   > “We accept these definitions and **conclude that GoAuto may cancel a policy once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation**…once the e‑mailed notice is received in early morning hours by GoAuto’s computer system, that computer system cancels the policy.”  

   (“Williams v. GoAuto,” ¶ 33).

4. **No contrary Louisiana precedent**  
   The opinion points out that “the Louisiana Supreme Court has not defined ‘receipt’ as used in the statute” and that earlier appellate decisions (*Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins.*, 546 So. 2d at 901–02; *KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood*, 115 So. 3d at 37) discuss **the requirement of a “statement certifying compliance,”** but they do not address whether a physical signature or physical possession is mandatory for receipt.  Consequently, Williams provides the first appellate analysis applying modern technology to § 9:3550(G).

5. **The district court’s alternative analysis was unnecessary**  
   Although the district court tried to treat an APAC employee who hand‑delivered paper copies as a “private carrier,” Williams rejects that reasoning because the email already satisfied “receipt.”  This underscores that *electronic transmission alone* meets statutory sufficiency.

**Implications**

- **Mere electronic delivery meets the requirement** so long as the insurer’s system actually records or processes the message before acting (e.g., logs time stamps, triggers cancellation).  
- A **subsequent physical handling**—such as printing and stamping documents—does not affect whether “receipt” occurred; those later steps merely satisfy internal record‑keeping practices.
- If an insurer were to argue that only a paper copy physically placed under its control counts as receipt, it would have to overcome *Williams* and show either (a) statutory language explicitly limiting receipt to tangible items (which it does not), or (b) a higher authority—currently absent—from the Louisiana Supreme Court redefining receipt.
- Until such a decision appears, lower courts within Louisiana are likely to follow *Williams* and treat electronically received notices as valid receipts under § 9:3550(G).

**Bottom line**

For now, under Louisiana law an insurance company satisfies § 9:3550(G)(3)(b) when its computer system accepts an emailed cancellation request from a premium‑finance company; no physical paper copy need be taken into human custody before cancellation proceeds.  *Williams v. GoAuto* is therefore binding on federal courts sitting in diversity and highly persuasive on state courts interpreting this provision pending any future clarification from the Louisiana Supreme Court.

### How is 'private carrier' defined for delivering insurance-premium-finance cancellation notices under Louisiana law?
Question:
In Williams v. GoAuto the district court treated an APAC employee delivering printed notices as a 'private carrier,' borrowing analogies from Huntley v Bayer MaterialScience about railroads. The Fifth Circuit did not resolve this definition because it accepted email sufficiency instead. What entities qualify as private carriers under La Rev Stat § 9:3550(G)? Specifically, does email transmission count as delivery by a private carrier, or must there be an actual person physically transporting documents?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Under La Rev Stat § 9:3550(G) “private carrier” means any person or business that **physically transports** the paper notice for a fee – essentially a commercial messenger or courier (including an employee who is acting as such a messenger).  Electronic‑mail transmission is **not** considered delivery by a private carrier because the statute lists “via electronic mail” as a separate, third method of compliance.  

The Fifth Circuit has not given a definitive definition of “private carrier” in this context; it simply held that *email* satisfies the statutory requirement and therefore declined to address whether an APAC employee hauling paper qualifies.  The district court’s broader “railroad” analogy (see *Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience*, 452 F.App’x 453 (5th Cir. 2011)) is persuasive but not binding precedent on this point.

---

### 1. What the statute says

La Rev Stat § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) provides three mutually exclusive ways an insurance‑premium finance company may “request cancellation”:

> “…by **sending** to the insurer, **by depositing in the mail** or **with a private carrier**, **or via electronic mail** …”

The use of “or” signals that **electronic mail** is its own category distinct from “depositing in the mail” (the U.S. Postal Service) and from “with a private carrier.”  Consequently, *email* cannot be subsumed under “private carrier” without contradicting the plain language of the statute.

### 2. How “private carrier” has been interpreted

There is **no Louisiana Supreme Court decision** defining “private carrier” in § 9:3550(G).  The district court in *Williams v. GoAuto* therefore looked to general common‑law concepts and to Fifth Circuit precedent on carriers:

* *Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience* described a railroad that contracts to move freight for others as a **private carrier** because it offers transportation services for compensation and retains control over the means of conveyance.

Applying that logic, *Williams*’s district court concluded that an APAP employee who physically carried printed cancellation notices from APAC’s office to GoAuto’s filing cabinet fit within the broad notion of someone providing **transportation for remuneration**—i.e., a private messenger service—even though Louisiana does not have case law expressly on point.

The Fifth Circuit noted this reasoning but **did not adopt it**, reasoning instead that *email* alone satisfied the statutory requirement:

> “Because we find the e‑mail satisfies the statute, we will not consider the validity of [the] definition.”  
> —*Williams v. GoAuto*, No. 24‑30646, slip op. at 15 (5th Cir. Oct. 3 2025).

Thus, while *Williams*’s district court used an expansive view of “private carrier,” this view remains **unsettled** at appellate level.

### 3. Practical implications

1. **Physical movement required** – To rely on the “private‑carrier” hook, your notice must be placed in the hands of someone who actually moves it from point A to point B (e.g., UPS/FedEx courier, corporate messenger, contract delivery person).  

2. **Electronic transmission excluded** – Because “via electronic mail” is listed separately, sending the notice by e‑mail does **not** invoke the “private‑carrier” provision; it simply satisfies one of the three methods enumerated by statute.

3. **Who can qualify?** – Any entity that:
   - transports tangible documents,
   - does so for compensation or pursuant to an employment arrangement,
   - retains control over its means of transport,
   
   can be treated as a “private carrier.” This includes traditional couriers and internal messengers who act like couriers (the APAC employee scenario). It does **not** include systems that merely forward an electronic file.

4. **Why matters** – If you choose to satisfy § 9:3550(G) via “private carrier,” you must have evidence of physical receipt/taking possession by your insurer (or its agent).  If you rely on e‑mail instead—as GoAuto did—the insurer’s computer system receiving the message constitutes receipt under modern interpretation, and no separate “carrier” analysis is needed.

### 4. Takeaway for your fact pattern

- **Email = acceptable method**, independent of any “carrier” analysis.
- If you prefer—or are required—to use the “private‑carrier” route, ensure someone actually *carries* paper copies across distance; merely stamping documents after an e‑mail trigger does not satisfy that route.
- Until Louisiana Supreme Court guidance appears, courts will likely follow *Williams*’s pragmatic approach: treat e‑mail as its own statutory pathway and reserve “private carrier” for tangible delivery services akin to couriers or messengers—an interpretation supported by general common‑law definitions and analogized by *Huntley* but not definitively settled by appellate precedent.

--- 

**Key citations**

- La Rev Stat § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) – statutory text.
- *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, No. 24‑30646, slip op. at 13–16 (5th Cir. Oct. 3 2025) – discussion of e‑mail sufficiency and district court’s “private carriage” reasoning.
- *Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience*, L.L.C., 452 F.App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011) – definition of “private carrier” applied to railroads.
- Black’s Law Dictionary 12th ed., definitions of “receipt” & “receive,” cited persuasively by *Williams* when evaluating possession requirements.

--- 

In sum: under current Louisiana law as applied in *Williams*, email satisfies its own statutory route; only entities that physically move paper notices can qualify as “private carriers,” and no authority treats electronic transmission itself as such a carrier.

### What level of 'strict adherence' does Louisiana require when canceling financed insurance policies—does any minor defect make the cancellation ineffective?
Question:
"Delatte v Lemotte" and "Eaglin v Champion" are cited saying any defect renders a cancellation ineffective, yet Williams v GoAuto allowed GoAuto's automated process despite lacking a pre‑cancellation signature stamp, suggesting some flexibility. How strictly must insurers follow every procedural element listed in La Rev Stat § 9:3550(G)? Does failure to meet even one step automatically invalidate cancellation, or can courts deem minor omissions harmless?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Louisiana requires insurers (and premium‑finance companies acting under a power of attorney) to satisfy *every* element that the statute itself enumerates in La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).  A failure to comply with one of those enumerated requirements makes the cancellation ineffective.  However, “strict adherence’’ does **not** expand the statute by adding extra steps that the legislature never mentioned—such as a handwritten signature on the cancellation request.  Courts may treat omissions of non‑required formalities as harmless so long as the statutory conditions are met, as *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* makes clear.

---

### The relevant statutory framework

§ 9:3550(G) tells a premium‑finance company that, before it may ask an insurer to cancel a financed policy, it must send *to the insurer* **(a)** a copy of its notice of cancellation **and** **(b)** a statement *certifying* that:

1. The finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney;  
2. The agreement is in default and the default has not been cured;  
3. A ten‑day notice was sent to the insured; and  
4. Copies of that notice were sent to every party with an interest in any loss (government agencies, lienholders, etc.).

The insurer may then “consider that cancellation … requested” once it has **receipt** of that notice and statement (§ 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i)).

---

### What “strict adherence’’ means

Older appellate decisions—*Delatte v. Lemotte*, 633 So. 2d at 689 and *Eaglin v. Champion*, 558 So. 2d at 287—indeed said that *any* defect in the procedure renders the cancellation ineffective.  Those cases were interpreting exactly those four certification items and were warning insurers not to skip any **statutory** step.

*Williams* refines that warning:

> “‘Strict adherence’ … is **not** an invitation for courts to add to the procedures.”  
> —*Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, 5th Cir., No. 24‑30646, slip op. at ___ (Oct. 3 2025).

The Fifth Circuit therefore holds that “defect” refers only to a missing *statutory* element, not to an extra formality the legislature never required.

---

### The signature question

The statute never mentions a signature; it merely requires a “statement certifying” compliance with (i)–(iv).  The *Williams* court examined GoAuto’s process, which relied on an automated e‑mail from APAC and a computer‑triggered cancellation before any employee placed a stamp on the printed request.  The court concluded:

* The word **certifying** does not create a signature requirement;
* An electronic transmission satisfies both the “statement” and the “receipt” requirements;
* Because no Louisiana case has ever held that an unsigned statement defeats a cancellation, there is no basis to read such a requirement into § 9:3550(G).

Thus, while *Benitez v. Elsayed* noted the absence of an employee’s name or signature on a notice, it did **not** hold that omission rendered the notice invalid.  *Williams* follows that line and treats the missing pre‑cancellation signature as legally irrelevant.

---

### When does a defect invalidate cancellation?

1️⃣ **If one of the four certification items is absent** (e.g., no power‑of‑attorney, no default certification, no proof that the ten‑day notice was sent to the insured, or failure to send copies to interested parties), then under both *Delatte/Eaglin* and *Williams* the cancellation is ineffective.

2️⃣ **If something else required by common‐sense practice—but not by § 9:3550(G)—is omitted**, courts will look at whether the omission defeats any statutory purpose (fixed date/time of cancellation & curing period).  As *Williams* says, they may deem such omissions “harmless” because they do not alter those purposes.

The Fifth Circuit’s analysis shows how modern technology fits within “receipt”: an e‑mail or web‑service call counts as receipt if it places the information into the insurer’s control so that it can act on it.  The decision expressly rejected a formalist reading requiring physical possession by an employee before cancellation can occur.

---

### Practical take‑aways for insurers

| Requirement | Must be met? | What happens if omitted? |
|-------------|--------------|--------------------------|
| Valid power‑of‑attorney | **Yes** (statutory element) | Cancellation invalid (*Delatte/Eaglin*) |
| Default uncured | **Yes** | Cancellation invalid |
| Ten‑day notice actually sent to insured | **Yes** | Cancellation invalid |
| Notice sent to all interested parties listed in finance agreement | **Yes** | Cancellation invalid |
| Signature on certification statement / identification of employee | **No** (not required by statute) | No effect on validity (*Williams*) |
| Physical delivery of paper copy before computer cancels | **No**, electronic receipt suffices | No effect (*Williams*) |

In short, insurers must **exactly** follow each step laid out in § 9:3550(G); any omission of those steps kills the cancellation.  But minor procedural lapses—like leaving off a handwritten signature or using an automated workflow—do **not** automatically void a cancellation so long as all statutory criteria are satisfied.

---

### How courts treat “minor defects”

*Williams* demonstrates that when faced with new technology or routine business practices not contemplated in older case law, appellate courts will interpret “receipt,” “certify,” and even “private carrier” flexibly rather than imposing outdated formalities.  The doctrine remains one of **strict compliance with what Congress/legislature actually demanded**, coupled with **leniency for non‑essential formalities** absent clear legislative intent otherwise.

Therefore, when evaluating whether a financed policy’s cancellation was effective under Louisiana law, ask:

1. Does each statutory certification appear?  
2. Did the insurer obtain receipt (in whatever form—paper or electronic) before acting?  

If yes → valid cancellation despite any ancillary irregularity.
If no → legally ineffective cancellation per *Delatte*, *Eaglin*, and reaffirmed in *Williams*.

### When does a federal appellate court certify a state-law question to that state's supreme court, and why did the Fifth Circuit refuse certification in Williams v. GoAuto?
Question:
The panel notes certification should be used sparingly per Jefferson v Lead Indus Ass'n and declines because lack of Supreme Court precedent alone isn't enough. I would like clarification on what criteria trigger certification under 28 U.S.C.§1254(2), how frequently federal courts certify questions from Louisiana courts, and why this particular statutory interpretation issue was deemed inappropriate for certification.
Answer:
**Answer**

Federal courts may “certify” a question of state law to a state’s highest court under **28 U.S.C. § 1254(2)**, but the statutes and the Fifth Circuit’s own precedent place a high bar on when that device may be used.  

Below is a brief rundown of (1) the statutory trigger for certification, (2) how the Fifth Circuit has interpreted that trigger (particularly in *Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n*), and (3) why the panel in *Williams v. GoAuto Ins.*, 2025 WL 2584517 (5th Cir.… Oct. 3 2025) declined to certify the disputed statutory‑interpretation question to the Louisiana Supreme Court.

---

### 1. Statutory trigger – 28 U.S.C. § 1254(2)

The statute authorises a federal court “to **certify** any question of law *as to which there is no controlling authority* … if it appears **necessary** for an adjudication of an issue before it.”  
Three elements are therefore read into the provision:

1. **Lack of controlling authority** – there must be no published decision of that state’s highest court (or other adequate authority) answering the precise question.
2. **Necessity** – without a resolution of that question the federal court cannot decide the case on its merits.
3. **Discretion** – even when those two elements are met, certification remains **discretionary**, not mandatory.

The statute also makes clear that a state may decline to answer a certified question, and that “the availability…of certification … [is] an important resource… [that] will not be exhausted by routine use.”*¹*

---

### 2. Fifth‑Circuit’s “sparing” approach – *Jefferson v. Lead Indus.*  

In *Jefferson* the panel articulated precisely how it exercises that discretion:

> “As a general proposition we are **chary** about certifying questions of law absent a **compelling reason** to do so; … That a term has **not yet been defined** by a state supreme court “is **not sufficient** to warrant certification.”*²*  

Thus, according to *Jefferson*, two additional practical thresholds shape certification:

* A mere absence of Supreme Court precedent is **insufficient** – there must be some indication that existing lower‑court decisions cannot resolve the issue.
* The question must be **material** and **unavoidably** affect outcome; if alternative interpretations are available or if reasonable adjudication can proceed without guidance, certification should be avoided.

The *Jefferson* rationale appears verbatim in *Williams*, where Judge Southwick writes:  

> “We are chary about certifying questions … The availability of certification is such an important resource … we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.”*³*

---

### 3. Why *Williams v.* rejected certification

#### (a) The panel found **adequate Louisiana authority**

The plaintiffs asked whether Louisiana’s insurance‑premium‑finance statute requires a **signature** on the premium‑finance company’s “certification” and whether “receipt” demands physical possession by GoAuto before cancellation can occur.

The Fifth Circuit observed that Louisiana appellate courts have already spoken on related points:

* *Benitez v. Elsayed*, 285 So.​ 3d 572 (La.App.​5th Ct. 2019) noted lack of a signature but did **not hold** its absence fatal.
* *KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood*, 115 So.​ 3d 34 (La.App.​4th Ct.) described a “statement certifying compliance.”
* *Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins.*, 546 So.​ 2d 898 (La.App.​1st Ct.) likewise treated cancellation as triggered upon receipt of “notice … and statement.”

Because those cases address essentially identical statutory language—“a statement certifying compliance”—the Fifth Circuit concluded there was **sufficient guidance** for it to resolve the dispute without calling upon the Louisiana Supreme Court.

#### (b) No **compelling necessity**

Even assuming some residual uncertainty about whether a signature or physical receipt is required, the panel judged that “the district court held GoAuto’s procedures complied with the statute… We conclude that … GoAuto may cancel … once its computer system receives the notice.”*⁴*

In other words, even under an expansive reading of “receipt,” GoAuto satisfied it via electronic transmission, and even under a narrow reading requiring a signature, existing appellate discussion indicated such a signature was not indispensable.

Because these alternative constructions allowed resolution on existing precedent—no dead‑end was reached—the panel found **no compelling reason** to invoke certification as required by *Jefferson*.

#### (c) Policy considerations

Finally, Judge Southwick echoed *Jefferson*’s policy warning:

> “…we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.”*⁵*

Thus even where uncertainty existed, preserving certification as an extraordinary remedy outweighed any marginal benefit from seeking definitive guidance from Lafayette.

---

### 4. How often does this happen in Louisiana?

*Williams* itself does **not provide quantitative data** on how many times Fifth‑Circuit judges have certified questions to Louisiana courts; it merely characterizes certification as something used “sparingly.” That language signals a consistent pattern—certification is viewed as an exceptional tool rather than routine—consistent with *Jefferson*.  

If you are looking for empirical counts you will need to consult separate research (e.g., Westlaw reports of certified‑question motions). The Fifth Circuit’s own opinions repeatedly stress restraint but stop short of offering statistics.

---

### Take‑aways for a law student

| Requirement | What *Williams* shows |
|-------------|------------------------|
| **Statutory basis** | §1254(2) allows but does not compel certification; discretion remains |
| **Fifth‑Circuit threshold** | Lack of Supreme Court precedent ≠ automatic certification (*Jefferson*) |
| **When omitted** | If lower‑court decisions already address analogous language—or if multiple plausible constructions exist—certification is declined |
| **Application in *Williams*** | Existing Louisiana appellate opinions on “statement certifying” provided enough guidance; electronic receipt satisfied statutory “receipt”; therefore no compelling reason existed |
| **Frequency claim** | The opinion says certifications are “used sparingly”; it gives no numbers |

In short, a federal appellate court will certify only when ***both*** (i) there truly is no controlling state authority on *that precise question*, ***and*** (ii) without such authority it cannot meaningfully decide the case—and even then only after careful discretion weighing preservation of this limited resource. In *Williams*, those thresholds were not met, so the panel correctly refused to send the question up to Louisiana’s highest court.

### What evidentiary burden applies at summary judgment review when parties dispute compliance with a statutory insurance-cancellation procedure?
Question:
Both sides argued over whether GoAuto complied with La Rev Stat § 9:3550(G). The district court granted summary judgment finding compliance based on its own reading of 'receipt' and 'certifying.' I'm unsure how courts allocate proof responsibilities at summary‑judgment stage for strict‑statutory‑compliance disputes—must the moving party prove every element beyond doubt, or can they rely on reasonable doubt raised by the nonmoving party?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
At summary‑judgment review a moving party must **produce admissible evidence that proves every element of the statutory requirement** and thereby demonstrate *that no genuine issue of material fact remains*. The non‑moving party does **not** have to prove “beyond a reasonable doubt” (that is a criminal standard); it simply needs to **show any specific factual dispute** or a plausible alternative construction of the statute that would keep the issue alive. The Fifth Circuit’s decision in **Williams v. GoAuto Ins., No. 24‑30646 (5th Cir. Oct. 3 2025)** applies exactly this rule, even though the case concerned “strict‑statutory‑compliance” allegations.

---

### 1.  The procedural rule

* **Rule 56(a), Fed. R.Civ.P.** places the initial **burden of production** on the movant (“the party moving for summary judgment”).  
* **Celotex Corp. v. Catrett**, 477 U.S. 803 (1986) – after satisfying the production burden, the movant must also persuade the court that there is *no genuine issue of material fact* (“the movant must show that there is no... dispute as to any material fact”).  
* **Anderson v. Liberty Lobby**, 477 U.S. 242 (1986) – if any reasonable jury could return a verdict for the non‑movant, summary judgment is inappropriate.  

In practice:

| Stage | Party | What it must do |
|-------|-------|-----------------|
| **Move** | Plaintiff/defendant filing the motion | Provide affidavits, declarations, documents, or other admissible evidence establishing each element of the statutory rule (e.g., receipt of notice, certification). |
| **Shift** | Opponent | Offer evidence that creates *a genuine dispute* about any element—this can be a single affidavit disputing receipt date or an argument that “certifying’’ requires a signature if supported by authority. |
| **Court** | Judge | Resolve any pure questions of law de novo (e.g., meaning of “receipt”), but if there is a factual conflict it will deny summary judgment.|

The standard is *not* “proof beyond all doubt”; it is “sufficient to eliminate any real question as to material fact.”

---

### 2.  How *Williams v. GoAuto* illustrates the rule

1️⃣ **The movant (GoAuto) met its production burden**  
   - The district court received GoAuto’s email logs showing automatic receipt by its computer system and copies of APAC’s “certification” statements on company letterhead.  
   - Those documents were treated as admissible evidence establishing each statutory prerequisite under La Rev Stat § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv) – namely that a valid power‑of‑attorney existed, default was uncured, notice was sent to the insured and interested third parties, and APAC certified those facts.

2️⃣ **The non‑moving parties could not create a genuine dispute**  
   - Williams and Wright offered only an alternate reading (“receipt” requires physical possession and a signature on the certification).  
   - The Fifth Circuit noted there is **no controlling Louisiana precedent requiring a signature** and accepted Black’s Law Dictionary’s ordinary meaning of “receipt” as taking possession—whether physical or electronic—so their interpretation did not generate a *material* factual disagreement.
   - Because they presented no concrete evidence showing GoAuto actually lacked possession or certification at the moment of cancellation, their argument remained speculative.

3️⃣ **Resulting burden analysis**  
   - The court affirmed because *GoAuto’s evidentiary record satisfied its initial burden*, and *the plaintiffs failed to meet their shifted burden* by producing specific facts contradicting those records.
   - The opinion explicitly says:  

     > “We conclude that Louisiana law does not require a signature on the notice… Once the e‑mailed notice is received in early morning hours by GoAuto’s computer system, that computer system cancels the policy… We affirm… because GoAuto’s procedures comply with the statute.”  

   This language epitomizes how a summary‑judgment court treats strict statutory compliance: it looks at whether the movant’s record shows compliance; absent concrete counter‑evidence, speculation about “reasonable doubt” does not suffice.

---

### 3.  Practical take‑aways for your situation

| Issue | What you need at summary judgment |
|-------|-------------------------------------|
| **Statutory element (e.g., receipt)** | A copy of the electronic notice together with system logs proving *when* GoAuto’s computer accepted it; optionally an affidavit describing the automated process as in *Williams*. |
| **Certification requirement** | The actual written statement from APAP (or its electronic equivalent) asserting each required fact; no separate signature needed unless state authority says otherwise (which Louisiana does not). |
| **Opposing party’s strategy** | Produce an affidavit or declaration showing *actual* lack of possession at cancellation time, or cite controlling authority requiring a personal signature if such authority exists; merely arguing “the statute sounds like it needs a hand‑signed document” will not create a genuine factual dispute in this circuit. |

If you are preparing a motion:

1️⃣ **Attach all relevant business records** (emails, system logs, stamped PDFs).  
2️⃣ **Swear under oath** that those records reflect what actually occurred—this satisfies Rule 56’s “admissible evidence” requirement without needing live testimony at this stage.  
3️⃣ Anticipate opponent’s possible attack on terminology (“receipt,” “certifying”) by including dictionary definitions or industry practice descriptions in your memorandum—as *Williams* did—to preempt claims that these terms are ambiguous enough to generate doubt.

---

### 4.  Bottom line

- **Moving party = proof through admissible evidence**, not “beyond all doubt.”  
- **Non‑moving party = any specific factual contradiction or credible alternative legal construction**, not merely general speculation or policy arguments.  
- In *Williams v. GoAuto*, the Fifth Circuit applied exactly this framework despite dealing with strict statutory compliance; because GoAuto supplied concrete documentation and plaintiffs offered none that materially disputed those facts, summary judgment was proper.

Thus, when you face a similar factual‐statutory clash at summary judgment, focus on producing concrete records for each statutory step and be prepared to counter any opponent’s attempt to raise only theoretical doubts about those records.

### If an insurer cancels a policy based on an allegedly defective notice under Louisiana law, can insureds still pursue breach-of-good-faith claims?
Question:
The plaintiffs argue that GoAuto's alleged failure to satisfy formalities makes its cancellation ineffective, which could support bad-faith liability even though no valid termination occurred until proper notice is given later. Does an ineffective cancellation automatically give rise to liability for bad faith or breach-of-contract-type duties under Louisiana insurance law, or must insurers first obtain valid termination before such claims arise?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Under Louisiana law an insurer is not automatically liable for a breach‑of‑good‑faith claim simply because a cancellation notice is alleged to be defective. A plaintiff must first show that the insurer actually failed to follow the statutory cancellation requirements *and* that this failure constitutes a concrete breach of a contractual or statutory duty that caused injury. In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the Fifth Circuit held that the insurer’s electronic receipt of APAC’s certification satisfied La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3) and therefore the policies were **validly cancelled**; consequently no bad‑faith claim could arise on the basis of an “ineffective” cancellation.

---

### The legal framework

1. **Statutory procedure for financed policies**  
   Louisiana Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(b) requires a premium‑finance company to **certify** certain facts (power of attorney, default, notice sent to the insured and interested parties) and then deliver that certification “to the insurer…by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, **or via electronic mail**.” Upon **receipt** of such notice, “the insurer may proceed to cancel.” *Delatte v. Lemotte*, 633 So. 2d at 689; *Eaglin v. Champion Ins.*, 558 So. 2d at 287.

2. **Good‑faith / fair‑dealing duties**  
   Louisiana recognizes an implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing in insurance contracts (see *State Farm Mut. Auto.* (La.) 970 So.2d at 61). A breach occurs only when an insurer **unreasonably** deprives the insured of benefits owed under a **validly existing policy**—for example by denying covered claims without basis or by cancelling without complying with § 9:3550(G).

3. **Effect of a defective notice**  
   The statute provides a *strict* checklist, but it does **not** require a handwritten signature on the certification; “certifying” can be satisfied by an electronic statement (§ 9:3550(G)(3)(a)). Likewise, “receipt” is satisfied when the insurer’s computer system obtains the electronic message—no physical possession is needed (*Williams* at 13–14).  

   Because compliance with these minimal textual requirements makes the cancellation effective, a defect that does not invalidate those requirements does **not**, by itself, create liability for bad faith.

---

### What *Williams* tells us

The Fifth Circuit affirmed the district court’s summary‑judgment dismissal because:

* **Statutory compliance was met** – GoAuto’s automated e‑mail and web‑service call fulfilled both “certifying” and “receipt” elements (§ 9:3550(G)(3)). The court rejected plaintiffs’ insistence on a signature or physical delivery as unwarranted additions to the statute (“strict adherence” does not invite courts “to add to the procedures”) (§ III.A).

* **No independent wrongdoing** – The plaintiffs offered no allegation that GoAuto acted *unreasonably* beyond what was required by statute (e.g., knowingly misrepresenting coverage or delaying payment). Since there was no proof of an actual breach of contract or statutory duty, there was nothing on which to base a good‑faith claim (§ III.B).

* **Bad‑faith cannot be inferred from an alleged “ineffective” cancellation alone** – The opinion notes that “any defect…results in an ineffective cancellation,” but stresses that liability arises only when such defect translates into actionable conduct (“the statutory section itself states…the insurer shall consider…cancellation requested…and may proceed”). Because GoAuto complied with § 9:3550(G), its cancellations were *effective*, precluding any bad‑faith theory grounded solely on procedural insufficiency.

Thus, in Louisiana an insurer must first **obtain a valid termination**—or at least *legally appear* to have complied with § 9:3550(G)—before any breach‑of‑good‑faith claim can succeed. An alleged procedural flaw without proof that it voided the termination does **not automatically trigger liability**.

---

### Practical takeaways for students

| Point | Why it matters |
|------|----------------|
| **Statutory compliance is dispositive** | If the insurer meets all textual requirements (certification + receipt), courts treat the cancellation as effective regardless of how informal the paperwork looks (no signature required). |
| **Good‑faith claims require a concrete breach** | Merely pointing out a technical omission does not satisfy the “unreasonable deprivation” element needed for bad‑faith actions under Louisiana law. |
| **Proof matters more than theory** | Plaintiffs must produce evidence showing how the insurer’s conduct actually harmed them—e.g., denied coverage after an invalid cancellation—not just raise hypothetical defects.|

In short, under current Louisiana precedent—as applied by *Williams v. GoAuto*—insureds cannot rely on an alleged defect in cancellation notice alone to sustain a breach‑of‑good‑faith suit; they must first demonstrate that the insurer failed to achieve a legally effective termination and that this failure itself amounts to an actionable breach.

### How do power-of-attorney provisions in premium-finance agreements allocate responsibility for meeting statutory cancellation requirements between finance company and insurer?
Question:
'APAC holds power-of-attorney allowing it to cancel policies upon default and must send certified notices to GoAuto per statute.' Yet GoAuto cancels automatically upon receiving APAC's electronic request. Who bears ultimate responsibility—must APAC certify compliance before sending its request while GoAuto merely needs "receipt"—if any step is missing which party is liable for violating La Rev Stat § 9:3550(G)?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Under Louisiana’s premium‑finance cancellation statute the *finance company* (APAC) bears the substantive burden of complying with every statutory condition and must “certify”—by means of a written statement—that those conditions are satisfied before it requests cancellation. The *insurer* (GoAuto) has only a minimal duty: once it **receives** the certified request (whether physically or electronically) it may cancel the policy. Consequently, if any element of the statutory checklist is missing or false, liability lies with APAC; GoAuto would be liable only if it cancelled without first having a proper “receipt” of the certified notice.

Below is a step‑by‑step explanation drawn from **Williams v. GoAuto Insurance**, 5th Cir. No. 24‑30646 (Oct. 3 2025).

---

### 1️⃣ The statutory scheme (La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G))

The law requires a **premium‑finance company** to send *to the insurer*:

1. **A copy of the notice of cancellation**  
2. **A statement certifying** that:
   - The financing agreement contains a valid power‑of‑attorney;
   - The agreement is in default and the default has not been cured;
   - A ten‑day cancellation notice was sent to the insured;
   - Copies of that notice were sent to all interested third parties.

(§ 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv)).  

The statute then provides that *upon receipt* of “such notice … and statement,” the insurer *may* proceed with cancellation (§ 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i)).

---

### 2️⃣ What “certifying” means

The Fifth Circuit read “certifying” as **a factual affirmation**, *not* a requirement that a particular employee sign the document:

- The opinion notes that “the statute makes no explicit reference to a signature” and that “the word ‘certifying’ would have to create such a requirement” if Congress had meant it (Williams, ¶ III.A).  
- No Louisiana precedent has imposed a signature requirement; decisions such as *Delatte v. Lemotte* and *Eaglin v. Champion* focus on strict compliance with content, not on who signs it.

Thus APAP’s e‑mail that includes a paragraph beginning “APAC certifies that …” satisfies the certification duty even though no individual’s handwritten signature appears on it.

---

### 3️⃣ What counts as “receipt”

The court accepted **electronic receipt** as sufficient:

- It applied Black’s Law Dictionary definitions (“receipt” = taking possession or custody) and concluded that once GoAuto’s computer system receives the e‑mail and processes it through a web service, GoAuto has “taken possession” in a modern sense (§ III.A).  
- The opinion rejects the plaintiffs’ argument that physical handling by an employee is required and affirms the district court’s view that an e‑mail plus an automated web‑service call satisfy § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i).

Therefore GoAuto’s automatic cancellation triggered by its system constitutes lawful “receipt.”

---

### 4️⃣ Allocation of responsibility

| Party | Statutory duty | What failure triggers liability |
|-------|----------------|--------------------------------|
| **APAC (finance company)** | • Draft and send the cancellation notice<br>• Include all four statements under § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)<br>• Ensure each statement is true | A false or omitted certification (e.g., failing to confirm a valid POA or that a ten‑day notice was sent) means APAC violated the statute and is liable under § 9:3550(G). |
| **GoAuto (insurer)** | • Receive the certified notice (electronically or otherwise)<br>• Cancel only after receipt | If GoAuto cancels *without* having received a compliant certified request, it would be in violation of § 9:3550(G)(3)(b). The court found this did not occur here because its system captured the e‑mail before cancellation.|

The Fifth Circuit emphasized that **the finance company carries the substantive compliance burden**; once its certified request reaches the insurer, any subsequent action by the insurer is permissible so long as “receipt” has occurred.

---

### 5️⃣ Application to your hypothetical

- **APAC must certify before sending** – The certification must be attached to the same communication that goes to GoAuto (the e‑mail). The presence of an electronic stamp later in the morning does not affect compliance because cancellation occurs after receipt.
- **GoAuto’s duty ends at receipt** – As soon as its computer systems ingest APAC’s e‑mail (“receipt”), it may cancel automatically.
- **Liability for a missing step** – If APAC omitted any required certification element (e.g., failed to disclose that a ten‑day notice was actually mailed), APAC—not GoAuto—would be responsible for any violation of § 9:3550(G). Conversely, if GoAuto cancelled before its system logged receipt, GoAuto would be at fault.

---

### 6️⃣ Key takeaways

1. **Finance company = certifier** – It must ensure all statutory prerequisites are true and evidenced in its request.
2. **Insurer = receiver** – It need only obtain a “receipt,” which modern courts treat as satisfied by electronic transmission.
3. **No signature requirement** – A plain statement labelled “certifies” suffices under current jurisprudence.
4. **Liability follows performance** – Failure on either side triggers liability only if that side neglects its specific statutory duty.

In *Williams v. GoAuto*, the Fifth Circuit concluded that both parties performed their respective duties correctly; therefore no violation of La Rev Stat § 9:3550(G) occurred and no liability attached to either party.

--- 

*If you encounter a situation where an insurance premium finance arrangement uses only an electronic request without any accompanying certification statement, remember that under Louisiana law—and according to *Williams*—the lack of such certification would place fault on the finance company.*
