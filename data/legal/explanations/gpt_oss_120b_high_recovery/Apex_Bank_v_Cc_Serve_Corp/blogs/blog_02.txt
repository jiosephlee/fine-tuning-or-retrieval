## Why This Case Matters  

The Federal Circuit’s decision in **Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.** (Fed. Cir. 2025) is a rare appellate pronouncement on a point that often feels like a footnote in trademark disputes: *how many third‑party uses are enough to show that a senior mark is weak?*  The court didn’t just rebuke the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) for an arithmetic misstep—it reminded us that the “sixth DuPont factor” is a qualitative inquiry rooted in market reality, not a simple tally of registrations.  Because the sixth factor governs both the scope of protection afforded to a senior mark and whether an opponent can overcome it under § 2(d) of the Lanham Act, any shift in its application ripples through filing strategies, opposition practice, and even how we think about “strength” versus “weakness” in trademark law.

---

## The Sixth DuPont Factor: A Quick Primer  

The **DuPont** factors—13 criteria distilled from *In re E.I. DuPont*—are the workhorse of likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis.  The sixth factor asks:

> *“The number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.”*  

At first glance this appears straightforward: if many competitors already use “Aspire” on credit‑card services, perhaps CC Serve’s “ASPIRE” cannot command exclusive rights.  The TTAB has traditionally looked for **“considerable” or “ubiquitous”** use—language borrowed from precedent such as *Jack Wolfskin* (797 F.3d 1363) and *Juice Generation* (794 F.3d 1334).  Those cases invoked vague numerical thresholds (e.g., 14 paw‑print registrations or 26 “notable” markings) as illustrative benchmarks rather than hard rules.

That ambiguity left practitioners hacking away at keyword searches, hoping a spreadsheet would magically tip their argument about weakness.  Apex Bank forces us to confront exactly how those numbers translate—or don’t—to real‑world commercial strength.

---

## What Apex Bank v. CC Serve Tells Us About “Considerable” Use  

### The TTAB’s Original Reasoning  

The Board identified **nine** registrations that contained “Aspire” (or variants) in connection with credit‑card services.  Relying on *Jack Wolfskin*, it concluded that nine was far short of what it considered “considerable” or “ubiquitous,” therefore insufficient to prove that CC Serve’s mark was weak enough to merit a narrow scope of protection.

### The Federal Circuit’s Critique  

Judge Hughes wrote that the Board **erroneously limited** its analysis to marks tied specifically to credit‑card services while ignoring **42 additional Aspire‑related marks** covering broader banking and financing activities—goods already deemed “highly similar” under the second DuPont factor.  Because the Board had already found **substantial overlap** between CC Serve’s credit‑card services and Apex’s banking/financing services, it could not switch gears and apply a stricter similarity test for the sixth factor.

Crucially, the court emphasized:

> *“The sixth DuPont factor does not require identical goods—only similar ones.”*  

Thus, any third‑party use of “Aspire” within the broader financial‑services arena should have been counted toward assessing commercial weakness.

### No Fixed Numerical Threshold  

Apex Bank did **not** set a new numeric ceiling (e.g., “you need at least fifteen similar marks”). Instead, it underscored that **context matters**:  
*   The *nature* of those marks—whether they belong to direct competitors or peripheral players—affects their weight.  
*   The *breadth* of related goods/services matters just as much as raw count.  

In short, the court rejected a mechanical approach (“nine is too few”) in favor of a holistic market view (“are there enough similarly situated users across all relevant classes to dilute the senior mark’s distinctiveness?”).

---

## Quantitative Thresholds vs. Qualitative Market Presence  

### The Temptation of Numbers  

Practitioners love numbers because they’re tidy; they are also easy for examiners and judges to trip over when presented as charts or spreadsheets. Yet both *Jack Wolfskin* and *Juice Generation* warned that **quantity alone is not dispositive**. In *Jack Wolfskin*, fourteen registrations were described as “voluminous,” but those registrations involved identical design elements that saturated a niche market—a fact more important than raw count.

### Market Saturation and Consumer Perception  

A more useful yardstick is **consumer exposure**:

1. **Geographic Spread** – Are competing ‘Aspire’ marks used nationally or confined to local markets?  
2. **Advertising Investment** – Do rivals pour significant ad dollars behind their Aspire branding?  
3. **Sales Volume & Market Share** – A handful of high‑profile users can outweigh dozens of dormant filings.  
4. **Channel Overlap** – If competing marks appear side‑by‑side on retailer websites or mobile apps where consumers shop for credit cards or banking products, confusion risk rises even with few registrations.

Apex Bank signals that appellate courts will look beyond checklist counting toward these substance‑based inquiries when assessing weakness under factor six.

## Practical Guidance for Litigants and Practitioners  

1. **Cast a Wide Net Early**  
   - Conduct comprehensive USPTO searches *across all International Classes* relevant to your client’s goods—even those you deem only tangentially related.
   - Document not just registration numbers but also filing dates, current status (live vs abandoned), and any evidence of actual use (e.g., URLs showing active webpages).

2. **Collect Market Evidence**  
   - Compile advertising spend reports (e.g., Nielsen Ad Intel) for each third‑party user.
   - Secure sales figures or market share data where possible; even estimates from industry reports can strengthen your narrative.
   - Where feasible, run consumer perception surveys focusing on brand awareness across the full product set—not just one subcategory.

3. **Separate “Weak” from “Weakly Related” Marks**  
   - If you have dozens of Aspire-like markings limited solely to unrelated sectors (e.g., sporting equipment), be prepared to argue they are *irrelevant* under factor six.
   - Conversely, bring forward every finance‑related user—even if its primary product is loan servicing rather than credit cards—because Apex shows courts view such overlap as sufficient similarity.

4. **Quantify Deserted Registrations**  
   - Highlight dormant or abandoned filings as weaker evidence; active use carries more weight.
   - Show timelines demonstrating whether competing uses predate your client’s filing—a chronological argument can buttress claims of preexisting market saturation.

5. **Strategic Timing**  
   - In an opposition under § 2(d), if you anticipate reliance on factor six, file evidentiary support early in opposition proceedings rather than waiting for appellate review.
   - For intent‑to‑use applications facing early objections (“likelihood-of-confusion”), include at least three robust instances of third‑party usage covering your full product spectrum—as part of your initial submission—to head off potential Board errors akin to those later corrected by Apex Bank.

## Doctrinal Implications and Potential Downstream Effects  

### Rebalancing Strength vs. Weakness  

By vacating the Board’s narrow reading, Apex Bank nudges TTAB examiners toward treating *weakness* as an empirical fact about market conditions rather than an abstract metric tied neatly to registration counts.  This could lead to:
- More detailed fact‑finding missions during oppositions.
- Greater willingness by boards to find senior marks ***not*** entitled to “inherent distinctiveness” protection when faced with a crowded field—even if those fields cut across multiple service categories.
- A subtle shift toward treating *the second DuPont factor* (goods similarity) as an interpretive anchor that constrains how we apply later factors—particularly six (similar marks).

### Spillover Into Other Contexts  

1. **Service Marks & Financial Services** – Industries with overlapping but distinct offerings (e.g., fintech platforms offering both payment processing and lending) will now see deeper scrutiny of third‑party usage across all related services.
2. **Emerging Technologies** – For AI or blockchain-based products where classification evolves rapidly, practitioners must anticipate future similarity expansions when assembling third-party evidence.
3. **International Registrations** – Although this case deals with U.S.-law statutory interpretation, foreign trademark offices often lean heavily on “similarity” analyses akin to DuPont; they may adopt analogous broader scopes after observing U.S.-circuit trends.
4. **Appeal Landscape** – Given that appellate courts apply de novo legal review yet require substantial evidence for factual findings—as reiterated by Judge Hughes—the decision reminds lower panels that their factual determinations must be firmly supported by evidence spanning all relevant goods/services.

---

## Conclusion  

*Apex Bank v.* does not provide a crisp number—“you need twenty similar marks”—but it delivers an equally valuable rule: **the sixth DuPont factor is fundamentally qualitative**, anchored in how saturated the marketplace truly is for comparable goods/services across all relevant contexts.  Courts will now look at the whole ecosystem of third‑party usage before deciding whether a senior mark warrants its full protective sweep or must accept a narrowed scope due to commercial weakness.

For trademark practitioners this means abandoning checklist mentalities in favor of marketplace intelligence: diligent cross‑class searches, concrete usage data, and persuasive consumer perception evidence become indispensable tools when arguing strength—or more often today—weakness under § 2(d).  As we move forward into increasingly blended service categories—from fintech platforms to AI-driven loyalty programs—the lessons from Apex Bank will echo whenever we ask whether “enough people are already using ‘Aspire’ so your client can’t own it.” 

In short: count your marks wisely—but count them *in context*.
