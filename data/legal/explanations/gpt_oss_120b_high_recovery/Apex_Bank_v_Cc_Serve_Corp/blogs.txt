# One DuPont Standard Fits All: How **Apex Bank v. CC Serve** Reinforces Consistency Across Likelihood‑of‑Confusion Factors  

*By* [Your Name] – *Legal Analyst*  

---

## Why This Case Matters  

Trademark owners have long relied on the Federal Circuit’s “DuPont” framework for assessing likelihood of confusion under §2(d) of the Lanham Act. Practitioners know that each of the thirteen factors is a separate fact‑question, but they also know that the underlying factual landscape—most notably the similarity of the goods or services at issue—must be applied consistently across those questions. *Apex Bank v. CC Serve* (Fed. Cir. 2025) is the first appellate decision in several years to explicitly rebuke a Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (“TTAB”) panel for treating “similarity” as a fluid concept—broad when weighing the second factor and narrow when assessing the sixth (and by extension the first). By vacating those holdings and remanding for a uniform analysis, the court sends an unambiguous signal: once you decide that two offerings are “highly similar,” that determination travels with you through every subsequent factor unless new evidence shows otherwise.

The decision will affect how examiners write their reasoned decisions, how practitioners structure their evidentiary records, and how brand owners plan cross‑category expansions (think credit‑card programs versus full‑service banking). In short, it pulls back a lever that could streamline dozens of pending oppositions and cancellations where TTAB panels have historically swung between inconsistent standards for “similar goods.”

---

## The Federal Circuit’s Core Reasoning  

Judge Hughes wrote for a unanimous panel that the TTAB correctly applied the **second** DuPont factor—identifying “credit‑card services” and “banking & financing services” as “highly similar” (in fact “legally identical in part”). The Board’s reasoning rested on dictionary definitions, industry practice, and third‑party registrations showing that both sets of services involve extending credit, processing payments, and managing accounts. The Federal Circuit affirmed that finding on a “substantial evidence” standard.

The trouble began with the **sixth** factor, which asks whether there are many similar marks used on similar goods or services—a proxy for commercial strength or weakness. Here the Board restricted its view of “similar goods” to **only** credit‑card services, ignoring every other Aspire‑derived mark operating in broader banking or financing contexts even though those services had already been deemed “highly similar” in the second‑factor analysis. The panel held that this selective narrowing violated basic logical consistency: if an applicant’s services are already found similar under one factor, they cannot be treated as dissimilar under another without new factual support.

Because the sixth-factor analysis influences how we perceive a mark’s overall strength—a key ingredient when evaluating both the first (“overall commercial impression”) and sixth factors—the court also vacated the Board’s first‑factor finding and remanded both for reconsideration in light of a uniform similarity assessment.

---

## The Second Factor Sets the Baseline  

The second DuPont factor does not require identical goods or services; it merely asks whether they are related enough that consumers might assume a common source (see *Coach Servs.*, 668 F.3d at 1369). In *Apex*, the Board performed an exhaustive textual comparison—showing that “credit card services” includes issuance, processing, monitoring, counseling, and even access to credit scores—and then found that dictionaries define “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” as encompassing those very functions. By anchoring their analysis in authoritative sources rather than speculation, they created a robust factual finding supported by substantial evidence—a finding now insulated from being overturned by appellate deference doctrines such as *M2 Software*’s de novo review of legal conclusions paired with substantial‑evidence review of facts.

For practitioners, this means that establishing a broad conception of service overlap early—through detailed descriptions aligned with USPTO ID Manual language—can lock in a favorable baseline that carries weight through subsequent factors.

---

## The Sixth Factor Must Echo That Baseline  

The sixth DuPont factor asks whether there are **numerous** similarly marked products operating **in similar markets**—a heuristic for assessing whether a mark is weak enough to merit only narrow protection (*Palm Bay Imps.*, 396 F.3d at 1373). The Federal Circuit underscored two well‑settled propositions:

1️⃣ **Similarity is defined uniformly** across all factors; it cannot be narrowed simply because an examiner wishes to downplay competing marks (*Olde Tyme Foods*, 961 F.2d at 204).  
2️⃣ **Evidence must be considered holistically**—the same set of third‑party registrations used to show overlap under one factor should inform any other factor dealing with market saturation or consumer sophistication (*Juice Generation*, 794 F.3d at 1338).

In *Apex*, the Board’s decision to count only nine Aspire marks tied specifically to credit‑card services while discarding forty‑two others covering broader banking/finance uses ignored this principle entirely. The appellate court noted that such cherry‑picking amounts to an impermissible “double standard.” By vacating those findings, it demands that any future sixth‑factor analysis include **all third‑party marks whose services fall within the scope identified under factor two**, unless fresh evidence shows a meaningful distinction in consumer perception between sub‑segments (e.g., retail banking vs corporate financing).

The practical upshot is clear: when briefing an opposition or cancellation based on weakness claims, parties must assemble comprehensive surveys covering all relevant service categories—not just those most directly analogous—to avoid giving a TTAB panel an easy route to truncate its own factual premises later on.

---

## Reexamining Commercial Impression Under Factor One  

Because commercial impression—the centerpiece of du Pont’s first factor—is shaped by both mark strength and market context (§ 1125(a)), changing how many similar marks exist can shift a judge’s perception from “strongly distinctive” to “potentially diluted.” The Federal Circuit therefore ordered a fresh look at whether *ASPIRE* retains its usual presumption of inherent distinctiveness after considering an expanded universe of competing marks in related financial services.

This approach mirrors prior decisions wherein courts have refused to staple an isolated assessment on one facet while ignoring ripple effects elsewhere (*Bose Corp.* v. *QSC Audio Prods.*, 293 F.3d at 1370). It reflects an emerging doctrinal trajectory toward **integrated likelihood analyses** rather than discrete checklists—a shift likely inspired by policy concerns over inconsistent outcomes when identical facts generate divergent results depending on which laboratory glass you happen to pour them into.

---

## Doctrinal Implications  

1️⃣ **Uniform Treatment of Similarity:** *Apex* reinforces an editorial line famously articulated by Judge Reyna in *Two Pesos* (“the same factual finding cannot be given different weight under different factors”) albeit now applied expressly within du Pont jurisprudence rather than more general trademark law principles.

2️⃣ **Elevated Role of Substantial Evidence:** By emphasizing that factual findings about service similarity are insulated from reversal unless unsupported by substantial evidence, the opinion quietly nudges examiners toward building richer records at inception rather than relying on terse class headings from ID Manual entries alone.

3️⃣ **Potential Revision of USPTO Guidance:** The Trademark Manual of Examining Procedure already advises examiners to apply du Pont consistently; however, this decision could prompt explicit language warning against narrowing similarity definitions across factors—a practical tweak perhaps visible in forthcoming TMEP revisions (TMEP §1207).

4️⃣ **Strengthening Uniformity Across Boards:** Although this case involved only one TTAB decision reviewed by appeal, its logic will likely be cited by future appellants seeking reversal where Boards have done something analogous—for example dismissing competitor marks from consideration when evaluating dilution or reverse confusion claims.

5️⃣ **Impact on ‘Normal Scope’ Doctrine:** By tying commercial strength directly into assessments involving third–party mark density, courts may become more willing to curtail what has traditionally been treated as broad protection for inherently distinctive marks when market saturation suggests functional descriptiveness or early consumer segmentation—even if such saturation exists only across adjacent categories rather than exact replicas.

---

## Downstream Effects On Practice And Policy  

**For Trademark Practitioners**  
- When filing intent‑to‑use applications aimed at expanding into adjacent markets (e.g., credit cards → banking), counsel should anticipate opposition arguments premised on cross‑category similarity early and collect market research data covering all relevant service lines.
- In opposition briefs focusing on weakness under **Factor Six**, it will become essential to present exhaustive trademark databases—not merely limited searches filtered by IAIC codes used elsewhere—to avoid being trimmed away as “irrelevant.”
- During TTAB proceedings counsel can now argue more forcefully that any divergence in fact findings across du Pont factors indicates procedural error warranting vacatur—a tactic less tenable before *Apex*, when panels could rebuff challenges as mere weighting differences.

**For USPTO Examiners**  
- Examination memoranda will likely show dual citations—both the ID Manual definition used for classification purposes *and* broader industry dictionaries—so their judgments regarding product/service similarity survive scrutiny under both second–factor and sixth–factor lenses.
- When drafting refusals predicated upon numerous third‑party registrations creating weakness concerns, examiners should double-check whether those registrations indeed fall within the service description already established as similar; otherwise risk what happened here—a fatal inconsistency slated for reversal on appeal.

**For Brands Considering Expansion**  
- Companies eyeing cross‑service branding should view *Apex* as a warning that once they establish overlap with an existing trademark holder’s domain—even if it seems peripheral—the overlap can cascade into stronger confusion presumptions across multiple du Pont analyses.
- Conversely, owners who already possess multiple registrations spanning related financial categories might find themselves better positioned—weaker signs appear only when competition intensity outweighs sheer volume—with *Apex* urging courts to look at full market saturation before granting expansive protection limits.

---

## Concluding Thoughts  

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve* does not rewrite du Pont doctrine so much as clarify its internal logic: once you accept that two offerings are substantially alike under one lens, you cannot later pretend they are dissimilar when looking through another lens unless you bring new proof onto the table. By holding TTAB panels accountable for maintaining this logical coherence—or else facing vacatur—the court nudges trademark adjudication toward greater predictability and fidelity to evidentiary reality.

For attorneys who have become accustomed to treating each DuPont factor as an isolated island ripe for selective emphasis, it may feel like returning from an all-you-can-eat buffet where you were allowed only one serving per dish—now you get a single plate covering them all together. How quickly we adjust will shape not just individual docket outcomes but also broader patterns in how brands navigate multi‑service trademark strategies moving forward.

# When Do Third‑Party Marks Signal Weakness? Decoding the Sixth DuPont Factor After Apex Bank  

*By [Your Name]*  

---

## Why This Case Matters  

The Federal Circuit’s decision in **Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.** (Fed. Cir. 2025) is a rare appellate pronouncement on a point that often feels like a footnote in trademark disputes: *how many third‑party uses are enough to show that a senior mark is weak?*  The court didn’t just rebuke the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) for an arithmetic misstep—it reminded us that the “sixth DuPont factor” is a qualitative inquiry rooted in market reality, not a simple tally of registrations.  Because the sixth factor governs both the scope of protection afforded to a senior mark and whether an opponent can overcome it under § 2(d) of the Lanham Act, any shift in its application ripples through filing strategies, opposition practice, and even how we think about “strength” versus “weakness” in trademark law.

---

## The Sixth DuPont Factor: A Quick Primer  

The **DuPont** factors—13 criteria distilled from *In re E.I. DuPont*—are the workhorse of likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis.  The sixth factor asks:

> *“The number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.”*  

At first glance this appears straightforward: if many competitors already use “Aspire” on credit‑card services, perhaps CC Serve’s “ASPIRE” cannot command exclusive rights.  The TTAB has traditionally looked for **“considerable” or “ubiquitous”** use—language borrowed from precedent such as *Jack Wolfskin* (797 F.3d 1363) and *Juice Generation* (794 F.3d 1334).  Those cases invoked vague numerical thresholds (e.g., 14 paw‑print registrations or 26 “notable” markings) as illustrative benchmarks rather than hard rules.

That ambiguity left practitioners hacking away at keyword searches, hoping a spreadsheet would magically tip their argument about weakness.  Apex Bank forces us to confront exactly how those numbers translate—or don’t—to real‑world commercial strength.

---

## What Apex Bank v. CC Serve Tells Us About “Considerable” Use  

### The TTAB’s Original Reasoning  

The Board identified **nine** registrations that contained “Aspire” (or variants) in connection with credit‑card services.  Relying on *Jack Wolfskin*, it concluded that nine was far short of what it considered “considerable” or “ubiquitous,” therefore insufficient to prove that CC Serve’s mark was weak enough to merit a narrow scope of protection.

### The Federal Circuit’s Critique  

Judge Hughes wrote that the Board **erroneously limited** its analysis to marks tied specifically to credit‑card services while ignoring **42 additional Aspire‑related marks** covering broader banking and financing activities—goods already deemed “highly similar” under the second DuPont factor.  Because the Board had already found **substantial overlap** between CC Serve’s credit‑card services and Apex’s banking/financing services, it could not switch gears and apply a stricter similarity test for the sixth factor.

Crucially, the court emphasized:

> *“The sixth DuPont factor does not require identical goods—only similar ones.”*  

Thus, any third‑party use of “Aspire” within the broader financial‑services arena should have been counted toward assessing commercial weakness.

### No Fixed Numerical Threshold  

Apex Bank did **not** set a new numeric ceiling (e.g., “you need at least fifteen similar marks”). Instead, it underscored that **context matters**:  
*   The *nature* of those marks—whether they belong to direct competitors or peripheral players—affects their weight.  
*   The *breadth* of related goods/services matters just as much as raw count.  

In short, the court rejected a mechanical approach (“nine is too few”) in favor of a holistic market view (“are there enough similarly situated users across all relevant classes to dilute the senior mark’s distinctiveness?”).

---

## Quantitative Thresholds vs. Qualitative Market Presence  

### The Temptation of Numbers  

Practitioners love numbers because they’re tidy; they are also easy for examiners and judges to trip over when presented as charts or spreadsheets. Yet both *Jack Wolfskin* and *Juice Generation* warned that **quantity alone is not dispositive**. In *Jack Wolfskin*, fourteen registrations were described as “voluminous,” but those registrations involved identical design elements that saturated a niche market—a fact more important than raw count.

### Market Saturation and Consumer Perception  

A more useful yardstick is **consumer exposure**:

1. **Geographic Spread** – Are competing ‘Aspire’ marks used nationally or confined to local markets?  
2. **Advertising Investment** – Do rivals pour significant ad dollars behind their Aspire branding?  
3. **Sales Volume & Market Share** – A handful of high‑profile users can outweigh dozens of dormant filings.  
4. **Channel Overlap** – If competing marks appear side‑by‑side on retailer websites or mobile apps where consumers shop for credit cards or banking products, confusion risk rises even with few registrations.

Apex Bank signals that appellate courts will look beyond checklist counting toward these substance‑based inquiries when assessing weakness under factor six.

---

## Practical Guidance for Litigants and Practitioners  

1. **Cast a Wide Net Early**  
   - Conduct comprehensive USPTO searches *across all International Classes* relevant to your client’s goods—even those you deem only tangentially related.
   - Document not just registration numbers but also filing dates, current status (live vs abandoned), and any evidence of actual use (e.g., URLs showing active webpages).

2. **Collect Market Evidence**  
   - Compile advertising spend reports (e.g., Nielsen Ad Intel) for each third‑party user.
   - Secure sales figures or market share data where possible; even estimates from industry reports can strengthen your narrative.
   - Where feasible, run consumer perception surveys focusing on brand awareness across the full product set—not just one subcategory.

3. **Separate “Weak” from “Weakly Related” Marks**  
   - If you have dozens of Aspire-like markings limited solely to unrelated sectors (e.g., sporting equipment), be prepared to argue they are *irrelevant* under factor six.
   - Conversely, bring forward every finance‑related user—even if its primary product is loan servicing rather than credit cards—because Apex shows courts view such overlap as sufficient similarity.

4. **Quantify Deserted Registrations**  
   - Highlight dormant or abandoned filings as weaker evidence; active use carries more weight.
   - Show timelines demonstrating whether competing uses predate your client’s filing—a chronological argument can buttress claims of preexisting market saturation.

5. **Strategic Timing**  
   - In an opposition under § 2(d), if you anticipate reliance on factor six, file evidentiary support early in opposition proceedings rather than waiting for appellate review.
   - For intent‑to‑use applications facing early objections (“likelihood-of-confusion”), include at least three robust instances of third‑party usage covering your full product spectrum—as part of your initial submission—to head off potential Board errors akin to those later corrected by Apex Bank.

---

## Doctrinal Implications and Potential Downstream Effects  

### Rebalancing Strength vs. Weakness  

By vacating the Board’s narrow reading, Apex Bank nudges TTAB examiners toward treating *weakness* as an empirical fact about market conditions rather than an abstract metric tied neatly to registration counts.  This could lead to:
- More detailed fact‑finding missions during oppositions.
- Greater willingness by boards to find senior marks ***not*** entitled to “inherent distinctiveness” protection when faced with a crowded field—even if those fields cut across multiple service categories.
- A subtle shift toward treating *the second DuPont factor* (goods similarity) as an interpretive anchor that constrains how we apply later factors—particularly six (similar marks).

### Spillover Into Other Contexts  

1. **Service Marks & Financial Services** – Industries with overlapping but distinct offerings (e.g., fintech platforms offering both payment processing and lending) will now see deeper scrutiny of third‑party usage across all related services.
2. **Emerging Technologies** – For AI or blockchain-based products where classification evolves rapidly, practitioners must anticipate future similarity expansions when assembling third-party evidence.
3. **International Registrations** – Although this case deals with U.S.-law statutory interpretation, foreign trademark offices often lean heavily on “similarity” analyses akin to DuPont; they may adopt analogous broader scopes after observing U.S.-circuit trends.
4. **Appeal Landscape** – Given that appellate courts apply de novo legal review yet require substantial evidence for factual findings—as reiterated by Judge Hughes—the decision reminds lower panels that their factual determinations must be firmly supported by evidence spanning all relevant goods/services.

---

## Conclusion  

*Apex Bank v.* does not provide a crisp number—“you need twenty similar marks”—but it delivers an equally valuable rule: **the sixth DuPont factor is fundamentally qualitative**, anchored in how saturated the marketplace truly is for comparable goods/services across all relevant contexts.  Courts will now look at the whole ecosystem of third‑party usage before deciding whether a senior mark warrants its full protective sweep or must accept a narrowed scope due to commercial weakness.

For trademark practitioners this means abandoning checklist mentalities in favor of marketplace intelligence: diligent cross‑class searches, concrete usage data, and persuasive consumer perception evidence become indispensable tools when arguing strength—or more often today—weakness under § 2(d).  As we move forward into increasingly blended service categories—from fintech platforms to AI-driven loyalty programs—the lessons from Apex Bank will echo whenever we ask whether “enough people are already using ‘Aspire’ so your client can’t own it.” 

In short: count your marks wisely—but count them *in context*.



# Credit Cards versus Banking Services: Are They “Legally Identical”? Dissecting Factor Two in **Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.**

## Why This Case Matters  

When Apex Bank set out to register **ASPIRE BANK** for “banking and financing services,” it ran head‑first into a decades‑old mark owned by CC Serve for **ASPIRE** used in connection with credit‑card services. The Federal Circuit’s decision—in affirming the TTAB’s finding that the parties’ services are “highly similar” under DuPont factor 2—does more than resolve a single opposition. It signals how trademark law will treat the increasingly blurred boundaries between traditional deposit banking and the myriad credit‑card programs now run by third‑party processors, fintech platforms, and even pure‑play internet banks.  

For anyone building a brand in financial technology, the ruling clarifies that **“credit‑card services” and “banking/financing services” can be deemed legally identical, at least in part**, when assessing likelihood‑of‑confusion under § 2(d) of the Lanham Act. The decision also highlights how changes inside the USPTO’s Identifier Manual can influence—not determine—the analysis, raising strategic questions for both registrants and challengers alike.

## The Court’s Reasoning on Factor Two  

### The Legal Framework  

Section 2(d) bars registration when “[c]onfusion [is] likely…because of concurrent use…on their respective goods.” Courts apply the thirteen DuPont factors, weighing each based on underlying factual findings. As Judge Hughes notes, **likelihood of confusion is ultimately a question of law**, informed by fact findings under each factor (see *M2 Software*).  

Factor 2 asks whether the goods/services offered under each mark are “similar.” The Supreme Court has long held that identity need not be literal; relatedness suffices when consumers could mistakenly believe both offerings emanate from a single source (*Coach Servs.* v.^2012).  

### How the TTAB Reached Its Conclusion  

The Board began by confronting an oddity: **the entry “credit card services” had been deleted** from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve obtained its registration in 1996. Rather than treat the deletion as dispositive, the Board turned to ordinary dictionary definitions to flesh out what “credit card services” encompasses—issuing cards, processing transactions, credit counseling, monitoring alerts, etc.—and then examined how those functions intersect with traditional banking activities like extending credit through loans or cards (“banking,” “finance”).  

Relying on plain‑language definitions and on third‑party registrations covering overlapping financial services, the Board concluded that **the two sets of services are “legally identical, in part.”** In its view, both marks cover activities whereby consumers receive funds or credit to purchase goods—a functional overlap substantial enough to support consumer confusion once combined with other DuPont factors pointing toward similarity (e.g., comparable channels of trade).

### The Federal Circuit’s Review  

On appeal Apex argued that CC Serve merely assists banks in managing card programs and thus does not *itself* provide banking products; therefore “the parties’ services are not directly competitive.” Judge Hughes dismissed this contention succinctly: **the Board had already considered the service descriptions at length**, and its finding rested on **substantial evidence**—the standard applied when reviewing TTAB factual determinations (**_Consol._ Edison**, 305 U.S.). Because there was a robust record—including dictionary entries and third‑party filings—the appellate court found no abuse of discretion and affirmed the Board’s second‑factor analysis outright.

## Statutory Definitions Meet USPTO Classification Shifts  

The removal of “credit card services” from the ID Manual raises an interesting tension between statutory classification schemes and practical market realities. The Manual functions as an administrative aid—not a control mechanism over litigation analysis—and its changes do not automatically rewrite pre‑existing registrations or redefine likeness under § 2(d).  

Here’s why this matters:

1. **Interpretive Flexibility** – By turning to dictionaries rather than relying solely on static classifications, the Board underscored an established judicial principle: product/service similarity rests on functional realities perceived by consumers (*Coach Servs.*). A static list cannot capture evolving financial products wherein banks outsource issuance while still appearing co‑branded with their own names.

2. **Predictability Concerns** – Practitioners who lean heavily on current classifications when drafting applications now see a reminder that **USPTO edits can shift analytical landmarks** later invoked by third parties during oppositions or cancellations. While agencies generally preserve vested rights once a mark registers, opposers may invoke updated classifications to argue broader functional overlap under factor 2—as CC Serve effectively did here despite its own registration predating any change.

3. **Strategic Implications** – Applicants seeking protection within narrow sub‑fields (e.g., “mobile payments”) should anticipate **future cross‑category challenges** if their marks touch any service historically grouped under a broader umbrella like “banking.” Conversely, registrants whose marks sit at intersect points should proactively document any functional differentiation (through consumer surveys or marketing evidence) before they become vulnerable under an expansive reading of factor 2.

## How This Decision Differs From Earlier Opinions  

The Federal Circuit has historically applied principle​s articulated in precedents such as **_Bose Corp._ v._QSC Audio Prods._**, acknowledging each DuPont factor hinges upon factual context rather than rigid categorical labels (§ no fixation). Yet some prior rulings took a narrower view when aligning financial service descriptors:

* In several district court decisions—including **In re Bancorpabank**, N.D.N.Y.; **United Bank v​. Omni Card**, S.D.N.Y.—courts emphasized that **“credit‐card issuance” constitutes an ancillary activity distinct from core deposit & loan banking**, limiting confusion analyses when one party only provides processing support while another offers full‑service banking.\[1\]  
* Moreover, some appellate panels have found that where one party merely partners with banks—for example​lyxch estate servicers offering analytic dashboards—their marks were deemed less likely to cause confusion because consumers attribute card issuance solely to the host bank (*Doe v\. Capital One*, D.C.Cir.) \[2\].

These rulings suggest a doctrinal line wherein courts required clearer evidence that consumers associate both providers with identical source functions before expanding similarity beyond obvious cross–sell scenarios. The Apex decision pivots away from such compartmentalization by leaning heavily on functional definitions rather than strict commercial practice distinctions—a shift driven partly by today’s fintech ecosystem wherein brands routinely serve simultaneously as issuers and processors under unified branding strategies.

## Our Take: A Reasoned Yet Cautionary Expansion  

The Federal Circuit correctly reiterated – via *M2 Software*—that likelihood assessments borrow heavily from underlying facts supplied at TTAB proceedings; thus it was improper to overturn well–supported factual findings simply because they feel doctrinally aggressive at first glance. However:

1️⃣ **Consumer Perception Might Diverge From Dictionary Logic**  
   While dictionaries describe both activities as involving extension of credit—a logical bridge—actual consumer surveys often reveal people distinguish between an entity they view as **“a bank”** versus one they label **“a card processor.”** A brand like Visa operates distinctively alongside major banks despite offering comparable credit facilities through seamless co‑branding arrangements—a nuance lost when redefining similarity purely via lexical overlap.\[3\] 

   Relying solely on textual definitions risks obscuring empirical reality—a problem amplified when emerging business models blur traditional roles but retain legacy consumer mindsets⁽4⁾.\[4\] The court’s reliance on dictionary meaning thus appears somewhat mechanistic and may prompt future litigants to demand contemporaneous consumer perception data alongside lexical analysis.


2️⃣ **Broadening Factor Two May Undermine Factor Six Consistency**  
   As Judge Hughes later observed concerning factor six—the number/nature of similar marks—the TTAB had drawn an overly narrow set when counting third-party Aspire marks confined only to credit Card vehicles even though many other registrations covered broader financing uses directly comparable to Apex’s proposed mark prospectus (“Aspire”, “Aspire Financial”, etc.). This inconsistency suggests an analytical vision where expanding similarity under one factor obliges parallel expansion across all remaining factors—a point underscored by vacatur/writ remand regarding factors one and six.\[5\]

3️⃣ **Policy Implications:**  
   By allowing legacy generic terms such as *Aspire* to gain protection across wide swaths—even encompassing core banking functions—trademark owners risk monopolizing otherwise descriptive vocabulary essential for competition among fintech start-ups aiming simply to convey aspirational qualities.\[6\] Such outcomes might stifle market entry unless challengers possess deep pockets capable of mounting costly oppositions based upon nuanced functional analyses.\[7\] 

Nevertheless material fact remains compelling_: Consumers today rarely differentiate whether their ASPIRE‐labeled card stemmed from CC Serve's processing arm or from Apex's partner bank—it appears under one brand umbrella regardless.\[8\] In those circumstances treating credit‐card issuance itself as effectively synonymous with providing financing seems well grounded procedurally even if it invites policy debates later via rulemaking or higher‐court clarification \([9]\).

## Doctrinal Ripple Effects  

### For Future Likelihood Analyses  

The decision magnifies three trends likely shaping upcoming disputes:

* **Functional Overlap Will Trump Nominal Classification:** Courts will increasingly scrutinize what aftermarket actions entail — i.e., extension of credit—rather than clinging strictly to USPTO class numbers (“36” vs.“35”) when evaluating factor 2.
* **Holistic Consideration Across All DuPont Factors:** Because conflicts among factors become evident (as seen here through vacatur/ remand tied together), tribunals will ensure consistent scope across each analysis stage—particularly avoiding divergent conceptions like narrow product sets for factor six versus broad ones for factor two.
* **Greater Emphasis On Empirical Consumer Evidence:** Given potential overreliance on dictionaries alone—and skepticism expressed herein—the next generation harmonics will likely request survey data demonstrating actual consumer confusion when ancillary financial offerings intersect.\[10\]

### Strategic Outlook For Market Participants  

Financial industry players seeking trademarks now face an expanded landscape:

* Existing banks should evaluate whether legacy service marks (e.g., loyalty program names) already enjoy blanket protection against fringe fintech uses—even if those latter entities do not handle deposits directly.
* Fintech entrants should consider employing supplemental qualifiers (“digital,” “mobile”) within their trade dress or seeking simultaneously multiple registrations covering narrower claims upon filing—avoid reliance solely upon broader commodities terminology susceptible to challenge via categorical overlap arguments.
* Trademark counsel must invest early discovery into how clients' marketing language positions their product relative to traditional banks versus processor roles; documenting distinct consumer pathways may become decisive evidence during dual examination phases (examination → opposition → appellate review).

### Potential Legislative or Administrative Response  

The USPTO may respond by issuing updated guidance concerning how postings in its Identifier Manual interact—or fail—to bind later TTAB assessments involving cross-category financial products—a clarification scarcely required before today’s fintech proliferation yet increasingly demanded now.\[11\]

Alternately Congress might explore statutory refinement defining “financial service”—perhaps reinstating clearer demarcations akin tothe pre‑2010 hierarchical classifications—to recalibrate balance between protecting expressive goodwill versus preserving free use descriptors vital within high‑competition sectors.\[12\]

## Concluding Thoughts  

*Apex Bank v.* CC Serve firm­ly establishes that trademark analysts must look beyond jargoned class labels toward substance-driven functionality when quantifying product similarity under DuPont factor 2. By anchoring its ruling primarily on dictionary meanings supplemented by external registration examples—and upholding those findings under substantial evidence standards—the Federal Circuit signaled devolution toward practical market realities inside likelihood assessments \[13\].

Yet this expansion carries warning flags: it raises consistency issues across other DuPont considerations and spotlights potential overreach into descriptive vocabularies crucial for newer players hunting footholds amid soaring fintech innovation.\[14\] As plaintiffs lean into follow-up district–court suits sharpening consumer perception data,
and defendants springboard upon lingering ambiguity within USPTO codes,
the jurisprudence born out of Apex will likely fertilize further rounds questioning where wherever borderlines between “banking’’and “credit–card’’services truly sit—and what peripheral brands get caught right at those juncture points.\[15\]

# De Novo Review Meets Substantial‑Evidence Scrutiny: What Apex Bank v. CC Serve Means for Trademark Appeals  

## Why This Decision Matters  

The Federal Circuit’s September 2025 opinion in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve* is a rare – and instructive – window into how appellate courts juggle two seemingly competing standards when they review Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) rulings. On one hand, the court reaffirmed that legal conclusions drawn by the Board are subject to **de novo** review, meaning the appellate panel may discard the Board’s reasoning and replace it with its own analysis. On the other hand, it reminded us that the same judges must apply a **substantial‑evidence** test to factual determinations embedded in each of the DuPont likelihood‑of‑confusion factors.  

For practitioners who regularly navigate TTAB oppositions, the opinion clarifies where a district or appellate court will intervene outright versus where it will merely police the evidentiary record. That distinction can shape everything from how much discovery a party undertakes before filing an opposition to how aggressively counsel should argue about the “relevant public” and third‑party marks at issue.  

## The Federal Circuit’s Review Blueprint  

The court began by grounding its approach in well‑established precedent: *M2 Software* and *Bose Corp.* teach that an appeal from a TTAB decision is reviewed **de novo** as to “legal conclusions” but only for “substantial evidence” as to any underlying “findings of fact.” In plain terms, if the Board’s reasoning about how a particular DuPont factor should be applied is legally faulty, the appellate court can redo that step entirely. If, however, the Board simply applied its chosen standard to a set of facts that are well‑supported by the record, those facts survive unscathed even if the panel disagrees with their ultimate weight.

That bifurcation proved central to *Apex*. The “second” DuPont factor – similarity of services – was deemed a factual inquiry based on service descriptions drawn from registration statements and industry dictionaries. The Federal Circuit found ample “relevant evidence” supporting the Board’s conclusion that credit‑card services and banking/financing services are “highly similar,” thus affirming that portion of the decision.

Conversely, when it turned to the “sixth” factor – number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods – the court identified a procedural misstep rather than a dispute over raw data. The Board had correctly identified nine third‑party “Aspire‑formative” marks tied to credit‑card services but then **arbitrarily excluded** over forty other Aspire‑related marks used in broader banking contexts because it limited its analysis to a narrower segment of consumers (i.e., credit‑card users). The appellate panel held that this restriction violated the principle that each factor’s factual inquiry must be consistent with findings under other factors—especially when another factor (the second) already attested to significant service overlap.

Because this error pertained to **how** the Board defined its factual universe—an issue more akin to legal reasoning than bare fact-finding—the court vacated both the sixth factor finding and, by extension, its first factor analysis (which hinges on commercial impression). The decision was sent back for a fresh evaluation that respects the broader similarity identified earlier in the opinion.

## Legal Conclusions vs. Factual Findings Across the DuPont Spectrum  

The *Apex* opinion inadvertently provides a useful checklist for spotting which DuPont factors are likely to be treated as “legal conclusions” versus those viewed as pure fact:

* **First Factor (Similarity of Marks)** – Courts routinely treat this as a mixed question: legal standards dictate what elements matter (sound, appearance, connotation), but applying those standards to specific marks often involves factual judgments about consumer perception. In *Apex*, because an error in factor six could reshape commercial impression assessments, the first factor was deemed inseparable from its legal analysis and thus remanded.
  
* **Second Factor (Similarity of Goods/Services)** – Largely factual once parties accept definitions from registries or dictionaries; thus subject to substantial‐evidence review.
  
* **Sixth Factor (Number/Nature of Similar Marks)** – Though it sounds empirical, deciding what counts as “similar goods” invites normative judgments about scope—a quasi‑legal determination at odds with strict fact appraisal.
  
* **Remaining Factors** (e.g., channels of trade, actual confusion) tend toward pure fact unless an appellate panel finds an improper legal framing influencing their weight.

The takeaway is that appellate courts will not hesitate to redo any factor whose *framework* appears legally flawed—even if it contains plenty of evidentiary support—while they will leave alone those analyses once they deem the supporting record adequate.

## What This Means for Litigants Contesting TTAB Decisions  

For trademark owners who find themselves on either side of a TTAB opposition or cancellation proceeding, *Apex* signals two practical imperatives:

1. **Build a Robust Evidentiary Record Early** – Since factual findings enjoy deference when backed by “substantial evidence,” propping up every element—especially service descriptions under ICAO classifications or consumer surveys—will make it harder for an appellate panel to overturn those determinations simply because it disagrees with their weight.
   
2. **Anticipate Cross‑Factor Consistency** – The Federal Circuit explicitly warned against treating one DuPont factor in isolation while imposing a tighter similarity test on another. Practitioners should preemptively align their arguments so that any asserted similarity under one factor logically extends—or at least does not contradict—their treatment under others. In *Apex*, failure to do so cost CC Serve its sixth‑factor win.
   
3. **Mind the Scope of Third‑Party Evidence** – The appellate panel highlighted that third‑party marks cannot be dismissed out‑of‑hand merely because they appear under different—but still related—service headings. A thorough compilation of all relevant third‑party registrations across related financial categories will better arm either party against claims of selective consideration.
   
4. **Consider Early Motion Practice** – If you foresee disputes over how broadly or narrowly the Board should interpret service similarities or market segments, filing motions requesting clarification—or even remand at TTAB level—might preserve strategic advantage before an appeal raises procedural bars.


## Potential Downstream Effects  

While *Apex* does not upend any longstanding doctrine—de novo review remains confined to legal errors—it does refine how courts enforce internal consistency among DuPont factors on appeal. A few implications loom:

* **Greater Scrutiny of Sixth‑Factor Analyses** – Boards may be compelled to cast wider nets when cataloguing third‑party marks, especially where services straddle multiple SIC/NAICS classifications. Expect more detailed briefs defining what constitutes “similar goods” beyond simple keyword matches.
   
* **Elevated Importance of Service Descriptions** – Because service similarity can become a linchpin influencing both second- and sixth-factor outcomes, applicants will likely invest more effort into crafting precise—and defensible—class listings during prosecution.
   
* **Possible Judicial Guidance on “Relevant Public”** – The opinion nudges courts toward adopting broader definitions of consumer groups where service overlap exists—a development that could blunt arguments asserting narrowly scoped confused publics.
   
* **Strategic Use of Appeals vs. District Court Actions** – Some practitioners may reconsider whether pursuing a § 1071(b) direct appeal (as here) offers clearer procedural terrain than filing a § 1071(a) civil action where district courts traditionally apply de novo review across-the-board. The Federal Circuit’s mixed standard reinforces that appeals provide an arena where factual findings retain substantial‐evidence protection yet still allow vigorous reexamination of legal reasoning.
   

Should subsequent panels extend this line of reasoning into other factors—say, channel‐of‐trade or actual confusion—the balance between board autonomy and judicial oversight could tilt further toward meticulous fact scrutiny without eroding established de novo safeguards.

## Bottom Line  

*Apex Bank v. CC Serve* reminds us why trademark appeals are as much about procedural rigor as they are about brand identity battles. By teasing apart when an appellate court can rewrite legal conclusions versus when it must merely test whether facts are adequately supported, the Federal Circuit has handed litigants clearer signals about where their arguments can thrive—and where they might fall flat if insufficiently documented.

For counsel navigating today’s crowded marketplace of financial services trademarks—and indeed any sector where overlapping product definitions blur traditional boundaries—this decision is both a cautionary tale and an opportunity: sharpen your factual foundation early, align every DuPont argument under one consistent view, and keep an eye on how higher courts may continue polishing this delicate procedural prism.*
