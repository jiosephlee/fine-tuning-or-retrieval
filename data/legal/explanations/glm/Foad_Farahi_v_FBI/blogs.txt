Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

## Indefinitely Pending: Exemption 7(A) and the Problem of Open-Ended Counterterrorism Investigations

The Freedom of Information Act embodies a basic presumption of government transparency, but that presumption bends dramatically when national security is involved. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the D.C. Circuit recently affirmed the FBI's decision to withhold almost the entirety of a 10,750-page file under FOIA Exemption 7(A), which protects records compiled for law enforcement purposes if their disclosure could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings. The case matters because it tests the outer limits of that exemption when applied to counterterrorism investigations that stretch across decades. The court’s decision offers a revealing look at how deference to the executive in national security cases can effectively neutralize FOIA’s temporal limits, creating what may function as an indefinite withholding mechanism for counterterrorism files.

## The "Pending or Reasonably Anticipated" Standard

To invoke Exemption 7(A), the government must show that release of the withheld records could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are "pending or reasonably anticipated." Under D.C. Circuit precedent, the government must identify a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding," though an ongoing criminal investigation counts as a "proceeding" for these purposes. So long as an investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by premature release, the exemption applies.

In *Farahi*, the court applied this standard with a heavy thumb on the scale in favor of the FBI. Judge Gregory Katsas, writing for the panel, emphasized that the FBI’s invocation of Exemption 7 is entitled to deference, particularly when national security concerns are implicated. This deference is not just a general principle; it is a doctrinal framework that shapes how the court evaluates the government’s evidence.

## A Surprisingly Terse Update

The procedural history of *Farahi* is central to the court’s reasoning. Farahi, an Iranian national who has lived in the United States since 1993, was placed in removal proceedings in 2002. In opposing his application for relief, DHS relied on FBI evidence documenting his alleged connections to high-level terrorists, including Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and Jose Padilla. Farahi subsequently FOIA’d his FBI file. The FBI withheld most of the records, citing an ongoing investigation.

The FBI initially supported its withholding with a declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of its Record/Information Dissemination Section, in 2019. The district court concluded that the FBI had "seemingly" met its burden but, given the passage of time, ordered the agency to update its assessment. When the FBI renewed its motion, it submitted a declaration from Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief. In his public declaration, Bender merely confirmed that the relevant investigations "remain ongoing."

Farahi argued that this statement was too conclusory to satisfy the "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" requirement. Judge Katsas agreed, candidly conceding that the Bender declaration "appears surprisingly terse" in isolation. However, the court upheld the withholding, reasoning that the declaration should not be read in isolation. Because the government had already established the pendency of enforcement proceedings in the 2019 Hardy declarations—both public and ex parte—the Bender declaration could be understood as a mere update confirming that nothing had changed.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

This reasoning move is analytically significant. By allowing a terse update to suffice when read against a prior, more detailed submission, the court may lower the bar for agencies seeking to maintain withholdings over long periods. If an agency can point to a robust initial declaration and then simply assert that the situation is unchanged years later, the "concrete prospective proceeding" requirement risks becoming a formality rather than a meaningful constraint.

The problem is compounded by the court’s reliance on the breadth of the underlying investigation. Judge Katsas noted that the FBI’s public allegations about Farahi—labeling him a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, connected to Al Qaeda members, and linked to convicted terrorists—made it "plausible" that the investigation remained ongoing. This creates a circular dynamic: the seriousness of the allegations is used to justify the plausibility of the ongoing investigation, which in turn justifies withholding the very records that might substantiate or undermine those allegations. In effect, the broader the investigation, the easier it is to maintain that it is still ongoing.

This dynamic effectively creates an open-ended exemption for counterterrorism files. So long as an investigation technically remains "ongoing"—and in the national security context, investigations can be kept open indefinitely—the FBI can invoke Exemption 7(A) to withhold records. The court’s deference to the executive, while consistent with prior D.C. Circuit precedent, leaves little room for FOIA requesters to challenge withholdings based on the passage of time.

The court’s segregability analysis further illustrates this problem. The FBI must release any reasonably segregable, nonexempt information. Farahi objected that there must be some releasable information in the vast volume of responsive records. The court rejected this argument, relying on the principle that if nonexempt material is interspersed line-by-line throughout a document, it is not reasonably segregable. The court also accepted the district court’s reliance on an ex parte declaration to resolve the segregability question, noting that Farahi had not opposed the acceptance of ex parte declarations.

While the court acknowledged that ex parte declarations "no doubt handicap FOIA plaintiffs," it treated this as an inherent feature of FOIA litigation involving classified or sensitive material. The result is that both the existence of the ongoing proceedings and the non-segregability of the records were established almost entirely through ex parte submissions that the requester could not challenge. The public record, by contrast, contained only a surprisingly terse update.

The decision in *Farahi* is a stark reminder of the challenges FOIA requesters face when seeking records related to national security investigations. The D.C. Circuit’s application of the "pending or reasonably anticipated" standard, combined with its deference to the FBI and its acceptance of ex parte evidence, effectively insulates counterterrorism files from disclosure. Whether this outcome is a necessary accommodation of national security imperatives or an undue expansion of Exemption 7(A) is a question the court did not squarely address. But the downstream effect is clear: in the context of open-ended counterterrorism investigations, Exemption 7(A) can function as an indefinite shield against transparency.



# Behind the Screen: Ex Parte Declarations and the Structural Asymmetry of National Security FOIA Litigation

The D.C. Circuit's decision last week in *Farahi v. FBI* arrives at a familiar destination—the affirmation of broad FBI withholding under FOIA Exemption 7(A)—but the path it takes there illuminates a structural feature of national security FOIA litigation that deserves more attention than it typically receives. The case is notable less for what the court held than for how the court held it: the government's justifications for withholding rested overwhelmingly on declarations submitted ex parte and under seal, invisible to the requester and to the public. The court acknowledged that this arrangement "handicaps" FOIA plaintiffs. But it concluded that this handicap is simply "inherent" in cases where public disclosure of the government's reasoning would itself defeat the purpose of the exemption. That framing has significant implications for the transparency values FOIA is supposed to serve.

## The Ex Parte Framework in *Farahi*

Foad Farahi sought his FBI file—some 10,750 pages and 80 CDs of potentially responsive records. The FBI withheld most of it, invoking Exemption 7(A), which protects records compiled for law enforcement purposes whose disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." The government supported its motion for summary judgment with declarations from David Hardy, then the Section Chief of the FBI's Record/Information Dissemination Section. Hardy submitted both a public declaration and an ex parte declaration, the latter filed under seal with the district court's permission. Farahi did not oppose the government's motion to file ex parte.

Years later, after the district court found that the FBI "seemingly" met its burden but ordered updated declarations given the passage of time, the government submitted a new set of public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, Hardy's successor. The public Bender declaration was, by the court's own admission, "surprisingly terse." It stated only that the relevant investigations "remain ongoing." The ex parte declaration "provided more details," but the court declined to disclose those details in its opinion. The court ultimately relied on the combination of the public and ex parte Hardy declarations and the ex parte Bender declaration to conclude that a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" continued to exist.

The segregability analysis followed a similar pattern. The district court explained that the FBI had provided a "detailed justification" for why the protected records were not reasonably segregable—but that justification came from Bender's supplemental ex parte submission. The court found this submission sufficient without placing its analysis of the declaration on the public record.

## The Waiver Trap

One of the most consequential procedural moments in *Farahi* came early: Farahi did not oppose the government's October 2020 motion to file the supplemental Hardy declaration ex parte and under seal. The court treated this non-opposition as a waiver. When Farahi later complained that the district court relied too heavily on ex parte declarations—particularly in the segregability analysis—the D.C. Circuit flatly rejected the argument. "Farahi did not oppose the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations," the court observed, "so he cannot challenge the district court's reliance on them."

This is doctrinally straightforward but practically troubling. At the point at which the government moved to file ex parte, Farahi had not yet seen the declaration. He had no way of knowing how heavily the court would rely on it, what it would contain, or how it would shape the proceedings. Opposing an ex parte motion at that stage would have required Farahi to object in the abstract—on the general principle that ex parte submissions are disfavored—without any sense of whether the specific content justified the secrecy. The waiver rule thus operates as a one-way ratchet: a plaintiff who declines to object blind effectively forfeits all future challenges to the court's reliance on materials he never saw.

To be fair, the court's approach is consistent with general waiver principles in civil litigation. A party who fails to object to a procedural mechanism generally cannot later challenge the consequences of that mechanism. But in the FOIA context, where the requester is already operating at a severe information disadvantage, the waiver doctrine interacts with the ex parte framework in ways that warrant more careful consideration than the court gave it here.

## The "Inherent" Handicap

The most revealing passage in the opinion comes near its end, where the court addresses Farahi's complaint about the district court's reliance on Bender's ex parte declaration in the segregability analysis. The court acknowledged that "ex parte declarations no doubt handicap FOIA plaintiffs." But it concluded that this problem "is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret."

This is not an unreasonable proposition. If the government's justification for withholding depends on describing the contents of the records, and if disclosing that description would itself reveal the information the exemption protects, then some degree of ex parte review may be inevitable. The court cited *Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. Department of Veterans Affairs* for this proposition, and the logic has a certain inexorable quality to it.

But the court's framing obscures an important question: at what point does the handicap become so severe that it undermines the adversarial process itself? In *Farahi*, the public record contained virtually nothing on which the plaintiff could mount a meaningful challenge. The public Bender declaration was "surprisingly terse," consisting of little more than a conclusory assertion that investigations remained "ongoing." The public Hardy declaration offered somewhat more but still operated at a high level of generality. The substantive justifications for withholding—the details of the investigation, the reasons why disclosure would interfere with it, the explanation for why no information was reasonably segregable—were all contained in ex parte filings that Farahi could not see or contest.

The court responded to this concern by emphasizing context. The public Hardy declaration, combined with what was already publicly known about Farahi—including the FBI's allegations in his immigration proceedings that he was "a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood," was "connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members," and was "linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists"—made plausible the government's representation that the investigation remained ongoing. But this is a different question from whether the government has carried its burden on the specific elements of Exemption 7(A). The fact that the FBI has made serious public allegations about Farahi does not, by itself, establish that an active investigation continues to gather evidence for a prospective criminal case. That showing depended on the ex parte declarations.

## Segregability and the "Information, Not Words" Principle

The segregability analysis highlights the problem acutely. FOIA requires agencies to take "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." The statute's focus, as the D.C. Circuit has long recognized, is on "information, … not simply words which the Government has written down." A small amount of non-exempt material randomly interspersed throughout a document is not "reasonably segregable" because releasing it would not convey meaningful information.

This is a sound principle, but its application in *Farahi* depended entirely on the ex parte Bender declaration. The district court found that the declaration provided a "detailed justification" for why no information was segregable across 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. But neither Farahi nor the public has any access to that justification. We are asked to take on faith that the FBI reviewed this vast trove of records and determined that every meaningful piece of information in them is inextricably intertwined with exempt material. Given the volume of records involved—10,750 pages is not a trivial collection—this is a remarkable conclusion, and one that the court's analysis does little to substantiate on the public record.

The court's response to Farahi's objection—that there "must be some segregable and releasable information" in such a large volume of records—was technically correct but substantively unsatisfying. The court simply restated the legal standard: if non-exempt material is interspersed line-by-line throughout exempt material, it is not reasonably segregable. But whether the material in *Farahi's* file actually meets that standard is a question the court answered by deferring to the ex parte declaration without any public explanation of what it contained.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

*Farahi* sits comfortably within the D.C. Circuit's established deference framework for national security FOIA cases. The court explicitly invoked the principle of deference to the executive "when national security concerns are implicated," citing *Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ* and its lineage. This deference is not new, but *Farahi* demonstrates how it operates in practice: the combination of deferential review and ex parte evidence creates a framework in which the government can withhold vast quantities of records on the basis of justifications that are entirely shielded from public scrutiny.

The downstream effects are predictable. Requesters in national security FOIA cases will find it increasingly difficult to mount meaningful challenges to withholding decisions. The strategic calculus around opposing ex parte motions will shift—requesters may feel compelled to object reflexively, even without knowing the content of the proposed filings, simply to preserve their ability to challenge reliance on those filings later. But reflexive objections may themselves be difficult to sustain, since courts will reasonably ask on what basis the requester opposes ex parte review before seeing the material at issue.

More broadly, *Farahi* raises questions about the point at which deference and ex parte review combine to eliminate the adversarial character of FOIA litigation. The government bears the burden of showing that an exemption applies. But when the government's evidentiary submissions are largely or entirely ex parte, and when the court's analysis of those submissions is conducted off the public record, the burden-shifting framework becomes difficult to evaluate. The court in *Farahi* did its best to explain its reasoning on the public record—detailing the public allegations against Farahi and the structure of the government's declarations—but the core of its analysis necessarily rested on materials it could not disclose.

This is the structural asymmetry the court acknowledged but did not resolve. Ex parte declarations may be "inherent" in certain FOIA cases, as the court said. But recognizing that something is inherent is not the same as accepting that it is unproblematic. The question for future cases is whether there are ways to narrow the information asymmetry—through more detailed public declarations, in camera review with summaries released to the requester, or clearer standards for when ex parte filings are truly necessary—without compromising the legitimate law enforcement and national security interests that Exemption 7(A) protects. *Farahi* did not attempt to answer that question. But it is one the D.C. Circuit will eventually need to confront.



# Stale Declarations and the Duty to Update: Procedural Practice in Long-Pending FOIA Cases

FOIA litigation often moves at a glacial pace. Cases can linger for years between the filing of a request and final judicial resolution, and the affidavits agencies submit to justify withholding can age significantly in the interim. The D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *Farahi v. FBI* offers a useful—and largely overlooked—procedural roadmap for how courts and agencies should handle the problem of stale declarations in protracted FOIA cases. While the opinion will likely be remembered for its straightforward application of Exemption 7(A) to national security records, its more enduring contribution may be its endorsement of a district court's authority to demand updated declarations and its treatment of those updates as supplements to—rather than replacements for—the original submissions.

## The Procedural Posture

Foad Farahi, an Iranian national with documented ties to multiple designated terrorist organizations, sought his FBI file through a FOIA request. The FBI located over 10,750 pages and 80 CDs of potentially responsive records, ultimately withholding most of them under Exemption 7(A), which protects records compiled for law enforcement purposes whose disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." The agency supported its initial summary judgment motion with public and ex parte declarations from David Hardy, then the Section Chief of the FBI's Record/Information Dissemination Section.

The problem was time. The FBI filed its summary judgment motion in October 2019. The district court did not rule until November 2022—more than three years later. Recognizing the problem, the court denied the motion without prejudice, noting the "significant passage of time" and ordering the FBI to update its assessment of whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated. The court also requested additional information on segregability, the details of which it could not state on the public record.

This sua sponte concern about temporal staleness is noteworthy. FOIA's statutory framework imposes no express time limit on the currency of agency declarations, and the D.C. Circuit has not articulated a bright-line rule. Yet the district court recognized that an affidavit attesting to ongoing proceedings in 2019 may have limited evidentiary value by 2022, particularly in the context of Exemption 7(A), where the government's burden hinges on demonstrating that enforcement proceedings are "pending or reasonably anticipated" at the time of judicial resolution.

## The D.C. Circuit's Endorsement

The D.C. Circuit affirmed, and in doing so, endorsed the district court's approach with implications that extend well beyond this case.

The critical analytical move came in the court's treatment of the supplemental declaration from Joseph Bender, who had replaced Hardy as Acting Section Chief of RIDS. Bender's public declaration was, by the court's own admission, "surprisingly terse." It stated that RIDS had contacted the agent responsible for overseeing Farahi's file and confirmed that investigations remained ongoing. Farahi argued that this conclusory assertion was insufficient under the D.C. Circuit's precedent in *CREW v. DOJ*, which warned that the "vague nature" of an assertion that investigations remain ongoing may not suffice to support an Exemption 7(A) invocation.

The court agreed that the Bender declaration, "considered in isolation," appeared deficient. But it refused to consider the declaration in isolation. Instead, it contextualized Bender's submission as an update to the Hardy declarations, which had "establish[ed] the pendency of enforcement proceedings" with greater specificity—including an ex parte declaration that "gave considerable detail about the nature and scope of the investigation." The court also noted that the district court had already concluded the FBI "seemingly" met its burden before ordering the update, further supporting the characterization of the Bender declaration as a confirmation that nothing had changed rather than a standalone justification.

This is a significant procedural holding, even if the court framed it as straightforward common sense. It establishes that supplemental declarations in FOIA cases need not re-establish the government's entire burden from scratch. Instead, they can function as temporal updates—confirming that the factual predicates underlying earlier, more detailed submissions remain intact. The original declarations provide the substantive foundation; the update provides the temporal bridge.

## Doctrinal Implications

This approach resolves a practical problem that has long vexed FOIA litigation but has received surprisingly little sustained doctrinal attention. When years pass between an agency's original declaration and judicial resolution, what exactly must the government do to refresh the record?

The *Farahi* court's answer is layered. The original declaration must satisfy the government's substantive burden with "reasonable specificity." The supplemental declaration need only confirm the continuing validity of the original's factual predicates. If the original declaration was detailed and the update is cursory but consistent, the combined record suffices. If the original was cursory and the update is also cursory, the government likely fails.

This formulation has several downstream consequences. First, it gives agencies a practical template for satisfying their burden in protracted cases. Rather than submitting entirely new declarations each time a court questions the currency of the record, agencies can file concise updates that incorporate by reference the substance of their earlier submissions. This reduces the burden on agencies and the risk that updated declarations will inadvertently omit details contained in earlier filings.

Second, it provides FOIA plaintiffs with a clear argumentative framework for challenging stale declarations. A plaintiff who believes the government's showing has grown attenuated with time should focus not merely on the brevity of any supplemental declaration, but on whether the original declaration was sufficiently detailed to support the exemption in the first place. If the original was robust, the plaintiff's task is harder: they must identify some reason to believe the factual predicates have changed—not merely argue that the update is terse.

Third, the opinion implicitly sanctions the practice of relying on ex parte declarations to satisfy the temporal update function. The Bender declaration included both public and ex parte components, and the district court relied on the ex parte submission to evaluate both the existence of ongoing proceedings and the adequacy of the FBI's segregability analysis. The D.C. Circuit acknowledged that ex parte declarations "handicap FOIA plaintiffs" but accepted this as an inherent feature of cases where public disclosure of the justification would itself reveal the protected information. Farahi did not oppose the acceptance of ex parte declarations, so the court did not directly address the question of whether a plaintiff who does object is entitled to any additional procedural protections.

## Unanswered Questions

The opinion leaves several important questions unresolved. The court did not specify how much time must pass before a court may require updated declarations, nor whether agencies have an independent obligation to refresh stale declarations absent a court order. The district court's sua sponte action in this case suggests that courts may raise temporal staleness on their own initiative, but the D.C. Circuit did not explicitly hold that they must do so.

More importantly, the opinion does not address what happens when the factual predicates underlying an original declaration genuinely change during the pendency of litigation. The *Farahi* facts presented an unusually clean case: the FBI's investigation was extraordinarily broad, involving multiple designated terrorist organizations and connections to high-level Al Qaeda operatives, and the government represented that the investigation remained ongoing. Where an investigation narrows, shifts focus, or is formally closed during litigation, the update function becomes more complex. A terse declaration that "investigations remain ongoing" may be technically accurate but substantively misleading if the nature of the ongoing proceedings has changed in ways that affect the applicability of the claimed exemption.

The D.C. Circuit's contextual approach suggests that courts should evaluate whether the update is consistent with the original declaration's factual predicates—not merely whether the update, viewed in isolation, is accurate. But the opinion does not explicitly establish this standard, and future cases will need to address the more difficult scenarios the court had the luxury of avoiding here.

## The Broader Picture

*Farahi* also sits at the intersection of two recurring tensions in FOIA litigation: the deference owed to agency determinations in national security cases and the procedural safeguards necessary to ensure that deference does not become a blank check. The court's deference to the FBI's exemption invocation was straightforward, citing well-established precedent that the agency's law enforcement determinations are entitled to particular deference when national security concerns are implicated. But the procedural story is more nuanced. The district court did not simply accept the FBI's original declarations at face value; it required the agency to come forward with updated information before granting summary judgment. The D.C. Circuit affirmed this approach without requiring the government to re-establish its entire evidentiary foundation.

The result is a practical accommodation. Agencies retain substantial deference in national security FOIA cases, but courts retain the authority to demand temporal currency. Plaintiffs retain the right to challenge vague or conclusory submissions, but they cannot prevail merely by pointing to the passage of time when the government has provided a detailed original declaration and a consistent update. For a case that could easily have been a routine Exemption 7(A) affirmation, *Farahi* offers a surprisingly useful procedural framework for navigating the persistent problem of stale declarations in long-pending FOIA litigation.



# Words vs. Information: The Segregability Standard and Vast Government Records

When the FBI withheld approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs of records from a FOIA requester in their entirety, the D.C. Circuit faced a deceptively simple question: does the sheer volume of responsive records mean that *something* must be segregable and releasable? In *Farahi v. FBI*, the court answered no—a holding that sheds light on how the "reasonably segregable" standard operates when agencies confront massive files interlaced with exempt material.

## The Case and the Segregability Dispute

Foad Farahi, an Iranian national who has lived in the United States since 1993, requested his full FBI file. The FBI identified a staggering volume of responsive records and, after review, withheld nearly all of them under FOIA Exemption 7(A), which protects records compiled for law enforcement purposes whose disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings. The government justified its wholesale withholding in part through ex parte declarations submitted under seal—declarations that both the district court and the D.C. Circuit reviewed but whose contents remain undisclosed.

The segregability question occupied a significant portion of the court's analysis. FOIA requires agencies to take "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information," and the statute mandates that "[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided" after deletion of exempt material. Farahi pressed a commonsense argument: given the vast volume of responsive records, it strains credulity that *no* meaningful information could be extracted and released. Surely, somewhere in those 10,750 pages, there must be individual words, phrases, or even passages that could be disclosed without compromising the investigation.

The court rejected this line of reasoning, and its reasoning deserves close attention.

## The Mead Data Central Principle: Information, Not Words

The analytical heart of the court's segregability discussion is its reliance on *Mead Data Central, Inc. v. Department of the Air Force*, a 1977 D.C. Circuit decision that articulated a crucial distinction in FOIA law. As the court explained, "the focus of the FOIA is information, not simply words which the Government has written down." This principle, drawn directly from *Mead*, holds that FOIA's segregability requirement is not a word-by-word or line-by-line erasure exercise. The question is whether coherent, meaningful *information* can be separated from exempt material—not whether individual words might technically be disclosed without harm.

The court then applied this principle through an established corollary: when a small amount of non-exempt material is "interspersed line-by-line throughout" a document, that material is not likely to be "reasonably segregable." Citing *Mead* and its progeny, the court acknowledged that while individual words or phrases in the requested records might be disclosed without harming the FBI's interests, that observation is "neither here nor there." The court framed the issue correctly: FOIA asks whether meaningful information can be extricated, not whether isolated words can survive redaction.

This framing is doctrinally sound and traces a clear line from *Mead* through *Juarez v. DOJ* and *Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. Department of Veterans Affairs*. But it also raises a practical tension that the court did not fully address.

## The Tension with FOIA's Statutory Mandate

The D.C. Circuit's approach sits in some tension with FOIA's statutory text, which states that "[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt." The word "any" is broad. It suggests a demanding standard—one that could be read to require agencies to hunt for even small fragments of releasable information, so long as they are "reasonably" segregable. The statute does not say "any meaningful information" or "any coherent passage"; it says "any reasonably segregable portion."

The *Mead* principle partially resolves this tension by redefining what counts as a "portion." If a "portion" must contain meaningful information to qualify as segregable, then scattered individual words are not "portions" in the relevant sense. This is a reasonable interpretive move, and it has the virtue of preventing FOIA from becoming an exercise in producing redacted gibberish. But it is an interpretive move nonetheless—one that reads a qualitative requirement into statutory language that, on its face, appears more mechanical.

The tension becomes more acute when the volume of withheld records is as large as it is here. When an agency withholds a few dozen pages in their entirety, the *Mead* principle is relatively uncontroversial. When the agency withholds over 10,000 pages and 80 CDs, the principle begins to look like something more: a practical safe harbor that permits agencies to avoid line-by-line review of enormous files by invoking the "inextricably intertwined" standard.

## The "Inextricably Intertwined" Standard as Practical Safe Harbor

The court's opinion invokes the established rule that the government may withhold records in their entirety "when nonexempt portions are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions." This standard, drawn from *Mead* and reaffirmed in *Juarez*, is well-established in D.C. Circuit law. But *Farahi* illustrates how the standard functions in practice when applied to vast collections of records.

The district court found that the FBI had provided a "detailed justification" for why the protected records were not reasonably segregable, based on a supplemental ex parte declaration from the FBI's Acting Section Chief. The D.C. Circuit deferred to this finding, noting that the district court had reviewed the ex parte submission and was satisfied that the agency had met its burden. What we cannot see, of course, is the substance of that justification—the court acknowledged as much, noting that it could not disclose the contents of the ex parte declaration.

This is where the doctrinal rubber meets the practical road. When an agency submits an ex parte declaration explaining why 10,750 pages cannot be segregated, and the district court accepts that explanation after an in camera review, the appellate court is in no position to second-guess the factual sufficiency of the justification. The D.C. Circuit was candid about this structural limitation: "ex parte declarations no doubt handicap FOIA plaintiffs," but that problem "is inherent in those FOIA cases where an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret."

The result is that in cases involving large volumes of records and national security concerns, the "inextricably intertwined" standard effectively operates as a practical safe harbor. The agency needs to provide a sufficiently detailed ex parte explanation, and the district court needs to credit it. Once those conditions are met, the appellate court is unlikely to disturb the finding—particularly when, as here, the case implicates counterterrorism investigations and the court has already signaled its deference to the executive branch in the national security context.

## Downstream Implications

The *Farahi* opinion does not break new doctrinal ground on segregability. The court applies *Mead* and its progeny in a straightforward manner. But the case is illustrative of how the segregability standard operates at the intersection of large-volume requests, national security exemptions, and ex parte review.

Three implications are worth noting. First, the opinion reinforces that the D.C. Circuit will not infer a segregability failure from the mere volume of withheld records. Requesters who argue that "there must be something releasable in there" will need to point to something more specific than the page count. Farahi's failure to identify particular categories of documents that he believed were overbroad proved fatal to his challenge—the court held that he had forfeited this argument by not developing it.

Second, the case underscores the structural asymmetry that ex parte declarations create in FOIA litigation. The court acknowledged this asymmetry but treated it as an inherent feature of certain FOIA cases rather than a problem requiring remediation. Litigants who fail to object to the use of ex parte declarations—as Farahi did—will find it difficult to challenge the court's reliance on those declarations on appeal.

Third, the opinion suggests that in national security cases, the combination of Exemption 7(A) and the "inextricably intertwined" segregability standard gives agencies substantial latitude to withhold entire files. Whether this latitude is warranted as a matter of policy is a question the court did not address. The court's job was to apply the law as it stands, and on that score, the opinion is a faithful application of existing doctrine. But the case is a reminder that existing doctrine, faithfully applied, can produce results in which a requester receives essentially nothing from a file of over 10,000 pages—and has limited tools to challenge that outcome.

## Conclusion

*Farahi* is a quiet case in doctrinal terms but a consequential one in practical effect. The D.C. Circuit's segregability analysis is consistent with decades of precedent, and the court was transparent about the limitations of its review. The opinion is best understood not as a new frontier in segregability law but as a clear illustration of how the existing framework operates when volume, national security, and ex parte review converge. The *Mead* principle—that FOIA protects information, not words—is doctrinally sound. The harder question, which *Farahi* raises but does not resolve, is whether that principle should function as a practical ceiling on what agencies must release from vast files, or whether the statute's plain command to disclose "any reasonably segregable portion" demands something more.
