Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Behind the Screen: Ex Parte Declarations and the Structural Asymmetry of National Security FOIA Litigation

The D.C. Circuit's decision last week in *Farahi v. FBI* arrives at a familiar destination—the affirmation of broad FBI withholding under FOIA Exemption 7(A)—but the path it takes there illuminates a structural feature of national security FOIA litigation that deserves more attention than it typically receives. The case is notable less for what the court held than for how the court held it: the government's justifications for withholding rested overwhelmingly on declarations submitted ex parte and under seal, invisible to the requester and to the public. The court acknowledged that this arrangement "handicaps" FOIA plaintiffs. But it concluded that this handicap is simply "inherent" in cases where public disclosure of the government's reasoning would itself defeat the purpose of the exemption. That framing has significant implications for the transparency values FOIA is supposed to serve.

## The Ex Parte Framework in *Farahi*

Foad Farahi sought his FBI file—some 10,750 pages and 80 CDs of potentially responsive records. The FBI withheld most of it, invoking Exemption 7(A), which protects records compiled for law enforcement purposes whose disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." The government supported its motion for summary judgment with declarations from David Hardy, then the Section Chief of the FBI's Record/Information Dissemination Section. Hardy submitted both a public declaration and an ex parte declaration, the latter filed under seal with the district court's permission. Farahi did not oppose the government's motion to file ex parte.

Years later, after the district court found that the FBI "seemingly" met its burden but ordered updated declarations given the passage of time, the government submitted a new set of public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, Hardy's successor. The public Bender declaration was, by the court's own admission, "surprisingly terse." It stated only that the relevant investigations "remain ongoing." The ex parte declaration "provided more details," but the court declined to disclose those details in its opinion. The court ultimately relied on the combination of the public and ex parte Hardy declarations and the ex parte Bender declaration to conclude that a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" continued to exist.

The segregability analysis followed a similar pattern. The district court explained that the FBI had provided a "detailed justification" for why the protected records were not reasonably segregable—but that justification came from Bender's supplemental ex parte submission. The court found this submission sufficient without placing its analysis of the declaration on the public record.

## The Waiver Trap

One of the most consequential procedural moments in *Farahi* came early: Farahi did not oppose the government's October 2020 motion to file the supplemental Hardy declaration ex parte and under seal. The court treated this non-opposition as a waiver. When Farahi later complained that the district court relied too heavily on ex parte declarations—particularly in the segregability analysis—the D.C. Circuit flatly rejected the argument. "Farahi did not oppose the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations," the court observed, "so he cannot challenge the district court's reliance on them."

This is doctrinally straightforward but practically troubling. At the point at which the government moved to file ex parte, Farahi had not yet seen the declaration. He had no way of knowing how heavily the court would rely on it, what it would contain, or how it would shape the proceedings. Opposing an ex parte motion at that stage would have required Farahi to object in the abstract—on the general principle that ex parte submissions are disfavored—without any sense of whether the specific content justified the secrecy. The waiver rule thus operates as a one-way ratchet: a plaintiff who declines to object blind effectively forfeits all future challenges to the court's reliance on materials he never saw.

To be fair, the court's approach is consistent with general waiver principles in civil litigation. A party who fails to object to a procedural mechanism generally cannot later challenge the consequences of that mechanism. But in the FOIA context, where the requester is already operating at a severe information disadvantage, the waiver doctrine interacts with the ex parte framework in ways that warrant more careful consideration than the court gave it here.

## The "Inherent" Handicap

The most revealing passage in the opinion comes near its end, where the court addresses Farahi's complaint about the district court's reliance on Bender's ex parte declaration in the segregability analysis. The court acknowledged that "ex parte declarations no doubt handicap FOIA plaintiffs." But it concluded that this problem "is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret."

This is not an unreasonable proposition. If the government's justification for withholding depends on describing the contents of the records, and if disclosing that description would itself reveal the information the exemption protects, then some degree of ex parte review may be inevitable. The court cited *Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. Department of Veterans Affairs* for this proposition, and the logic has a certain inexorable quality to it.

But the court's framing obscures an important question: at what point does the handicap become so severe that it undermines the adversarial process itself? In *Farahi*, the public record contained virtually nothing on which the plaintiff could mount a meaningful challenge. The public Bender declaration was "surprisingly terse," consisting of little more than a conclusory assertion that investigations remained "ongoing." The public Hardy declaration offered somewhat more but still operated at a high level of generality. The substantive justifications for withholding—the details of the investigation, the reasons why disclosure would interfere with it, the explanation for why no information was reasonably segregable—were all contained in ex parte filings that Farahi could not see or contest.

The court responded to this concern by emphasizing context. The public Hardy declaration, combined with what was already publicly known about Farahi—including the FBI's allegations in his immigration proceedings that he was "a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood," was "connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members," and was "linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists"—made plausible the government's representation that the investigation remained ongoing. But this is a different question from whether the government has carried its burden on the specific elements of Exemption 7(A). The fact that the FBI has made serious public allegations about Farahi does not, by itself, establish that an active investigation continues to gather evidence for a prospective criminal case. That showing depended on the ex parte declarations.

## Segregability and the "Information, Not Words" Principle

The segregability analysis highlights the problem acutely. FOIA requires agencies to take "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." The statute's focus, as the D.C. Circuit has long recognized, is on "information, … not simply words which the Government has written down." A small amount of non-exempt material randomly interspersed throughout a document is not "reasonably segregable" because releasing it would not convey meaningful information.

This is a sound principle, but its application in *Farahi* depended entirely on the ex parte Bender declaration. The district court found that the declaration provided a "detailed justification" for why no information was segregable across 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. But neither Farahi nor the public has any access to that justification. We are asked to take on faith that the FBI reviewed this vast trove of records and determined that every meaningful piece of information in them is inextricably intertwined with exempt material. Given the volume of records involved—10,750 pages is not a trivial collection—this is a remarkable conclusion, and one that the court's analysis does little to substantiate on the public record.

The court's response to Farahi's objection—that there "must be some segregable and releasable information" in such a large volume of records—was technically correct but substantively unsatisfying. The court simply restated the legal standard: if non-exempt material is interspersed line-by-line throughout exempt material, it is not reasonably segregable. But whether the material in *Farahi's* file actually meets that standard is a question the court answered by deferring to the ex parte declaration without any public explanation of what it contained.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

*Farahi* sits comfortably within the D.C. Circuit's established deference framework for national security FOIA cases. The court explicitly invoked the principle of deference to the executive "when national security concerns are implicated," citing *Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ* and its lineage. This deference is not new, but *Farahi* demonstrates how it operates in practice: the combination of deferential review and ex parte evidence creates a framework in which the government can withhold vast quantities of records on the basis of justifications that are entirely shielded from public scrutiny.

The downstream effects are predictable. Requesters in national security FOIA cases will find it increasingly difficult to mount meaningful challenges to withholding decisions. The strategic calculus around opposing ex parte motions will shift—requesters may feel compelled to object reflexively, even without knowing the content of the proposed filings, simply to preserve their ability to challenge reliance on those filings later. But reflexive objections may themselves be difficult to sustain, since courts will reasonably ask on what basis the requester opposes ex parte review before seeing the material at issue.

More broadly, *Farahi* raises questions about the point at which deference and ex parte review combine to eliminate the adversarial character of FOIA litigation. The government bears the burden of showing that an exemption applies. But when the government's evidentiary submissions are largely or entirely ex parte, and when the court's analysis of those submissions is conducted off the public record, the burden-shifting framework becomes difficult to evaluate. The court in *Farahi* did its best to explain its reasoning on the public record—detailing the public allegations against Farahi and the structure of the government's declarations—but the core of its analysis necessarily rested on materials it could not disclose.

This is the structural asymmetry the court acknowledged but did not resolve. Ex parte declarations may be "inherent" in certain FOIA cases, as the court said. But recognizing that something is inherent is not the same as accepting that it is unproblematic. The question for future cases is whether there are ways to narrow the information asymmetry—through more detailed public declarations, in camera review with summaries released to the requester, or clearer standards for when ex parte filings are truly necessary—without compromising the legitimate law enforcement and national security interests that Exemption 7(A) protects. *Farahi* did not attempt to answer that question. But it is one the D.C. Circuit will eventually need to confront.
