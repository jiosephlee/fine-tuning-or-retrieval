Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Stale Declarations and the Duty to Update: Procedural Practice in Long-Pending FOIA Cases

FOIA litigation often moves at a glacial pace. Cases can linger for years between the filing of a request and final judicial resolution, and the affidavits agencies submit to justify withholding can age significantly in the interim. The D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *Farahi v. FBI* offers a useful—and largely overlooked—procedural roadmap for how courts and agencies should handle the problem of stale declarations in protracted FOIA cases. While the opinion will likely be remembered for its straightforward application of Exemption 7(A) to national security records, its more enduring contribution may be its endorsement of a district court's authority to demand updated declarations and its treatment of those updates as supplements to—rather than replacements for—the original submissions.

## The Procedural Posture

Foad Farahi, an Iranian national with documented ties to multiple designated terrorist organizations, sought his FBI file through a FOIA request. The FBI located over 10,750 pages and 80 CDs of potentially responsive records, ultimately withholding most of them under Exemption 7(A), which protects records compiled for law enforcement purposes whose disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." The agency supported its initial summary judgment motion with public and ex parte declarations from David Hardy, then the Section Chief of the FBI's Record/Information Dissemination Section.

The problem was time. The FBI filed its summary judgment motion in October 2019. The district court did not rule until November 2022—more than three years later. Recognizing the problem, the court denied the motion without prejudice, noting the "significant passage of time" and ordering the FBI to update its assessment of whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated. The court also requested additional information on segregability, the details of which it could not state on the public record.

This sua sponte concern about temporal staleness is noteworthy. FOIA's statutory framework imposes no express time limit on the currency of agency declarations, and the D.C. Circuit has not articulated a bright-line rule. Yet the district court recognized that an affidavit attesting to ongoing proceedings in 2019 may have limited evidentiary value by 2022, particularly in the context of Exemption 7(A), where the government's burden hinges on demonstrating that enforcement proceedings are "pending or reasonably anticipated" at the time of judicial resolution.

## The D.C. Circuit's Endorsement

The D.C. Circuit affirmed, and in doing so, endorsed the district court's approach with implications that extend well beyond this case.

The critical analytical move came in the court's treatment of the supplemental declaration from Joseph Bender, who had replaced Hardy as Acting Section Chief of RIDS. Bender's public declaration was, by the court's own admission, "surprisingly terse." It stated that RIDS had contacted the agent responsible for overseeing Farahi's file and confirmed that investigations remained ongoing. Farahi argued that this conclusory assertion was insufficient under the D.C. Circuit's precedent in *CREW v. DOJ*, which warned that the "vague nature" of an assertion that investigations remain ongoing may not suffice to support an Exemption 7(A) invocation.

The court agreed that the Bender declaration, "considered in isolation," appeared deficient. But it refused to consider the declaration in isolation. Instead, it contextualized Bender's submission as an update to the Hardy declarations, which had "establish[ed] the pendency of enforcement proceedings" with greater specificity—including an ex parte declaration that "gave considerable detail about the nature and scope of the investigation." The court also noted that the district court had already concluded the FBI "seemingly" met its burden before ordering the update, further supporting the characterization of the Bender declaration as a confirmation that nothing had changed rather than a standalone justification.

This is a significant procedural holding, even if the court framed it as straightforward common sense. It establishes that supplemental declarations in FOIA cases need not re-establish the government's entire burden from scratch. Instead, they can function as temporal updates—confirming that the factual predicates underlying earlier, more detailed submissions remain intact. The original declarations provide the substantive foundation; the update provides the temporal bridge.

## Doctrinal Implications

This approach resolves a practical problem that has long vexed FOIA litigation but has received surprisingly little sustained doctrinal attention. When years pass between an agency's original declaration and judicial resolution, what exactly must the government do to refresh the record?

The *Farahi* court's answer is layered. The original declaration must satisfy the government's substantive burden with "reasonable specificity." The supplemental declaration need only confirm the continuing validity of the original's factual predicates. If the original declaration was detailed and the update is cursory but consistent, the combined record suffices. If the original was cursory and the update is also cursory, the government likely fails.

This formulation has several downstream consequences. First, it gives agencies a practical template for satisfying their burden in protracted cases. Rather than submitting entirely new declarations each time a court questions the currency of the record, agencies can file concise updates that incorporate by reference the substance of their earlier submissions. This reduces the burden on agencies and the risk that updated declarations will inadvertently omit details contained in earlier filings.

Second, it provides FOIA plaintiffs with a clear argumentative framework for challenging stale declarations. A plaintiff who believes the government's showing has grown attenuated with time should focus not merely on the brevity of any supplemental declaration, but on whether the original declaration was sufficiently detailed to support the exemption in the first place. If the original was robust, the plaintiff's task is harder: they must identify some reason to believe the factual predicates have changed—not merely argue that the update is terse.

Third, the opinion implicitly sanctions the practice of relying on ex parte declarations to satisfy the temporal update function. The Bender declaration included both public and ex parte components, and the district court relied on the ex parte submission to evaluate both the existence of ongoing proceedings and the adequacy of the FBI's segregability analysis. The D.C. Circuit acknowledged that ex parte declarations "handicap FOIA plaintiffs" but accepted this as an inherent feature of cases where public disclosure of the justification would itself reveal the protected information. Farahi did not oppose the acceptance of ex parte declarations, so the court did not directly address the question of whether a plaintiff who does object is entitled to any additional procedural protections.

## Unanswered Questions

The opinion leaves several important questions unresolved. The court did not specify how much time must pass before a court may require updated declarations, nor whether agencies have an independent obligation to refresh stale declarations absent a court order. The district court's sua sponte action in this case suggests that courts may raise temporal staleness on their own initiative, but the D.C. Circuit did not explicitly hold that they must do so.

More importantly, the opinion does not address what happens when the factual predicates underlying an original declaration genuinely change during the pendency of litigation. The *Farahi* facts presented an unusually clean case: the FBI's investigation was extraordinarily broad, involving multiple designated terrorist organizations and connections to high-level Al Qaeda operatives, and the government represented that the investigation remained ongoing. Where an investigation narrows, shifts focus, or is formally closed during litigation, the update function becomes more complex. A terse declaration that "investigations remain ongoing" may be technically accurate but substantively misleading if the nature of the ongoing proceedings has changed in ways that affect the applicability of the claimed exemption.

The D.C. Circuit's contextual approach suggests that courts should evaluate whether the update is consistent with the original declaration's factual predicates—not merely whether the update, viewed in isolation, is accurate. But the opinion does not explicitly establish this standard, and future cases will need to address the more difficult scenarios the court had the luxury of avoiding here.

## The Broader Picture

*Farahi* also sits at the intersection of two recurring tensions in FOIA litigation: the deference owed to agency determinations in national security cases and the procedural safeguards necessary to ensure that deference does not become a blank check. The court's deference to the FBI's exemption invocation was straightforward, citing well-established precedent that the agency's law enforcement determinations are entitled to particular deference when national security concerns are implicated. But the procedural story is more nuanced. The district court did not simply accept the FBI's original declarations at face value; it required the agency to come forward with updated information before granting summary judgment. The D.C. Circuit affirmed this approach without requiring the government to re-establish its entire evidentiary foundation.

The result is a practical accommodation. Agencies retain substantial deference in national security FOIA cases, but courts retain the authority to demand temporal currency. Plaintiffs retain the right to challenge vague or conclusory submissions, but they cannot prevail merely by pointing to the passage of time when the government has provided a detailed original declaration and a consistent update. For a case that could easily have been a routine Exemption 7(A) affirmation, *Farahi* offers a surprisingly useful procedural framework for navigating the persistent problem of stale declarations in long-pending FOIA litigation.
