Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What counts as "enforcement proceedings" under FOIA Exemption 7(A)—do ongoing criminal investigations qualify by themselves?
Question:
In Farahi v. FBI, the court says that "ongoing criminal investigations count as 'proceedings'" under Exemption 7(A), citing CREW v. DOJ. I'm confused because I thought "proceedings" implied something more formal—like a filed case or a formal adjudication. How can a criminal investigation that hasn't resulted in charges be a "proceeding"? What's the threshold for when an investigation becomes a "proceeding" under Exemption 7(A)? Does the government just need to say the investigation is gathering evidence for a possible future case, as the court suggests quoting Juarez?
Answer:
## Answer

Your intuition that "proceedings" ordinarily implies something formal is understandable, but Exemption 7(A) uses a broader, specialized definition. The D.C. Circuit has expressly held that **ongoing criminal investigations qualify as "proceedings"** under the exemption, even before any charges are filed or any formal case is opened.

### The governing standard

The court in *Farahi* states the rule clearly:

> "Ongoing criminal investigations count as 'proceedings' for these purposes, and the government may show such proceedings simply by attesting to their 'existence.'" (citing *CREW v. DOJ*, 746 F.3d at 1097–98, 1099)

It then quotes *Juarez v. DOJ* for the operative threshold:

> "So long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies." (518 F.3d at 59)

So the court does not require a filed indictment, a formal adjudication, or any specific charging decision. An investigation that is **actively gathering evidence toward a potential future prosecution** suffices.

### But the government cannot be conclusory

The opinion does set some limits. The proceeding must be a "**concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding**," not a vague or hypothetical one (*CREW*, 746 F.3d at 1097). And *Farahi* explicitly acknowledges that "the 'vague nature' of an assertion that investigations remain ongoing may not suffice to support the invocation of Exemption 7(A)" (quoting *CREW*, 746 F.3d at 1098).

That concern was actually at issue in *Farahi* itself. The FBI's updated Bender declaration—which simply stated that investigations "remain ongoing"—was "surprisingly terse" standing alone. The court sustained it only because it was not meant to be read in isolation: the earlier Hardy declarations had already established, in detail, the existence and scope of the investigation, and Bender's submission was merely confirming that nothing had changed in the interim. The court also relied on the breadth and seriousness of the underlying investigation—documented ties to designated terrorist organizations and convicted terrorists—to find the government's representation plausible.

### So what is the threshold?

Drawing the opinion together, the government meets its burden under Exemption 7(A) when it shows, **with reasonable specificity**, that:

1. **Records were compiled for law enforcement purposes**—here, the FBI's national-security and counterterrorism investigation (*Farahi* § IV.A);
2. **An investigation is ongoing** and continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case (*Juarez*, 518 F.3d at 59); and
3. **Disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere** with that investigation—for example, by tipping off targets about the scope of the inquiry (*Farahi* § IV.B, citing *CNSS*, 331 F.3d at 929).

The government need not point to a filed case or formal charges. But a bare, conclusory assertion that an investigation is "ongoing" may be insufficient, particularly without supporting detail or context. The court will look at the totality of the agency's submissions—public and ex parte—to determine whether the government has carried its burden.

### Practical takeaway

Exemption 7(A)'s "enforcement proceedings" requirement is meaningfully easier to satisfy than the formal-sounding term "proceedings" might suggest. In the D.C. Circuit, an active criminal investigation is enough, provided the government explains with reasonable specificity what the investigation involves and how disclosure would interfere with it. The real analytical focus in these cases is usually not whether a "proceeding" exists, but whether the government's showing is sufficiently concrete and specific to satisfy its burden of proof.
