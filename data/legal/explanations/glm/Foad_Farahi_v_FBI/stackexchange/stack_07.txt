Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What are the requirements for category-based withholding in FOIA, and what happens if a plaintiff fails to specifically challenge the categories?
Question:
The Farahi opinion mentions that agencies using category-based withholding must use categories that are "sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied" to each category, citing CREW. Farahi objected that the FBI's categories were overbroad, but the court said he "provided no specific explanation" and therefore forfeited his argument. What does a plaintiff need to do to properly challenge category overbreadth? Does the plaintiff need to have seen the documents to challenge the categories, or can they challenge the categories based on the agency's description alone? How specific does the objection need to be?
Answer:
## Answer

The opinion addresses category-based withholding in Section IV.C, though it does so briefly. Here is what can be drawn from it.

### The Legal Standard

Under *CREW v. DOJ*, 746 F.3d 1082, 1088 (D.C. Cir. 2014), when an agency withholds documents on a category-by-category basis, it must use categories that are **"sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied"** to each category (quoting *Gallant v. NLRB*, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994)). This standard is about the *agency's* burden: the categories must be defined clearly enough that the court can evaluate whether the claimed exemption actually fits each category.

### What a Plaintiff Must Do to Challenge Overbreadth

The opinion tells us what Farahi did *wrong* rather than what a plaintiff must do *right*. The court states that Farahi "briefly objects" to the categories but **"provides no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad."** The consequence was forfeiture under *Khine v. DHS*, 943 F.3d 959, 967 (D.C. Cir. 2019).

From this, the clear takeaway is that a plaintiff must do more than assert that categories are overbroad. The plaintiff must provide a **specific explanation** of *why* the categories are overbroad—that is, why they are not "sufficiently distinct" to allow the court to evaluate whether the claimed exemptions properly apply to each category. A bare assertion of overbreadth, without more, is insufficient.

### Can a Plaintiff Challenge Categories Without Seeing the Documents?

The opinion does not explicitly address this question. However, the legal standard the court applies suggests that the challenge operates on the **agency's description of the categories**, not on the contents of the withheld documents. The test is whether the categories are "sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied." This is an assessment of whether the agency's categorical descriptions are adequate; it does not require the plaintiff to have seen the underlying documents. The plaintiff can presumably challenge the categories based on the agency's own declarations and descriptions.

### How Specific Must the Objection Be?

Again, the opinion does not provide a detailed roadmap, but it signals the floor. The court notes that Farahi "briefly" objects and provides "no specific explanation." This suggests two failures: the objection was too cursory in form, and it lacked substantive content. At minimum, a plaintiff should **identify the specific categories at issue and explain why each fails the distinctness standard**—for example, by explaining why a given category sweeps together documents to which different exemptions might apply, making it impossible for the court to assess whether the claimed exemption fits.

### Summary

The opinion establishes a clear allocation of burdens: the agency must define sufficiently distinct categories, and the plaintiff must provide a specific explanation if it wishes to challenge them. A plaintiff who merely asserts overbreadth without explaining *why* the categories are inadequate forfeits the argument. While the opinion does not directly address whether the plaintiff must have seen the documents, the nature of the standard—evaluating the adequacy of the agency's categorical descriptions—suggests that the challenge can be mounted based on the agency's own submissions.
