Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### When are ex parte declarations appropriate in FOIA litigation, and what can a plaintiff do to challenge them?
Question:
In Farahi, the FBI submitted ex parte declarations under seal, and the court relied on them for both the Exemption 7(A) analysis and the segregability determination. The court says Farahi "did not oppose the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations, so he cannot challenge the district court's reliance on them." But how could Farahi meaningfully oppose something he couldn't see? And the court acknowledges that ex parte declarations "handicap FOIA plaintiffs." What procedural options does a FOIA plaintiff have to push back on ex parte submissions when the subject matter is classified or sensitive? Is there any recourse, or is the plaintiff just stuck once the court allows them?
Answer:
## When Ex Parte Declarations Are Appropriate

The opinion articulates the governing standard through its citation to *Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep't of Veterans Affairs*, 57 F.4th 1061, 1067 (D.C. Cir. 2023): ex parte declarations are appropriate when they are **"the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret."** The court acknowledges that ex parte submissions "handicap FOIA plaintiffs," but treats this as an inherent and unavoidable feature of FOIA litigation involving classified or sensitive material—not as a defect that renders the practice improper. As the court puts it, that problem "is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret."

So the threshold question is necessity: if the agency can justify its withholding through public declarations alone, ex parte submission is not warranted. Only when public explanations would themselves reveal the protected information does the court treat ex parte submission as justified.

## What a Plaintiff Can Do

The opinion's treatment of Farahi's challenge reveals several procedural options—and one critical forfeiture.

**1. Oppose the motion to file ex parte when it is made.** This is the most important takeaway. The court's statement that Farahi "did not oppose the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations, so he cannot challenge the district court's reliance on them" makes clear that a plaintiff *can* oppose the acceptance of exparte declarations at the district court level—and that failing to do so forfeits the argument on appeal. The FBI's ex parte submission was initiated by motion (here, a motion to file the supplemental Hardy declaration "under seal and ex parte"), and Farahi had the opportunity to contest that motion. A plaintiff opposing such a motion could argue that the agency has not demonstrated that ex parte treatment is truly necessary under the *Perioperative Services* standard—for example, by contending that the agency could provide a more detailed public declaration with redactions, or that the information in question is not so sensitive as to require complete secrecy from the opposing party.

**2. Challenge the adequacy of the public declarations and demand greater specificity before ex parte submission.** The opinion shows the district court itself doing something like this: it initially denied summary judgment without prejudice because the Hardy declarations were potentially stale and because the court "needed more information to address segregability, for reasons it could not state on the public record." A plaintiff can press the court to require the agency to supplement its public filings with greater detail before resorting to sealed submissions, narrowing the scope of whatever ultimately must be submitted ex parte.

**3. Challenge staleness and seek updates.** Farahi successfully persuaded the district court that the passage of time undermined the FBI's initial showing, resulting in an order for updated declarations. While not a direct challenge to ex parte submission, this is a mechanism to test whether the government's justifications—public or sealed—remain current and adequately supported.

**4. Raise specific, developed arguments about overbreadth and categorization.** The opinion notes that Farahi objected to the FBI's document categories but "provides no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad," leading to forfeiture. A plaintiff who develops specific, concrete challenges to an agency's withholding categories forces the court and the agency to justify their approach with greater precision, which may reduce reliance on ex parte submissions.

## The Hard Reality

The opinion is candid that once ex parte declarations are accepted, the plaintiff faces structural limitations. The court states that "nothing required the court to put on the public record its analysis of that declaration," meaning the court can rely on sealed material without explaining its reasoning in a way the plaintiff can meaningfully review or rebut. This is the "handicap" the court acknowledges.

The key practical lesson from *Farahi* is therefore timing. The critical moment to push back is **when the agency moves to file ex parte**, not after. A plaintiff who fails to oppose at that stage—as Farahi did—loses the ability to challenge the court's reliance on those declarations on appeal. The opposition should be specific: argue that the agency has not met the *Perioperative Services* standard of showing ex parte submission is the *only* way to decide the dispute without disclosure, and propose alternatives (more detailed redacted declarations, in camera document review with a public explanation of methodology, narrower scope of sealed material). Once the court accepts the ex parte submission without opposition, the plaintiff is largely stuck with the consequences.
