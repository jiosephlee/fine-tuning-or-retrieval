Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 2: # Factual Background and Procedural Posture

## Farahi's Background: Iranian National, Immigration Proceedings, and Alleged Terrorism Connections

Foad Farahi is an Iranian national who has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security placed him in removal proceedings based on a visa overstay. Over the course of his immigration case, Farahi sought various forms of relief, including asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. To oppose these requests, DHS presented evidence—both documents and live testimony from an FBI agent—regarding Farahi's connections to individuals described as high-level terrorists. The Eleventh Circuit, in reviewing his immigration appeal, recounted the government's public allegations in detail: Farahi was described as a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members, linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists including Jose Padilla, and a supporter of charities that financed terrorism, including the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation.

These public allegations are significant for understanding the FOIA litigation that followed. The FBI's public and sealed declarations in the FOIA case referenced an ongoing national security and international terrorism investigation. While the court could not disclose the contents of the sealed submissions, the public allegations from Farahi's immigration proceedings provided corroborating context that supported the FBI's representations about the nature and scope of its investigation.

## The FOIA Request for FBI Files and the Agency's Initial Response

Farahi submitted a Freedom of Information Act request seeking his FBI file. The agency identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs of potentially responsive material. When the FBI failed to meet the statutory deadline for responding, Farahi sued. Over the next several years, the FBI reviewed the potentially responsive records, disclosed some to Farahi, and withheld most of them. This extended review process reflects a common practical reality in FOIA litigation involving large, sensitive files: agencies must painstakingly examine each record to determine whether it falls within a statutory exemption, and where national security concerns are implicated, the review can be especially time-consuming.

The concept of a "responsive" record is foundational to FOIA practice. An agency must produce only those records that fall within the scope of the request—meaning they are reasonably described by the requester and actually exist within the agency's files. Here, the volume of potentially responsive material—over ten thousand pages plus dozens of CDs—meant the FBI's review was substantial, and the delay in processing gave rise to the lawsuit even before the agency had completed its work.

## The October 2019 Hardy Declaration and Initial Summary Judgment Motion

After completing its review, the FBI moved for summary judgment in October 2019. In FOIA cases, summary judgment operates somewhat differently than in ordinary civil litigation. Because FOIA cases often turn on the agency's justification for withholding records—information that may itself be sensitive—the government typically supports its motions with detailed affidavits from agency officials. These declarations explain, often at a high level of generality, why the withheld records fall within a particular exemption.

The FBI's initial submission included a declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS), the FBI unit responsible for processing FOIA requests. Hardy's public declaration asserted that the withheld records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their release could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that were pending or reasonably anticipated. Critically, Hardy also explained that disclosure of information regarding the ongoing investigation could tip off targets about the scope of the investigation—a justification the D.C. Circuit has widely accepted in cases invoking FOIA Exemption 7(A). Hardy attested that the relevant investigation remained "ongoing."

## The Ex Parte Hardy Declaration Submitted Under Seal (October 2020)

In addition to his public declaration, the FBI moved to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte. The term "ex parte" means that the submission was provided to the court without being shared with the opposing party. In FOIA litigation, ex parte submissions are sometimes necessary when the very information the government seeks to withhold would be revealed by explaining its reasons for withholding it in a public filing. The government moved to file this declaration in October 2020, and notably, Farahi did not oppose the motion. The district court granted it.

The use of ex parte declarations in FOIA cases reflects a structural tension in the statute. On one hand, FOIA plaintiffs need information to challenge the government's withholding decisions. On the other hand, the government cannot disclose on the public record the very material it is seeking to protect. The D.C. Circuit has acknowledged that ex parte declarations inevitably "handicap" FOIA plaintiffs, but has held that this disadvantage is "inherent in those FOIA cases where an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." The court in *Farahi* would later invoke this principle when rejecting Farahi's objection to the district court's reliance on ex parte materials.

## The District Court's November 2022 Ruling: Denial Without Prejudice Due to Staleness and Segregability Concerns

In November 2022—roughly three years after the FBI's initial summary judgment motion—the district court issued its first ruling on the motion. The court concluded that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that the FBI had "seemingly" met its burden to show that their release could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. However, the court identified two problems that prevented it from granting summary judgment.

First, the court was concerned about staleness. The Hardy declarations were several years old by the time the court ruled. In FOIA Exemption 7(A) cases, the government must show that enforcement proceedings are currently pending or reasonably anticipated—not merely that they were pending at some point in the past. The passage of time between the FBI's original submissions and the court's ruling raised a legitimate question about whether the investigation remained active. The court accordingly ordered the FBI to update its assessment of whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated.

Second, the court identified a concern regarding segregability. Under FOIA, even when records fall within an exemption, the agency must take reasonable steps to segregate and release any nonexempt information within those records. The court concluded that it needed more information to address this issue, and notably, it stated that it could not explain its reasons on the public record—presumably because doing so would reveal information contained in the ex parte declaration that the government was seeking to protect. For both reasons, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice, meaning the FBI could renew its motion with additional submissions.

## The Bender Supplemental Declarations and Renewed Summary Judgment Motion

In response to the district court's order, the FBI submitted updated declarations and renewed its motion for summary judgment. This time, the agency submitted both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who was then the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. The submission of a new declaration from a different official is a common procedural step when a court orders an agency to update its evidentiary record.

Bender's public declaration explained that RIDS had contacted the FBI agent responsible for overseeing Farahi's file, who confirmed that the investigations remained ongoing. Bender's ex parte declaration provided additional detail, though the public version was notably terse—stating only that the relevant investigations "remain ongoing." The court would later address Farahi's argument that this brevity was insufficient, ultimately concluding that the Bender declaration should be read as an update to the more detailed Hardy declarations rather than as a standalone document. Bender also provided additional information addressing the district court's segregability concerns, again in part through the ex parte submission.

## The District Court's Grant of Summary Judgment and Farahi's Appeal

The district court granted the FBI's renewed motion for summary judgment. On the central Exemption 7(A) question, the court credited the FBI's confirmation that the relevant proceedings remained ongoing. On the segregability question, the court conducted a review of the ex parte submission and concluded that the FBI had provided a "detailed justification" for why the exempt material was not reasonably segregable, thereby satisfying the agency's burden.

Farahi appealed to the D.C. Circuit, raising several challenges. He argued that the passage of time cast doubt on whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated. He contended that the Bender declaration was too conclusory to support the FBI's invocation of Exemption 7(A). He objected that the FBI's document categories were overbroad. And he challenged the district court's segregability analysis, both on the merits and on the ground that the court had relied too heavily on ex parte declarations. The D.C. Circuit's resolution of these arguments is the subject of the following chapters.
