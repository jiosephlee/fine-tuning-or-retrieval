Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 3: # Standards of Review and Evidentiary Framework

## De Novo Review of Summary Judgment in FOIA Cases

When a FOIA plaintiff challenges an agency's withholding of records, the case typically reaches the court of appeals after the district court has resolved the dispute on summary judgment. In *Farahi v. FBI*, as in all FOIA cases, the D.C. Circuit reviews the grant of summary judgment de novo. This means the court of appeals gives no deference to the district court's factual findings or legal conclusions; instead, the appellate court independently assesses whether the government was entitled to judgment as a matter of law. The court cited *Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press v. FBI*, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021), for this standard. De novo review is particularly appropriate in FOIA cases because the ultimate question—whether the agency lawfully withheld records—is a legal one, turning on whether the agency's justifications satisfy the applicable statutory exemptions.

## The Government's Burden of Proof

Although the standard of review is de novo, the government bears the burden of demonstrating that a FOIA exemption applies. This allocation of proof is statutory: 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B) provides that the burden is on the agency to sustain its action. In practice, this means the FBI cannot simply assert that records are exempt; it must provide the court with a basis to conclude that withholding is justified. The government typically meets this burden through detailed declarations explaining the nature of the withheld records and the reasons for their withholding. In *Farahi*, the FBI submitted both public declarations and ex parte declarations—documents reviewed by the court but not disclosed to Farahi—to carry this burden. The use of ex parte declarations reflects the practical reality that, in some FOIA cases, the only way for the government to justify its withholding without disclosing the very material it seeks to protect is to submit evidence that the plaintiff cannot see.

## Deference to the FBI as a Law Enforcement Agency

The D.C. Circuit has long held that when the FBI invokes a FOIA exemption, its determination is entitled to a fair measure of deference. The court in *Farahi* quoted *Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998), for the principle that "[b]ecause the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference." This deference is grounded in institutional competence: the FBI, as the primary federal law enforcement agency, is better positioned than courts to assess whether its records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and whether disclosure might compromise its work. Deference, however, is not a blank check. The agency must still meet its burden through declarations that provide "reasonable specificity" about why the exemption applies and why withheld material cannot be further segregated.

## Heightened Deference in National Security and Counterterrorism Cases

The deference owed to the FBI is particularly strong when the withheld records implicate national security. The court in *Farahi* emphasized this point by citing *Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ*, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (*CNSS*), in which the D.C. Circuit "consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated." This principle applies specifically to Exemption 7(A) cases involving counterterrorism investigations. The rationale is that courts lack the expertise and institutional capacity to second-guess executive branch assessments of how disclosure might affect ongoing national security investigations. In *Farahi*, this principle was directly relevant because the withheld records related to investigations into Farahi's alleged connections to foreign terrorist organizations and named terrorists, including Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and Jose Padilla. The court's repeated invocation of this deference principle underscores that the more sensitive the national security interest, the more latitude the FBI enjoys in justifying its withholding.

## The Two-Part Test for Exemption 7(A)

Exemption 7(A) of FOIA protects records "compiled for law enforcement purposes" if their disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). The court in *Farahi* identified two distinct elements that the government must satisfy to invoke this exemption. First, the withheld records must have been compiled for law enforcement purposes. Second, their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are either pending or reasonably anticipated. The court drew this framework from *Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Washington v. DOJ*, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (*CREW*), which held that proceedings must be "pending or reasonably anticipated" to qualify for protection. The two-part test functions as a gatekeeping mechanism: the government must clear both thresholds, and a failure on either element defeats the exemption claim.

## The Nexus Test for Law Enforcement Purposes

The first element of Exemption 7(A) requires that the records be "compiled for law enforcement purposes." Because the FBI is a law enforcement agency, its determination on this point receives deference, but it must still establish a rational basis. Drawing from *Campbell v. DOJ* and *Pratt v. Webster*, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982), the court explained that the agency's declarations must establish "a rational nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties," as well as "a connection between an individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law." This nexus test prevents the FBI from shielding records merely by labeling an internal matter a "law enforcement" activity. The test requires a showing that the investigation plausibly relates to the agency's actual law enforcement responsibilities and that a specific individual or incident connects to a potential federal violation. In *Farahi*, the FBI satisfied this test through the public Hardy declaration, which described the records as compiled "in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism," supplemented by Farahi's own concessions about the nature of the FBI's investigation into his alleged terrorist connections.

## The 'Inextricably Intertwined' Standard for Withholding Records in Full

Even when an exemption applies, FOIA requires agencies to take reasonable steps to segregate and release nonexempt information. 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii). This reflects FOIA's basic policy favoring disclosure: an agency may withhold only the protected material and must release any reasonably segregable portions. However, the government may withhold records in their entirety when nonexempt portions are "inextricably intertwined with exempt portions." The court in *Farahi* cited *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008), and *Mead Data Central, Inc. v. U.S. Department of Air Force*, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977), for this standard. The "inextricably intertwined" doctrine recognizes that in some records, exempt and nonexempt material are so interwoven that separating them would be impossible or would produce meaningless fragments. The court in *Farahi* applied this principle to reject Farahi's argument that some information in the vast volume of withheld records must be releasable. Citing *Mead Data Central*, the court noted that if nonexempt material is randomly "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," it is not likely to be "reasonably segregable," because "the focus of the FOIA is information, not simply words which the Government has written down." Individual words or phrases might technically be disclosed without harm, but if their disclosure would not convey meaningful information, segregation serves no purpose. The court also confirmed that it may rely on government affidavits to determine that records cannot be further segregated, provided those affidavits show "with reasonable specificity" why further segregation is impossible. In *Farahi*, the district court relied on Bender's ex parte supplemental declaration, which provided a "detailed justification" for why the protected records were not reasonably segregable. The D.C. Circuit affirmed this approach, noting that Farahi had not opposed the acceptance of ex parte declarations and that the use of such declarations is sometimes the only way to resolve FOIA disputes without disclosing the very material the government seeks to protect.
