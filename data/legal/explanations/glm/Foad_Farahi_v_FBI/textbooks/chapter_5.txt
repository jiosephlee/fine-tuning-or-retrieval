Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 5: # Segregability Analysis and Ex Parte Declarations

## The Statutory Segregability Requirement and the FBI's Burden

Even when a FOIA exemption justifies withholding records, the agency's obligation does not end there. FOIA imposes an additional duty: the agency must take "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii). The statute also provides that "[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt." Id. § 552(b). This segregability requirement reflects FOIA's foundational commitment to maximizing public access to government information. An agency cannot simply withhold an entire document because some portions are exempt; it must identify and release the nonexempt material, unless doing so is not reasonably possible.

In this case, the FBI bore the burden of demonstrating that the withheld records contained no reasonably segregable portions. The government generally bears the burden of showing that a FOIA exemption applies, see 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B), and this burden extends to the segregability question. When an agency withholds records in their entirety, it must justify that decision by explaining why the nonexempt material cannot be separated from the exempt material. The D.C. Circuit has held that the government may withhold records in their entirety "when nonexempt portions are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions." *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting *Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep't of Air Force*, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). A court may rely on government affidavits that show "with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated." Id.

## The District Court's Reliance on Bender's Ex Parte Declaration

The procedural history of the segregability issue in this case is important. When the district court first addressed the FBI's summary judgment motion in November 2022, it concluded that the FBI had "seemingly" met its burden under Exemption 7(A) but identified a problem with segregability. The court needed more information to assess whether nonexempt material could be segregated and released—information the court could not state on the public record. The court therefore denied summary judgment without prejudice and ordered the FBI to provide additional justification.

The FBI responded with updated declarations from Joseph Bender, then the Acting Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Bender provided both a public declaration and an ex parte declaration, the latter submitted under seal and outside Farahi's view. The ex parte declaration supplied more detail addressing the district court's segregability concerns. In its second order, the district court explained that the FBI had provided a "detailed justification" for why the protected records were not reasonably segregable, based on Bender's supplemental ex parte submission. The court found that this submission satisfied the agency's burden on segregability.

## The Standard for Non-Segregable Information: Mead Data Central

The court's segregability analysis drew on a foundational D.C. Circuit decision, *Mead Data Central, Inc. v. U.S. Department of the Air Force*, 566 F.2d 242 (D.C. Cir. 1977). *Mead* established that when a small amount of nonexempt material is "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," that material is not likely to be "reasonably segregable." Id. at 261. The logic is straightforward: if nonexempt fragments are scattered throughout exempt records, extracting them would produce isolated words or phrases devoid of meaningful context. The court in *Mead* also articulated a principle central to this case: "the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down." Id. at 261 n.55. This distinction between information and mere words would prove decisive in Farahi's case.

## Farahi's Volume-Based Argument and the Court's Response

Farahi objected to the district court's segregability ruling by pointing to the sheer volume of responsive records. The FBI had identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs of potentially responsive information. Farahi argued that across such a vast quantity of records, there must necessarily be some segregable and releasable information. The intuition is understandable: how could all of that material be so thoroughly intertwined with exempt content that nothing at all could be released?

The court rejected this argument. Drawing on *Mead*, the court explained that even if individual words or phrases in the requested records could be disclosed without harming the FBI's law enforcement interests, that fact was "neither here nor there." The question under FOIA is whether meaningful, reasonably segregable *information* exists—not whether individual words can be mechanically extracted. If nonexempt material is randomly interspersed throughout exempt records, releasing those fragments would not advance FOIA's purpose of providing access to government information. The court cited *Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs*, 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023), in support of this principle. Farahi was correct that isolated words might be disclosable, but the district court properly concluded that no meaningful information was reasonably segregable.

## Farahi's Challenge to the District Court's Reliance on Ex Parte Declarations

Farahi also complained that the district court relied too heavily on Bender's ex parte declaration in conducting its segregability analysis. This objection raises a structural tension in FOIA litigation. Ex parte declarations—submitted by the government outside the plaintiff's view—necessarily handicap FOIA plaintiffs. The plaintiff cannot challenge what they cannot see. Yet in certain cases, the very information sought to be kept secret is also the information needed to justify the withholding. Requiring the government to disclose its segregability analysis on the public record could defeat the purpose of the exemption.

The court acknowledged this tension but found Farahi's challenge unpersuasive for two independent reasons. First, Farahi had not opposed the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations. The FBI had moved to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte in October 2020, and Farahi did not oppose that motion. Having acquiesced in the use of ex parte submissions, Farahi could not later challenge the district court's reliance on them. Second, the court explained that nothing required the district court to put its analysis of the ex parte declaration on the public record. Doing so could itself reveal the sensitive information the exemption was designed to protect.

## The Inherent Necessity of Ex Parte Declarations in Certain FOIA Cases

The court grounded its approach in *Perioperative Services*, which recognized that the problem of ex parte declarations handicapping plaintiffs "is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up). This principle is especially forceful in cases involving national security and counterterrorism investigations, where the sensitivity of the underlying information is at its peak. The D.C. Circuit has "consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated," including in Exemption 7(A) cases. *Ctr. for Nat'l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ*, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003). In such cases, the court must balance the plaintiff's interest in challenging the government's withholding against the risk that public disclosures would compromise the very law enforcement proceedings the exemption protects.

The practical result is that courts must sometimes accept that they can review the government's justifications only in camera and ex parte. This does not mean the government gets a free pass—the court still must assess whether the agency has met its burden with reasonable specificity. But it does mean that the plaintiff's ability to challenge the segregability analysis is necessarily limited by the sensitivity of the underlying records.

## The Court's Affirmance

Having addressed Farahi's arguments on both segregability and the use of ex parte declarations, the court affirmed the district court's grant of summary judgment to the FBI. The court held that the district court correctly concluded that no meaningful information was reasonably segregable from the withheld records. The Bender ex parte declaration, considered in context alongside the Hardy declarations, provided adequate justification for the FBI's decision to withhold the records in their entirety. The court also rejected Farahi's challenge to the district court's reliance on ex parte submissions, noting both his failure to oppose their acceptance and the inherent necessity of ex parte review in cases of this nature. The court's disposition was straightforward: "For these reasons, we affirm the district court."
