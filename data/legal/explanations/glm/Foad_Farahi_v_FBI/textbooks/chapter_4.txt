Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 4: # Application of Exemption 7(A) to Farahi's Records

This chapter examines how the D.C. Circuit applied Exemption 7(A) to the records the FBI withheld from Foad Farahi. As explained in the prior chapter, Exemption 7(A) protects records "compiled for law enforcement purposes" if their disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." The court organized its analysis around these two elements, addressing each in turn. The government bore the burden of proof on both, and the court reviewed the district court's grant of summary judgment de novo.

## Element 1 — Law Enforcement Purposes: The FBI's Nexus Showing

The first element of Exemption 7(A) requires that the withheld records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes." Because the FBI is a law-enforcement agency, its decision to invoke Exemption 7 is entitled to "a fair measure of deference." The court noted that such deference is "particularly appropriate for records implicating national security," citing its consistent practice of deferring to the executive branch in FOIA cases involving national security concerns, including Exemption 7(A) cases involving counterterrorism investigations.

Deference, however, does not mean the agency may simply assert law enforcement purpose without explanation. The FBI's declarations must "establish a rational nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties," as well as "a connection between an individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law." This dual requirement—drawn from the D.C. Circuit's decisions in *Campbell v. DOJ* (1998) and *Pratt v. Webster* (1982)—ensures that agencies do not invoke Exemption 7(A) as a blanket shield for any records they happen to hold. The agency must identify, with reasonable specificity, both the law enforcement duty it was pursuing and the connection between the requester's file and a potential violation.

The court concluded that the FBI satisfied this burden. The public Hardy declaration explained that the withheld records were compiled "in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject's association with individuals directly involved in such violations." The ex parte Hardy declaration—submitted under seal and reviewed by the court in camera—supplied "considerable detail about the nature of that investigation." The court found this showing sufficient to establish the required nexus between the investigation and the FBI's national security and counterterrorism law enforcement duties, as well as the connection between Farahi and a potential violation of federal law.

## Farahi's Concessions About His Connections to Terrorist Organizations

The court's conclusion on the law enforcement purposes element was reinforced by what Farahi himself had conceded in the district court. In his opposition to summary judgment, Farahi acknowledged that the FBI had investigated him for ties to one or more organizations designated by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as ties to an individual later detained as an enemy combatant and eventually convicted in federal court on terrorism-related charges.

These concessions were significant. Ordinarily, a FOIA plaintiff might dispute the government's characterization of the investigation's purpose, arguing that the agency was using law enforcement authority for some other objective. But Farahi's own filings confirmed the FBI's account that his file pertained to national security and international terrorism investigations. This removed any doubt the court might have had about the sufficiency of the nexus showing. The court stated bluntly: "We have no doubt that the FBI carried its burden to show that Farahi's file was compiled for law enforcement purposes."

The court also referenced what the FBI had publicly alleged about Farahi in his separate immigration proceedings before the Eleventh Circuit. According to that court's 2024 opinion, the FBI had presented evidence that Farahi was "a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood," was "connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members," was "linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists" including Jose Padilla, and had supported "charities that financed terrorism" including the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. The court used these public allegations not as independent proof but as contextual confirmation of the investigation's law enforcement character.

## Element 2 — Interference with Pending or Anticipated Proceedings

The second element of Exemption 7(A) requires that disclosure of the withheld records "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." The D.C. Circuit has interpreted this provision to require that the enforcement proceedings be "pending or reasonably anticipated." Ongoing criminal investigations count as "proceedings" for this purpose, and the government may establish their existence "simply by attesting to their existence." The key principle, as the court articulated quoting *Juarez v. DOJ* (2007), is that "so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies."

The government need not show that a prosecution has been initiated or is imminent. The focus is on whether the investigation is ongoing and whether premature disclosure of its fruits could compromise it. One widely accepted justification under this element is that disclosure could tip off investigative targets about the scope and focus of the investigation, allowing them to destroy evidence, coordinate stories, or take other evasive action.

## The Hardy Public and Ex Parte Declarations as the Primary Evidentiary Foundation

The government's evidentiary foundation for the interference element rested primarily on the declarations of David Hardy, the Section Chief of the FBI's Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Hardy submitted both a public declaration and an ex parte declaration. The public declaration explained that disclosure of information regarding the ongoing investigation could reasonably be expected to interfere with it "by tipping off the targets about the scope of the investigation"—a justification the court described as "widely accepted in Exemption 7(A) cases." The public declaration also attested that the relevant investigation remained "ongoing."

The ex parte Hardy declaration, submitted under seal, "elaborated on the investigation" and provided "considerable detail about the nature and scope of the investigation." The court relied on this ex parte submission as a critical complement to the public declaration, because the public declaration necessarily could not disclose the specific investigative details that would demonstrate the concreteness of the proceedings. The court's opinion does not reveal the contents of this declaration, but the court's references to it make clear that it found the detail sufficient to establish that the investigation was not merely hypothetical.

## The Bender Declaration as a Supplemental Update

The procedural history complicated the government's showing. After the FBI submitted the Hardy declarations and moved for summary judgment in October 2019, significant time passed before the district court ruled. In November 2022—more than three years later—the district court addressed the motion. It concluded that the FBI had "seemingly" met its burden on the interference element but was concerned that the declarations might be stale. The court ordered the FBI to update its assessment of whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated, and denied summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI responded with updated declarations from Joseph Bender, then the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public declaration, Bender explained that RIDS had contacted the agent responsible for overseeing Farahi's file, who confirmed that investigations remained ongoing. The ex parte Bender declaration provided more details. The district court credited the FBI's confirmation "that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing" and granted summary judgment.

## Farahi's Staleness Argument and Invocation of CREW's "Vague Nature" Concern

On appeal, Farahi argued that the passage of time cast doubt on whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated. He invoked the D.C. Circuit's decision in *CREW v. DOJ* (2014), where the court noted that the "vague nature" of an assertion that investigations remain ongoing may not suffice to support invocation of Exemption 7(A). Farahi contended that the Bender declaration's assertion that investigations "remain ongoing" was similarly conclusory and vague.

This argument had surface appeal. As the court acknowledged, "the Bender declaration, considered in isolation, appears surprisingly terse." A declaration that simply states investigations are "ongoing," without more, might not satisfy the government's burden, particularly after a significant passage of time. The *CREW* court had warned against accepting such generic attestations without some supporting detail.

## The Court's Reasoning: Reading the Declarations Together

The court rejected Farahi's argument, but not by defending the adequacy of the Bender declaration standing alone. Instead, the court held that context "counsels against considering the Bender declaration in isolation." The court's reasoning rested on several interlocking points.

First, the government had already established the pendency of enforcement proceedings through the public Hardy declaration—which attested to their existence—and the ex parte Hardy declaration—which gave "considerable detail about the nature and scope of the investigation." These declarations were the primary evidentiary foundation.

Second, the government submitted the Bender declaration "not to establish the pendency of enforcement proceedings from scratch, but only to confirm that nothing had changed while its initial summary-judgment motion was pending." The purpose of the update was to verify the continued vitality of an already-established showing, not to make an entirely new one.

Third, in denying the government's initial motion without prejudice, the district court had already concluded that the FBI "seemingly" met its burden to show pending enforcement proceedings. The court treated the district court's "seemingly" finding as a baseline that the Bender declaration was asked to update, not as a void that the Bender declaration had to fill from nothing.

The court thus read the declarations as a sequence: the Hardy declarations established the investigation's existence and scope in detail; the Bender declaration confirmed that nothing had changed. Read together, the declarations satisfied the government's burden.

## Contextual Support: The Investigation's Breadth and the District Court's Prior Finding

The court found additional support in the breadth of the underlying investigation. While it could not disclose the contents of Hardy's ex parte declaration, the court pointed to what the FBI had publicly alleged about Farahi in his immigration proceedings: connections to the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, Khaled Sheikh Mohamed, Al Qaeda members, Jose Padilla, and terrorism-financing charities. These public allegations, drawn from the Eleventh Circuit's 2024 opinion in Farahi's immigration case, provided contextual support for the FBI's representation that the investigation remained ongoing.

The court also noted that the Bender declaration's terseness was less problematic given this context. Where the underlying investigation concerns serious national security matters involving multiple connections to designated terrorist organizations and convicted terrorists, a confirmation that the investigation "remains ongoing" is more plausible than the same assertion might be in a more limited or mundane investigation. The court concluded that the FBI had met its burden to show that a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" continues to exist, quoting *CREW* and *Juarez*.

## Farahi's Forfeited Challenge to FBI Document Categorization

Farahi raised one additional objection: that the government had not adequately justified how it defined the categories of documents used to explain its withholding decisions. The legal principle he invoked is well established. When an agency withholds documents on a category-by-category basis, it must use categories that are "sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied" to each category, as the D.C. Circuit held in *CREW* (quoting *Gallant v. NLRB* (1994)). Overbroad categories can mask improper withholding by lumping together documents that should be treated differently.

The court rejected this argument—not on the merits, but because Farahi forfeited it. "Farahi provides no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad." Without a specific challenge to specific categories, the court had no basis to evaluate whether the categories were improperly defined. The court cited *Khine v. DHS* (2019) for the principle that arguments not specifically developed are forfeited. This aspect of the opinion serves as a reminder that FOIA plaintiffs must do more than assert a possible defect in the agency's categorization scheme; they must identify with specificity which categories are problematic and why.
