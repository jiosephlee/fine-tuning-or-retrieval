Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# The FOIA Framework and Exemption 7(A)

## FOIA's General Disclosure Mandate Under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(3)

The Freedom of Information Act rests on a simple but powerful premise: the federal government works for the public, and the public is entitled to see what the government is doing. As the court explains at the outset of *Farahi v. FBI*, FOIA "requires federal agencies to release records to members of the public upon request." This general disclosure mandate is codified at 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), which gives any person the right to request records from federal agencies and obligates those agencies to produce responsive records.

The statute's default setting is openness. When an individual submits a FOIA request, the agency must search for records responsive to the request and produce them. If the agency fails to act within the statutory deadline, the requester may sue in federal court to compel compliance—which is exactly what Foad Farahi did here when the FBI missed its deadline for responding to his request for his own file.

This case illustrates the scale at which FOIA operates in practice. Farahi's request led the FBI to locate approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs of potentially responsive information. The agency then undertook a years-long process of reviewing those records, disclosing some, and withholding most. This illustrates that FOIA's disclosure mandate is not self-executing; it requires agencies to make sometimes resource-intensive judgments about what must be released and what may be withheld.

## The Role of Statutory Exemptions Under 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)

FOIA's disclosure mandate is not absolute. The statute enumerates a series of exemptions that permit agencies to withhold records in specified circumstances. These exemptions are codified at 5 U.S.C. § 552(b). As the court notes, the general rule is that agencies must release records "unless an enumerated statutory exemption applies."

The exemptions reflect a policy judgment that certain government interests—such as national security, personal privacy, and law enforcement—sometimes outweigh the public's interest in disclosure. The burden of demonstrating that an exemption applies falls on the government. In FOIA litigation, the agency must justify its withholding decisions, typically through declarations submitted to the court explaining why particular records fall within one or more exemptions.

In this case, the FBI invoked one specific exemption: Exemption 7(A). The litigation thus turned on whether the FBI could carry its burden of showing that the withheld records satisfied the requirements of that exemption. Understanding the court's analysis requires a careful examination of the statutory text and the governing case law interpreting it.

## Text and Elements of Exemption 7(A)

Exemption 7(A) is one of several law-enforcement-related exemptions within FOIA. It covers records "compiled for law enforcement purposes" if their disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). The exemption thus has two core elements: first, that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes, and second, that their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.

The first element—compilation for law enforcement purposes—reflects the exemption's focus on records connected to the government's law enforcement function. Because the FBI is a law-enforcement agency, courts give its determination on this point "a fair measure of deference." Still, the agency must "establish a rational nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties," as well as a "connection between an individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law." In *Farahi*, this element was satisfied by the FBI's declarations explaining that the records were compiled in furtherance of investigations into national security and international terrorism violations, combined with Farahi's own concession that the FBI had investigated him for ties to designated foreign terrorist organizations.

The second element—reasonable interference with enforcement proceedings—is the heart of the dispute in this case. The government must show that disclosure of the withheld records could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are "pending or reasonably anticipated." The court accepted the FBI's explanation that disclosure could tip off targets about the scope of the ongoing investigation—a justification the D.C. Circuit has recognized as a valid basis for invoking Exemption 7(A).

## The 'Pending or Reasonably Anticipated' Standard for Enforcement Proceedings

The court's analysis of the second element of Exemption 7(A) is governed by the D.C. Circuit's decision in *Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington v. DOJ* ("CREW"), 746 F.3d 1082 (D.C. Cir. 2014), which holds that enforcement proceedings must be "pending or reasonably anticipated" for the exemption to apply. This requirement prevents agencies from invoking Exemption 7(A) based on speculative or hypothetical proceedings that have no concrete basis.

Ongoing criminal investigations count as "proceedings" under this standard. The government need not show that charges have been filed or that a prosecution is imminent. Rather, it must demonstrate the existence of a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding." The court in *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54 (D.C. Cir. 2008), explained that "so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies." The government may satisfy its burden simply by attesting to the existence of such proceedings, though in some cases a "vague" assertion may be insufficient.

The *Farahi* case presents a wrinkle on this standard: what happens when significant time passes between the agency's initial showing and the court's resolution of the dispute? Farahi argued that the passage of time cast doubt on whether enforcement proceedings remained pending. The district court itself recognized this concern when it initially denied the FBI's first summary-judgment motion without prejudice, ordering the agency to update its assessment. The D.C. Circuit ultimately held that the government's updated declaration, read in context alongside the earlier and more detailed declarations, was sufficient to confirm that the proceedings remained ongoing. The court also found that the breadth of the underlying investigation—implicating connections to designated foreign terrorist organizations and convicted terrorists—made the government's representation plausible. This analysis illustrates that the "pending or reasonably anticipated" standard is not a one-time showing but may require updating when material time has elapsed during litigation.

## The Segregability Requirement Under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) and § 552(b)

Even when records fall within a FOIA exemption, the agency's obligation to disclose is not entirely extinguished. The statute imposes a segregability requirement: agencies must take "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii). Section 552(b) reinforces this obligation by providing that "[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt."

The segregability requirement serves an important function: it ensures that exemptions do not become a license for blanket withholding. If a record contains both exempt and nonexempt material, the agency must release the nonexempt portions if they can be reasonably segregated from the exempt material. However, the requirement has limits. The government may withhold records in their entirety when nonexempt portions "are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions." In such cases, extracting the nonexempt information would be impracticable or would result in the disclosure of protected material.

The court's analysis in *Farahi* illustrates an important principle about the segregability requirement: it focuses on meaningful information, not isolated words. Farahi argued that given the vast volume of responsive records—thousands of pages and dozens of CDs—some information must be segregable and releasable. The court rejected this argument, drawing on *Mead Data Central, Inc. v. U.S. Department of Air Force*, 566 F.2d 242 (D.C. Cir. 1977), for the principle that if a small amount of nonexempt material is "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," it is not "reasonably segregable." As the court explained, "the focus of the FOIA is information, not simply words which the Government has written down." Individual words or phrases might technically be disclosed without harming law enforcement interests, but if they do not convey meaningful information independent of the exempt material, they are not reasonably segregable.

The segregability analysis also intersected with the procedural question of ex parte submissions. The district court relied on an ex parte declaration from the FBI to evaluate whether exempt material was reasonably segregable. The D.C. Circuit upheld this approach, noting that while ex parte declarations "handicap FOIA plaintiffs," this problem is "inherent in those FOIA cases where an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." Because Farahi had not opposed the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations, he could not later challenge the court's reliance on them.

# Factual Background and Procedural Posture

## Farahi's Background: Iranian National, Immigration Proceedings, and Alleged Terrorism Connections

Foad Farahi is an Iranian national who has resided in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security placed him in removal proceedings based on a visa overstay. Over the course of his immigration case, Farahi sought various forms of relief, including asylum, withholding of removal, protection under the Convention Against Torture, and voluntary departure. To oppose these requests, DHS presented evidence—both documents and live testimony from an FBI agent—regarding Farahi's connections to individuals described as high-level terrorists. The Eleventh Circuit, in reviewing his immigration appeal, recounted the government's public allegations in detail: Farahi was described as a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members, linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists including Jose Padilla, and a supporter of charities that financed terrorism, including the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation.

These public allegations are significant for understanding the FOIA litigation that followed. The FBI's public and sealed declarations in the FOIA case referenced an ongoing national security and international terrorism investigation. While the court could not disclose the contents of the sealed submissions, the public allegations from Farahi's immigration proceedings provided corroborating context that supported the FBI's representations about the nature and scope of its investigation.

## The FOIA Request for FBI Files and the Agency's Initial Response

Farahi submitted a Freedom of Information Act request seeking his FBI file. The agency identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs of potentially responsive material. When the FBI failed to meet the statutory deadline for responding, Farahi sued. Over the next several years, the FBI reviewed the potentially responsive records, disclosed some to Farahi, and withheld most of them. This extended review process reflects a common practical reality in FOIA litigation involving large, sensitive files: agencies must painstakingly examine each record to determine whether it falls within a statutory exemption, and where national security concerns are implicated, the review can be especially time-consuming.

The concept of a "responsive" record is foundational to FOIA practice. An agency must produce only those records that fall within the scope of the request—meaning they are reasonably described by the requester and actually exist within the agency's files. Here, the volume of potentially responsive material—over ten thousand pages plus dozens of CDs—meant the FBI's review was substantial, and the delay in processing gave rise to the lawsuit even before the agency had completed its work.

## The October 2019 Hardy Declaration and Initial Summary Judgment Motion

After completing its review, the FBI moved for summary judgment in October 2019. In FOIA cases, summary judgment operates somewhat differently than in ordinary civil litigation. Because FOIA cases often turn on the agency's justification for withholding records—information that may itself be sensitive—the government typically supports its motions with detailed affidavits from agency officials. These declarations explain, often at a high level of generality, why the withheld records fall within a particular exemption.

The FBI's initial submission included a declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS), the FBI unit responsible for processing FOIA requests. Hardy's public declaration asserted that the withheld records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their release could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that were pending or reasonably anticipated. Critically, Hardy also explained that disclosure of information regarding the ongoing investigation could tip off targets about the scope of the investigation—a justification the D.C. Circuit has widely accepted in cases invoking FOIA Exemption 7(A). Hardy attested that the relevant investigation remained "ongoing."

## The Ex Parte Hardy Declaration Submitted Under Seal (October 2020)

In addition to his public declaration, the FBI moved to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte. The term "ex parte" means that the submission was provided to the court without being shared with the opposing party. In FOIA litigation, ex parte submissions are sometimes necessary when the very information the government seeks to withhold would be revealed by explaining its reasons for withholding it in a public filing. The government moved to file this declaration in October 2020, and notably, Farahi did not oppose the motion. The district court granted it.

The use of ex parte declarations in FOIA cases reflects a structural tension in the statute. On one hand, FOIA plaintiffs need information to challenge the government's withholding decisions. On the other hand, the government cannot disclose on the public record the very material it is seeking to protect. The D.C. Circuit has acknowledged that ex parte declarations inevitably "handicap" FOIA plaintiffs, but has held that this disadvantage is "inherent in those FOIA cases where an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." The court in *Farahi* would later invoke this principle when rejecting Farahi's objection to the district court's reliance on ex parte materials.

## The District Court's November 2022 Ruling: Denial Without Prejudice Due to Staleness and Segregability Concerns

In November 2022—roughly three years after the FBI's initial summary judgment motion—the district court issued its first ruling on the motion. The court concluded that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that the FBI had "seemingly" met its burden to show that their release could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. However, the court identified two problems that prevented it from granting summary judgment.

First, the court was concerned about staleness. The Hardy declarations were several years old by the time the court ruled. In FOIA Exemption 7(A) cases, the government must show that enforcement proceedings are currently pending or reasonably anticipated—not merely that they were pending at some point in the past. The passage of time between the FBI's original submissions and the court's ruling raised a legitimate question about whether the investigation remained active. The court accordingly ordered the FBI to update its assessment of whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated.

Second, the court identified a concern regarding segregability. Under FOIA, even when records fall within an exemption, the agency must take reasonable steps to segregate and release any nonexempt information within those records. The court concluded that it needed more information to address this issue, and notably, it stated that it could not explain its reasons on the public record—presumably because doing so would reveal information contained in the ex parte declaration that the government was seeking to protect. For both reasons, the court denied summary judgment without prejudice, meaning the FBI could renew its motion with additional submissions.

## The Bender Supplemental Declarations and Renewed Summary Judgment Motion

In response to the district court's order, the FBI submitted updated declarations and renewed its motion for summary judgment. This time, the agency submitted both public and ex parte declarations from Joseph Bender, who was then the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. The submission of a new declaration from a different official is a common procedural step when a court orders an agency to update its evidentiary record.

Bender's public declaration explained that RIDS had contacted the FBI agent responsible for overseeing Farahi's file, who confirmed that the investigations remained ongoing. Bender's ex parte declaration provided additional detail, though the public version was notably terse—stating only that the relevant investigations "remain ongoing." The court would later address Farahi's argument that this brevity was insufficient, ultimately concluding that the Bender declaration should be read as an update to the more detailed Hardy declarations rather than as a standalone document. Bender also provided additional information addressing the district court's segregability concerns, again in part through the ex parte submission.

## The District Court's Grant of Summary Judgment and Farahi's Appeal

The district court granted the FBI's renewed motion for summary judgment. On the central Exemption 7(A) question, the court credited the FBI's confirmation that the relevant proceedings remained ongoing. On the segregability question, the court conducted a review of the ex parte submission and concluded that the FBI had provided a "detailed justification" for why the exempt material was not reasonably segregable, thereby satisfying the agency's burden.

Farahi appealed to the D.C. Circuit, raising several challenges. He argued that the passage of time cast doubt on whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated. He contended that the Bender declaration was too conclusory to support the FBI's invocation of Exemption 7(A). He objected that the FBI's document categories were overbroad. And he challenged the district court's segregability analysis, both on the merits and on the ground that the court had relied too heavily on ex parte declarations. The D.C. Circuit's resolution of these arguments is the subject of the following chapters.

# Standards of Review and Evidentiary Framework

## De Novo Review of Summary Judgment in FOIA Cases

When a FOIA plaintiff challenges an agency's withholding of records, the case typically reaches the court of appeals after the district court has resolved the dispute on summary judgment. In *Farahi v. FBI*, as in all FOIA cases, the D.C. Circuit reviews the grant of summary judgment de novo. This means the court of appeals gives no deference to the district court's factual findings or legal conclusions; instead, the appellate court independently assesses whether the government was entitled to judgment as a matter of law. The court cited *Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press v. FBI*, 3 F.4th 350, 361 (D.C. Cir. 2021), for this standard. De novo review is particularly appropriate in FOIA cases because the ultimate question—whether the agency lawfully withheld records—is a legal one, turning on whether the agency's justifications satisfy the applicable statutory exemptions.

## The Government's Burden of Proof

Although the standard of review is de novo, the government bears the burden of demonstrating that a FOIA exemption applies. This allocation of proof is statutory: 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B) provides that the burden is on the agency to sustain its action. In practice, this means the FBI cannot simply assert that records are exempt; it must provide the court with a basis to conclude that withholding is justified. The government typically meets this burden through detailed declarations explaining the nature of the withheld records and the reasons for their withholding. In *Farahi*, the FBI submitted both public declarations and ex parte declarations—documents reviewed by the court but not disclosed to Farahi—to carry this burden. The use of ex parte declarations reflects the practical reality that, in some FOIA cases, the only way for the government to justify its withholding without disclosing the very material it seeks to protect is to submit evidence that the plaintiff cannot see.

## Deference to the FBI as a Law Enforcement Agency

The D.C. Circuit has long held that when the FBI invokes a FOIA exemption, its determination is entitled to a fair measure of deference. The court in *Farahi* quoted *Campbell v. DOJ*, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998), for the principle that "[b]ecause the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference." This deference is grounded in institutional competence: the FBI, as the primary federal law enforcement agency, is better positioned than courts to assess whether its records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and whether disclosure might compromise its work. Deference, however, is not a blank check. The agency must still meet its burden through declarations that provide "reasonable specificity" about why the exemption applies and why withheld material cannot be further segregated.

## Heightened Deference in National Security and Counterterrorism Cases

The deference owed to the FBI is particularly strong when the withheld records implicate national security. The court in *Farahi* emphasized this point by citing *Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ*, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (*CNSS*), in which the D.C. Circuit "consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated." This principle applies specifically to Exemption 7(A) cases involving counterterrorism investigations. The rationale is that courts lack the expertise and institutional capacity to second-guess executive branch assessments of how disclosure might affect ongoing national security investigations. In *Farahi*, this principle was directly relevant because the withheld records related to investigations into Farahi's alleged connections to foreign terrorist organizations and named terrorists, including Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and Jose Padilla. The court's repeated invocation of this deference principle underscores that the more sensitive the national security interest, the more latitude the FBI enjoys in justifying its withholding.

## The Two-Part Test for Exemption 7(A)

Exemption 7(A) of FOIA protects records "compiled for law enforcement purposes" if their disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). The court in *Farahi* identified two distinct elements that the government must satisfy to invoke this exemption. First, the withheld records must have been compiled for law enforcement purposes. Second, their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are either pending or reasonably anticipated. The court drew this framework from *Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Washington v. DOJ*, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (*CREW*), which held that proceedings must be "pending or reasonably anticipated" to qualify for protection. The two-part test functions as a gatekeeping mechanism: the government must clear both thresholds, and a failure on either element defeats the exemption claim.

## The Nexus Test for Law Enforcement Purposes

The first element of Exemption 7(A) requires that the records be "compiled for law enforcement purposes." Because the FBI is a law enforcement agency, its determination on this point receives deference, but it must still establish a rational basis. Drawing from *Campbell v. DOJ* and *Pratt v. Webster*, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982), the court explained that the agency's declarations must establish "a rational nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties," as well as "a connection between an individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law." This nexus test prevents the FBI from shielding records merely by labeling an internal matter a "law enforcement" activity. The test requires a showing that the investigation plausibly relates to the agency's actual law enforcement responsibilities and that a specific individual or incident connects to a potential federal violation. In *Farahi*, the FBI satisfied this test through the public Hardy declaration, which described the records as compiled "in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism," supplemented by Farahi's own concessions about the nature of the FBI's investigation into his alleged terrorist connections.

## The 'Inextricably Intertwined' Standard for Withholding Records in Full

Even when an exemption applies, FOIA requires agencies to take reasonable steps to segregate and release nonexempt information. 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii). This reflects FOIA's basic policy favoring disclosure: an agency may withhold only the protected material and must release any reasonably segregable portions. However, the government may withhold records in their entirety when nonexempt portions are "inextricably intertwined with exempt portions." The court in *Farahi* cited *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008), and *Mead Data Central, Inc. v. U.S. Department of Air Force*, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977), for this standard. The "inextricably intertwined" doctrine recognizes that in some records, exempt and nonexempt material are so interwoven that separating them would be impossible or would produce meaningless fragments. The court in *Farahi* applied this principle to reject Farahi's argument that some information in the vast volume of withheld records must be releasable. Citing *Mead Data Central*, the court noted that if nonexempt material is randomly "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," it is not likely to be "reasonably segregable," because "the focus of the FOIA is information, not simply words which the Government has written down." Individual words or phrases might technically be disclosed without harm, but if their disclosure would not convey meaningful information, segregation serves no purpose. The court also confirmed that it may rely on government affidavits to determine that records cannot be further segregated, provided those affidavits show "with reasonable specificity" why further segregation is impossible. In *Farahi*, the district court relied on Bender's ex parte supplemental declaration, which provided a "detailed justification" for why the protected records were not reasonably segregable. The D.C. Circuit affirmed this approach, noting that Farahi had not opposed the acceptance of ex parte declarations and that the use of such declarations is sometimes the only way to resolve FOIA disputes without disclosing the very material the government seeks to protect.

# Application of Exemption 7(A) to Farahi's Records

This chapter examines how the D.C. Circuit applied Exemption 7(A) to the records the FBI withheld from Foad Farahi. As explained in the prior chapter, Exemption 7(A) protects records "compiled for law enforcement purposes" if their disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." The court organized its analysis around these two elements, addressing each in turn. The government bore the burden of proof on both, and the court reviewed the district court's grant of summary judgment de novo.

## Element 1 — Law Enforcement Purposes: The FBI's Nexus Showing

The first element of Exemption 7(A) requires that the withheld records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes." Because the FBI is a law-enforcement agency, its decision to invoke Exemption 7 is entitled to "a fair measure of deference." The court noted that such deference is "particularly appropriate for records implicating national security," citing its consistent practice of deferring to the executive branch in FOIA cases involving national security concerns, including Exemption 7(A) cases involving counterterrorism investigations.

Deference, however, does not mean the agency may simply assert law enforcement purpose without explanation. The FBI's declarations must "establish a rational nexus between the investigation and one of the agency's law enforcement duties," as well as "a connection between an individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law." This dual requirement—drawn from the D.C. Circuit's decisions in *Campbell v. DOJ* (1998) and *Pratt v. Webster* (1982)—ensures that agencies do not invoke Exemption 7(A) as a blanket shield for any records they happen to hold. The agency must identify, with reasonable specificity, both the law enforcement duty it was pursuing and the connection between the requester's file and a potential violation.

The court concluded that the FBI satisfied this burden. The public Hardy declaration explained that the withheld records were compiled "in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism to include pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject's association with individuals directly involved in such violations." The ex parte Hardy declaration—submitted under seal and reviewed by the court in camera—supplied "considerable detail about the nature of that investigation." The court found this showing sufficient to establish the required nexus between the investigation and the FBI's national security and counterterrorism law enforcement duties, as well as the connection between Farahi and a potential violation of federal law.

## Farahi's Concessions About His Connections to Terrorist Organizations

The court's conclusion on the law enforcement purposes element was reinforced by what Farahi himself had conceded in the district court. In his opposition to summary judgment, Farahi acknowledged that the FBI had investigated him for ties to one or more organizations designated by the State Department as foreign terrorist organizations, as well as ties to an individual later detained as an enemy combatant and eventually convicted in federal court on terrorism-related charges.

These concessions were significant. Ordinarily, a FOIA plaintiff might dispute the government's characterization of the investigation's purpose, arguing that the agency was using law enforcement authority for some other objective. But Farahi's own filings confirmed the FBI's account that his file pertained to national security and international terrorism investigations. This removed any doubt the court might have had about the sufficiency of the nexus showing. The court stated bluntly: "We have no doubt that the FBI carried its burden to show that Farahi's file was compiled for law enforcement purposes."

The court also referenced what the FBI had publicly alleged about Farahi in his separate immigration proceedings before the Eleventh Circuit. According to that court's 2024 opinion, the FBI had presented evidence that Farahi was "a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood," was "connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members," was "linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists" including Jose Padilla, and had supported "charities that financed terrorism" including the Holy Land Foundation and the Global Relief Foundation. The court used these public allegations not as independent proof but as contextual confirmation of the investigation's law enforcement character.

## Element 2 — Interference with Pending or Anticipated Proceedings

The second element of Exemption 7(A) requires that disclosure of the withheld records "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." The D.C. Circuit has interpreted this provision to require that the enforcement proceedings be "pending or reasonably anticipated." Ongoing criminal investigations count as "proceedings" for this purpose, and the government may establish their existence "simply by attesting to their existence." The key principle, as the court articulated quoting *Juarez v. DOJ* (2007), is that "so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies."

The government need not show that a prosecution has been initiated or is imminent. The focus is on whether the investigation is ongoing and whether premature disclosure of its fruits could compromise it. One widely accepted justification under this element is that disclosure could tip off investigative targets about the scope and focus of the investigation, allowing them to destroy evidence, coordinate stories, or take other evasive action.

## The Hardy Public and Ex Parte Declarations as the Primary Evidentiary Foundation

The government's evidentiary foundation for the interference element rested primarily on the declarations of David Hardy, the Section Chief of the FBI's Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Hardy submitted both a public declaration and an ex parte declaration. The public declaration explained that disclosure of information regarding the ongoing investigation could reasonably be expected to interfere with it "by tipping off the targets about the scope of the investigation"—a justification the court described as "widely accepted in Exemption 7(A) cases." The public declaration also attested that the relevant investigation remained "ongoing."

The ex parte Hardy declaration, submitted under seal, "elaborated on the investigation" and provided "considerable detail about the nature and scope of the investigation." The court relied on this ex parte submission as a critical complement to the public declaration, because the public declaration necessarily could not disclose the specific investigative details that would demonstrate the concreteness of the proceedings. The court's opinion does not reveal the contents of this declaration, but the court's references to it make clear that it found the detail sufficient to establish that the investigation was not merely hypothetical.

## The Bender Declaration as a Supplemental Update

The procedural history complicated the government's showing. After the FBI submitted the Hardy declarations and moved for summary judgment in October 2019, significant time passed before the district court ruled. In November 2022—more than three years later—the district court addressed the motion. It concluded that the FBI had "seemingly" met its burden on the interference element but was concerned that the declarations might be stale. The court ordered the FBI to update its assessment of whether enforcement proceedings were still pending or reasonably anticipated, and denied summary judgment without prejudice.

The FBI responded with updated declarations from Joseph Bender, then the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. In his public declaration, Bender explained that RIDS had contacted the agent responsible for overseeing Farahi's file, who confirmed that investigations remained ongoing. The ex parte Bender declaration provided more details. The district court credited the FBI's confirmation "that the relevant proceedings remain ongoing" and granted summary judgment.

## Farahi's Staleness Argument and Invocation of CREW's "Vague Nature" Concern

On appeal, Farahi argued that the passage of time cast doubt on whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated. He invoked the D.C. Circuit's decision in *CREW v. DOJ* (2014), where the court noted that the "vague nature" of an assertion that investigations remain ongoing may not suffice to support invocation of Exemption 7(A). Farahi contended that the Bender declaration's assertion that investigations "remain ongoing" was similarly conclusory and vague.

This argument had surface appeal. As the court acknowledged, "the Bender declaration, considered in isolation, appears surprisingly terse." A declaration that simply states investigations are "ongoing," without more, might not satisfy the government's burden, particularly after a significant passage of time. The *CREW* court had warned against accepting such generic attestations without some supporting detail.

## The Court's Reasoning: Reading the Declarations Together

The court rejected Farahi's argument, but not by defending the adequacy of the Bender declaration standing alone. Instead, the court held that context "counsels against considering the Bender declaration in isolation." The court's reasoning rested on several interlocking points.

First, the government had already established the pendency of enforcement proceedings through the public Hardy declaration—which attested to their existence—and the ex parte Hardy declaration—which gave "considerable detail about the nature and scope of the investigation." These declarations were the primary evidentiary foundation.

Second, the government submitted the Bender declaration "not to establish the pendency of enforcement proceedings from scratch, but only to confirm that nothing had changed while its initial summary-judgment motion was pending." The purpose of the update was to verify the continued vitality of an already-established showing, not to make an entirely new one.

Third, in denying the government's initial motion without prejudice, the district court had already concluded that the FBI "seemingly" met its burden to show pending enforcement proceedings. The court treated the district court's "seemingly" finding as a baseline that the Bender declaration was asked to update, not as a void that the Bender declaration had to fill from nothing.

The court thus read the declarations as a sequence: the Hardy declarations established the investigation's existence and scope in detail; the Bender declaration confirmed that nothing had changed. Read together, the declarations satisfied the government's burden.

## Contextual Support: The Investigation's Breadth and the District Court's Prior Finding

The court found additional support in the breadth of the underlying investigation. While it could not disclose the contents of Hardy's ex parte declaration, the court pointed to what the FBI had publicly alleged about Farahi in his immigration proceedings: connections to the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, Khaled Sheikh Mohamed, Al Qaeda members, Jose Padilla, and terrorism-financing charities. These public allegations, drawn from the Eleventh Circuit's 2024 opinion in Farahi's immigration case, provided contextual support for the FBI's representation that the investigation remained ongoing.

The court also noted that the Bender declaration's terseness was less problematic given this context. Where the underlying investigation concerns serious national security matters involving multiple connections to designated terrorist organizations and convicted terrorists, a confirmation that the investigation "remains ongoing" is more plausible than the same assertion might be in a more limited or mundane investigation. The court concluded that the FBI had met its burden to show that a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" continues to exist, quoting *CREW* and *Juarez*.

## Farahi's Forfeited Challenge to FBI Document Categorization

Farahi raised one additional objection: that the government had not adequately justified how it defined the categories of documents used to explain its withholding decisions. The legal principle he invoked is well established. When an agency withholds documents on a category-by-category basis, it must use categories that are "sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied" to each category, as the D.C. Circuit held in *CREW* (quoting *Gallant v. NLRB* (1994)). Overbroad categories can mask improper withholding by lumping together documents that should be treated differently.

The court rejected this argument—not on the merits, but because Farahi forfeited it. "Farahi provides no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad." Without a specific challenge to specific categories, the court had no basis to evaluate whether the categories were improperly defined. The court cited *Khine v. DHS* (2019) for the principle that arguments not specifically developed are forfeited. This aspect of the opinion serves as a reminder that FOIA plaintiffs must do more than assert a possible defect in the agency's categorization scheme; they must identify with specificity which categories are problematic and why.

# Segregability Analysis and Ex Parte Declarations

## The Statutory Segregability Requirement and the FBI's Burden

Even when a FOIA exemption justifies withholding records, the agency's obligation does not end there. FOIA imposes an additional duty: the agency must take "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii). The statute also provides that "[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt." Id. § 552(b). This segregability requirement reflects FOIA's foundational commitment to maximizing public access to government information. An agency cannot simply withhold an entire document because some portions are exempt; it must identify and release the nonexempt material, unless doing so is not reasonably possible.

In this case, the FBI bore the burden of demonstrating that the withheld records contained no reasonably segregable portions. The government generally bears the burden of showing that a FOIA exemption applies, see 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B), and this burden extends to the segregability question. When an agency withholds records in their entirety, it must justify that decision by explaining why the nonexempt material cannot be separated from the exempt material. The D.C. Circuit has held that the government may withhold records in their entirety "when nonexempt portions are inextricably intertwined with exempt portions." *Juarez v. DOJ*, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (quoting *Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep't of Air Force*, 566 F.2d 242, 260 (D.C. Cir. 1977)). A court may rely on government affidavits that show "with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated." Id.

## The District Court's Reliance on Bender's Ex Parte Declaration

The procedural history of the segregability issue in this case is important. When the district court first addressed the FBI's summary judgment motion in November 2022, it concluded that the FBI had "seemingly" met its burden under Exemption 7(A) but identified a problem with segregability. The court needed more information to assess whether nonexempt material could be segregated and released—information the court could not state on the public record. The court therefore denied summary judgment without prejudice and ordered the FBI to provide additional justification.

The FBI responded with updated declarations from Joseph Bender, then the Acting Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Bender provided both a public declaration and an ex parte declaration, the latter submitted under seal and outside Farahi's view. The ex parte declaration supplied more detail addressing the district court's segregability concerns. In its second order, the district court explained that the FBI had provided a "detailed justification" for why the protected records were not reasonably segregable, based on Bender's supplemental ex parte submission. The court found that this submission satisfied the agency's burden on segregability.

## The Standard for Non-Segregable Information: Mead Data Central

The court's segregability analysis drew on a foundational D.C. Circuit decision, *Mead Data Central, Inc. v. U.S. Department of the Air Force*, 566 F.2d 242 (D.C. Cir. 1977). *Mead* established that when a small amount of nonexempt material is "interspersed line-by-line throughout the document," that material is not likely to be "reasonably segregable." Id. at 261. The logic is straightforward: if nonexempt fragments are scattered throughout exempt records, extracting them would produce isolated words or phrases devoid of meaningful context. The court in *Mead* also articulated a principle central to this case: "the focus of the FOIA is information, … not simply words which the Government has written down." Id. at 261 n.55. This distinction between information and mere words would prove decisive in Farahi's case.

## Farahi's Volume-Based Argument and the Court's Response

Farahi objected to the district court's segregability ruling by pointing to the sheer volume of responsive records. The FBI had identified approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs of potentially responsive information. Farahi argued that across such a vast quantity of records, there must necessarily be some segregable and releasable information. The intuition is understandable: how could all of that material be so thoroughly intertwined with exempt content that nothing at all could be released?

The court rejected this argument. Drawing on *Mead*, the court explained that even if individual words or phrases in the requested records could be disclosed without harming the FBI's law enforcement interests, that fact was "neither here nor there." The question under FOIA is whether meaningful, reasonably segregable *information* exists—not whether individual words can be mechanically extracted. If nonexempt material is randomly interspersed throughout exempt records, releasing those fragments would not advance FOIA's purpose of providing access to government information. The court cited *Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs*, 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023), in support of this principle. Farahi was correct that isolated words might be disclosable, but the district court properly concluded that no meaningful information was reasonably segregable.

## Farahi's Challenge to the District Court's Reliance on Ex Parte Declarations

Farahi also complained that the district court relied too heavily on Bender's ex parte declaration in conducting its segregability analysis. This objection raises a structural tension in FOIA litigation. Ex parte declarations—submitted by the government outside the plaintiff's view—necessarily handicap FOIA plaintiffs. The plaintiff cannot challenge what they cannot see. Yet in certain cases, the very information sought to be kept secret is also the information needed to justify the withholding. Requiring the government to disclose its segregability analysis on the public record could defeat the purpose of the exemption.

The court acknowledged this tension but found Farahi's challenge unpersuasive for two independent reasons. First, Farahi had not opposed the district court's acceptance of ex parte declarations. The FBI had moved to file a supplemental Hardy declaration under seal and ex parte in October 2020, and Farahi did not oppose that motion. Having acquiesced in the use of ex parte submissions, Farahi could not later challenge the district court's reliance on them. Second, the court explained that nothing required the district court to put its analysis of the ex parte declaration on the public record. Doing so could itself reveal the sensitive information the exemption was designed to protect.

## The Inherent Necessity of Ex Parte Declarations in Certain FOIA Cases

The court grounded its approach in *Perioperative Services*, which recognized that the problem of ex parte declarations handicapping plaintiffs "is inherent in those FOIA cases where, as here, an ex parte declaration is the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret." 57 F.4th at 1067 (cleaned up). This principle is especially forceful in cases involving national security and counterterrorism investigations, where the sensitivity of the underlying information is at its peak. The D.C. Circuit has "consistently reiterated the principle of deference to the executive in the FOIA context when national security concerns are implicated," including in Exemption 7(A) cases. *Ctr. for Nat'l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ*, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003). In such cases, the court must balance the plaintiff's interest in challenging the government's withholding against the risk that public disclosures would compromise the very law enforcement proceedings the exemption protects.

The practical result is that courts must sometimes accept that they can review the government's justifications only in camera and ex parte. This does not mean the government gets a free pass—the court still must assess whether the agency has met its burden with reasonable specificity. But it does mean that the plaintiff's ability to challenge the segregability analysis is necessarily limited by the sensitivity of the underlying records.

## The Court's Affirmance

Having addressed Farahi's arguments on both segregability and the use of ex parte declarations, the court affirmed the district court's grant of summary judgment to the FBI. The court held that the district court correctly concluded that no meaningful information was reasonably segregable from the withheld records. The Bender ex parte declaration, considered in context alongside the Hardy declarations, provided adequate justification for the FBI's decision to withhold the records in their entirety. The court also rejected Farahi's challenge to the district court's reliance on ex parte submissions, noting both his failure to oppose their acceptance and the inherent necessity of ex parte review in cases of this nature. The court's disposition was straightforward: "For these reasons, we affirm the district court."
