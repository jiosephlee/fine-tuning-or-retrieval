Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Why was summary judgment appropriate for determining whether GoAuto's cancellation procedures complied with the statute?
Question:
In Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, the parties filed cross-motions for summary judgment 'related solely to whether GoAuto's procedure for cancelling the financed insurance policies satisfied Louisiana law.' The district court granted GoAuto's motion and dismissed all claims. I'm trying to understand the procedural posture: How can a court grant summary judgment when there seem to be factual questions about the timing of signatures, who received what when, and whether the computer systems interacted as described? Is statutory compliance always a question of law suitable for summary judgment, or are there situations where factual disputes about compliance procedures would preclude summary judgment? What standard did the court apply?
Answer:
## Summary judgment was appropriate because the material facts were undisputed; only the legal meaning of the statute was at issue.

Your question seems to assume there were genuine factual disputes about how GoAuto's cancellation process worked. The opinion doesn't support that assumption. The parties filed **cross-motions** for summary judgment on the narrow question of whether GoAuto's procedure "satisfied Louisiana law." Cross-motions for summary judgment typically signal that both sides agree on what happened—the facts are established through discovery—and disagree only on the legal conclusion those facts support.

Here, the facts about GoAuto's procedures were established and not in genuine dispute: APAC's computer system automatically e-mails GoAuto a cancellation request with the required certifications; GoAuto's computer system cancels the policy upon receipt; an APAC employee later applies a signature stamp to printed documents; and since 2017 a GoAuto employee stamps the document as "received." Both sides agreed this is how the process worked. The dispute was about **what the statute requires** given those undisputed facts—specifically, whether the statute demands a signature before cancellation and whether electronic receipt by a computer system counts as "receipt" by the insurer.

### The legal standard

Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56, summary judgment is appropriate when there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. The Fifth Circuit reviews a grant of summary judgment **de novo**, applying the same standard as the district court.

The court framed the question as one of statutory interpretation: what does "certifying" require, and what does "receipt" mean under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)? These are questions of law, not fact. As the court explained, the statute "makes no explicit reference to a signature," and no Louisiana Supreme Court opinion has defined "receipt" in this context. Resolving those questions required the court to interpret the statute and apply it to the undisputed procedures—an exercise in legal reasoning suitable for summary judgment.

### When factual disputes *would* preclude summary judgment

Statutory compliance is not always resolvable on summary judgment. If, for example, there were a genuine dispute about whether APAC actually sent the notice, whether the notice contained the required certifications, whether the computer system actually received and processed the request, or whether payments had in fact been made—those would be factual disputes requiring trial. Summary judgment would be inappropriate because the court could not determine statutory compliance without first resolving contested facts.

But that was not this case. The Plaintiffs did not dispute that the e-mails were sent and received or that the certifications were included. They argued that the statute, properly interpreted, requires more than what GoAuto did—a **pre-cancellation signature** and **physical possession by a human employee**. Those are legal arguments about what the statute demands, not factual disputes about what occurred.

### How the court resolved the legal questions

On the signature issue, the court held that the statute requires the premium finance company to "certif[y]" certain information but does not require a signature. The court noted the statute identifies "the insurance premium finance company" as the entity doing the certifying, and declined to add a procedural requirement the legislature did not include: "Strict adherence to the statute is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures."

On the receipt issue, the court adopted the Black's Law Dictionary definition of "receipt" as the act of receiving, especially by taking physical possession. It then concluded that GoAuto's computer system receiving the e-mail satisfied the statute: "This is a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did. GoAuto's method of receiving the notice and reacting to it satisfies the statute."

### In short

Summary judgment was appropriate because the parties agreed on the facts of GoAuto's cancellation process and disagreed only on whether that process satisfied the statute. When the material facts are undisputed and the case turns on statutory interpretation, summary judgment is the proper vehicle for resolving the dispute. The factual details you mention—the timing of signatures, the computer system interaction—were not in genuine dispute; they were the agreed-upon facts to which the court applied its legal interpretation of the statute.
