Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

### Can a computer system's automated receipt satisfy a statutory 'receipt' requirement, or must a human take possession?
Question:
In Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, the court held that GoAuto's computer system receiving APAC's email constituted 'receipt' under Louisiana's insurance statute, even though no human at GoAuto saw or handled the notice until after the policy was automatically cancelled. The plaintiffs argued that 'receipt' requires human possession. The court used Black's Law Dictionary definitions of 'receipt' and 'receive' to support its conclusion. I'm confused about how courts handle statutory requirements when technology has changed the practical meaning of terms like 'receipt.' Does it matter that the statute was presumably written with human actors in mind? Are there other areas of law where courts have had to decide whether electronic or automated receipt satisfies statutory language?
Answer:
## Answer

The short answer is yes—a computer system's automated receipt can satisfy a statutory "receipt" requirement, at least under the reasoning in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*. The Fifth Circuit held that GoAuto's computer system receiving APAC's email constituted "receipt" under Louisiana's insurance-premium finance statute, even though no human at GoAuto saw or handled the notice until after the policy was automatically cancelled.

### The court's analytical framework

The court began with the text. The Louisiana statute provided that "[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested." La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Neither the Louisiana Supreme Court nor the statute itself defined "receipt." Faced with that gap, the court turned to Black's Law Dictionary, which defines "receipt" as "[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession" and "receive" as "[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source." The court accepted those definitions and concluded that GoAuto could cancel "once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation."

The plaintiffs argued that the legislature's use of "receipt"—as opposed to simply requiring the request be "sent"—required a human at GoAuto to take possession before cancellation. The court rejected this. It found that once APAC's computer system sent the email and GoAuto's web service received it, GoAuto had "receipt" for statutory purposes. The court described the GoAuto web service as "like a listener" that is "always listening, waiting to be called," and when the APAC system calls it, the GoAuto system "performs the activity"—that is, cancels the policy.

### Does it matter that the statute was written with human actors in mind?

The court's answer, in substance, is no. The opinion characterizes the statute as "a modern statute set in current times" and states plainly that "company computers do much that individuals once did." This reasoning signals that when a statute is silent about who or what must "receive" a notice, courts will not import a human-actor requirement that the text does not impose.

Notably, the court did not engage in any analysis of legislative intent or drafting history. It did not ask whether the Louisiana legislature contemplated automated receipt when it enacted or amended the statute. Instead, the court relied on the dictionary definitions of "receipt" and "receive" and applied those meanings to the technological reality before it.

### Strict adherence did not mean adding requirements

An important feature of the court's reasoning was its treatment of Louisiana's "strict adherence" standard. Louisiana courts require strict adherence to the statutory cancellation procedures; any defect renders the cancellation ineffective. But the court was careful to distinguish strict adherence from judicial supplementation: "'Strict adherence' to the procedures for cancellation is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures." The court thus refused to read a human-receipt requirement into a statute that the legislature did not include—even under a strict adherence standard.

This is a significant move. The plaintiffs had a plausible argument that "strict adherence" should mean that every statutory term is given its most demanding reading. The court rejected that framing: strict adherence means following what the statute actually requires, not augmenting the statute with additional procedural hurdles.

### The broader pattern

The opinion does not survey other areas of law, so it does not directly address whether courts have reached similar conclusions in other statutory contexts. What it does provide is a clear method for handling statutory terms in light of technological change:

1. **Identify the statutory text** and determine whether the term is defined.
2. **If the term is undefined, consult dictionary definitions** to establish ordinary meaning.
3. **Apply the ordinary meaning to the current technological context** without importing requirements the text does not impose.
4. **Decline to add procedural requirements** under the guise of strict adherence.

The court's statement that "company computers do much that individuals once did" signals a willingness to treat automated systems as adequate statutory actors when the statutory text does not specify human action.

One caveat: the court emphasized that this was "a modern statute set in current times." The opinion leaves open whether a court would apply the same reasoning to a statute enacted long before automated systems existed, particularly if legislative history or statutory context suggested the legislature specifically contemplated human action. The opinion's reasoning appears anchored in part to the statute's modern context—a factor worth keeping in mind when analogizing to other statutes.
