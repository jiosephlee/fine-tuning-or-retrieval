Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

Chapter 4: # The Receipt Requirement: Did GoAuto Properly 'Receive' the Cancellation Request?

## The Plaintiffs' Argument: Shared Corporate Infrastructure Defeats Receipt

Having addressed whether APAC's certification required a signature, the Fifth Circuit turned to the plaintiffs' second principal argument: that GoAuto never properly "received" the cancellation request at all. The plaintiffs grounded this argument in the shared physical and corporate infrastructure between GoAuto and APAC, identifying two distinct periods in which different structural problems allegedly prevented receipt.

Before 2017, APAC employees would physically take the printed cancellation documents to a filing cabinet shared by GoAuto and APAC. The plaintiffs pointed out, however, that the shared filing room belonged not to GoAuto Insurance Company itself, but to GoAuto Management Services—a separate corporate entity. The implication was that physical possession by a related but distinct corporate entity did not constitute receipt by the insurer that had issued the policy.

After 2017, the procedure changed. The signed cancellation document was physically taken to a GoAuto employee who stamped it as "received" and placed it in a shared filing cabinet. But the plaintiffs contended that the employee who affixed the stamp was actually an APAC employee, not a GoAuto employee. In their view, this meant that even the post-2017 procedure did not result in GoAuto itself taking possession of the cancellation request. The shared corporate relationship between GoAuto and APAC thus permeated every step of the physical handling of cancellation documents, and the plaintiffs argued that this intertwining meant the statutory requirement of receipt by the insurer was never satisfied.

## The Plaintiffs' Interpretation: "Receipt" Means More Than "Sending"

The plaintiffs' structural argument was paired with a textual one. They emphasized that the Louisiana legislature used the word "receipt" in the statute, not merely "sending." Under the statute, the premium finance company must send the cancellation request to the insurer, but cancellation cannot proceed until the insurer has "receipt" of that request. The plaintiffs contended that this deliberate statutory choice required GoAuto to actually take possession of the notice before cancelling the policy. In other words, the statute created a two-step process: first, APAC sends the request; second, GoAuto receives it. The plaintiffs argued that the second step—actual possession by GoAuto—was a prerequisite to cancellation, and because of the shared infrastructure problems they identified, that second step never occurred.

## The Statutory Framework: What the Statute Says About Receipt

The statutory provision at the center of this dispute is Louisiana Revised Statute § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i), which provides: "Upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract." This provision sits at the intersection of two stages in the cancellation process. The prior subsection, § 9:3550(G)(3)(a), governs what the premium finance company must send to the insurer: a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a certifying statement. The (b)(i) provision then addresses what happens next: once the insurer receives that notice and statement, the insurer "shall consider" cancellation to have been requested and "may proceed to cancel."

The statute thus envisions a sequence in which the premium finance company initiates the process by sending the required documents, and the insurer acts upon receiving them. The statutory text, however, does not specify what "receipt" means. It does not say whether receipt requires physical possession by a human being, electronic delivery to a computer system, or something else entirely. The statute also does not distinguish between receipt by the insurer's employee, receipt by the insurer's computer, or receipt by a related corporate entity acting on the insurer's behalf.

## The Absence of Definitional Guidance

The Fifth Circuit observed that the Louisiana Supreme Court had never defined "receipt" as used in § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). The statute itself contains no definition of the term. Louisiana appellate courts had not provided meaningful guidance on the question either. This left the Fifth Circuit, sitting in diversity and applying Louisiana law, without a controlling state-court interpretation of a key statutory term.

This absence of authoritative state law created both a challenge and an opportunity. The challenge was that the Fifth Circuit, as a federal court predicting how the Louisiana Supreme Court would interpret its own statute, had to proceed cautiously. The opportunity was that the lack of contrary authority meant the court could rely on ordinary dictionary definitions and common sense to determine what "receipt" required in the modern business context. The court chose the latter path, noting that it was not free to add glosses to the statute that the Louisiana legislature had not included, but also not free to ignore the practical realities of how modern insurance companies conduct business.

## Black's Law Dictionary Definitions

To fill the definitional gap, the Fifth Circuit turned to Black's Law Dictionary, a standard reference for interpreting undefined legal terms. The dictionary defined "receipt" as "[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession." It defined "receive" as "[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source." These definitions emphasized two features: the act of taking possession and the transfer from an outside source to the recipient.

The court accepted these definitions and concluded that GoAuto could satisfy the statutory requirement of receipt once it took "possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation." The dictionary definitions did not require that a human being physically handle the document, nor did they require that the recipient be a specific corporate entity rather than an agent or system acting on its behalf. The definitions spoke in terms of possession and transfer, concepts that could apply to electronic communications as readily as to physical documents.

## The Computerized Cancellation Process

The key to the court's analysis was its understanding of how GoAuto actually received and processed cancellation requests. The court quoted at length from a GoAuto deponent who explained the electronic interaction between APAC's and GoAuto's computer systems. The APAC system "makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date." The deponent described the GoAuto web service as being "like a listener. It's always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it's called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity."

This description was central to the court's reasoning. The APAC system sent the cancellation request electronically to GoAuto's system, which received it automatically and, upon receipt, cancelled the policy. The process was entirely automated. No human being at GoAuto reviewed the request, approved the cancellation, or even saw the notice before the policy was cancelled. The cancellation occurred in the early morning hours, triggered by the interaction between the two computer systems. Only later in the morning, after the policy had already been cancelled, did an APAC employee affix a signature stamp to the printed cancellation documents and physically deliver them to a GoAuto employee for stamping and filing.

The plaintiffs' argument about shared infrastructure thus missed the critical point: the physical handling of documents that occurred later in the morning was not the moment of receipt. Receipt had already occurred electronically, when APAC's computer system transmitted the cancellation request to GoAuto's computer system and GoAuto's system took possession of it. The subsequent physical steps—stamping, signing, filing—were record-keeping measures, not the act of receipt itself.

## No Human Involvement Until After Cancellation

The court emphasized that no person was involved in the receipt and cancellation process until after the GoAuto computer system had already effected the cancellation. The court stated plainly: "No person is involved until later in the morning after the GoAuto computer system effects cancellation." At that later time, an APAC employee used a signature stamp on the printed documents, and the documents were physically transferred to a GoAuto employee for stamping and filing.

This temporal sequence was important for two reasons. First, it meant that the plaintiffs' arguments about which corporate entity employed the person who stamped the documents were beside the point. The stamping occurred after receipt and after cancellation, so it could not be the act of receipt the statute required. Second, it underscored the court's broader point that the statute was "a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did." The court was willing to recognize that receipt could be accomplished by a computer system acting on the insurer's behalf, just as it had long been accomplished by employees acting on the insurer's behalf.

## The Court's Conclusion: Electronic Receipt Satisfies the Statute

The Fifth Circuit concluded that GoAuto's method of receiving the notice and reacting to it satisfied the statute. The court framed its holding in straightforward terms: "What is necessary prior to cancellation is that the insurer had 'receipt' of notice of cancellation sent by the premium finance company." The court found that the electronic transmission from APAC's computer system to GoAuto's computer system constituted receipt under the ordinary meaning of that term. GoAuto's system took possession of the request from an outside source—APAC's system—and acted upon it by cancelling the policy.

The court's reasoning reflected several interpretive principles. First, it adhered to the statutory text, which required receipt but did not specify the manner of receipt. Second, it declined to add requirements that the legislature had not included, such as a signature requirement or human involvement. Third, it recognized the practical realities of modern business operations, in which computer-to-computer communication is a standard and accepted method of transferring information between entities. Fourth, it relied on dictionary definitions that were consistent with both traditional and electronic forms of receipt.

The court also noted that "strict adherence" to the statute, as required by Louisiana courts, was not an invitation for courts to add procedural steps the legislature had not enacted. This principle, which the court had invoked earlier in addressing the signature question, applied equally to the receipt question. The statute required receipt; it did not require receipt by a particular person, at a particular location, or in a particular format.

## The District Court's Alternative Reasoning: The "Private Carrier" Theory

Before concluding, the Fifth Circuit briefly addressed the district court's alternative reasoning. The district court had held that GoAuto's cancellation procedures complied with the statute in a different way: it found that "the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier." The statute allows the premium finance company to send the cancellation request "by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail." The district court had reasoned that the APAC employee who physically delivered the cancellation documents to GoAuto qualified as a "private carrier."

The district court recognized an absence of Louisiana case law interpreting "private carrier" and found guidance in a Fifth Circuit case involving a railroad, Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App'x 453 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court's analogy was, as the Fifth Circuit described it, "imaginative and unconventional." Because the Fifth Circuit found that the e-mail transmission satisfied the statute independently, it declined to consider the validity of the district court's "private carrier" reasoning. The court's choice not to reach the alternative basis was consistent with the general principle that federal appellate courts should avoid deciding issues unnecessarily, particularly ones involving novel interpretations of state law. By resolving the case on the narrower ground of electronic receipt, the court left the meaning of "private carrier" in this statutory context for another day.
