Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

Chapter 3: # The Signature Requirement: Does 'Certifying' Mean Signing?

## Plaintiffs' Argument: The Timing of the Signature

The plaintiffs' central challenge to GoAuto's cancellation procedures targets the sequence of events surrounding the signature on APAC's certification. Under GoAuto's established procedures, when an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice that the policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment arrives by midnight on the tenth day, APAC's computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail containing a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. Upon receipt of that e-mail, GoAuto's computer system cancels the policy automatically—no human involvement is required at this stage. Only later that morning does an APAC employee affix a signature stamp to the printed cancellation documents.

The plaintiffs seized on this temporal gap. They argued that because no signature appears on APAC's certification until after the policy has already been cancelled by the computer system, the cancellation was premature and therefore ineffective. Their theory depends on a critical interpretive move: that the statutory requirement of "certifying" certain information necessarily implies that a human being must sign the certification before the insurer may act on it. If a signature is required before cancellation, and the signature comes only after cancellation, then every cancellation GoAuto processed through this automated system was invalid—or so the argument ran.

## The Statutory Text: Silence on Signatures

The Fifth Circuit began its analysis with the text of the governing statute. Louisiana Revised Statute § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) specifies what a premium finance company must include when it requests cancellation of an insurance policy due to an uncured default. The statute requires the company to send the insurer "a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying" four categories of information: that the finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney; that the agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured; that a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured upon default; and that copies of the notice were sent to all persons shown by the agreement to have an interest in any loss.

Critically, the statute makes no explicit reference to a signature. Nothing in the statutory text states that the certification must be signed, that a named individual must attest to the certified information, or that any particular form of authentication is required. The plaintiffs' argument therefore required the court to find that the word "certifying" itself carries an implicit signature requirement—that to "certify" information, in this statutory context, is necessarily to sign one's name to it.

## Whether "Certifying" Implicitly Requires a Signature

The court's approach to this question illustrates a fundamental principle of statutory interpretation: courts generally will not read requirements into a statute that the legislature did not include. The Fifth Circuit noted that if a signature requirement exists, it must be found in the word "certifying," because the statute mentions nothing else that could supply one. The court then examined whether Louisiana courts had interpreted "certifying" in this statute to require a signature.

The court's inquiry was complicated by the relative scarcity of Louisiana case law directly addressing this question. As the court observed, "no Louisiana judicial opinion considering insurance-premium finance companies' procedures under the statute has addressed the need for a signature" with one limited exception. This absence of authority itself became part of the court's reasoning: without clear guidance from Louisiana courts that "certifying" demands a signature, the Fifth Circuit was reluctant to impose such a requirement on its own.

## Louisiana Case Law Review

The court surveyed the relevant Louisiana jurisprudence to determine whether any case had established or implied a signature requirement. The cases the court examined proved largely unhelpful to the plaintiffs' position, though each offered a slightly different angle on what the statute requires.

In **KMJ Services, Inc. v. Hood**, a Louisiana appellate court paraphrased the statutory requirement as needing "a statement certifying compliance with" the statute. This formulation focused on the content of the statement—what it must certify—rather than on any formal requirement such as a signature. The court's language treated the certification as a substantive representation that the statutory conditions had been met, not as a formal act requiring personal attestation.

In **Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd's Insurance Co.**, the court described the process as allowing cancellation "[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company." This framing emphasized the insurer's receipt of the notice and statement as the triggering event, not the presence of a signature on the statement. The court's focus was on the transmission and receipt of the document, not on its form of authentication.

The court also cited **Lewis v. Julien**, which the Fifth Circuit described as "similarly unhelpful on this question." None of these cases directly addressed whether a signature was required for an effective certification under the statute.

## Benitez v. Elsayed: The One Case Mentioning Lack of Signature

The most relevant case for the plaintiffs' argument was **Benitez v. Elsayed**, a Louisiana appellate decision that mentioned the absence of a signature in its discussion of a deficient cancellation notice. In Benitez, the court noted that the notice "does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to 'certify' any of the statements contained therein." This passage at first glance appears supportive of the plaintiffs' position, as it lists the lack of a signature among the notice's shortcomings.

However, the Fifth Circuit read Benitez more narrowly. The court emphasized that Benitez identified multiple deficiencies in the notice—it lacked a named employee, lacked a signature, and, critically, did not purport to "certify" any of the required statements at all. The Benitez court did not hold that the absence of a signature alone would disqualify a cancellation if the other statutory elements were satisfied. The lack of a signature was part of a constellation of problems, not an independently fatal defect. This distinction was essential to the Fifth Circuit's analysis, because it meant that Benitez could not be read as establishing a per se signature requirement.

## Distinguishing Benitez: Certification Was Present Here

The Fifth Circuit then drew a sharp distinction between the facts in Benitez and the facts before it. In Benitez, the notice failed to "certify" any of the required information—it did not contain the substantive representations the statute demands. In the present case, each notice from APAC to GoAuto explicitly certified the relevant information. APAC's request for cancellation, sent on APAC's letterhead, stated that "APAC certifies" four categories of information: the existence of a valid power of attorney, the existence of an uncured default, that a ten-day notice of cancellation had been sent to the insured, and that copies of the notice had been sent to all persons with an interest in any loss.

Thus, the deficiency that the Fifth Circuit appeared to view as most significant in Benitez—the total absence of any certification—was not present here. The APAC notices did contain substantive certifications of all four statutorily required categories. The only feature shared with the deficient notice in Benitez was the absence of an individual's signature and the failure to identify a specific employee. Because Benitez did not establish that these features alone rendered a cancellation ineffective, and because the APAC notices actually contained the certifications that Benitez's notice lacked, the Fifth Circuit found Benitez insufficient to support the plaintiffs' argument.

The court also noted that the notices in this record, like those in Benitez, did not identify any particular employee by printed name. But the court saw no reason, "without clearer Louisiana authority," that a company could not certify information without a named employee being identified. This reasoning reflects a practical concern: the statute's requirements focus on the substance of what is certified, not on the identity of the person doing the certifying.

## The Court's Reasoning: The Company, Not an Individual, Certifies

The Fifth Circuit's conclusion rested significantly on the statutory text's identification of who effects the certification. The statute states that it is "the insurance premium finance company" that effects cancellation by "certifying" certain information in a notice. The statute does not refer to an agent, an employee, or an authorized representative of the company performing the certification—it identifies the company itself as the certifying entity.

This textual choice by the legislature carried interpretive weight for the court. If the legislature had intended to require a personal attestation by a named individual, it could have said so. Instead, it assigned the certification function to the corporate entity. The court's reasoning suggests that when a statute identifies an entity rather than a person as the actor, the formal requirements for the act should be understood in light of how such entities operate. A company certifies through its systems and procedures, and the statute does not specify that those systems must include a human signature at any particular point in the process.

The court also considered the practical context in which these cancellations occur. The statute is, as the court described it, "a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did." The automated system by which APAC's computer sends the certification e-mail to GoAuto's computer, which then processes the cancellation, represents the kind of electronic, system-to-system transaction that modern insurance operations routinely employ. The statute's silence on signatures, combined with its identification of the company as the certifying party, allowed the court to conclude that the automated certification satisfied the statutory requirement.

## Conclusion: Strict Adherence Does Not Mean Adding Requirements

The Fifth Circuit tied its analysis together with a principle that has broader significance for statutory interpretation. Louisiana courts require "strict adherence" to the statutory procedures for cancellation of financed insurance policies. The plaintiffs invoked this standard to argue that the absence of a signature constituted a defect that rendered the cancellations ineffective. The Fifth Circuit accepted the strict adherence standard but rejected the plaintiffs' interpretation of what it requires.

The court stated the principle directly: "'Strict adherence' to the procedures for cancellation is not an invitation for courts to add to the procedures." Strict adherence means that the procedures the legislature actually enacted must be followed exactly. It does not mean that courts may supplement those procedures with additional requirements—such as a signature—that the legislature did not include. The statute requires a statement certifying four categories of information, sent by the premium finance company to the insurer. APAC's notices contained exactly that. The absence of a human signature before cancellation did not constitute a deviation from the statutory procedure, because the statute contains no signature requirement to deviate from.

The court concluded: "We conclude that Louisiana law does not require a signature on the notice sent by the premium finance company to the insurer." This holding resolves the question presented and underscores a lesson that extends beyond this case: when a statute specifies what must be done but is silent on how a particular step must be authenticated, courts will generally not read in formal requirements the legislature omitted, even under a standard of strict adherence. The rigor of "strict adherence" operates to ensure compliance with what the statute actually says, not to expand the statute beyond its text.
