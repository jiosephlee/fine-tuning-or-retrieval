Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# 'Receipt' in the Age of APIs: Statutory Interpretation When Computers Talk to Computers

When a Louisiana statute says an insurer must "receive" a cancellation notice before terminating a policy, does it matter that no human being ever saw the notice? That is the question the Fifth Circuit answered in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, and the answer—no, it does not matter—has implications that extend well beyond Louisiana insurance law. As more regulatory and commercial processes become fully automated, courts will increasingly confront statutes that use language rooted in a world of paper, mail, and human hands. *Williams* offers an early test case for how courts should handle that translation, and the Fifth Circuit's approach is both revealing and, in some respects, unsatisfying.

## The Case and Its Stakes

The plaintiffs in *Williams* were insureds whose GoAuto automobile policies had been canceled after they defaulted on premium-financing agreements with American Premium Assistance Company (APAC). Under Louisiana law, a premium finance company may request cancellation of a policy by sending the insurer a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a certified statement confirming the existence of a valid power of attorney, the uncured default, and that proper notice was sent to the insured and interested parties. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The insurer may then cancel the policy "[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company." La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i).

Louisiana courts require "strict adherence" to these statutory requirements; "[a]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy." The stakes are significant: if cancellation is ineffective, the policy remains in force, meaning the insurer may be liable for claims arising after the purported cancellation date. Given that premium financing is common among policyholders who cannot afford to pay an entire annual premium upfront—and that such policyholders may be less likely to default carefully on their installment obligations—the volume of potential disputes over cancellation procedures is substantial.

GoAuto's cancellation process was almost entirely automated. When an insured failed to make a timely payment, APAC sent a ten-day notice. If payment was not submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC's computer system automatically sent GoAuto an email containing the cancellation notice and the required certification. GoAuto's computer system—a "web service" described by a deponent as "always listening, waiting to be called"—received the email and automatically canceled the policy. No human being was involved until later that morning, when an APAC employee affixed a signature stamp to printed cancellation documents and delivered them to a GoAuto employee who stamped them as "received."

The plaintiffs argued that this process failed to satisfy the statute in two principal ways. First, the certification lacked a signature at the time of cancellation. Second, GoAuto never actually "received" the cancellation request because no human at GoAuto took possession of it before the automated system acted.

## The Signature Question—and Why It Matters Less

The Fifth Circuit's rejection of the signature requirement was straightforward and defensible. The statute requires that the premium finance company certify certain information, but it says nothing about a signature. The court acknowledged one Louisiana appellate decision, *Benitez v. Elsayed*, that noted the absence of a signature among a notice's shortcomings, but correctly observed that *Benitez* did not hold that a missing signature alone invalidated an otherwise proper certification. The statute attributes the certification to "the insurance premium finance company"—an entity, not an individual—and the court reasonably concluded that an entity can certify information without identifying a human signatory.

This holding is unlikely to generate much controversy. Corporate certifications are routinely executed without individual signatures in electronic contexts, and the statute's silence on the point makes the plaintiffs' position difficult to sustain. The more interesting and consequential question is the second one.

## "Receipt" and the Problem of Automated Processing

The plaintiffs' argument on receipt was more textured. The statute uses the word "receipt," which the plaintiffs contended requires the insurer to take possession of the cancellation request before canceling the policy. In GoAuto's process, the cancellation occurred automatically upon the computer system's receipt of the email—before any human at GoAuto was aware of it. The plaintiffs argued that the legislature's use of "receipt," rather than a simple sending requirement, implied that a human at the insurer needed to take possession.

The Fifth Circuit rejected this argument, but its reasoning deserves close examination. The court began by noting that the Louisiana Supreme Court has not defined "receipt" as used in the statute and that the statute itself contains no definition. The court then turned to Black's Law Dictionary, which defines "receipt" as "[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession" and "receive" as "[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source." From these definitions, the court concluded that "GoAuto may cancel a policy once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation."

The court then applied this conclusion to the automated process. It described the GoAuto web service as a "listener" that responds when "called" by APAC's system, and concluded: "This is a modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did. GoAuto's method of receiving the notice and reacting to it satisfies the statute."

There are two moves here, and they rest on different analytical foundations. The first move—defining "receipt" as taking possession, delivery, or custody—is conventional dictionary-based interpretation. The second move—concluding that a computer system's automated receipt satisfies this definition—is where the court does something more interesting, and more contestable.

## The Conceptual Gap

The difficulty is that Black's Law Dictionary's definitions, upon which the court relied, are anchored in physical possession. "Taking physical possession" is the paradigmatic example the dictionary gives for "receipt." "To come into possession of" implies an entity capable of possessing something. When a computer system automatically processes an email and executes a cancellation, it is not obvious that anyone—or anything—has "taken possession" in any meaningful sense. The email has been received by a server, parsed by an application, and acted upon by a web service. No human has come into possession of anything. The computer system itself does not "possess" the email in any anthropomorphic sense; it processes data.

One could argue that the court's reliance on physical-possession definitions actually cuts against its conclusion. If "receipt" means taking physical possession, and if only natural persons (or perhaps corporate entities acting through human agents) can take physical possession, then an automated computer process does not satisfy the statutory requirement. The court's logical bridge—that "company computers do much that individuals once did"—is not interpretation so much as adaptation. It is a policy judgment that the statute should accommodate modern commercial practices, not a conclusion that flows from the statutory text or the dictionary definitions the court cited.

This is not to say the court reached the wrong result. The Louisiana statute expressly permits transmission "via electronic mail," which strongly suggests the legislature contemplated digital communication between the finance company and the insurer. It would be odd to allow email transmission but require human possession of the transmitted document before the insurer could act on it. And the statute's reference to the insurer proceeding to cancel "upon receipt" is most naturally read as identifying the trigger for cancellation, not prescribing a human-in-the-loop requirement. But the court could have reached this conclusion more directly by reasoning from the statute's express authorization of email transmission, rather than by invoking dictionary definitions rooted in physical possession and then silently extending them to cover automated processing.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The most significant implication of *Williams* is its tacit endorsement of the principle that when a statute authorizes electronic communication, automated processing of that communication can satisfy statutory prerequisites even without human involvement. This principle will matter well beyond insurance cancellation. Consider the many contexts in which statutes require that an entity "receive," "accept," or "acknowledge" something: regulatory filings, consumer notices, contract formations, and government benefit determinations, to name a few. If automated receipt suffices in the insurance-cancellation context, it is hard to see why it would not suffice in these other contexts as well.

This raises a deeper question that *Williams* does not fully address: should courts distinguish between statutes that expressly contemplate electronic communication and those that do not? The Louisiana statute at issue in *Williams* was drafted or amended to permit email transmission, making it relatively easy to conclude that the legislature contemplated digital processes. But many statutes use "receipt" language without any reference to electronic communication. For those statutes, the argument for automated receipt is weaker. A court applying *Williams*'s logic to a statute drafted in 1965 that requires "receipt" of a notice would be making a much larger interpretive leap, effectively updating the statute to accommodate technology the legislature never considered.

The court's decision also has practical implications for the insurance industry. By approving GoAuto's fully automated cancellation process, the Fifth Circuit has effectively blessed a model in which premium finance companies and insurers can coordinate cancellation through direct system-to-system integration, without human review. This is efficient, and efficiency is generally good for consumers to the extent it reduces administrative costs. But it also eliminates a human checkpoint that might catch errors—incorrect default determinations, misidentified insureds, or improper certifications. The Louisiana statute's "strict adherence" requirement was designed to ensure that cancellation occurs only when the statutory prerequisites are met. Automated processing may satisfy the letter of the statute while reducing the practical protection the strict-adherence doctrine was meant to provide.

The court's refusal to certify the question to the Louisiana Supreme Court is also worth noting. The Fifth Circuit acknowledged that the Louisiana Supreme Court has not defined "receipt" under the statute, and the interpretation of that term has significant implications for the state's insurance industry. The court's reason for declining certification—that "a term has not yet been defined by a state supreme court 'is not sufficient to warrant certification'"—is technically correct as a statement of Fifth Circuit policy, but it understates the significance of the question. The Louisiana Supreme Court might well disagree with the Fifth Circuit's conclusion that automated computer receipt satisfies the statute, particularly given the strict-adherence doctrine's protective purpose. By resolving the question itself, the Fifth Circuit has effectively made state law on an important question of insurance regulation without input from the state's highest court.

## A Framework for the Digital Statutory Interpretation Problem

*Williams* illustrates a problem that courts will face with increasing frequency: how to apply statutory language rooted in analog-era concepts to digital-era practices. There are at least three approaches courts might take, each with different implications.

The first is what we might call strict textualism: if the statute says "receipt" and the dictionary defines receipt as physical possession, then automated computer processing does not satisfy the requirement unless a human takes possession. This approach respects the original meaning of the statutory text but may produce absurd results when applied to modern commercial practices that the legislature presumably intended to accommodate.

The second is the approach the Fifth Circuit took in *Williams*: invoke dictionary definitions rooted in physical possession, then extend them to cover automated processing through the observation that "company computers do much that individuals once did." This approach reaches sensible practical results but does so through a logical move that is not fully articulated in the opinion. The gap between the dictionary definition and its application is bridged by an implicit policy judgment rather than by textual analysis.

The third approach—and the one I think courts should adopt—is what we might call contextual technological adaptation: examine the statute as a whole to determine whether the legislature contemplated or intended to accommodate electronic communication, and if so, interpret the statute's operative terms in light of that intent. Under this approach, the Louisiana statute's express authorization of email transmission would be the key analytical move. Having permitted email, the legislature presumably intended that email could effectuate the statutory process, including receipt. The dictionary definitions would be secondary, used to confirm rather than to drive the analysis. This approach is more transparent than the one the Fifth Circuit took, and it provides a more principled basis for distinguishing between statutes that contemplate electronic communication and those that do not.

## Conclusion

*Williams* is, in one sense, a narrow case about insurance cancellation procedures in Louisiana. But it raises a question that will recur across many areas of law: when a statute says an entity must "receive" something, and the entity's computer receives it without any human awareness, has "receipt" occurred? The Fifth Circuit answered yes, and that answer is probably correct as applied to this statute. But the court's reasoning—relying on dictionary definitions rooted in physical possession and then silently extending them to automated processing—leaves something to be desired. Courts facing similar questions in the future would do well to ground their analysis more directly in the statutory context, and to be more candid about the policy judgments that necessarily accompany the application of analog-era statutory language to digital-era practices.
