Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Judicial Inference vs. Expert Proof: The District Court's Error in Constructing an Infringement Theory from Fragmented Testimony

When a jury returns a nine-figure infringement verdict, the trial court's role on JMOL is not to rescue the verdict by assembling an infringement theory from scattered fragments of expert testimony that the expert himself never coherently articulated. That is the core lesson of the Federal Circuit's decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, a case that offers a crisp illustration of the boundary between permissible inference and impermissible construction—and a reminder that the substantial evidence standard has real teeth when a patentee's infringement case rests on self-contradictory expert opinion.

## The Stakes and the Setup

Finesse Wireless sued AT&T and Nokia, alleging that Nokia's radios—specifically their PIM cancellation (PIM-C) feature—infringed two patents related to mitigating intermodulation product interference. The jury found all asserted claims infringed and awarded over $166 million in lump-sum damages. The Eastern District of Texas denied the defendants' JMOL motions on noninfringement and damages. The Federal Circuit reversed in full, vacating both the infringement finding and the damages award.

The decision matters not only for the parties—it wipes out a substantial verdict—but also for what it says about the trial court's obligations when evaluating JMOL motions. The Federal Circuit identified a structural error in how the district court approached the sufficiency of the evidence: rather than asking whether Finesse's expert had actually articulated a coherent infringement theory, the district court stitched one together from testimony fragments and then deferred to a jury verdict built on that judicially constructed theory.

## The '134 Patent: An Expert Who Could Not Land on a Theory

The '134 patent claims a method for reducing intermodulation product interference by sampling a passband of received signals that includes both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals," then isolating and canceling the interference. The critical limitation—1[a]—requires the radio's receiver to sample a passband that includes both categories of signals.

Finesse's infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, relied on a Nokia technical document to map the accused radios' operation onto the claimed method. He consistently and repeatedly testified that the "signals of interest" corresponded to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and that the "interference generating signals" corresponded to the modeled PIM path—both depicted along a dashed red path in the Nokia diagram. This mapping was set out in his expert report and repeated at trial across multiple exchanges.

The problem emerged on cross-examination. The modeled PIM path is generated downstream of the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC)—the very receiver that performs the sampling. A signal cannot be sampled before it is generated. If the modeled PIM path is the "interference generating signals," then limitation 1[a] cannot be met, because the accused radios' receiver does not sample a passband that includes interference generating signals.

Confronted with this contradiction, Dr. Wells pivoted. He began referencing "two signals on that red path" and mentioned x1 and x2—the two inputs to the RF ADC. But he never mapped x1 and x2 onto "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals" as the claims require. He continued to testify that the two signals were the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path. When asked whether x1 was the modeled PIM path, he offered only that "x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path." He never completed the analytical bridge from x1 and x2 to the claimed signal categories.

The district court nonetheless denied JMOL, finding that "Dr. Wells testified that there are two signals entering the RF ADC, and that those are signals of interest and the interference generating signals. Dr. Wells is clearly referring to x1 and x2." The Federal Circuit's response was direct: "There is nothing clear about Dr. Wells' testimony."

This is the analytical heart of the decision. The district court did not merely draw a reasonable inference from ambiguous evidence; it constructed the missing link in Finesse's infringement theory by judicial interpolation. Dr. Wells never testified that x1 and x2 correspond to "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." Finesse itself attempted to supply that mapping only on appeal, by piecing together testimony from pages ninety pages apart in the trial record. Even then, the mapping required a chain of inferences that the expert himself never offered.

The Federal Circuit grounded its analysis in *Johns Hopkins University v. Datascope Corp.*, 543 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2008), where the court held that self-contradictory expert testimony cannot satisfy the patentee's burden of proof. In *Datascope*, the expert opined that an accused device contacted a vessel in three dimensions but elsewhere testified that it contacted in only two places. Here, Dr. Wells repeatedly identified the modeled PIM path as the "interference generating signals" and then, when that mapping was shown to be impossible, gestured toward x1 and x2 without ever completing the analytical work. The court treated this as the same species of self-contradiction that defeated the infringement finding in *Datascope*.

## The '775 Patent: Seven Multiplications in Search of an Expert

The '775 patent presented a different but related problem. The claims require generating intermodulation product cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals—S1, S2, and S3—in seven specific combinations. The district court construed "three signals S1, S2 and S3" as "separately identifiable" but not "unique," meaning the same signal could serve as more than one of the three. The Federal Circuit affirmed that construction, rejecting AT&T's argument that Finesse had surrendered claim scope over two-signal products during prosecution. The amendment from "two or three signals" to "three signals" was not a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal, the court held, because Finesse had also overcome the prior art rejection on independent grounds relating to passive versus active IMPs.

But the court found that no reasonable jury could have found the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications. The Nokia document on which Dr. Wells relied listed only three distinct multiplications involving modulus and complex conjugate operations—not the seven straightforward signal multiplications recited in the claims. Dr. Wells offered no testimony mapping the three multiplications onto the seven claimed. Finesse attempted to supply a mapping on appeal, but that mapping was flawed because it required, for example, mapping S3 onto the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|—a sleight of hand that collapses the "three separately identifiable signals" construction into something far more elastic than the claims or the construction supports.

At oral argument, Finesse shifted again, arguing the Nokia document actually shows ten multiplications. But Finesse never identified which subset of those ten corresponds to the seven claimed, nor did it explain how the ten relate to the three that Dr. Wells identified at trial. The court treated this as yet another instance of Finesse attempting to construct an infringement theory on appeal that was never presented to the jury.

## The Proper Role of the Trial Court on JMOL

The doctrinal significance of this decision lies in its clarification of what a trial court may and may not do when evaluating JMOL in a patent case. Under Fifth Circuit law, which the Federal Circuit applied here, JMOL is appropriate when "the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion." This is a demanding standard, but it is not a rubber stamp.

The district court's error was not merely that it drew an inference from the evidence. Inferences are permissible, even necessary, in evaluating JMOL. The error was that the district court drew an inference that the expert himself never offered and then treated that judicially constructed inference as though it were part of the evidentiary record. Dr. Wells never said x1 and x2 map onto "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." The district court said it for him. That is a category error: the court did not evaluate the sufficiency of the evidence; it supplemented the evidence and then evaluated its own supplementation.

The Federal Circuit's opinion, though concise on this point, sends a clear signal. When a patentee's infringement case depends on expert testimony, the expert must articulate a coherent theory that maps the accused device onto the claimed limitations. The court cannot do that work for the expert by piecing together fragments from distant pages of the trial transcript. And when the expert's testimony is self-contradictory—particularly when the contradiction goes to a limitation that, if unmet, defeats the entire infringement theory—the jury's verdict cannot stand.

## Downstream Implications

The decision has several implications for patent litigation practice. First, it reinforces the *Datascope* principle that self-contradictory expert testimony is insufficient as a matter of law, not merely a credibility issue for the jury. Trial courts in patent cases should be more willing to grant JMOL when an expert's infringement theory collapses under cross-examination and the expert cannot articulate a coherent alternative.

Second, it limits the ability of patentees to construct infringement theories on appeal from testimony fragments that were never assembled into a coherent whole at trial. The Federal Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that it reviews the evidence presented to the jury, not theories advanced for the first time on appeal. This decision applies that principle with particular force where the appellate theory requires the court to make inferential leaps that the expert himself never made.

Third, it may have practical consequences in the Eastern District of Texas, where jury verdicts in patent cases have historically enjoyed significant deference on JMOL. The Federal Circuit's willingness to reverse under the Fifth Circuit's demanding "so strongly and overwhelmingly" standard suggests that the substantial evidence threshold, while high, is not toothless—and that trial courts in that venue should not assume that a jury verdict will survive appellate review simply because the evidence was contested.

Finally, for damages practice, the vacatur of the $166 million lump-sum award underscores the practical consequence of JMOL reversals in patent cases: when infringement falls, the damages award falls with it, regardless of whether the damages theory was independently sound. This is unsurprising as a doctrinal matter—without infringement, there is no liability—but it highlights the importance of getting the infringement theory right from the start.

## Conclusion

*Finesse Wireless* is a clean application of a straightforward principle: the patentee bears the burden of proving infringement, and that burden is not satisfied by an expert who contradicts himself and a trial court that fills the gap. The Federal Circuit's reversal is a reminder that JMOL is not merely a procedural hurdle but a substantive check on jury verdicts that rest on insufficient or incoherent evidence. For litigators, the lesson is to ensure that expert infringement opinions articulate a complete and consistent mapping from the accused device to the claimed limitations—or risk losing the verdict on appeal.
