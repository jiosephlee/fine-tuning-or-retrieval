Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# When Experts Contradict Themselves: The Insufficiency of Self-Contradictory Testimony Under *Johns Hopkins v. Datascope*

## Why This Case Matters

Patent litigation often turns on expert testimony, and courts routinely afford juries wide latitude to weigh conflicting evidence. But what happens when an expert's testimony is not merely conflicting with the opposing side's evidence, but internally contradictory—shifting mid-stream under the pressure of cross-examination? The Federal Circuit's recent decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* offers a sharp reminder that the substantial evidence standard has limits, and that when the party bearing the burden of proof builds its case on self-contradictory expert testimony, no reasonable jury can sustain a verdict on that foundation.

The case also demonstrates the practical stakes of the doctrine. Finesse walked away from a $166 million jury verdict—the Federal Circuit wiped it out entirely because the plaintiff's expert could not maintain a consistent mapping between the accused products and the asserted claims.

## The '134 Patent and Dr. Wells' Shifting Testimony

The '134 patent claims a method for mitigating intermodulation product interference in radios. A key limitation—1[a]—requires "over-sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals." In plain terms, the receiver must sample a passband that contains both the desired signals and the signals that generate the interfering intermodulation products.

Finesse's infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, relied on a Nokia technical document depicting the operation of the accused radios. On direct and early cross-examination, Dr. Wells was consistent and clear: he mapped "signals of interest" to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and "interference generating signals" to the "modeled PIM path." Both signals were depicted on a dashed red path in the Nokia diagram, and Dr. Wells confirmed this mapping repeatedly—on direct, on cross, and in his expert report.

The problem was that this mapping is technologically impossible. The modeled PIM path is generated *downstream* of the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC)—the very component Dr. Wells identified as the sampler. A receiver cannot sample a signal that has not yet been generated. The modeled PIM path, in other words, cannot be among the "received signals" that the RF ADC samples, because it does not exist at the time of sampling.

When confronted with this contradiction on cross-examination, Dr. Wells pivoted. He began testifying about "two signals on that red path" and referenced "x1 and x2" as "the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path." But critically, he never mapped x1 or x2 onto the claimed "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." He continued to identify the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path as the two signals on the red path, even after the impossibility of his original mapping had been pointed out. The testimony was, as the court put it, "confusing and unclear even after he was made aware of his alleged misimpression."

## The District Court's Salvage Attempt and the Federal Circuit's Rejection

The district court acknowledged that the modeled PIM path was "the only thing Dr. Wells expressly mapped to the 'interference generating signals'" and that the PIM model is generated in the GROOT FPGA—downstream of the RF ADC. If the modeled PIM path is the interference generating signal, limitation 1[a] cannot be met. But the district court allowed the verdict to stand, persuaded by Finesse's argument that the modeled PIM path was not actually the "interference generating signals" and that Dr. Wells was really referring to x1 and x2 all along. The district court found that "Dr. Wells is clearly referring to x1 and x2."

The Federal Circuit rejected this inference with notable directness. As the court observed, "there is nothing clear about Dr. Wells' testimony." Dr. Wells never testified that x1 and x2 correspond to the claimed "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." The inference that he meant x1 and x2 was constructed by Finesse only on appeal, by piecing together fragments of testimony ninety pages apart in the record—one reference to x1 and x2 as "inputs" and one reference to the red path including "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." Even if Dr. Wells had testified that x1 and x2 were the claimed signals, he repeatedly and simultaneously testified that the DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path were the claimed signals, offering no coherent explanation for the contradiction.

## The *Johns Hopkins v. Datascope* Doctrine

The court's doctrinal anchor was *Johns Hopkins University v. Datascope Corp.*, 543 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2008), which establishes that when a party bearing the burden of proof rests its case on an expert's self-contradictory testimony, the court may conclude the evidence is insufficient to satisfy that burden as a matter of law. In *Johns Hopkins*, the expert opined that the accused device contacted a vessel "in three dimensions" but also testified that the device only contacted the vessel "in two places." The Federal Circuit held that no reasonable jury could find literal infringement based on such contradictory testimony.

*Finesse* applies the doctrine in a particularly instructive way because the contradiction was not a simple slip of the tongue. Dr. Wells had a clear, consistent, and repeated mapping throughout his direct testimony and expert report. The contradiction only emerged when the impossibility of that mapping was exposed on cross-examination. His attempted pivot was not a clarification—it was a tacit admission that his original infringement theory was flawed, followed by a vague gesture toward alternative signals without the necessary claim mapping.

## The Tension with the Substantial Evidence Standard

The substantial evidence standard is deferential by design. Under Fifth Circuit law, which governs the JMOL standard here, the court reverses "only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion." That is a high bar, and the Federal Circuit has shown considerable reluctance to disturb jury verdicts in patent cases, particularly on infringement questions that turn on factual disputes about how accused products operate.

*Johns Hopkins* creates an important exception to this deference. The doctrine recognizes that the substantial evidence standard presupposes evidence that a reasonable jury *could* credit. When the only evidence supporting an element of infringement is an expert's testimony, and that testimony is internally contradictory on the critical point, the evidence does not merely conflict with the opposing side's case—it fails to provide the jury with a coherent theory to credit in the first place. The jury is not weighing two competing narratives; it is being asked to construct a theory that the expert himself never articulated clearly.

This is a subtle but consequential distinction. The Federal Circuit is not holding that juries lack authority to resolve ambiguities in expert testimony or that courts should second-guess credibility determinations. The court is holding that there is a baseline requirement of coherence: an expert must provide a discernible, non-contradictory opinion mapping the accused product to the claim limitations. When the expert's testimony oscillates between mutually exclusive mappings without explanation, it falls below that threshold.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The decision has several practical implications for patent litigants and their experts.

First, it underscores the importance of preparing experts not just to survive cross-examination but to maintain a consistent and coherent infringement theory under pressure. Dr. Wells' original mapping may have been an error, but the dispositive problem was his failure to clearly and consistently adopt an alternative. An expert who recognizes a flaw in their analysis on cross-examination must be able to articulate a corrected theory that maps cleanly onto the claim limitations—and must do so in terms the jury can follow. Vague gestures toward alternative signals without explicit claim mapping will not sustain a verdict.

Second, the case clarifies the limits of what district courts may do to rehabilitate contradictory expert testimony at the JMOL stage. The district court here attempted to construct a coherent infringement theory on Finesse's behalf, inferring that Dr. Wells "clearly" meant x1 and x2 when the record showed nothing of the kind. The Federal Circuit's message is that courts cannot fill gaps in an expert's testimony by cobbling together disparate record references to construct a mapping the expert never made. The burden of proof rests with the plaintiff, and the plaintiff's evidence must stand on its own.

Third, the case illustrates the all-or-nothing character of patent verdicts built on thin infringement evidence. The $166 million damages award was vacated not because of any flaw in the damages theory, but because the infringement predicate collapsed. Parties pursuing large damages awards should ensure their infringement proofs are robust enough to survive the *Johns Hopkins* doctrine, because a contradictory expert can take the entire verdict down with them.

Finally, the '775 patent analysis, while involving a different doctrinal problem—failure to prove the seven claimed multiplications—reinforces the same theme. Finesse attempted to supply mappings on appeal that Dr. Wells never presented to the jury. The Federal Circuit declined to consider evidence that was never before the factfinder, noting that "Finesse attempts to clarify the mapping on appeal, but that evidence was never presented to the jury." Together, both patent analyses emphasize a consistent principle: the jury's verdict must be supported by what the expert actually said at trial, not by what the party wishes the expert had said or what the party can construct from the record after the fact.

## Conclusion

*Finesse Wireless* is a useful corrective for patent plaintiffs tempted to rely on experts who can articulate an infringement theory on direct examination but cannot hold that theory together under scrutiny. The *Johns Hopkins* doctrine remains a meaningful constraint on the substantial evidence standard, and the Federal Circuit has shown it will enforce that constraint when an expert's testimony shifts in ways that leave the jury without a coherent theory to credit. For litigators, the lesson is straightforward: an infringement opinion must be internally consistent and clearly mapped to the claim limitations, or the verdict it supports is built on sand.



# Narrowing Claims Without Surrendering Scope: "Three Signals," Prosecution History Disclaimer, and the Clear and Unambiguous Standard

When a patentee narrows claims during prosecution to overcome prior art, how much claim scope is actually surrendered? The Federal Circuit's recent decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* offers a clean illustration of the doctrinal boundary between a narrowing amendment and a prosecution history disclaimer—and a reminder that the two are not always the same thing. The case also answers a practically important question: when a claim requires "three signals," does using two signals plus a copy of one of those signals suffice? The answer turns on the distinction between "separately identifiable" and "unique," a distinction the court treated as dispositive.

## The Amendment and the Argument

The '775 patent claims a method for mitigating passive intermodulation product (PIM) interference in co-located radio transceivers. The key claim limitation requires generating cancellation signals by "digitally multiplying and filtering" seven specific third-order combinations of "three signals S1, S2 and S3." During prosecution, Finesse's original claims recited "digitally multiplying two or three signals." The examiner rejected those claims as obvious over the Filipovic reference, which teaches generating cancellation signals using two inputs, combined with secondary references. Finesse overcame the rejection by amending the claims to recite "three signals S1, S2 and S3" in seven multiplications and by arguing that Filipovic was limited to mitigating active IMPs, not the passive IMPs that the claimed method addresses. The examiner then allowed the claims.

AT&T argued on appeal that Finesse had surrendered claim scope over products using only two unique input signals by amending "two or three signals" to "three signals." Because the accused Nokia radios use only two unique signals—x1 and x2—AT&T contended they fell outside the surrendered scope. This is a classic prosecution history disclaimer argument: a patentee who narrows claims to overcome prior art cannot later recapture the surrendered territory under the doctrine of equivalents or through broad claim construction.

## The Clear and Unambiguous Standard

The Federal Circuit rejected AT&T's disclaimer argument. The court's reasoning tracks the framework set out in *Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788 (Fed. Cir. 2019), which requires that a disavowal of claim scope be "clear and unambiguous" to bind the patentee. Under this standard, not every narrowing amendment constitutes a disclaimer; the prosecution history must show the patentee clearly and unambiguously disavowed the broader scope.

Two features of the prosecution history were critical here. First, the examiner's rejection was overcome at least in part on grounds independent of the number-of-signals amendment. Finesse argued, and the examiner apparently accepted, that Filipovic was distinguishable because it addressed active IMPs rather than passive IMPs. When a narrowing amendment is not the sole basis for overcoming a rejection, the court is less willing to infer a clear disavowal of the broader scope. The logic is straightforward: if the patentee could have maintained broader claims by prevailing on the independent distinction alone, it is difficult to say the narrowing amendment was a deliberate surrender of the contested territory.

Second, the amended claims require "three signals S1, S2 and S3," but the district court construed this to mean three "separately identifiable" signals, not three "unique" signals. The court credited inventor testimony that S1, S2, and S3 "represent signals generally and can be the same signal or different signals." Under this construction, using two unique signals plus a copy of one of those signals can yield three separately identifiable signals—say, S1 = x1, S2 = x2, and S3 = x2—and thus still fall within the claim scope. The amendment narrowed the claims from "two or three" to "three," but did not clearly and unambiguously require three *unique* signals. Without that clear requirement, the court found no disclaimer covering products with only two unique inputs.

## "Separately Identifiable" Versus "Unique": A Doctrinally Significant Distinction

The distinction between "separately identifiable" and "unique" signals does a lot of work in this opinion, and it is worth examining closely. The court's reasoning effectively separates two questions that are often conflated in prosecution history analysis: how many signals must exist in the physical system, and how many signals must be operated upon by the claimed method. A product with two unique input signals can produce three separately identifiable signals if one of those inputs is copied or referenced more than once in the multiplication chain. The claim requires seven specific multiplications of three signals; it does not require that all three signals originate from distinct physical sources.

This distinction has practical consequences for digital signal processing patents, where copies, complex conjugates, and modulus operations on the same underlying signal are routine. If "three signals" were construed to require three unique physical inputs, a wide range of cancellation algorithms that mathematically operate on three signal values—some derived from the same source—would be excluded. By allowing "separately identifiable" signals to include copies, the court preserves claim coverage for implementations that are mathematically equivalent but architecturally different.

## The Limits of the Analysis—and the Verdict

Notably, the court's refusal to find a prosecution history disclaimer did not save Finesse's infringement verdict. The court reversed the denial of JMOL on noninfringement for an entirely separate reason: Finesse's expert, Dr. Wells, failed to show that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications. The Nokia technical document he relied on listed only three distinct multiplications, and neither Dr. Wells nor Finesse ever clearly mapped those three—or the ten multiplications Finesse later pointed to at oral argument—onto the seven multiplications required by the claim. The court found Dr. Wells's testimony confusing and self-contradictory, and concluded that no reasonable jury could have found infringement on this record.

So the '775 patent lives to fight another day—its claims were not narrowed by prosecution history disclaimer—but Finesse lost because it could not prove the accused products perform every claimed limitation. The prosecution history disclaimer ruling is a win for the patentee's claim construction, but it is a Pyrrhic victory in this case.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The decision reinforces a relatively patentee-friendly approach to prosecution history disclaimer under *Continental Circuits*. The "clear and unambiguous" standard sets a high bar for finding disclaimer, and the court's willingness to credit independent grounds for overcoming a rejection makes it harder for accused infringers to argue that any narrowing amendment operates as a surrender. Practitioners prosecuting patents in the digital signal processing space—and in fields where mathematical operations on signal copies are common—should take note of the "separately identifiable" construction as a potentially useful framing for preserving claim coverage across implementations that use fewer unique physical inputs than the claim's signal count might suggest.

At the same time, the case is a cautionary tale for infringement litigation. The court's willingness to reverse a jury verdict based on expert testimony that was confusing, self-contradictory, and insufficiently mapped to the claim limitations is a reminder that substantial evidence is not a rubber stamp. When an expert's testimony oscillates between different mappings—and never clearly resolves which signals correspond to which claim elements—the Federal Circuit will not hesitate to find the evidence insufficient, even under the deferential Fifth Circuit JMOL standard. The $166 million damages award, vacated as a consequence, underscores the stakes.

The broader lesson is that claim construction and prosecution history analysis can open doors that expert testimony then fails to walk through. Finesse won the construction fight over "three signals" but lost the infringement battle because its expert could not clearly connect the accused products to the seven multiplications the claims require. For patentees, the case underscores the importance of maintaining a consistent, clear infringement theory from expert report through trial. For accused infringers, it shows that even when prosecution history disclaimer arguments fail, careful scrutiny of the infringement evidence can still yield a reversal.



## The Mapping Problem: When Expert Testimony Falls Short of Proving Every Claim Limitation

In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit overturned a $166 million jury verdict, providing a clarion lesson on the evidentiary standards required to sustain an infringement finding. The case underscores that a patentee’s expert must do more than point to an accused product and assert it meets the claim limitations; the expert must clearly and specifically map the accused product’s features to every element of the asserted claims, and must do so at trial. 

## The Doctrinal Setup

The dispute centered on U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775, which claims a method for mitigating intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radios by digitally multiplying three signals in seven specific multiplications to generate cancellation signals. The district court construed the claim limitation “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable” signals, but not necessarily “unique” signals. This meant that using two unique signals, plus a copy of one of those signals, could potentially satisfy the “three signals” limitation. However, the claims still explicitly required the performance of seven specific multiplications of those three signals.

## The Evidentiary Gap at Trial

To prove infringement of the ’775 patent, Finesse relied on its expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, who testified that Nokia’s accused radios performed the claimed multiplications based on a Nokia technical document. The problem, as the Federal Circuit identified, was a fatal evidentiary gap. The Nokia document only disclosed three distinct multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—not the seven required by the claims. More critically, Dr. Wells never testified as to how those three distinct multiplications mapped onto the seven claimed multiplications.

This failure proved fatal to the verdict. The Federal Circuit emphasized that under the substantial evidence standard, a reasonable jury must be able to find infringement based on the evidence presented at trial. When the party bearing the burden of proof rests its case on expert testimony that fails to clearly link the accused product to a specific claim limitation, the verdict cannot stand. The court noted that Dr. Wells did not explain the mapping, and the Nokia document itself involved modulus and complex conjugate operations, meaning it did not even show the seven multiplications being performed as claimed.

## The Appellate Pivot and the "Ten Multiplications" Theory

Perhaps the most instructive part of the opinion is the court’s rejection of Finesse’s attempts to cure this evidentiary deficiency on appeal. Recognizing the gap left by Dr. Wells, Finesse tried to supplement the record with new mapping arguments raised for the first time in its appellate brief and at oral argument. Finesse argued that the accused radios actually performed ten multiplications, not just three, and that a subset of those ten corresponded to the seven claimed multiplications.

The Federal Circuit was unmoved. The court firmly rejected this appellate pivot, noting that this mapping was never presented to the jury. Furthermore, Finesse and Dr. Wells never explained which specific subset of the ten multiplications listed in the Nokia document corresponded to the seven claimed multiplications. Nor did they provide a mapping from the ten multiplications to the three distinct multiplications that Finesse had previously relied on at trial. As the court made clear, arguments and evidence not presented to the jury cannot form the basis of substantial evidence. The party with the burden of proof must present its case, in full, at trial.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The *Finesse* decision reinforces the principle that the burden of proof rests squarely on the patentee at trial and that appellate courts will not rescue a party that failed to meet that burden. It underscores that expert testimony must be clear, consistent, and complete. Conclusory assertions or unexplained mappings are insufficient to support a jury verdict, especially in complex technological cases where mapping claim limitations to technical documents is intricate.

This case serves as a warning against litigation strategies that defer crucial evidentiary linkages to appellate briefing. Downstream, this may lead to more rigorous challenges to expert testimony under judgment as a matter of law (JMOL). Parties will likely be more aggressive in highlighting gaps in an expert’s mapping of claim limitations to the accused product. The Federal Circuit’s insistence on a complete evidentiary record at trial ensures that jury verdicts are grounded in the evidence actually presented, not on retrospective explanations crafted for appellate review.



# Judicial Inference vs. Expert Proof: The District Court's Error in Constructing an Infringement Theory from Fragmented Testimony

When a jury returns a nine-figure infringement verdict, the trial court's role on JMOL is not to rescue the verdict by assembling an infringement theory from scattered fragments of expert testimony that the expert himself never coherently articulated. That is the core lesson of the Federal Circuit's decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, a case that offers a crisp illustration of the boundary between permissible inference and impermissible construction—and a reminder that the substantial evidence standard has real teeth when a patentee's infringement case rests on self-contradictory expert opinion.

## The Stakes and the Setup

Finesse Wireless sued AT&T and Nokia, alleging that Nokia's radios—specifically their PIM cancellation (PIM-C) feature—infringed two patents related to mitigating intermodulation product interference. The jury found all asserted claims infringed and awarded over $166 million in lump-sum damages. The Eastern District of Texas denied the defendants' JMOL motions on noninfringement and damages. The Federal Circuit reversed in full, vacating both the infringement finding and the damages award.

The decision matters not only for the parties—it wipes out a substantial verdict—but also for what it says about the trial court's obligations when evaluating JMOL motions. The Federal Circuit identified a structural error in how the district court approached the sufficiency of the evidence: rather than asking whether Finesse's expert had actually articulated a coherent infringement theory, the district court stitched one together from testimony fragments and then deferred to a jury verdict built on that judicially constructed theory.

## The '134 Patent: An Expert Who Could Not Land on a Theory

The '134 patent claims a method for reducing intermodulation product interference by sampling a passband of received signals that includes both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals," then isolating and canceling the interference. The critical limitation—1[a]—requires the radio's receiver to sample a passband that includes both categories of signals.

Finesse's infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, relied on a Nokia technical document to map the accused radios' operation onto the claimed method. He consistently and repeatedly testified that the "signals of interest" corresponded to the downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference and that the "interference generating signals" corresponded to the modeled PIM path—both depicted along a dashed red path in the Nokia diagram. This mapping was set out in his expert report and repeated at trial across multiple exchanges.

The problem emerged on cross-examination. The modeled PIM path is generated downstream of the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC)—the very receiver that performs the sampling. A signal cannot be sampled before it is generated. If the modeled PIM path is the "interference generating signals," then limitation 1[a] cannot be met, because the accused radios' receiver does not sample a passband that includes interference generating signals.

Confronted with this contradiction, Dr. Wells pivoted. He began referencing "two signals on that red path" and mentioned x1 and x2—the two inputs to the RF ADC. But he never mapped x1 and x2 onto "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals" as the claims require. He continued to testify that the two signals were the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path. When asked whether x1 was the modeled PIM path, he offered only that "x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path." He never completed the analytical bridge from x1 and x2 to the claimed signal categories.

The district court nonetheless denied JMOL, finding that "Dr. Wells testified that there are two signals entering the RF ADC, and that those are signals of interest and the interference generating signals. Dr. Wells is clearly referring to x1 and x2." The Federal Circuit's response was direct: "There is nothing clear about Dr. Wells' testimony."

This is the analytical heart of the decision. The district court did not merely draw a reasonable inference from ambiguous evidence; it constructed the missing link in Finesse's infringement theory by judicial interpolation. Dr. Wells never testified that x1 and x2 correspond to "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." Finesse itself attempted to supply that mapping only on appeal, by piecing together testimony from pages ninety pages apart in the trial record. Even then, the mapping required a chain of inferences that the expert himself never offered.

The Federal Circuit grounded its analysis in *Johns Hopkins University v. Datascope Corp.*, 543 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2008), where the court held that self-contradictory expert testimony cannot satisfy the patentee's burden of proof. In *Datascope*, the expert opined that an accused device contacted a vessel in three dimensions but elsewhere testified that it contacted in only two places. Here, Dr. Wells repeatedly identified the modeled PIM path as the "interference generating signals" and then, when that mapping was shown to be impossible, gestured toward x1 and x2 without ever completing the analytical work. The court treated this as the same species of self-contradiction that defeated the infringement finding in *Datascope*.

## The '775 Patent: Seven Multiplications in Search of an Expert

The '775 patent presented a different but related problem. The claims require generating intermodulation product cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals—S1, S2, and S3—in seven specific combinations. The district court construed "three signals S1, S2 and S3" as "separately identifiable" but not "unique," meaning the same signal could serve as more than one of the three. The Federal Circuit affirmed that construction, rejecting AT&T's argument that Finesse had surrendered claim scope over two-signal products during prosecution. The amendment from "two or three signals" to "three signals" was not a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal, the court held, because Finesse had also overcome the prior art rejection on independent grounds relating to passive versus active IMPs.

But the court found that no reasonable jury could have found the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications. The Nokia document on which Dr. Wells relied listed only three distinct multiplications involving modulus and complex conjugate operations—not the seven straightforward signal multiplications recited in the claims. Dr. Wells offered no testimony mapping the three multiplications onto the seven claimed. Finesse attempted to supply a mapping on appeal, but that mapping was flawed because it required, for example, mapping S3 onto the signal x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|—a sleight of hand that collapses the "three separately identifiable signals" construction into something far more elastic than the claims or the construction supports.

At oral argument, Finesse shifted again, arguing the Nokia document actually shows ten multiplications. But Finesse never identified which subset of those ten corresponds to the seven claimed, nor did it explain how the ten relate to the three that Dr. Wells identified at trial. The court treated this as yet another instance of Finesse attempting to construct an infringement theory on appeal that was never presented to the jury.

## The Proper Role of the Trial Court on JMOL

The doctrinal significance of this decision lies in its clarification of what a trial court may and may not do when evaluating JMOL in a patent case. Under Fifth Circuit law, which the Federal Circuit applied here, JMOL is appropriate when "the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion." This is a demanding standard, but it is not a rubber stamp.

The district court's error was not merely that it drew an inference from the evidence. Inferences are permissible, even necessary, in evaluating JMOL. The error was that the district court drew an inference that the expert himself never offered and then treated that judicially constructed inference as though it were part of the evidentiary record. Dr. Wells never said x1 and x2 map onto "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." The district court said it for him. That is a category error: the court did not evaluate the sufficiency of the evidence; it supplemented the evidence and then evaluated its own supplementation.

The Federal Circuit's opinion, though concise on this point, sends a clear signal. When a patentee's infringement case depends on expert testimony, the expert must articulate a coherent theory that maps the accused device onto the claimed limitations. The court cannot do that work for the expert by piecing together fragments from distant pages of the trial transcript. And when the expert's testimony is self-contradictory—particularly when the contradiction goes to a limitation that, if unmet, defeats the entire infringement theory—the jury's verdict cannot stand.

## Downstream Implications

The decision has several implications for patent litigation practice. First, it reinforces the *Datascope* principle that self-contradictory expert testimony is insufficient as a matter of law, not merely a credibility issue for the jury. Trial courts in patent cases should be more willing to grant JMOL when an expert's infringement theory collapses under cross-examination and the expert cannot articulate a coherent alternative.

Second, it limits the ability of patentees to construct infringement theories on appeal from testimony fragments that were never assembled into a coherent whole at trial. The Federal Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that it reviews the evidence presented to the jury, not theories advanced for the first time on appeal. This decision applies that principle with particular force where the appellate theory requires the court to make inferential leaps that the expert himself never made.

Third, it may have practical consequences in the Eastern District of Texas, where jury verdicts in patent cases have historically enjoyed significant deference on JMOL. The Federal Circuit's willingness to reverse under the Fifth Circuit's demanding "so strongly and overwhelmingly" standard suggests that the substantial evidence threshold, while high, is not toothless—and that trial courts in that venue should not assume that a jury verdict will survive appellate review simply because the evidence was contested.

Finally, for damages practice, the vacatur of the $166 million lump-sum award underscores the practical consequence of JMOL reversals in patent cases: when infringement falls, the damages award falls with it, regardless of whether the damages theory was independently sound. This is unsurprising as a doctrinal matter—without infringement, there is no liability—but it highlights the importance of getting the infringement theory right from the start.

## Conclusion

*Finesse Wireless* is a clean application of a straightforward principle: the patentee bears the burden of proving infringement, and that burden is not satisfied by an expert who contradicts himself and a trial court that fills the gap. The Federal Circuit's reversal is a reminder that JMOL is not merely a procedural hurdle but a substantive check on jury verdicts that rest on insufficient or incoherent evidence. For litigators, the lesson is to ensure that expert infringement opinions articulate a complete and consistent mapping from the accused device to the claimed limitations—or risk losing the verdict on appeal.
