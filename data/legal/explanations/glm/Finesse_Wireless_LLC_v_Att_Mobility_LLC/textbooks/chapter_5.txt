Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 5: # Damages, Vacatur, and Final Disposition

## The Jury's Damages Award

After finding all asserted claims of the '134 and '775 patents valid and infringed, the jury awarded Finesse Wireless LLC $166,303,391 in lump-sum damages. This award covered the remaining life of both patents. A lump-sum damages award, as opposed to a running royalty, is a single payment that compensates the patentee for the accused infringer's use of the patented technology over a defined period. Here, the jury determined that AT&T's use of the PIM cancellation (PIM-C) feature in Nokia radios justified nearly $166.4 million in compensation for the entirety of the remaining patent terms.

The size and structure of this award became significant on appeal, but not for the reasons one might expect. The Federal Circuit did not assess whether the amount was reasonable or whether the lump-sum format was appropriate. Instead, the damages award's fate was tied entirely to the court's resolution of the infringement issue.

## Appellants' Post-Trial Motions on Damages

Following the verdict, Appellants moved for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on damages or, in the alternative, for a new trial on damages. These were distinct motions seeking different forms of relief. A JMOL motion challenges whether the evidence was legally sufficient to support the jury's verdict—here, whether the damages award was supported by adequate evidence. A motion for a new trial, by contrast, asks the court to retry the damages question, typically because the verdict was against the weight of the evidence, the damages were excessive, or some legal error tainted the proceedings. The alternative structure reflected Appellants' fallback strategy: if the court would not overturn the damages verdict entirely, it should at least order a fresh determination.

## The District Court's Denial of Both Damages Motions

The district court denied both the JMOL on damages and the motion for a new trial. With respect to JMOL, the court concluded that the evidence was sufficient to support the jury's damages award. With respect to the new trial motion, the court concluded that the verdict did not warrant disturbing under the applicable standard. These rulings left the $166 million lump-sum award intact and formed part of the basis for Appellants' appeal.

## The Federal Circuit's Standard of Review

The Federal Circuit applied regional circuit law—here, Fifth Circuit law—to its review of the denial of a new trial on damages. The Fifth Circuit reviews a denial of a new trial for abuse of discretion. This deferential standard means the appellate court will not overturn the district court's decision lightly; it must conclude that the district court exercised its discretion in a way that was arbitrary, unreasonable, or based on an error of law. The Federal Circuit noted this standard at the outset of its damages discussion, signaling that it would evaluate the district court's ruling under a framework that affords substantial respect to the trial court's judgment.

As it happens, however, the court did not need to apply this standard to resolve the damages question.

## Vacatur of the Damages Award

The Federal Circuit's resolution of the damages issue was straightforward but consequential. Because the court reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of both the '134 and '775 patents, there was no longer any infringement liability to support a damages award. Damages in patent cases are a remedy that flows from a finding of infringement; they are not an independent cause of action. When the underlying infringement verdict cannot stand, the damages award built upon it must also fall.

The court accordingly vacated the damages award. Vacatur is distinct from reversal. To reverse a judgment means to overturn it, typically substituting the appellate court's judgment for that of the lower court. To vacate means to set aside or nullify the judgment, leaving the matter as if the vacated determination had never been made. Here, the court vacated rather than reversed because the proper disposition was not to substitute a different damages figure but to eliminate the damages award altogether, given that no infringement had occurred.

This outcome illustrates an important structural principle in patent litigation: the damages phase depends on the liability phase. The Federal Circuit did not evaluate whether the jury's $166 million award was excessive, whether the lump-sum format was appropriate, or whether the district court erred in denying the new trial motion. None of those questions mattered once the court determined that no reasonable jury could have found infringement. The damages award was a derivative remedy, and when the infringement finding was withdrawn, the damages award lost its foundation.

## Final Disposition

The court's final disposition was concise: "For the foregoing reasons, we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacate the damages award." The court entered its judgment as "REVERSED AND VACATED" and awarded costs to Defendants-Appellants AT&T and Nokia.

The award of costs to the prevailing parties on appeal reflects the general principle that the successful appellant is entitled to recover the costs of the appeal from the losing party. This does not include attorney's fees, which are not at issue here, but covers items such as filing fees and other expenses associated with the appellate proceedings.

The disposition brought the case to a close at the appellate level, though the practical effect was to send a strong signal to the district court that the infringement claims could not proceed on the evidence presented. By reversing the denial of JMOL of noninfringement, the Federal Circuit effectively directed entry of judgment in favor of AT&T and Nokia on the infringement question, and by vacating the damages award, it eliminated the financial consequence of the jury's now-overturned verdict.
