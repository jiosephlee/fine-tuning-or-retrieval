Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Why does the Federal Circuit apply Fifth Circuit law for JMOL review but its own law for claim construction?
Question:
In this opinion, the court says it reviews the denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit (Fifth Circuit), but then says it reviews claim construction de novo and any underlying factual findings for clear error. I understand the Federal Circuit has nationwide jurisdiction over patent appeals, but I'm confused about how it decides which body of procedural law to apply. Why is JMOL review governed by regional circuit law while claim construction follows Federal Circuit precedent? Is there a principled basis for this split, or is it just historical accident?
Answer:
## The distinction turns on whether the issue is substantive patent law or procedural.

The Federal Circuit's split authority is not historical accident — it follows directly from Congress's decision to give the Federal Circuit exclusive jurisdiction over patent appeals for the purpose of achieving **national uniformity in patent law**, not to create a unified body of civil procedure.

### Substantive patent law → Federal Circuit law

Claim construction is a matter of substantive patent law. The Federal Circuit reviews claim construction de novo and any underlying factual findings for clear error under its own precedent. The opinion states this directly:

> "We review claim construction de novo and any underlying factual findings supporting the district court's construction for clear error." *Wi-LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc.*, 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016).

Because the meaning of patent claims is a question of patent law that affects patent rights nationwide, the Federal Circuit applies its own law to ensure that a patent claim means the same thing regardless of where the case is heard. This is the core reason the court exists.

### Procedural matters → Regional circuit law

JMOL standards, by contrast, are procedural. They govern how a district court evaluates the sufficiency of evidence at trial — a question of civil procedure, not patent law. The Federal Circuit applies the law of the regional circuit (here, the Fifth Circuit) because:

1. **No need for national uniformity.** JMOL standards are the same whether the case involves patents, contracts, or torts. There is no patent-specific reason to depart from regional circuit law.

2. **Respect for regional procedural expertise.** District courts operate within regional circuits and apply those circuits' procedural rules. The Federal Circuit borrows the regional circuit's standard to maintain consistency between how a district court applies JMOL and how that decision is reviewed on appeal.

The opinion applies this framework:

> "We review a district court's denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit, here the Fifth Circuit." *Id.* (citing Fifth Circuit law for the de novo, "strongly and overwhelmingly" standard).

Notably, the court applies the same approach to new trial motions — also procedural — by reviewing under Fifth Circuit law for abuse of discretion.

### The principled basis

The Federal Circuit's mandate is to unify patent law, not procedure. When an issue is one that Congress intended to be uniform nationwide (claim construction, infringement, validity, damages methodology), the Federal Circuit applies its own law. When an issue is procedural and has no patent-specific dimension (JMOL sufficiency of evidence, new trial motions), the Federal Circuit defers to the regional circuit, which has general supervisory authority over the district courts within its territory.

Both propositions are stated in the same cited case — *Wi-LAN* — which itself reflects the Federal Circuit's settled understanding that its jurisdiction over patent cases does not extend to creating a freestanding body of procedural law for matters within the regional circuits' traditional domain.

### When is expert testimony so self-contradictory that it cannot support a jury verdict?
Question:
The court reverses the JMOL denial on the '134 patent because Dr. Wells' testimony was "confusing and unclear" and he gave contradictory testimony about whether the "interference generating signals" mapped to the modeled PIM path or to x1 and x2. The court cites Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp. for the proposition that self-contradictory expert testimony cannot support a jury verdict. But I thought credibility determinations and weighing conflicting testimony were jury functions. At what point does contradictory expert testimony cross the line from a credibility issue for the jury to insufficient evidence as a matter of law? Is there a specific test, or is it just case-by-case judgment?
Answer:
## When Self-Contradictory Expert Testimony Fails as a Matter of Law

Your instinct is right that credibility determinations are generally for the jury, and courts are reluctant to override that role. But there is a meaningful boundary, and the Federal Circuit's opinion here illustrates where it falls.

### The Governing Standard

The key distinction is between **conflicting or impeached testimony**—which remains a jury question—and **self-contradictory testimony on a dispositive issue from the party bearing the burden of proof, where the expert never reconciles the contradiction and no other evidence fills the gap**. The latter is insufficient as a matter of law.

The court articulates this principle through *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, 543 F.3d 1342, 1349 (Fed. Cir. 2008): "When the party with the burden of proof . . . rests its case on an expert's self-contradictory testimony, we may conclude the evidence is insufficient to satisfy that standard." The court applies this under the substantial evidence framework—"if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial." The question becomes whether a reasonable jury could actually find infringement when the only evidence on a claim limitation is internally inconsistent and unresolved.

### Why Dr. Wells' Testimony Crossed the Line

The problem was not simply that Dr. Wells was confused or impeached on a peripheral point. The contradiction went to the heart of limitation 1[a] of the '134 patent—whether the accused radios sample a passband containing both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." This was the element Finesse had to prove, and Dr. Wells was their only source of evidence on it.

The contradiction had a specific structure:

1. **Direct testimony mapped "interference generating signals" to the modeled PIM path.** Dr. Wells said so repeatedly—in his expert report, on direct, and under cross-examination. The court notes this was "consistent and repeated."

2. **Physical reality made that mapping impossible.** The modeled PIM path is generated downstream of the RF ADC, after sampling occurs. As the court explains, "the modeled PIM path cannot be the claimed 'interference generating signals'" because "signals cannot be sampled before they are generated."

3. **When confronted, the expert pivoted but never resolved the contradiction.** Dr. Wells mentioned x1 and x2 as "the two inputs" on the red path, but "did not testify that x1 and x2 map onto the 'signals of interest' and 'interference generating signals' and instead continued to testify 'the – two signals are the downlink TX reference and the modeled PIM path.'" He "repeatedly testified the DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path correspond to the claimed signals, and offered no clear or detailed explanation for his contradictory testimony."

4. **Finesse's attempted reconstruction was the court's, not the expert's.** Finesse tried to stitch together testimony from pages ninety pages apart in the record to argue Dr. Wells really meant x1 and x2 all along. The court rejected this: "There is nothing clear about Dr. Wells' testimony. Dr. Wells never testified x1 and x2 correspond to the 'signals of interest' and 'interference generating signals.'"

This is what distinguishes the case from an ordinary credibility question. The jury was not choosing between two coherent accounts; it was asked to construct a theory of infringement that the expert himself never articulated.

### The Line, Stated as a Principle

Synthesizing from the opinion, the boundary seems to turn on several factors working together:

- **The contradiction concerns a claim limitation the burden-bearing party must prove**, not a collateral or cumulative issue.
- **The expert's testimony is the only evidence** supporting that limitation—there is no independent basis for the jury to find infringement.
- **The contradiction is fundamental**, not merely a matter of emphasis or degree—e.g., the expert's original mapping is physically impossible given the operation of the accused device.
- **The expert fails to reconcile or clarify** the contradiction when given the opportunity, leaving the jury with no coherent infringement theory to credit.
- **The party's attempt to salvage the testimony requires the factfinder to construct a theory the expert never actually articulated**, piecing together fragments of testimony.

When all of these are present, the court treats the testimony as failing the substantial evidence standard rather than presenting a credibility question. The jury cannot perform its factfinding function when the evidence it is asked to credit does not coherently state what the party must prove.

### What Would Have Kept This in the Jury's Hands

The opinion suggests, by contrast, that had Dr. Wells clearly testified x1 and x2 correspond to "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals," and offered an explanation for his earlier references to the modeled PIM path, the jury could have credited that testimony over the conflicting statements. The issue was not that he gave contradictory answers—it was that **he never gave the jury a coherent, reconciled account to credit in the first place**. The court's phrase captures it: "This sort of confusing change of course is not sufficient to support the jury verdict."

### Is There a Bright-Line Test?

There is no formal multi-factor test announced in this opinion. The court frames it as a case-specific application of the substantial evidence standard, guided by *Johns Hopkins*. But the pattern described above gives you a workable framework for identifying when contradictory expert testimony becomes legally insufficient rather than merely impeached.

### How does the "clear and unambiguous disavowal" standard apply when a patentee narrows claim language during prosecution?
Question:
On the '775 patent, the appellants argued Finesse surrendered claim scope over two-signal products when it amended the claims from "two or three signals" to "three signals" during prosecution to overcome the Filipovic reference. The court disagreed, saying the amendment doesn't rise to the level of "clear and unambiguous" disavowal under Continental Circuits v. Intel. But I'm confused about the boundary here. The patentee changed "two or three" to "three"—that seems like a deliberate narrowing. What distinguishes a routine claim amendment from a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal? Does the standard require the patentee to explicitly disavow scope, or can disavowal be inferred from the amendment itself combined with the prosecution context?
Answer:
## What Distinguishes a Routine Amendment from a "Clear and Unambiguous" Disavowal

The short answer is that **a narrowing amendment alone does not constitute a clear and unambiguous disavowal** unless the prosecution history, viewed as a whole, shows the patentee clearly surrendered the broader scope. The standard requires more than the fact of narrowing—it requires that the patentee's actions and statements unambiguously communicate surrender of the specific claim scope at issue.

### The Continental Circuits Standard

Under *Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019), prosecution history disclaimer requires a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal of claim scope. This is a demanding standard. The Federal Circuit has consistently held that ambiguous or attenuated arguments in the prosecution history are insufficient to narrow claim scope beyond what the claim language itself requires. The court will not infer a disclaimer from an amendment unless the prosecution record as a whole clearly demonstrates the patentee surrendered the relevant territory.

### Why the Amendment Here Did Not Constitute Disavowal

The appellants argued that by amending the claims from "two or three signals" to "three signals S1, S2 and S3" to overcome the Filipovic reference, Finesse surrendered claim scope over two-signal products. The court rejected this argument for two reasons grounded in the prosecution record:

**First, the amendment was not the sole basis for overcoming the rejection.** The examiner rejected the original "two or three signals" claims as obvious over Filipovic and secondary references. But Finesse overcame that rejection "in part by explaining 'Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs' and not passive IMPs that the claimed method also addresses." The court noted that Finesse distinguished the prior art on a substantive technical ground—active versus passive intermodulation products—in addition to amending the claims. This matters because when a patentee overcomes a rejection through argumentation that distinguishes the prior art on its own terms, the concurrent claim amendment does not necessarily carry the independent force of a disclaimer of the broader scope.

**Second, the amended language did not unambiguously require three *unique* signals.** The court concluded that the amendment to "three signals S1, S2 and S3" requires three "separately identifiable" signals but "does not require any specific number of unique input signals." In other words, two unique signals plus a copy of one of them could satisfy the "three signals" limitation. The claim language itself, as construed, did not clearly exclude two-signal products. Because the amended claims could still read on products using only two unique inputs, there was no clear and unambiguous surrender of that scope.

### The Boundary Between Routine Narrowing and Disavowal

The court's reasoning draws a clear line: **a routine narrowing amendment made to overcome prior art is not, by itself, a disavowal of all scope covered by the original, broader language.** The key distinction is whether the prosecution history, taken as a whole, shows the patentee clearly and unambiguously disavowed the specific scope now at issue.

Several factors relevant here pushed against finding disavowal:

- The patentee overcame the rejection in part on grounds independent of the amendment (active vs. passive IMPs).
- The amended claim language, as properly construed, did not unambiguously exclude the accused products (three "separately identifiable" signals can include copies).
- Nothing in the prosecution history showed Finesse explicitly arguing it was surrendering two-signal products.

By contrast, a disavowal would have been found if, for example, Finesse had expressly stated during prosecution that it was limiting its claims to three *unique* input signals to distinguish Filipovic, or if the amendment and accompanying arguments made clear that two-signal products were being excluded.

### Can Disavowal Be Inferred from the Amendment Plus Context?

Yes—the standard does not require an explicit statement of disavowal. Disavowal can be inferred from the combination of the amendment and the prosecution context. But the inference must be the only reasonable reading of the record. Here, the court found that the prosecution history was at most ambiguous on whether Finesse surrendered two-signal products: the amendment could be explained by the substantive distinction from Filipovic, and the claim language itself did not require three unique signals. Where the record admits more than one reasonable interpretation, the "clear and unambiguous" standard is not met.

### Takeaway

A claim amendment from broader to narrower language is necessary but not sufficient for prosecution history disclaimer. The question is whether the prosecution record, viewed holistically, shows the patentee clearly and unambiguously surrendered the specific scope the defendant seeks to exclude. Where the patentee overcame the rejection in part through substantive argumentation, and where the amended claim language as construed still reads on the accused products, the standard is not satisfied.

### What does it mean for signals to be "separately identifiable" but not "unique" in patent claim construction?
Question:
The district court construed "three signals S1, S2 and S3" in the '775 patent to be "separately identifiable" but not "unique." The Federal Circuit seemed to accept this construction. I'm struggling with what this actually means in practice. If S1, S2, and S3 can be the same signal (not unique), in what sense are they "separately identifiable"? Can you give an example of signals that are separately identifiable but not unique? Is this saying you can use two distinct signals and a copy of one of them, as the district court found? What is the practical significance of this distinction for infringement analysis?
Answer:
## What "Separately Identifiable" but Not "Unique" Means

The distinction is about whether the claim requires three *distinct* signals, or merely three signals that can be pointed to individually even if some are identical copies of each other.

**"Not unique"** means S1, S2, and S3 do not have to be three different signals. S1 could equal S2, or S2 could equal S3, etc. The claim does not require three distinct input signals.

**"Separately identifiable"** means you must still be able to identify three signals in the accused device that correspond to S1, S2, and S3 — even if one is a copy of another. The signals must be distinguishable as separate inputs to the multiplication step, but they need not carry different information.

## The Concrete Example from the Opinion

The district court gave exactly the example you asked about: using two distinct signals plus a copy of one of them satisfies the "three separately identifiable signals" construction. As the court found, "using two signals, plus a copy of one of those signals, was sufficient to meet its construction of three separately identifiable signals." J.A. 38. So if a device uses signals x1 and x2, and feeds a copy of x1 back in as a third input, you have three separately identifiable signals (x1, x2, copy of x1) even though only two are unique.

The Federal Circuit accepted this construction, holding that Finesse's amendment "requires three separately identifiable signals and does not require any specific number of unique input signals." (citing J.A. 968–69).

## The Practical Significance for Infringement

This construction matters because it determines whether a device using only two distinct input signals can infringe at all. If "three signals" meant three *unique* signals, AT&T could have argued non-infringement simply by showing the accused Nokia radios use only two inputs (x1 and x2). The "separately identifiable" construction closes off that argument — but it opened a different one.

The practical catch, and the reason the Federal Circuit ultimately reversed, is that even if you can meet the "three signals" limitation with two unique signals plus a copy, you still must perform all seven claimed multiplications. The claim requires seven specific third-order products: S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, and S2×S3×S3. When only two unique signals are used, some of these seven products collapse into duplicates of each other (e.g., if S1 = S3, then S1×S1×S3 = S1×S1×S1 and S1×S3×S3 = S1×S1×S1, and S2×S2×S3 = S2×S2×S1, which is the same as S1×S2×S2). The district court found that three distinct multiplications could each "correspond to two or three of the seven claimed multiplications," and that was sufficient. J.A. 38–39.

The Federal Circuit rejected the infringement verdict on a different ground: Finesse's expert never clearly mapped the accused device's three multiplications onto the seven claimed multiplications, and the Nokia document showed operations involving modulus and complex conjugate operations — not the straightforward multiplications the claim recites. The court held "no reasonable jury could have found that the accused radios perform the seven claimed multiplications."

So the distinction's practical significance is this: "separately identifiable but not unique" broadens the claim to cover devices with fewer than three distinct inputs, but it does not relieve the patentee of proving that all seven claimed multiplications are actually performed. The construction helps the patentee clear the threshold issue of signal count, but the patentee still bears the burden of showing each claimed multiplication occurs.

### Why was the argument that S1, S2, and S3 must be "unique" signals found waived, and what is the Rule 50(a) waiver requirement?
Question:
The district court rejected AT&T's argument that "S1, S2 and S3" must be unique signals as "waived" because they failed to raise it at the Rule 50(a) stage. I'm confused about the procedural mechanics here. What is the Rule 50(a) stage, and what exactly must a party do to preserve an issue for JMOL? Is there a difference between what must be argued in a Rule 50(a) motion before the case goes to the jury versus a Rule 50(b) motion after the verdict? If you don't raise an argument in 50(a), are you completely barred from raising it in 50(b) and on appeal, or are there exceptions?
Answer:
## Answer

The district court found AT&T's "unique signals" argument waived because AT&T did not raise that specific argument in its pre-verdict Rule 50(a) motion, and Rule 50(b) only permits renewal of arguments already preserved at the Rule 50(a) stage.

### What Rule 50(a) Is and Why It Matters

Rule 50(a) governs motions for judgment as a matter of law made **before the case is submitted to the jury**—typically at the close of the opposing party's evidence and again at the close of all evidence. The movant argues that, based on the evidence presented at trial, no reasonable jury could find for the non-moving party on a particular claim or issue.

Rule 50(b), by contrast, is a **renewed** JMOL motion filed **after the jury verdict**. Critically, Rule 50(b) is not a new motion—it is a renewal of the earlier Rule 50(a) motion. This means a party may only raise in a Rule 50(b) motion the **specific grounds** that were raised in its Rule 50(a) motion. Arguments not raised at the Rule 50(a) stage are waived and cannot be raised in a Rule 50(b) motion or on appeal.

The rationale is straightforward: the Rule 50(a) motion gives the district court the opportunity to resolve the issue before the jury deliberates (potentially avoiding an unnecessary verdict and retrial), and it alerts the opposing party to the argument so they have a chance to cure any evidentiary deficiency before the case goes to the jury.

### What Happened Here

At the Rule 50(b) stage (post-verdict JMOL), AT&T argued that "S1, S2 and S3" must be **unique** signals, meaning the accused radios—which use only two unique signals (x1 and x2) plus a copy of one—cannot infringe. The district court found this argument was **not raised** in AT&T's Rule 50(a) motion and therefore was waived. The court then applied its earlier summary judgment construction—that "S1, S2 and S3" need only be "separately identifiable," not unique—and held that two signals plus a copy satisfied that construction. 

Because the argument was waived, the district court did not consider the merits of whether "S1, S2 and S3" must be unique. The waiver was procedural: AT&T failed to raise the specific argument at the required stage.

### Is There a Difference Between 50(a) and 50(b) Arguments?

Yes, but only in timing and posture—not in substance. In a Rule 50(a) motion, you ask the court to take the issue from the jury before deliberations. In a Rule 50(b) motion, you ask the court to overturn the verdict. But for preservation purposes, the **substantive arguments must be the same**. You cannot add new grounds for JMOL after the verdict that you did not raise before it.

### Are There Exceptions?

The opinion does not discuss exceptions to the waiver rule, and the Federal Circuit (applying Fifth Circuit law, as noted at the outset of the JMOL discussion) did not address the issue in detail because it ultimately reversed on other grounds. The general rule in the Fifth Circuit and most circuits is strict: failure to raise an argument in a Rule 50(a) motion waives it for Rule 50(b) and appeal purposes. Some circuits have recognized narrow exceptions—for example, where an argument is implicit in a Rule 50(a) motion, or where the argument could not have been made before the verdict—but these are limited and were not invoked here.

Notably, the Federal Circuit addressed AT&T's **related** prosecution history estoppel argument on the merits (disagreeing with AT&T that Finesse "surrendered claim scope over two signals" by amending claims during prosecution), and it independently agreed with AT&T that no reasonable jury could find the seven claimed multiplications were performed. That latter ground was sufficient to reverse the JMOL denial, making the waiver of the "unique signals" argument ultimately immaterial to the outcome on appeal.

### Why is the damages award vacated rather than just remanded for recalculation when infringement is reversed?
Question:
At the end of the opinion, the court says it vacates the damages award because it reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims. I understand that if there's no infringement, there should be no damages, but I'm confused about the procedural mechanics. Why vacate rather than reverse? What's the difference between vacating a damages award and reversing it? And does this mean the entire damages issue is moot, or could there be some scenario where damages survive even partially if some claims or theories of infringement had been upheld?
Answer:
## What "Vacate" Means Here and Why the Court Used It

The short answer is that **"vacate" and "reverse" are distinct remedial tools, and the court used "vacate" because the damages award was a dependent consequence of the liability finding it was reversing—not an independently erroneous ruling the court needed to correct.**

### Vacate vs. Reverse

When an appellate court **reverses** a lower court's decision, it is saying that decision was wrong on the merits and substitutes the correct legal outcome. Here, the court reversed the *denial* of JMOL of noninfringement, which means it is directing entry of judgment of noninfringement in favor of AT&T and Nokia. The court determined no reasonable jury could have found infringement, so the correct outcome is the opposite of what the district court entered.

When an appellate court **vacates** a judgment or award, it nullifies it—wiping the slate clean—without necessarily ruling on the merits of that judgment itself. Vacatur is the appropriate remedy when a ruling cannot stand because the foundation it was built on has been removed, but the appellate court doesn't need to (or doesn't) reach the question of whether the ruling was independently correct.

### Why Vacate the Damages Award

The damages award of $166,303,391 was a lump-sum award premised entirely on the jury's finding that all asserted claims of both patents were infringed. The district court had denied both JMOL on damages and a new trial on damages, and those denials were also on appeal. But the Federal Circuit didn't reach the merits of either damages issue. As the court stated:

> "Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the '134 and '775 patents, we vacate the damages award."

The logical structure is: since there is no infringement, there can be no damages. The court didn't need to decide whether the damages methodology was flawed, whether the lump-sum calculation was supported by substantial evidence, or whether a new trial on damages was warranted. Those questions became irrelevant once the liability finding fell. Vacatur cleanly disposes of the damages award as a consequence of the reversal on liability, without the court opining on the correctness of the damages determination.

Had the court instead "reversed" the damages award, it would impliedly be ruling that the damages award itself was legally erroneous—addressing the merits of the JMOL on damages or the new trial motion. The court chose not to do that. The damages award wasn't reversed because it was wrong; it was vacated because the infringement finding it depended on no longer exists.

### Is the Entire Damages Issue Moot?

Practically, yes. The court reversed noninfringement for **all** asserted claims of **both** patents. There is no surviving liability to support any award. The opinion's language—"for all asserted claims"—is doing critical work here. Because no claim was upheld, there is no remaining basis for any damages calculation, partial or otherwise.

### The Hypothetical: If Some Claims Had Survived

Your instinct is correct that the outcome could differ in a partial reversal. Had the court affirmed infringement on some claims while reversing on others, the damages award might have needed partial recalibration rather than outright vacatur. In that scenario, the court might have:

- **Vacated and remanded** for recalculation of damages tied only to the surviving infringed claims, if the original award was predicated on a mix of claims and could be disaggregated.
- **Affirmed** the damages award if it could stand independently on the surviving claims and the damages methodology wasn't claim-specific.

But that is not this case. The court's reversal of noninfringement is total—it covers every asserted claim of both patents. With no surviving infringement, there is nothing to recalculate, and vacatur of the entire damages award is the clean and complete remedy.

### How does the substantial evidence standard interact with an expert's failure to map accused product features to specific claim limitations?
Question:
On the '775 patent, Dr. Wells identified three distinct multiplications in the Nokia document but never testified how those three map onto the seven claimed multiplications. Finesse tried to provide this mapping for the first time on appeal. The court held no reasonable jury could find infringement. I'm trying to understand the evidentiary standard here. If the expert testified the accused product infringes, isn't it the jury's role to decide whether that testimony is credible? When does an expert's failure to provide a specific element-by-element mapping become insufficient as a matter of law versus just a weak case for the jury to weigh? What if the jury could have inferred the mapping itself?
Answer:
**The short answer**

An expert's conclusory testimony that a product infringes is not, by itself, substantial evidence. The expert must provide the jury with a basis to find that each claim limitation is met. When the expert fails to map accused product features to specific claim limitations—especially where the claim requires a specific number of operations and the evidence shows a different number—the testimony is insufficient as a matter of law, and the jury cannot cure the gap by inferring a mapping that was never explained.

---

**The standard**

The Federal Circuit reviews infringement verdicts for substantial evidence, meaning "a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial." A JMOL is proper when "the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion."

The jury's role is to weigh credibility and draw reasonable inferences, but only from evidence that actually exists. The substantial evidence standard does not permit a jury to speculate about facts not in evidence to fill holes in an expert's opinion.

---

**When missing mapping is insufficient as a matter of law**

On the '775 patent, the claim required performing seven specific multiplications of three signals. Dr. Wells relied on a Nokia document that listed only three distinct multiplications. He never testified how those three mapped onto the seven claimed multiplications. The court held no reasonable jury could find infringement.

This was not merely a weak case for the jury to weigh. The court identified several problems:

1. **The underlying document did not show seven multiplications.** The Nokia document "only lists three distinct multiplications" and "there is no evidence any multiplications are repeated such that all seven claimed multiplications are performed, as required by limitation 1[d]."

2. **The expert never provided the mapping.** "Dr. Wells did not testify how those three distinct multiplications map onto the seven claimed multiplications."

3. **The attempted mapping on appeal was never presented to the jury.** Finesse tried to supply the mapping for the first time on appeal, but "that evidence was never presented to the jury." The court cannot consider evidence not before the jury, and the jury cannot infer a mapping that was never explained.

4. **The proffered mapping was flawed.** Even on appeal, Finesse's mapping required mapping a single claimed signal (S3) onto three different things (x2, its complex conjugate x2', and its modulus |x2|), which the court found did not work.

The court also rejected Finesse's oral argument that the document showed ten multiplications, because Finesse "nowhere explain[ed] which subset of the ten multiplications listed in the Nokia document corresponds to the seven claimed multiplications."

The takeaway: when a claim requires a specific set of operations and the expert identifies a different set without explaining how they correspond, the testimony does not provide a basis for a reasonable jury to find infringement. The gap is too large for the jury to fill by inference.

---

**The jury's role and credibility**

The jury does decide whether expert testimony is credible, but only when the testimony actually addresses the claim limitations. An expert's opinion that "the accused product infringes" is not a substitute for evidence that the product meets each limitation. If the expert never maps the accused product to a specific limitation, there is nothing for the jury to weigh on that limitation. The jury's credibility determination cannot create evidence that does not exist.

---

**Contrast: self-contradictory testimony**

The court's analysis of the '134 patent illustrates a related but distinct ground for insufficiency. There, Dr. Wells initially mapped "interference generating signals" to the modeled PIM path, but when confronted with the fact that the modeled PIM path is generated after sampling (and thus cannot be sampled), he pivoted to say he meant two signals (x1 and x2) on the red path, without ever mapping x1 and x2 to the claimed signals. The court found this "confusing change of course" was insufficient under *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, which holds that when "the party with the burden of proof rests its case on an expert's self-contradictory testimony," the evidence may be insufficient as a matter of law.

---

**Summary**

- **A weak case for the jury:** the expert provides a mapping, but the jury may doubt its accuracy or the expert's credibility.
- **Insufficient as a matter of law:** the expert does not provide a mapping at all, identifies fewer operations than the claim requires without explanation, or offers contradictory testimony without resolving the contradiction. The jury cannot infer the missing mapping from silence or speculate about which subset of operations the claim covers.
