Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### How does the "clear and unambiguous disavowal" standard apply when a patentee narrows claim language during prosecution?
Question:
On the '775 patent, the appellants argued Finesse surrendered claim scope over two-signal products when it amended the claims from "two or three signals" to "three signals" during prosecution to overcome the Filipovic reference. The court disagreed, saying the amendment doesn't rise to the level of "clear and unambiguous" disavowal under Continental Circuits v. Intel. But I'm confused about the boundary here. The patentee changed "two or three" to "three"—that seems like a deliberate narrowing. What distinguishes a routine claim amendment from a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal? Does the standard require the patentee to explicitly disavow scope, or can disavowal be inferred from the amendment itself combined with the prosecution context?
Answer:
## What Distinguishes a Routine Amendment from a "Clear and Unambiguous" Disavowal

The short answer is that **a narrowing amendment alone does not constitute a clear and unambiguous disavowal** unless the prosecution history, viewed as a whole, shows the patentee clearly surrendered the broader scope. The standard requires more than the fact of narrowing—it requires that the patentee's actions and statements unambiguously communicate surrender of the specific claim scope at issue.

### The Continental Circuits Standard

Under *Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp.*, 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019), prosecution history disclaimer requires a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal of claim scope. This is a demanding standard. The Federal Circuit has consistently held that ambiguous or attenuated arguments in the prosecution history are insufficient to narrow claim scope beyond what the claim language itself requires. The court will not infer a disclaimer from an amendment unless the prosecution record as a whole clearly demonstrates the patentee surrendered the relevant territory.

### Why the Amendment Here Did Not Constitute Disavowal

The appellants argued that by amending the claims from "two or three signals" to "three signals S1, S2 and S3" to overcome the Filipovic reference, Finesse surrendered claim scope over two-signal products. The court rejected this argument for two reasons grounded in the prosecution record:

**First, the amendment was not the sole basis for overcoming the rejection.** The examiner rejected the original "two or three signals" claims as obvious over Filipovic and secondary references. But Finesse overcame that rejection "in part by explaining 'Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs' and not passive IMPs that the claimed method also addresses." The court noted that Finesse distinguished the prior art on a substantive technical ground—active versus passive intermodulation products—in addition to amending the claims. This matters because when a patentee overcomes a rejection through argumentation that distinguishes the prior art on its own terms, the concurrent claim amendment does not necessarily carry the independent force of a disclaimer of the broader scope.

**Second, the amended language did not unambiguously require three *unique* signals.** The court concluded that the amendment to "three signals S1, S2 and S3" requires three "separately identifiable" signals but "does not require any specific number of unique input signals." In other words, two unique signals plus a copy of one of them could satisfy the "three signals" limitation. The claim language itself, as construed, did not clearly exclude two-signal products. Because the amended claims could still read on products using only two unique inputs, there was no clear and unambiguous surrender of that scope.

### The Boundary Between Routine Narrowing and Disavowal

The court's reasoning draws a clear line: **a routine narrowing amendment made to overcome prior art is not, by itself, a disavowal of all scope covered by the original, broader language.** The key distinction is whether the prosecution history, taken as a whole, shows the patentee clearly and unambiguously disavowed the specific scope now at issue.

Several factors relevant here pushed against finding disavowal:

- The patentee overcame the rejection in part on grounds independent of the amendment (active vs. passive IMPs).
- The amended claim language, as properly construed, did not unambiguously exclude the accused products (three "separately identifiable" signals can include copies).
- Nothing in the prosecution history showed Finesse explicitly arguing it was surrendering two-signal products.

By contrast, a disavowal would have been found if, for example, Finesse had expressly stated during prosecution that it was limiting its claims to three *unique* input signals to distinguish Filipovic, or if the amendment and accompanying arguments made clear that two-signal products were being excluded.

### Can Disavowal Be Inferred from the Amendment Plus Context?

Yes—the standard does not require an explicit statement of disavowal. Disavowal can be inferred from the combination of the amendment and the prosecution context. But the inference must be the only reasonable reading of the record. Here, the court found that the prosecution history was at most ambiguous on whether Finesse surrendered two-signal products: the amendment could be explained by the substantive distinction from Filipovic, and the claim language itself did not require three unique signals. Where the record admits more than one reasonable interpretation, the "clear and unambiguous" standard is not met.

### Takeaway

A claim amendment from broader to narrower language is necessary but not sufficient for prosecution history disclaimer. The question is whether the prosecution record, viewed holistically, shows the patentee clearly and unambiguously surrendered the specific scope the defendant seeks to exclude. Where the patentee overcame the rejection in part through substantive argumentation, and where the amended claim language as construed still reads on the accused products, the standard is not satisfied.
