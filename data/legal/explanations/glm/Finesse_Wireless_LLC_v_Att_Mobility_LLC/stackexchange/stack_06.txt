Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### Why is the damages award vacated rather than just remanded for recalculation when infringement is reversed?
Question:
At the end of the opinion, the court says it vacates the damages award because it reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims. I understand that if there's no infringement, there should be no damages, but I'm confused about the procedural mechanics. Why vacate rather than reverse? What's the difference between vacating a damages award and reversing it? And does this mean the entire damages issue is moot, or could there be some scenario where damages survive even partially if some claims or theories of infringement had been upheld?
Answer:
## What "Vacate" Means Here and Why the Court Used It

The short answer is that **"vacate" and "reverse" are distinct remedial tools, and the court used "vacate" because the damages award was a dependent consequence of the liability finding it was reversing—not an independently erroneous ruling the court needed to correct.**

### Vacate vs. Reverse

When an appellate court **reverses** a lower court's decision, it is saying that decision was wrong on the merits and substitutes the correct legal outcome. Here, the court reversed the *denial* of JMOL of noninfringement, which means it is directing entry of judgment of noninfringement in favor of AT&T and Nokia. The court determined no reasonable jury could have found infringement, so the correct outcome is the opposite of what the district court entered.

When an appellate court **vacates** a judgment or award, it nullifies it—wiping the slate clean—without necessarily ruling on the merits of that judgment itself. Vacatur is the appropriate remedy when a ruling cannot stand because the foundation it was built on has been removed, but the appellate court doesn't need to (or doesn't) reach the question of whether the ruling was independently correct.

### Why Vacate the Damages Award

The damages award of $166,303,391 was a lump-sum award premised entirely on the jury's finding that all asserted claims of both patents were infringed. The district court had denied both JMOL on damages and a new trial on damages, and those denials were also on appeal. But the Federal Circuit didn't reach the merits of either damages issue. As the court stated:

> "Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the '134 and '775 patents, we vacate the damages award."

The logical structure is: since there is no infringement, there can be no damages. The court didn't need to decide whether the damages methodology was flawed, whether the lump-sum calculation was supported by substantial evidence, or whether a new trial on damages was warranted. Those questions became irrelevant once the liability finding fell. Vacatur cleanly disposes of the damages award as a consequence of the reversal on liability, without the court opining on the correctness of the damages determination.

Had the court instead "reversed" the damages award, it would impliedly be ruling that the damages award itself was legally erroneous—addressing the merits of the JMOL on damages or the new trial motion. The court chose not to do that. The damages award wasn't reversed because it was wrong; it was vacated because the infringement finding it depended on no longer exists.

### Is the Entire Damages Issue Moot?

Practically, yes. The court reversed noninfringement for **all** asserted claims of **both** patents. There is no surviving liability to support any award. The opinion's language—"for all asserted claims"—is doing critical work here. Because no claim was upheld, there is no remaining basis for any damages calculation, partial or otherwise.

### The Hypothetical: If Some Claims Had Survived

Your instinct is correct that the outcome could differ in a partial reversal. Had the court affirmed infringement on some claims while reversing on others, the damages award might have needed partial recalibration rather than outright vacatur. In that scenario, the court might have:

- **Vacated and remanded** for recalculation of damages tied only to the surviving infringed claims, if the original award was predicated on a mix of claims and could be disaggregated.
- **Affirmed** the damages award if it could stand independently on the surviving claims and the damages methodology wasn't claim-specific.

But that is not this case. The court's reversal of noninfringement is total—it covers every asserted claim of both patents. With no surviving infringement, there is nothing to recalculate, and vacatur of the entire damages award is the clean and complete remedy.
