Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Statutory Borrowing as Constitutional Adjudication: Section 1348's Reliance on Mail and Wire Fraud Precedent

When Congress enacted Section 1348 as part of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act in 2002, it created a criminal securities fraud statute that borrowed heavily—some might say reflexively—from the familiar architecture of the mail and wire fraud statutes. The Fifth Circuit's decision last week in *United States v. Constantinescu* illustrates how deeply this cross-statutory borrowing has become embedded in the jurisprudence, and why it deserves more critical attention than it has received. The case is a straightforward pump-and-dump prosecution on its facts, but the court's analytical method raises a prior question: what does it mean for the legitimate scope of a criminal statute when its content is supplied, in significant part, by doctrines developed under different statutory schemes?

## The Case and the Borrowing Move

The defendants in *Constantinescu* were social-media influencers who allegedly profited $114 million by promoting securities they secretly intended to sell once their followers drove up the price. The district court dismissed the indictment, reasoning that it alleged only a deprivation of "potentially valuable economic information"—the theory the Supreme Court rejected in *Ciminelli v. United States* (2023)—rather than a deprivation of a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit reversed, holding that the indictment alleged a straightforward fraudulent-inducement scheme: defendants misrepresented their positions to trick followers into parting with money to buy securities.

What is notable about the Fifth Circuit's reasoning is not the result but the method. The court observed that "[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit" and that Section 1348 "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes," so "courts have given the terms similar treatment," routinely "relying on mail and wire fraud cases in analyzing securities fraud charges." Having established this borrowing framework, the court then applied two recent Supreme Court wire fraud decisions—*Ciminelli* and *Kousisis v. United States* (2025)—to resolve the securities fraud claims before it. The *Kousisis* holding that fraud does not require the victim to suffer net economic loss was transposed directly into the Section 1348 context to reject the defendants' argument that they intended only to profit, not to harm.

The court's approach is entirely consistent with how most circuits have treated Section 1348. But the ease of the transplantation should give us pause.

## The Textual Problem

Section 1348 criminalizes any "scheme to defraud any person in connection with" a security and any scheme "to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of" a security. The language parallels Sections 1341 and 1343, which prohibit schemes "to defraud" and schemes "to obtain money or property" by false pretenses. The textual parallelism is real, and it provides a plausible basis for looking to mail and wire fraud precedent for guidance on shared terms like "scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud."

But textual parallelism is not textual identity. Section 1348 contains its own qualifying phrases—"in connection with any security" and "in connection with the purchase or sale of any security"—that have no analogues in the mail and wire fraud statutes. These phrases presumably narrow the statute's reach in ways that the general fraud statutes do not, and they may also inform the meaning of the shared terms in context. A "scheme to defraud" in connection with a security may not mean precisely the same thing as a "scheme to defraud" carried out through the mails, even if the words are identical.

The borrowing approach tends to flatten these contextual differences. When the Fifth Circuit says that courts "give the terms similar treatment," it effectively treats the shared language as having a single, transstatutory meaning—one fixed by the accumulated body of mail and wire fraud jurisprudence. This is a significant interpretive choice, and it is one that most courts have made without sustained justification.

## The Circularity Concern

The borrowing approach also creates a potential circularity problem, one that *Constantinescu* itself illustrates. The Supreme Court in *Ciminelli* constrained the scope of the federal fraud statutes by holding that the "right-to-control" theory—which treats a victim's right to make informed economic decisions as a protected property interest—cannot serve as the basis for a fraud conviction because the right to valuable economic information is not a "traditional property interest." That holding was rooted in the Court's understanding of the property requirement in the mail and wire fraud statutes, informed by common-law property concepts and historical usage.

When the Fifth Circuit borrows *Ciminelli* to construe Section 1348, it imports not just a doctrinal rule but a constitutional-adjacent limitation on criminal liability—one developed under a different statute with a different legislative history and a different regulatory context. The result is that Section 1348's scope is effectively determined by the boundaries the Supreme Court has drawn around mail fraud, even though Congress enacted Section 1348 years after the mail fraud statutes and in response to a very different problem—corporate and securities fraud in the post-Enron era.

This is not necessarily wrong. The statutes do share key terms, and the Supreme Court's fraud jurisprudence has long treated the various fraud provisions as a coherent family. But the circularity becomes apparent when one considers that the Court's recent fraud decisions—*Ciminelli*, *Kousisis*, and before them *Kelly v. United States* (the Bridgegate case)—have been preoccupied with limiting the apparently boundless reach of the general fraud statutes. Those limiting principles were developed with the mail and wire fraud statutes' potentially sweeping scope in mind. Section 1348, by contrast, is already cabined by its securities-specific nexus requirement. Importing limitations designed to rein in a broader statute into a statute that is already narrower may be doubly constraining in ways Congress did not contemplate—and may not have wanted.

## Fair Notice and Due Process

Perhaps the most serious concern is fair notice. The Due Process Clause requires that criminal statutes provide adequate warning of what conduct is prohibited. A defendant charged under Section 1348 who consults the statutory text will find language about schemes to defraud "in connection with" securities. He may not anticipate that his conduct will be evaluated under a body of doctrine developed primarily to address mail fraud schemes involving government contracts, construction bids, or healthcare billing—and that the line between lawful and unlawful conduct will be drawn by reference to Supreme Court decisions that never mention securities.

The defendants in *Constantinescu* did not seriously press this argument, and the Fifth Circuit did not address it. The court's approach—treating the fraud statutes as a unified interpretive field—has the virtue of doctrinal efficiency, and it produces a result that seems intuitively correct: a pump-and-dump scheme is fraud by any reasonable definition. But efficiency and intuition are not the same as textual fidelity, and the fair-notice question becomes more acute in closer cases where the conduct at issue looks less like classic fraud and more like aggressive marketing or puffery that happens to involve securities.

The concern is not hypothetical. Section 1348 has been used to prosecute insider trading, market manipulation, and various forms of misrepresentation by market participants. The scope of each application depends significantly on how "scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud" are construed—and those constructions are increasingly supplied by the Supreme Court's general fraud jurisprudence rather than by securities law's own developed understandings of these concepts.

## Toward a More Principled Borrowing

None of this is to say that cross-statutory interpretation is inherently problematic. Courts routinely look to analogous statutes for interpretive guidance, and the textual overlap between Section 1348 and the general fraud statutes provides a reasonable basis for doing so. But the practice would benefit from greater analytical discipline. Courts borrowing from the mail and wire fraud tradition to construe Section 1348 should explain why the borrowed doctrine fits the statutory context—not simply assert that the statutes share key terms and therefore receive "similar treatment." They should consider whether Section 1348's securities-specific language narrows or alters the borrowed principles. And they should be candid about the fair-notice implications of transposing limitations—and expansions—developed under one criminal statute onto another.

The Fifth Circuit's opinion in *Constantinescu* reaches a sensible outcome through an analytical move that has become almost invisible in fraud jurisprudence. That invisibility is itself the problem. When the content of a criminal statute is determined largely by doctrines developed under other statutes, we should at least be able to see the seams. In *Constantinescu*, the seams are nowhere in sight.
