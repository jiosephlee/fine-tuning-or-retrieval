Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 2: # Standard of Review and the Legal Framework of Securities Fraud Under Section 1348

## De Novo Review of Indictment Sufficiency

Before reaching the merits of the securities fraud charges, the Fifth Circuit first addressed the standard governing its review. When a defendant moves to dismiss an indictment for failure to state an offense, the appellate court reviews the sufficiency of that indictment de novo. This means the court of appeals examines the indictment anew, giving no deference to the district court's legal conclusions. The court cited United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023), for this proposition.

The de novo standard is accompanied by an important analytical posture: the court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true. This is a critical principle for law students to grasp. When assessing whether an indictment states an offense, the court does not assess whether the government can ultimately prove its case at trial, nor does it weigh the credibility of the allegations. Instead, the court assumes the facts as alleged are accurate and asks only whether those facts, if proven, would constitute the charged offense. The court drew this principle from United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011), which in turn quoted United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999). This posture means that a motion to dismiss an indictment is a test of the indictment's legal sufficiency, not a test of the government's evidence.

## The Three Requirements for a Sufficient Indictment

The Fifth Circuit then articulated the specific requirements that an indictment must meet to be legally sufficient. Drawing from United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996), the court identified three functions an indictment must serve. First, the indictment must enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense. This means the indictment must contain factual allegations that, if taken as true, establish every element the government would need to prove at trial. Second, the indictment must fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him, ensuring the defendant understands what conduct is being prosecuted and can prepare a defense. Third, the indictment must provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions, meaning it must be specific enough that a future prosecution for the same conduct could be identified and barred.

The court distilled these requirements into a single formulation, quoting United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000): "to be sufficient, an indictment must allege each material element of the offense." This summary encapsulates the core inquiry on a motion to dismiss. The question is whether the indictment's factual allegations, accepted as true, cover every material element of the charged crime. If any element is missing, the indictment is fatally defective. In this case, the district court had concluded that the indictment failed this test because it did not allege that defendants schemed to deprive victims of any traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit's de novo review of that conclusion forms the heart of the opinion.

## Section 1348's Prohibition on Securities Fraud

The court then turned to the substantive statute at issue: 18 U.S.C. § 1348. This statute prohibits two categories of misconduct. First, it criminalizes schemes to "defraud any person in connection with . . . any security." Second, it prohibits schemes "to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security." The first prohibition targets schemes to defraud generally, while the second targets schemes specifically aimed at obtaining money or property through false statements, in both cases tied to securities transactions.

The court noted that "[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit." This observation is significant because it signals that the Fifth Circuit had limited directly on-point precedent to guide its analysis of § 1348's elements. The court therefore looked elsewhere for interpretive guidance, which it found in the closely related mail and wire fraud statutes.

## Borrowing Key Concepts from Mail and Wire Fraud Statutes

To fill the interpretive gap, the court relied on United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023), which established that § 1348 "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes." Because of this textual and conceptual overlap, courts have given the terms in § 1348 "similar treatment" to their counterparts in the mail and wire fraud statutes. This means that when construing the elements of securities fraud under § 1348, the Fifth Circuit looks to the body of law developed under the mail and wire fraud statutes for guidance, and "often rel[ies] on mail and wire fraud cases in analyzing securities fraud charges."

This borrowing principle is more than a convenient cross-reference. It reflects a deeper structural relationship among the federal fraud statutes. Congress enacted § 1348 as part of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act in 2002, and it uses language closely paralleling the mail fraud statute (18 U.S.C. § 1341) and the wire fraud statute (18 U.S.C. § 1343). Because these statutes share the core concept of a "scheme to defraud," courts construing § 1348 have found it appropriate to draw on the well-developed body of mail and wire fraud precedent. For the student, the practical takeaway is that cases decided under the mail and wire fraud statutes are relevant authority when analyzing securities fraud under § 1348.

## The "Scheme to Defraud" Element

Drawing on Greenlaw, the court identified the first key element of securities fraud: a "scheme to defraud." This element requires a material misrepresentation "intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived." The court quoted this formulation from United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018), a mail and wire fraud case applied here through the borrowing principle. Several components of this definition deserve attention.

First, the misrepresentation must be material. A material misstatement is one that would naturally tend to influence the decision of the person to whom it is addressed. Trivial or immaterial falsehoods do not satisfy this element. Second, the misrepresentation must be intended to deceive. This means the defendant must have made the statement with awareness of its falsity and a purpose of misleading the audience. Third, the deception must be aimed at obtaining something of value, such as money. This requirement connects the scheme to a concrete object of the fraud—the defendant must be seeking to gain something of value through the deception. The court noted that the Supreme Court has articulated this same concept in broader terms: a defendant commits securities fraud when he "engaged in deception" and "[o]btaining the victim's money or property" was "an object of his fraud," citing Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)).

## The "Intent to Defraud" Element

The second key element the court identified is "intent to defraud." Under Greenlaw and Evans, this element requires "an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit." This is a two-part mental state requirement. The defendant must intend to deceive the victim, and must also intend that some harm flow from that deception. Importantly, as the opinion later clarifies, the "harm" component does not require the defendant to intend that the victim suffer a net economic loss. The defendant's intent to obtain money or property from the victim through deception is sufficient to satisfy the harm prong, even if the defendant does not seek to leave the victim worse off overall. The court drew this principle from Kousisis, which held that a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he "schem[es] to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off."

The combined effect of these two elements is that a defendant commits securities fraud under § 1348 when he engages in deception aimed at obtaining the victim's money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of a security. The scheme to defraud and the intent to defraud are distinct but related requirements: the scheme element focuses on the nature and object of the fraudulent conduct, while the intent element focuses on the defendant's state of mind regarding both the deception and its consequences.

## Defendants' Concession and Challenge

With this legal framework established, the court turned to the defendants' specific contentions. Notably, the defendants conceded that the indictment adequately alleges deception. This concession was significant because it removed from dispute the question of whether the indictment alleged false or misleading statements. The defendants did not argue that their social media posts were accurately described or that the indictment failed to allege misrepresentations. Instead, they challenged the indictment on two other grounds.

First, the defendants argued that the indictment did not allege a "scheme to defraud" because it merely alleged that they intended to deprive their followers of potentially valuable economic information rather than a traditional property interest. The district court had agreed with this reasoning, concluding that the indictment failed to state an offense. Second, the defendants argued that the indictment did not allege an "intent to defraud" because it alleged that they intended to profit from their scheme, not to harm any victim. In their view, seeking to enrich themselves was distinct from seeking to injure their followers.

These two arguments framed the central legal question on appeal: whether the indictment, accepted as true, alleged both a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud within the meaning of § 1348. The court answered this question by applying the recently decided Supreme Court cases of Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306 (2023), and Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), to the specific allegations of the indictment. The court's analysis of these arguments forms the subject of the next chapter.
