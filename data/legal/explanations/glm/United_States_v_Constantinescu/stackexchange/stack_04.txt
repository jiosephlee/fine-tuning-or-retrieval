Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What is the "mirror image" requirement in fraud cases, and why doesn't it apply here?
Question:
The court in Constantinescu cites United States v. Baker for the proposition that fraud statutes do not impose a "mirror image" requirement where the victim's loss of money or property must equal the defendant's gain. I'm not sure I understand what this "mirror image" requirement is. Is the idea that in some fraud cases, the victim's loss and the defendant's gain are identical (like stealing $100 from someone's wallet), but in other cases they might not match? How does this relate to the Kousisis principle that a defendant can be liable even when providing something of equal value in return? And why is this concept relevant to the pump-and-dump scheme in Constantinescu where the defendants profited $114 million?
Answer:
## The "Mirror Image" Requirement and Why It Doesn't Apply

The "mirror image" requirement, as discussed in *Baker*, would demand that the victim's loss of money or property correspond exactly to the defendant's gain—like your $100 wallet example, where the thief's gain and the victim's loss are identical. Under this requirement, the government would need to show that the defendant's profit came directly from the victim's loss and that the two amounts match.

*Baker* rejected this as a requirement under the fraud statutes. The Fifth Circuit held that the fraud statutes do not demand that "the victim's loss of money or property supplied the defendant's gain." In other words, the government need not prove that what the defendant gained came from what the victim lost, or that the amounts are equal.

### Connection to *Kousisis*

This principle is closely related to *Kousisis*. The Supreme Court there held that a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he "schem[es] to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." A defendant may be liable even when he "provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return." Both *Baker* and *Kousisis* stand for the same core proposition: the fraud statutes focus on whether the defendant used deception to obtain the victim's money or property—not on whether the victim's net loss mirrors the defendant's gain.

### Relevance to the Pump-and-Dump Scheme

In *Constantinescu*, the defendants induced their social media followers to purchase securities through false and misleading posts. The followers parted with their money and received securities in return. Under a mirror image requirement, one might argue that the fraud is incomplete because the followers received something of value for their money, so their loss doesn't match the defendants' $114 million gain. But *Baker* and *Kousisis* foreclose this argument.

The court held that "defendants' followers' receipt of market-value securities does not obviate the fraud charges" because the fraud statutes "do not require actual economic harm." The fraud was complete when the defendants induced the followers to part with their money under false pretenses—a "fraudulent-inducement theory" under which a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property."

The $114 million profit matters because it demonstrates the defendants' **object**: obtaining money. Under *Kousisis*, the question is whether obtaining the victim's money or property was "an object" of the fraud. The defendants' $114 million profit shows that money was indeed the object of their scheme—they "had money in mind." The mirror image concept is relevant because even though the defendants gave followers securities in exchange for their money—meaning the followers' losses may not equal $114 million—the fraud statutes don't require that matching. The defendants' gain and the victims' loss need not be mirror images.
