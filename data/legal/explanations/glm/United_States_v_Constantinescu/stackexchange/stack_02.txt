Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### Does securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 require the victim to suffer a net economic loss?
Question:
In Constantinescu, the defendants argue that the indictment fails to allege intent to defraud because they intended to profit, not to harm their followers. The court rejects this argument, citing Kousisis for the proposition that fraud statutes don't require the victim to suffer net pecuniary loss. The court says the followers' receipt of "market-value securities" doesn't obviate the fraud charges. I'm confused about this concept. If the followers bought securities at an artificially inflated price due to the defendants' misrepresentations, didn't they suffer an economic loss? Or is the court saying that even if the securities were worth exactly what the followers paid, the fraud would still be complete? How does the "no net economic loss required" principle from Kousisis apply in this pump-and-dump context where the victims did seem to lose money?
Answer:
## Answer

The short answer is no. Securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 does not require the victim to suffer a net economic loss. The fraud is complete when the defendant, through material misrepresentations, induces the victim to part with money or property—regardless of whether the victim receives something of equal value in return.

Your confusion stems from conflating two separate questions: (1) whether the victims in this particular pump-and-dump scheme actually lost money, and (2) whether the law requires net economic loss as an element of the offense. The court's discussion of *Kousisis* addresses the second question, which is the one that matters at the indictment stage.

### The "no net loss" principle from *Kousisis*

The Supreme Court in *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), addressed the fraudulent-inducement theory of fraud, under which "a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss." The defendant in *Kousisis* argued that the fraud statutes required him to have sought to "hurt the victim's bottom line." The Court rejected that argument, holding that a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he "schem[es] to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off."

The *Constantinescu* court applies this principle directly. The defendants argued that the indictment failed to allege intent to defraud because they "intended to profit—not harm any victim." The court responded that this "is a distinction without a difference" because the fraud statutes do not require the defendant to intend net economic harm—they require only that the defendant intended to deceive and to obtain the victim's money through that deception. As the court put it, quoting *Kousisis*: "a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses—regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss."

### Why the court raises the "market-value securities" point

You ask whether the court is saying the fraud would be complete even if the securities were worth exactly what the followers paid. Yes—that is precisely the court's point. The court states: "Because the fraud statutes do not require actual economic harm, defendants' followers' receipt of 'market-value securities' does not obviate the fraud charges." It then cites *Kousisis* for the proposition that "a defendant may be liable in fraud even when he 'provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return.'"

The fraud is complete at the moment of the fraudulent inducement—the moment the victims parted with their money based on material misrepresentations. What the victims received in return, and whether it was worth what they paid, does not bear on whether the elements of the offense are satisfied.

### Why this matters in the pump-and-dump context

You are right that in a typical pump-and-dump, victims often suffer actual losses because they buy at artificially inflated prices and the price collapses when the defendants sell. But the court does not need to reach that factual question at the indictment stage, where it must "take the allegations of the indictment as true and determine whether an offense has been stated." The defendants raised a legal argument: they claimed the indictment failed to allege intent to defraud because they only intended to profit, not to cause harm. The court's response is that under *Kousisis*, the intent-to-defraud element does not require intent to cause net economic loss. The indictment's allegation that defendants "pumped and dumped to gain a profit" shows they "had money in mind," which is sufficient. The followers' receipt of securities—whether or not those securities were worth the purchase price—does not negate the fraud.

### Summary

The "no net economic loss" principle is not about whether these particular victims lost money. It is about the legal standard: the fraud statutes punish the fraudulent *obtaining* of money or property, not the fraudulent infliction of net economic harm. The court invokes *Kousisis* to establish that the defendants' intent to profit (rather than to harm) does not negate the intent-to-defraud element, because the law does not require proof that the defendant intended to leave the victim economically worse off.
