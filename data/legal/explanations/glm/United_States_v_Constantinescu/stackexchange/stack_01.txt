Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What is the difference between the "right-to-control theory" rejected in Ciminelli and the "fraudulent-inducement theory" upheld in Kousisis?
Question:
I'm reading United States v. Constantinescu and the court spends a lot of time distinguishing between the "right-to-control theory" from Ciminelli v. United States and the "fraudulent-inducement theory" from Kousisis v. United States. The court says Ciminelli rejected the right-to-control theory because depriving someone of "valuable economic information" isn't a traditional property interest, but Kousisis upheld the fraudulent-inducement theory because it involves tricking victims into handing over money or property. I'm struggling to understand the practical difference between these two theories. In both cases, the defendant is lying to the victim to get something of value, right? Why does it matter whether we characterize the victim's loss as "valuable economic information" versus "money or property"? Can someone explain the doctrinal distinction and why the Supreme Court treated them differently?
Answer:
## The distinction turns on what the victim is deprived of

The practical difference is straightforward once you focus on what the defendant's scheme targets:

- **Right-to-control theory (Ciminelli):** The victim's "property" is characterized as the right to make informed economic decisions — i.e., "valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions." The victim parts with no money or property; they are merely deprived of information they *would have used* to decide whether to part with money or property. The Supreme Court held this is not a "traditional property interest" and therefore cannot support a fraud conviction.

- **Fraudulent-inducement theory (Kousisis):** The victim's property is actual "money or property" that the defendant tricks them into handing over. The defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." The fraud is complete the moment the victim is induced to part with money or property under materially false pretenses — even if the defendant gives something of equal value in return.

## Why the characterization matters

The federal fraud statutes protect **traditional property interests** — money and property — not intangible interests like the right to receive accurate information before making an economic decision. As the Fifth Circuit explains, Ciminelli rejected the right-to-control theory precisely because "the right to valuable economic information... is not a traditional property interest." The government there was trying to prosecute a fraud where the victim lost nothing more than the opportunity to make a fully informed choice.

Kousisis, by contrast, involved a victim who was induced to actually hand over money or property. Kousisis lied to obtain government contracts, and the government paid him under those contracts. The Supreme Court confirmed this theory "protects money and property" and "is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory." The key point: the defendant's deception caused the victim to part with something recognized as property under the statute.

## Applied to Constantinescu

The Fifth Circuit distinguished Ciminelli because the indictment here does not rely on followers losing "valuable economic information." It alleges that defendants "induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities — that is, to part with their money or property." That is classic fraudulent inducement: the followers were tricked into handing over money to buy securities. The fact that followers received securities of equivalent market value in return does not defeat the fraud, because under Kousisis, the fraud statutes do not require the defendant to intend net economic harm — only that "obtaining the victim's money or property" was "an object of his fraud."

## In short

The line is between **depriving someone of information** (not a protected property interest) and **tricking someone into transferring money or property** (a protected interest). If the prosecution's theory is that the victim lost only the ability to make an informed economic decision, Ciminelli bars the conviction. If the theory is that the victim was induced to actually hand over money or property, Kousisis permits it — regardless of whether the victim received equivalent value in return.
