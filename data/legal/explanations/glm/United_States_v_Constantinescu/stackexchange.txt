Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What is the difference between the "right-to-control theory" rejected in Ciminelli and the "fraudulent-inducement theory" upheld in Kousisis?
Question:
I'm reading United States v. Constantinescu and the court spends a lot of time distinguishing between the "right-to-control theory" from Ciminelli v. United States and the "fraudulent-inducement theory" from Kousisis v. United States. The court says Ciminelli rejected the right-to-control theory because depriving someone of "valuable economic information" isn't a traditional property interest, but Kousisis upheld the fraudulent-inducement theory because it involves tricking victims into handing over money or property. I'm struggling to understand the practical difference between these two theories. In both cases, the defendant is lying to the victim to get something of value, right? Why does it matter whether we characterize the victim's loss as "valuable economic information" versus "money or property"? Can someone explain the doctrinal distinction and why the Supreme Court treated them differently?
Answer:
## The distinction turns on what the victim is deprived of

The practical difference is straightforward once you focus on what the defendant's scheme targets:

- **Right-to-control theory (Ciminelli):** The victim's "property" is characterized as the right to make informed economic decisions — i.e., "valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions." The victim parts with no money or property; they are merely deprived of information they *would have used* to decide whether to part with money or property. The Supreme Court held this is not a "traditional property interest" and therefore cannot support a fraud conviction.

- **Fraudulent-inducement theory (Kousisis):** The victim's property is actual "money or property" that the defendant tricks them into handing over. The defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." The fraud is complete the moment the victim is induced to part with money or property under materially false pretenses — even if the defendant gives something of equal value in return.

## Why the characterization matters

The federal fraud statutes protect **traditional property interests** — money and property — not intangible interests like the right to receive accurate information before making an economic decision. As the Fifth Circuit explains, Ciminelli rejected the right-to-control theory precisely because "the right to valuable economic information... is not a traditional property interest." The government there was trying to prosecute a fraud where the victim lost nothing more than the opportunity to make a fully informed choice.

Kousisis, by contrast, involved a victim who was induced to actually hand over money or property. Kousisis lied to obtain government contracts, and the government paid him under those contracts. The Supreme Court confirmed this theory "protects money and property" and "is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory." The key point: the defendant's deception caused the victim to part with something recognized as property under the statute.

## Applied to Constantinescu

The Fifth Circuit distinguished Ciminelli because the indictment here does not rely on followers losing "valuable economic information." It alleges that defendants "induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities — that is, to part with their money or property." That is classic fraudulent inducement: the followers were tricked into handing over money to buy securities. The fact that followers received securities of equivalent market value in return does not defeat the fraud, because under Kousisis, the fraud statutes do not require the defendant to intend net economic harm — only that "obtaining the victim's money or property" was "an object of his fraud."

## In short

The line is between **depriving someone of information** (not a protected property interest) and **tricking someone into transferring money or property** (a protected interest). If the prosecution's theory is that the victim lost only the ability to make an informed economic decision, Ciminelli bars the conviction. If the theory is that the victim was induced to actually hand over money or property, Kousisis permits it — regardless of whether the victim received equivalent value in return.

### Does securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 require the victim to suffer a net economic loss?
Question:
In Constantinescu, the defendants argue that the indictment fails to allege intent to defraud because they intended to profit, not to harm their followers. The court rejects this argument, citing Kousisis for the proposition that fraud statutes don't require the victim to suffer net pecuniary loss. The court says the followers' receipt of "market-value securities" doesn't obviate the fraud charges. I'm confused about this concept. If the followers bought securities at an artificially inflated price due to the defendants' misrepresentations, didn't they suffer an economic loss? Or is the court saying that even if the securities were worth exactly what the followers paid, the fraud would still be complete? How does the "no net economic loss required" principle from Kousisis apply in this pump-and-dump context where the victims did seem to lose money?
Answer:
## Answer

The short answer is no. Securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 does not require the victim to suffer a net economic loss. The fraud is complete when the defendant, through material misrepresentations, induces the victim to part with money or property—regardless of whether the victim receives something of equal value in return.

Your confusion stems from conflating two separate questions: (1) whether the victims in this particular pump-and-dump scheme actually lost money, and (2) whether the law requires net economic loss as an element of the offense. The court's discussion of *Kousisis* addresses the second question, which is the one that matters at the indictment stage.

### The "no net loss" principle from *Kousisis*

The Supreme Court in *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), addressed the fraudulent-inducement theory of fraud, under which "a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss." The defendant in *Kousisis* argued that the fraud statutes required him to have sought to "hurt the victim's bottom line." The Court rejected that argument, holding that a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he "schem[es] to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off."

The *Constantinescu* court applies this principle directly. The defendants argued that the indictment failed to allege intent to defraud because they "intended to profit—not harm any victim." The court responded that this "is a distinction without a difference" because the fraud statutes do not require the defendant to intend net economic harm—they require only that the defendant intended to deceive and to obtain the victim's money through that deception. As the court put it, quoting *Kousisis*: "a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses—regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss."

### Why the court raises the "market-value securities" point

You ask whether the court is saying the fraud would be complete even if the securities were worth exactly what the followers paid. Yes—that is precisely the court's point. The court states: "Because the fraud statutes do not require actual economic harm, defendants' followers' receipt of 'market-value securities' does not obviate the fraud charges." It then cites *Kousisis* for the proposition that "a defendant may be liable in fraud even when he 'provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return.'"

The fraud is complete at the moment of the fraudulent inducement—the moment the victims parted with their money based on material misrepresentations. What the victims received in return, and whether it was worth what they paid, does not bear on whether the elements of the offense are satisfied.

### Why this matters in the pump-and-dump context

You are right that in a typical pump-and-dump, victims often suffer actual losses because they buy at artificially inflated prices and the price collapses when the defendants sell. But the court does not need to reach that factual question at the indictment stage, where it must "take the allegations of the indictment as true and determine whether an offense has been stated." The defendants raised a legal argument: they claimed the indictment failed to allege intent to defraud because they only intended to profit, not to cause harm. The court's response is that under *Kousisis*, the intent-to-defraud element does not require intent to cause net economic loss. The indictment's allegation that defendants "pumped and dumped to gain a profit" shows they "had money in mind," which is sufficient. The followers' receipt of securities—whether or not those securities were worth the purchase price—does not negate the fraud.

### Summary

The "no net economic loss" principle is not about whether these particular victims lost money. It is about the legal standard: the fraud statutes punish the fraudulent *obtaining* of money or property, not the fraudulent infliction of net economic harm. The court invokes *Kousisis* to establish that the defendants' intent to profit (rather than to harm) does not negate the intent-to-defraud element, because the law does not require proof that the defendant intended to leave the victim economically worse off.

### Why does the securities fraud statute (§ 1348) borrow concepts from mail and wire fraud statutes, and what does this mean for interpreting its elements?
Question:
In Constantinescu, the Fifth Circuit notes that 18 U.S.C. § 1348 "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes" and that courts often rely on mail and wire fraud cases when analyzing securities fraud charges. The court then cites United States v. Greenlaw for this proposition. I don't understand why this borrowing occurs. Is it because the statutes have similar text? Are there legislative history reasons? And practically, does this mean that any development in mail/wire fraud jurisprudence automatically applies to securities fraud under § 1348? Are there any limits to this borrowing principle, or could a future Supreme Court decision about mail fraud completely change how § 1348 is interpreted?
Answer:
## Why § 1348 Borrows from Mail and Wire Fraud

The Fifth Circuit's statement that § 1348 "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes" rests primarily on **textual parallelism**. All three statutes share the core phrase "scheme to defraud," and § 1348's two prongs closely track the structure of §§ 1341 and 1343: one prong prohibits schemes "to defraud," and the other prohibits schemes "to obtain... by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises... any money or property." Because the operative language is shared, courts treat the terms as having the same meaning unless there is reason to do otherwise.

The opinion doesn't discuss legislative history, so I won't speculate on that. The practical motivation the opinion does surface is **gap-filling**: the court notes there is "scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit." When direct precedent on § 1348 is sparse, the well-developed body of mail and wire fraud law provides a ready interpretive resource for the shared terms—"scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud."

## What This Means in Practice

The opinion demonstrates the borrowing in action. The court derives the elements of § 1348 from *Greenlaw*, which itself drew on *Evans*—a mail/wire fraud case—for the definitions of "scheme to defraud" (a material misrepresentation "intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value") and "intent to defraud" (intent to deceive and to cause harm from the deceit). The court then applies *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*, both of which were mail/wire fraud cases, directly to the § 1348 context. So yes—developments in fraud jurisprudence at the Supreme Court level do shape how § 1348 is interpreted, at least in the Fifth Circuit.

## Limits on the Borrowing

The opinion indicates at least two limits:

1. **"Key concepts," not wholesale importation.** The court says § 1348 borrows "key concepts"—specifically "scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud"—not every doctrinal development from mail/wire fraud. Elements unique to mail fraud (use of the mails) or wire fraud (use of interstate wires) have no analogue in § 1348, which instead requires a connection to securities.

2. **The property requirement constrains both statutes equally.** *Ciminelli* held that the fraud statutes protect only "traditional property interests," not the right to valuable economic information. The court applies that limit to § 1348, distinguishing the indictment (which alleged a fraudulent-inducement scheme to deprive victims of *money*) from the rejected right-to-control theory. Similarly, *Kousisis* confirmed that the fraud statutes require that obtaining the victim's money or property be an "object" of the scheme, even if the victim suffers no net loss. These limits apply to § 1348 not because they are borrowed, but because the shared text carries the same substantive boundary.

So a future Supreme Court decision narrowing or expanding the "scheme to defraud" concept in the mail/wire fraud context would likely affect § 1348 in the Fifth Circuit—but only to the extent the decision interprets language that the two statutes actually share.

### What is the "mirror image" requirement in fraud cases, and why doesn't it apply here?
Question:
The court in Constantinescu cites United States v. Baker for the proposition that fraud statutes do not impose a "mirror image" requirement where the victim's loss of money or property must equal the defendant's gain. I'm not sure I understand what this "mirror image" requirement is. Is the idea that in some fraud cases, the victim's loss and the defendant's gain are identical (like stealing $100 from someone's wallet), but in other cases they might not match? How does this relate to the Kousisis principle that a defendant can be liable even when providing something of equal value in return? And why is this concept relevant to the pump-and-dump scheme in Constantinescu where the defendants profited $114 million?
Answer:
## The "Mirror Image" Requirement and Why It Doesn't Apply

The "mirror image" requirement, as discussed in *Baker*, would demand that the victim's loss of money or property correspond exactly to the defendant's gain—like your $100 wallet example, where the thief's gain and the victim's loss are identical. Under this requirement, the government would need to show that the defendant's profit came directly from the victim's loss and that the two amounts match.

*Baker* rejected this as a requirement under the fraud statutes. The Fifth Circuit held that the fraud statutes do not demand that "the victim's loss of money or property supplied the defendant's gain." In other words, the government need not prove that what the defendant gained came from what the victim lost, or that the amounts are equal.

### Connection to *Kousisis*

This principle is closely related to *Kousisis*. The Supreme Court there held that a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he "schem[es] to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." A defendant may be liable even when he "provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return." Both *Baker* and *Kousisis* stand for the same core proposition: the fraud statutes focus on whether the defendant used deception to obtain the victim's money or property—not on whether the victim's net loss mirrors the defendant's gain.

### Relevance to the Pump-and-Dump Scheme

In *Constantinescu*, the defendants induced their social media followers to purchase securities through false and misleading posts. The followers parted with their money and received securities in return. Under a mirror image requirement, one might argue that the fraud is incomplete because the followers received something of value for their money, so their loss doesn't match the defendants' $114 million gain. But *Baker* and *Kousisis* foreclose this argument.

The court held that "defendants' followers' receipt of market-value securities does not obviate the fraud charges" because the fraud statutes "do not require actual economic harm." The fraud was complete when the defendants induced the followers to part with their money under false pretenses—a "fraudulent-inducement theory" under which a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property."

The $114 million profit matters because it demonstrates the defendants' **object**: obtaining money. Under *Kousisis*, the question is whether obtaining the victim's money or property was "an object" of the fraud. The defendants' $114 million profit shows that money was indeed the object of their scheme—they "had money in mind." The mirror image concept is relevant because even though the defendants gave followers securities in exchange for their money—meaning the followers' losses may not equal $114 million—the fraud statutes don't require that matching. The defendants' gain and the victims' loss need not be mirror images.

### What standard applies when reviewing the dismissal of an indictment, and why does the court take the indictment's allegations as true?
Question:
In Constantinescu, the Fifth Circuit says it reviews the sufficiency of an indictment de novo and is "required to take the allegations of the indictment as true." The court then determines whether an offense has been stated. I'm confused about this procedural posture. If the district court dismissed the indictment, why does the appellate court take the allegations as true rather than evaluating whether the government actually has evidence to support them? Is this different from a motion for judgment of acquittal where the court would evaluate the evidence? Also, does this mean the defendants can still challenge the evidence at trial, or has the court essentially precluded them from arguing they didn't commit the alleged acts?
Answer:
The distinction you're sensing is real and important: reviewing the **sufficiency of an indictment** is a legal question about whether the charging document alleges a crime, while a motion for judgment of acquittal evaluates whether the government's **evidence** is sufficient to convict. These operate at different stages and ask different questions.

### Why allegations are taken as true

When a defendant moves to dismiss an indictment for failure to state an offense, the question is purely legal: assuming everything the indictment says is true, does it charge a violation of the criminal statute? The court is not assessing whether the government can *prove* those facts—it is asking whether the *alleged* facts, if true, constitute the offense charged.

This is why the Fifth Circuit, citing *United States v. Fontenot*, says it is "required to take the allegations of the indictment as true and to determine whether an offense has been stated." The opinion applies this standard directly: it assumes the defendants "post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security" and asks whether those alleged facts, under the securities fraud statute and the Supreme Court's decisions in *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*, constitute a "scheme to defraud" with the requisite "intent to defraud."

This is analogous to a Rule 12(b)(6) motion in civil practice: you test the legal sufficiency of the pleading, not the strength of the proof.

### How this differs from a motion for judgment of acquittal

A motion for judgment of acquittal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 29 comes *after* the government has presented its evidence at trial. At that stage, the court evaluates whether, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, a rational jury could find the defendant guilty beyond a reasonable doubt. The question shifts from "do the allegations state an offense?" to "did the government prove the offense?"

In *Constantinescu*, the district court dismissed the indictment *before trial*, concluding as a matter of law that the indictment's allegations—even if true—did not state an offense because they rested on deprivation of economic information rather than a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit reviewed that legal conclusion de novo, and in doing so it had no occasion to evaluate evidence because no evidence had been presented.

### Can the defendants still challenge the evidence at trial?

Yes. Taking the indictment's allegations as true for purposes of sufficiency review does not establish those facts for trial. The government still bears the full burden of proving every element beyond a reasonable doubt. The defendants retain every trial right: they may challenge the admissibility of evidence, cross-examine witnesses, present their own evidence, move for judgment of acquittal under Rule 29, and argue to the jury that the government has failed to meet its burden.

What the appellate court's decision resolves is narrow: the indictment is legally sufficient to proceed. The defendants won below on the argument that no crime was charged at all, and the Fifth Circuit reversed that ruling. Whether the government can actually prove a "pump and dump" scheme—and whether the defendants can rebut that showing—is a question for trial.

### What must an indictment allege to sufficiently charge securities fraud, and how does this case compare to cases where indictments were found deficient?
Question:
The court in Constantinescu lists the requirements for a sufficient indictment: (1) enumerate each prima facie element, (2) fairly inform the defendant of the charges, and (3) provide a double jeopardy defense. The court then concludes the indictment sufficiently alleges a "scheme to defraud" and "intent to defraud." But I'm struggling to understand what makes this indictment sufficient when the district court found it deficient. The district court apparently concluded the indictment only alleged deprivation of "potentially valuable economic information" rather than a traditional property interest. What specific language in the indictment did the Fifth Circuit find sufficient to allege a scheme to deprive followers of money? Are there examples of indictments that failed to meet this standard, and what was missing from them?
Answer:
## What Made the Indictment Sufficient

The Fifth Circuit found the indictment sufficient because it alleged a **fraudulent-inducement scheme**—not a right-to-control theory. The critical language is in paragraphs 13–14 of the indictment, which describe the pump-and-dump mechanism: defendants would purchase a security, then "pump" the price by "posting false and misleading information about the security on [social media] so that other investors were induced to purchase the security," and then "dump" by secretly selling for a profit. The court emphasized that this alleges defendants induced followers "to purchase securities—that is, **to part with their money or property**." That is the key distinction: the indictment alleges a scheme to obtain the followers' money, not merely to deprive them of information.

## Why Ciminelli Was Deficient

The deficient case the court draws from is **Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306 (2023)**. There, the government prosecuted Ciminelli for wire fraud using what the Supreme Court called the "right-to-control theory." Under that theory, the government argued the victim was defrauded of "potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions." The Court held that the federal fraud statutes protect only **traditional property interests**, and because "the right to valuable economic information… is not a traditional property interest," the right-to-control theory "cannot form the basis for a fraud conviction."

The district court in Constantinescu appears to have read the indictment as alleging something similar—deprivation of "potentially valuable economic information" rather than a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit corrected this: the indictment does not mention the right-to-control theory at all. Instead, it alleges that defendants used misrepresentations to trick followers into buying securities, which is a scheme to deprive them of **money**.

## The Fraudulent-Inducement Theory vs. the Right-to-Control Theory

The court relied on **Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025)** to clarify the distinction. In Kousisis, the defendant lied to obtain government contracts by misrepresenting that he would use a disadvantaged-business subcontractor. The Court confirmed that the "fraudulent-inducement theory"—where "a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property"—is valid and "is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory." The right-to-control theory protects only intangible information interests; the fraudulent-inducement theory "protects money and property."

So the structural difference is:

- **Ciminelli (deficient):** The "property" alleged to be taken was the victim's right to make informed economic decisions—i.e., valuable economic information. Not a traditional property interest.
- **Constantinescu (sufficient):** The property alleged to be taken was the followers' **money**, which they parted with to purchase securities based on defendants' misrepresentations. A traditional property interest.

## The Intent-to-Defraud Element

On intent to defraud, defendants argued the indictment showed only an intent to profit, not an intent to harm victims. The court rejected this, again relying on Kousisis. The Supreme Court held that the fraud statutes do not require a victim to suffer a net economic loss; a defendant violates the statutes when he "schem[es] to obtain the victim's money or property, regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." The court found the indictment's allegations that defendants pumped and dumped to gain profit showed they "had money in mind," and that "a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses." The fact that followers received market-value securities in return does not negate the fraud.

## In Summary

What made this indictment sufficient was its allegation of a **fraudulent-inducement scheme**: defendants made material misrepresentations to induce followers to part with money by purchasing securities, with the object of obtaining that money for themselves. What made Ciminelli deficient was the absence of any allegation that the defendant sought to obtain the victim's money or property—the government there relied solely on the victim's deprivation of intangible economic information. The Fifth Circuit's holding turns on this distinction: the Constantinescu indictment alleged a scheme to get the followers' money; Ciminelli's prosecution alleged only a scheme to deprive the victim of information.

### How does the "object of the fraud" requirement work, and what does it mean that defendants "had money in mind"?
Question:
In Constantinescu, the court quotes Kousisis for the proposition that a defendant commits securities fraud when "obtaining the victim's money or property" was "an object of his fraud." Later, the court says the indictment's allegation that defendants pumped and dumped to gain a profit shows they "had money in mind." I'm confused about what "object" means here. Is this a subjective test (what the defendant actually intended) or an objective test (what a reasonable person would infer from the scheme)? Also, the phrase "had money in mind" seems informal—does this refer to a specific legal standard? If the defendants had multiple objectives (like gaining social media followers, building their brand, AND making money), does the fraud statute require money to be the primary object, or just one of several objects?
Answer:
## The "object of the fraud" is the defendant's purpose, and it need only be *one* object among several

### Subjective, not objective

The opinion frames "object" as a question of the defendant's own purpose in carrying out the scheme—not what a reasonable person would infer from the scheme's design. The court states that "defendants' object was to obtain money, and they did so by fraudulently inducing their followers to purchase securities." It then quotes *Kousisis* for the proposition that the indictment's allegation that defendants "pumped and dumped to gain a profit shows that they 'had money in mind.'" 145 S. Ct. at 1397. This language—both "object" and "had money in mind"—is directed at what the defendants themselves sought to accomplish through their deception. The court is examining the indictment's allegations about the defendants' intent and the design of their scheme, not conducting a reasonable-person inquiry.

Note that "had money in mind" is a direct quotation from the Supreme Court's opinion in *Kousisis*, not informal gloss by the Fifth Circuit. It is simply the Court's shorthand for the same concept: the defendant's fraud was purposefully directed at obtaining the victim's money or property.

### "An object" means one of potentially several

The court quotes *Kousisis* for the standard that a defendant commits securities fraud when "obtaining the victim's money or property" was "**an object** of his fraud." The indefinite article "an" is doing real work here. The fraud statutes do not require that obtaining money be the sole purpose of the scheme, nor does the opinion suggest it must be the primary one. The court's reasoning supports this reading: it identifies the profit motive as sufficient based on the indictment's allegations that defendants pumped and dumped securities "to gain a profit," without ever suggesting that profit must have been the exclusive or dominant motive. Had the statute required money to be the *primary* object, one would expect the court to say so—or at minimum to address the possibility that the defendants had other goals alongside making money. It does neither.

So if defendants had multiple objectives—building a social media following, promoting their brand, and making money—the fraud statute is satisfied so long as obtaining the victims' money or property was *one* of the objects of the deception. The opinion does not require a hierarchy among those objects.

### How it works in practice

The opinion illustrates the standard through its application. The indictment alleged that defendants "post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security." The court treated this as sufficient because it showed the defendants' scheme was designed to obtain their followers' money by tricking them into buying securities at artificially inflated prices. The defendants' argument that they intended only to profit—not to harm victims—was rejected as "a distinction without a difference," because the fraud statutes focus on whether the defendant schemed to obtain the victim's money or property, not on whether the defendant sought to cause net economic loss. In other words, the "object" inquiry asks whether obtaining money was part of the defendant's purpose in deceiving the victim—and here, the indictment's allegation of a $114 million profit from the pump-and-dump scheme was enough to establish that it was.
