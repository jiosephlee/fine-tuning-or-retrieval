Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# The Pump-and-Dump Scheme: Facts and Procedural Posture

## The Defendants and Their Social Media Presence

The case of *United States v. Constantinescu* arises from an alleged securities fraud scheme conducted through social media. The defendants are eight individuals: Edward Constantinescu, Perry "PJ" Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight. According to the government's allegations, each defendant cultivated a large following across various social media platforms and held himself out as a skilled stock trader. Defendants frequently posted about their trading activities, positioning themselves as knowledgeable market participants whose social media content could be relied upon by their followers for investment decisions. This combination of large audiences and apparent trading expertise forms the factual foundation of the government's case: the defendants' social media presence gave them the ability to influence their followers' investment behavior, which the government alleges they exploited to perpetrate fraud.

## The Mechanics of the Alleged Pump-and-Dump Scheme

At the heart of the indictment is what is commonly known as a "pump and dump" scheme. Understanding how such a scheme operates is essential to understanding the charges. The government alleges that defendants employed a three-step process. First, a defendant would purchase a security—taking a position in the market before any public manipulation began. Second, the defendant would "pump" the price of that security by posting false and misleading information about it on social media, inducing followers to purchase the security and thereby artificially inflating its price. The alleged misrepresentations included false statements about the defendants' own trading positions and about the potential price the security might reach. Third, once the price had been driven up by the influx of follower purchases, the defendant would "dump"—secretly selling the security at the inflated price for a profit, while followers were left holding securities purchased at artificially high prices. The indictment alleges that defendants profited approximately $114 million from this scheme. The critical feature of a pump-and-dump scheme is the asymmetry of information: the defendants knew they were driving up the price through deception and intended to sell at the peak, while their followers, unaware of the manipulation, purchased believing the defendants' representations about the security's prospects.

## The Indictment and Charges

A grand jury returned a superseding indictment charging the defendants with federal securities fraud offenses. To understand the procedural posture, it is helpful to recall what an indictment is and what role it plays in a criminal case. An indictment is a formal charging document issued by a grand jury that sets forth the essential facts constituting the offense charged. It need not prove the defendant's guilt; it need only allege the elements of the offense sufficiently to notify the defendant of the charges and to permit a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions. The indictment here charged defendants with two categories of offenses. First, it charged conspiracy to commit securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1349, which criminalizes an agreement between two or more persons to commit securities fraud, coupled with an overt act in furtherance of the conspiracy. Second, it charged multiple counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 2. Section 1348 is the primary securities fraud statute, prohibiting schemes to defraud in connection with securities. Section 2 is the general federal aiding-and-abetting statute, which extends criminal liability to those who assist another in committing a federal offense. The combination of these charges reflects the government's theory that defendants both agreed to and personally engaged in fraudulent conduct.

## Knight's Guilty Plea and the Remaining Defendants' Motion to Dismiss

After the indictment was returned, the procedural paths of the defendants diverged. Daniel Knight pleaded guilty, choosing to cooperate or at least to accept responsibility rather than contest the charges. The remaining defendants elected to challenge the indictment before trial by filing a motion to dismiss. A motion to dismiss an indictment tests the legal sufficiency of the charging document rather than the government's evidence. At this stage, the court does not assess whether the government can prove its allegations at trial; rather, the court assumes the allegations in the indictment are true and asks whether those allegations, taken as true, state an offense under the relevant criminal statute. This is an important procedural feature of the case: the district court was not evaluating whether defendants actually committed securities fraud, but whether the indictment's factual allegations, accepted as true, described conduct that the securities fraud statute prohibits.

## The District Court's Dismissal

The district court granted the defendants' motion to dismiss the indictment. Its reasoning turned on a specific and significant legal question: what must the government allege to state a fraud offense under the securities fraud statute? The district court concluded that the indictment failed to state an offense because it merely alleged that defendants sought to deprive their followers of "potentially valuable economic information" rather than a traditional property interest. This reasoning reflects a particular understanding of what fraud requires. In fraud law, the government must allege that the defendant schemed to deprive the victim of something the law recognizes as property. The district court appears to have viewed the indictment as resting on the theory that defendants' followers were deprived of information—namely, the truth about defendants' trading positions and intentions—rather than being deprived of money or property in a more traditional sense. Under this view, the indictment did not allege that followers lost money or property; it alleged only that they were denied information that would have been valuable in making investment decisions. Because the federal fraud statutes protect traditional property interests, the district court reasoned, the deprivation of valuable economic information alone was insufficient to sustain a fraud charge.

## The Government's Appeal

Following the district court's dismissal, the government exercised its right to appeal. In the federal criminal system, the government may appeal certain pretrial rulings, including the dismissal of an indictment, subject to statutory limitations. The government filed a timely appeal to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, arguing that the district court erred in concluding that the indictment failed to allege a scheme to deprive victims of a traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit's task on appeal was to review the sufficiency of the indictment de novo—meaning it would independently assess whether the indictment alleged each element of the charged offenses, giving no deference to the district court's legal conclusions. This de novo standard of review is significant because it means the court of appeals conducts its own fresh analysis of the legal question presented, rather than asking merely whether the district court's decision was reasonable or supported by the record. The central issue on appeal, as framed by the parties and the court, was whether the indictment alleged a scheme and intent to defraud sufficient to state an offense under the securities fraud statute—a question that required the Fifth Circuit to confront recent Supreme Court guidance on the scope of federal fraud law.

# Standard of Review and the Legal Framework of Securities Fraud Under Section 1348

## De Novo Review of Indictment Sufficiency

Before reaching the merits of the securities fraud charges, the Fifth Circuit first addressed the standard governing its review. When a defendant moves to dismiss an indictment for failure to state an offense, the appellate court reviews the sufficiency of that indictment de novo. This means the court of appeals examines the indictment anew, giving no deference to the district court's legal conclusions. The court cited United States v. Rafoi, 60 F.4th 982, 993 (5th Cir. 2023), for this proposition.

The de novo standard is accompanied by an important analytical posture: the court is required to take the allegations of the indictment as true. This is a critical principle for law students to grasp. When assessing whether an indictment states an offense, the court does not assess whether the government can ultimately prove its case at trial, nor does it weigh the credibility of the allegations. Instead, the court assumes the facts as alleged are accurate and asks only whether those facts, if proven, would constitute the charged offense. The court drew this principle from United States v. Fontenot, 665 F.3d 640, 644 (5th Cir. 2011), which in turn quoted United States v. Crow, 164 F.3d 229, 234 (5th Cir. 1999). This posture means that a motion to dismiss an indictment is a test of the indictment's legal sufficiency, not a test of the government's evidence.

## The Three Requirements for a Sufficient Indictment

The Fifth Circuit then articulated the specific requirements that an indictment must meet to be legally sufficient. Drawing from United States v. Gaytan, 74 F.3d 545, 551 (5th Cir. 1996), the court identified three functions an indictment must serve. First, the indictment must enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense. This means the indictment must contain factual allegations that, if taken as true, establish every element the government would need to prove at trial. Second, the indictment must fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him, ensuring the defendant understands what conduct is being prosecuted and can prepare a defense. Third, the indictment must provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions, meaning it must be specific enough that a future prosecution for the same conduct could be identified and barred.

The court distilled these requirements into a single formulation, quoting United States v. Guzman-Ocampo, 236 F.3d 233, 236 (5th Cir. 2000): "to be sufficient, an indictment must allege each material element of the offense." This summary encapsulates the core inquiry on a motion to dismiss. The question is whether the indictment's factual allegations, accepted as true, cover every material element of the charged crime. If any element is missing, the indictment is fatally defective. In this case, the district court had concluded that the indictment failed this test because it did not allege that defendants schemed to deprive victims of any traditional property interest. The Fifth Circuit's de novo review of that conclusion forms the heart of the opinion.

## Section 1348's Prohibition on Securities Fraud

The court then turned to the substantive statute at issue: 18 U.S.C. § 1348. This statute prohibits two categories of misconduct. First, it criminalizes schemes to "defraud any person in connection with . . . any security." Second, it prohibits schemes "to obtain, by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises, any money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of . . . any security." The first prohibition targets schemes to defraud generally, while the second targets schemes specifically aimed at obtaining money or property through false statements, in both cases tied to securities transactions.

The court noted that "[t]here is scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit." This observation is significant because it signals that the Fifth Circuit had limited directly on-point precedent to guide its analysis of § 1348's elements. The court therefore looked elsewhere for interpretive guidance, which it found in the closely related mail and wire fraud statutes.

## Borrowing Key Concepts from Mail and Wire Fraud Statutes

To fill the interpretive gap, the court relied on United States v. Greenlaw, 84 F.4th 325, 339 n.6 (5th Cir. 2023), which established that § 1348 "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes." Because of this textual and conceptual overlap, courts have given the terms in § 1348 "similar treatment" to their counterparts in the mail and wire fraud statutes. This means that when construing the elements of securities fraud under § 1348, the Fifth Circuit looks to the body of law developed under the mail and wire fraud statutes for guidance, and "often rel[ies] on mail and wire fraud cases in analyzing securities fraud charges."

This borrowing principle is more than a convenient cross-reference. It reflects a deeper structural relationship among the federal fraud statutes. Congress enacted § 1348 as part of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act in 2002, and it uses language closely paralleling the mail fraud statute (18 U.S.C. § 1341) and the wire fraud statute (18 U.S.C. § 1343). Because these statutes share the core concept of a "scheme to defraud," courts construing § 1348 have found it appropriate to draw on the well-developed body of mail and wire fraud precedent. For the student, the practical takeaway is that cases decided under the mail and wire fraud statutes are relevant authority when analyzing securities fraud under § 1348.

## The "Scheme to Defraud" Element

Drawing on Greenlaw, the court identified the first key element of securities fraud: a "scheme to defraud." This element requires a material misrepresentation "intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived." The court quoted this formulation from United States v. Evans, 892 F.3d 692, 711–12 (5th Cir. 2018), a mail and wire fraud case applied here through the borrowing principle. Several components of this definition deserve attention.

First, the misrepresentation must be material. A material misstatement is one that would naturally tend to influence the decision of the person to whom it is addressed. Trivial or immaterial falsehoods do not satisfy this element. Second, the misrepresentation must be intended to deceive. This means the defendant must have made the statement with awareness of its falsity and a purpose of misleading the audience. Third, the deception must be aimed at obtaining something of value, such as money. This requirement connects the scheme to a concrete object of the fraud—the defendant must be seeking to gain something of value through the deception. The court noted that the Supreme Court has articulated this same concept in broader terms: a defendant commits securities fraud when he "engaged in deception" and "[o]btaining the victim's money or property" was "an object of his fraud," citing Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382, 1390–91 (2025) (quoting Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306, 312 (2023)).

## The "Intent to Defraud" Element

The second key element the court identified is "intent to defraud." Under Greenlaw and Evans, this element requires "an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit." This is a two-part mental state requirement. The defendant must intend to deceive the victim, and must also intend that some harm flow from that deception. Importantly, as the opinion later clarifies, the "harm" component does not require the defendant to intend that the victim suffer a net economic loss. The defendant's intent to obtain money or property from the victim through deception is sufficient to satisfy the harm prong, even if the defendant does not seek to leave the victim worse off overall. The court drew this principle from Kousisis, which held that a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he "schem[es] to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off."

The combined effect of these two elements is that a defendant commits securities fraud under § 1348 when he engages in deception aimed at obtaining the victim's money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of a security. The scheme to defraud and the intent to defraud are distinct but related requirements: the scheme element focuses on the nature and object of the fraudulent conduct, while the intent element focuses on the defendant's state of mind regarding both the deception and its consequences.

## Defendants' Concession and Challenge

With this legal framework established, the court turned to the defendants' specific contentions. Notably, the defendants conceded that the indictment adequately alleges deception. This concession was significant because it removed from dispute the question of whether the indictment alleged false or misleading statements. The defendants did not argue that their social media posts were accurately described or that the indictment failed to allege misrepresentations. Instead, they challenged the indictment on two other grounds.

First, the defendants argued that the indictment did not allege a "scheme to defraud" because it merely alleged that they intended to deprive their followers of potentially valuable economic information rather than a traditional property interest. The district court had agreed with this reasoning, concluding that the indictment failed to state an offense. Second, the defendants argued that the indictment did not allege an "intent to defraud" because it alleged that they intended to profit from their scheme, not to harm any victim. In their view, seeking to enrich themselves was distinct from seeking to injure their followers.

These two arguments framed the central legal question on appeal: whether the indictment, accepted as true, alleged both a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud within the meaning of § 1348. The court answered this question by applying the recently decided Supreme Court cases of Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306 (2023), and Kousisis v. United States, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), to the specific allegations of the indictment. The court's analysis of these arguments forms the subject of the next chapter.

# Scheme to Defraud: Distinguishing the Right-to-Control Theory from Fraudulent Inducement

## Ciminelli and the Right-to-Control Theory: Deprivation of Potentially Valuable Economic Information

To understand why the Fifth Circuit reversed the district court's dismissal of the indictment in *Constantinescu*, students must first grasp the legal landscape the Supreme Court reshaped in *Ciminelli v. United States*, 598 U.S. 306 (2023). In *Ciminelli*, a construction company owner paid a lobbyist to help secure state-funded development projects. The federal government prosecuted him for wire fraud, but it did so on a particular theory: the so-called "right-to-control" theory. Under that theory, the government sought to establish fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions. In other words, the government did not allege that the defendant tricked the victim out of money or property in the traditional sense. Instead, the victim's "property" was said to be the right to control its own assets by making fully informed decisions. The Supreme Court took up the case to decide whether this theory could support a federal fraud conviction.

## Why the Right-to-Control Theory Fails: Valuable Economic Information Is Not a Traditional Property Interest

The Supreme Court held that the right-to-control theory could not form the basis of a fraud conviction. The federal fraud statutes, the Court explained, protect only traditional property interests—money and property in the conventional sense. The "right to valuable economic information," the Court reasoned, is not itself a traditional property interest. A victim's ability to make informed economic decisions, while undoubtedly important, is not the same as the victim's money or property. Because the fraud statutes are limited to schemes to deprive victims of traditional property, a prosecution that rests solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information cannot sustain a fraud conviction. This holding set an important boundary: prosecutors cannot expand federal fraud law into a general prohibition on dishonesty in business dealings by reframing a victim's lost informational advantage as a property loss.

## The District Court's Reliance on Ciminelli and Why It Was Misplaced

The district court in *Constantinescu* read *Ciminelli* broadly. It dismissed the indictment on the ground that the allegations merely described a scheme to deprive social-media followers of "potentially valuable economic information" rather than a traditional property interest. In the district court's view, the defendants' followers were not alleged to have been deprived of money or property; they were simply denied information—namely, the truth about the defendants' trading positions and intentions—that would have allowed them to make better investment decisions. The Fifth Circuit concluded that this reading of the indictment was mistaken. The indictment did not rely on a right-to-control theory at all. It did not allege that defendants schemed to deprive followers of information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions. Rather, it alleged that defendants used misrepresentations to induce their followers to purchase securities—that is, to part with their money or property. The district court's error was in treating a case about fraudulent inducement as though it were a case about the right to control assets through informed decision-making.

## The Fraudulent-Inducement Theory: Using Material Misstatements to Trick Victims into Handing Over Money or Property (Kousisis)

The Fifth Circuit's analysis depends heavily on the Supreme Court's decision in *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), which the *Constantinescu* court used to define and validate the "fraudulent-inducement theory." Under this theory, a defendant commits fraud when he uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract or transaction that requires the victim to hand over money or property. In *Kousisis*, the defendant lied to a state department of transportation about his compliance with a requirement to use a disadvantaged-business subcontractor. His misrepresentation induced the state to award him contracts—money and property changed hands—but the defendant argued that no fraud occurred because the state received the construction work it paid for and suffered no net economic loss. The Supreme Court rejected that argument, holding that a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he schemes to obtain the victim's money or property, regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off. The fraudulent-inducement theory thus protects victims who are tricked into handing over money or property, even if they receive something of equal value in return.

## Why the Fraudulent-Inducement Theory Is Distinct from and Not a Repackaging of the Right-to-Control Theory

A central concern in *Ciminelli* was that the right-to-control theory risked turning any material misstatement in a business context into federal fraud, regardless of whether the defendant actually sought to obtain the victim's money or property. The fraudulent-inducement theory, by contrast, requires precisely that: the defendant must use a material misstatement to trick the victim into handing over money or property. The Supreme Court in *Kousisis* confirmed that the fraudulent-inducement theory "is not a repackaging of the right-to-control theory." The right-to-control theory treats the victim's intangible right to make informed decisions as the protected property interest. The fraudulent-inducement theory treats the victim's money or property—the thing the victim was tricked into handing over—as the protected interest. The two theories therefore differ not merely in form but in substance: one protects an informational right that is not a traditional property interest, while the other protects the traditional property interests that the fraud statutes were designed to cover.

## How the Indictment Alleges Defendants Fraudulently Induced Followers to Purchase Securities—i.e., to Part with Money or Property

The Fifth Circuit examined the indictment's allegations to determine which theory the government was actually advancing. The indictment alleged that defendants would purchase a security, "pump" its price by posting false and misleading information on social media to induce their followers to buy the security, and then "dump" by secretly selling their own holdings at the artificially inflated price. Critically, the indictment alleged that defendants' misrepresentations were designed to induce followers "to purchase the security"—that is, to part with their money or property in exchange for securities. This is the hallmark of fraudulent inducement: a material misstatement used to trick a victim into handing over money or property. The followers were not merely denied information; they were actively misled into making purchases they would not otherwise have made. The object of the scheme, as alleged, was to obtain the followers' money by defrauding them into buying securities at inflated prices, after which defendants sold their own holdings for profit.

## Why the Indictment Does Not Run Afoul of Ciminelli

The Fifth Circuit concluded that the indictment did not violate the principle announced in *Ciminelli* because it did not rely on the deprivation of valuable economic information as the protected property interest. Instead, it alleged a straightforward scheme to deprive followers of money or property by fraudulently inducing them to purchase securities. The defendants' followers were alleged to have been deceived into parting with their money—money that the defendants then extracted through their "dump" of artificially inflated securities. Because the fraudulent-inducement theory protects traditional property interests—money and property—and because the Supreme Court in *Kousisis* confirmed that this theory is distinct from the right-to-control theory rejected in *Ciminelli*, the indictment stated a cognizable scheme to defraud. The district court's dismissal was therefore erroneous, and the Fifth Circuit reversed and remanded for further proceedings.

# Intent to Defraud: Profit Without Net Economic Harm Under Kousisis

## Defendants' Argument: Intent to Profit, Not to Harm

Having addressed the "scheme to defraud" element, the court turned to the second element of securities fraud: "intent to defraud." Under Fifth Circuit law, the intent-to-defraud element requires "an intent to (1) deceive, and (2) cause some harm to result from the deceit." Defendants conceded that the indictment adequately alleged deception—the first component of intent. But they challenged the second component, arguing that the indictment alleged only that they intended to profit from their scheme, not that they intended to harm their followers. In their view, a fraudulent scheme aimed at enriching the perpetrators, without an accompanying purpose to inflict economic injury on the victims, could not satisfy the intent-to-defraud element.

This argument had intuitive appeal. The defendants' followers were induced to buy securities, and they received securities in return—securities that had some market value. If the victims got something of value for their money, and the defendants merely sought to profit rather than to impoverish anyone, how could the defendants be said to have intended "harm"? The district court's dismissal of the indictment lent credence to this framing. But as the Fifth Circuit explained, the Supreme Court had recently and squarely rejected the premise underlying defendants' argument.

## Kousisis and the Fraudulent-Inducement Theory in the Intent-to-Defraud Context

The court's analysis centered on *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), a Supreme Court decision that addressed the fraudulent-inducement theory of fraud in the context of the intent element. In *Kousisis*, the defendant lied to obtain government contracts. The state required contractors to subcontract with a disadvantaged business, and Kousisis falsely represented that his company would fulfill that requirement. After winning the contracts, Kousisis used the disadvantaged-business subcontractor as a "mere 'pass-through' entity," meaning the subcontractor performed no real work but was used to satisfy the contractual requirement on paper.

The government charged Kousisis under what the Court called the "fraudulent-inducement theory," under which a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss." The key phrase is "regardless of whether the fraudster . . . seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss." The Court thus framed the fraudulent-inducement theory as encompassing situations where the defendant obtains the victim's money or property through deception, even if the defendant gives something of value back and even if the defendant does not specifically aim to leave the victim economically worse off.

This was directly relevant to the defendants in *Constantinescu*. Their scheme—inducing followers to purchase securities through false and misleading social-media posts—fit squarely within the fraudulent-inducement theory. The defendants used material misstatements to trick their followers into buying securities, which required those followers to hand over their money. The fact that the followers received securities in return, and that the defendants' primary goal was their own profit rather than their followers' impoverishment, did not place the scheme outside the reach of the fraud statutes.

## The Supreme Court's Holding That Fraud Statutes Do Not Require Net Pecuniary Loss

The crux of defendants' argument was that the intent-to-defraud element requires the government to allege that the defendant intended to cause the victim an economic loss. *Kousisis* explicitly rejected this position. Kousisis had argued that the fraudulent-inducement theory could not support a fraud conviction because it permitted conviction "in the absence of evidence that 'the defendant sought to hurt the victim's bottom line.'" The Supreme Court rejected that assertion, holding that a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he "schem[es] to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off."

This holding resolved a significant question about the scope of the federal fraud statutes. The statutes prohibit schemes to obtain money or property by means of false or fraudulent pretenses. They do not, the Court confirmed, require proof that the defendant intended to cause net economic harm to the victim. The fraud is complete when the defendant obtains the victim's money or property through material misrepresentation, whether or not the victim ultimately suffers a net loss. The defendant's intent to obtain the victim's property through deceit is itself the culpable mental state that the statutes require.

The Court's reasoning drew on the text of the fraud statutes, which prohibit schemes to "obtain" money or property by fraudulent means. The focus is on the defendant's objective—to obtain the victim's property—and on the fraudulent means by which that objective is pursued. The statutes do not say "schemes to cause net economic loss" or "schemes to leave the victim worse off." They say "schemes to obtain," and that is what the defendant must intend to do.

## Why Receipt of Market-Value Securities Does Not Obviate the Fraud Charges

Applying *Kousisis* to the facts alleged in the indictment, the court addressed a natural question: if the defendants' followers received securities in exchange for their money, and those securities had market value, where was the fraud? The answer lay in *Kousisis*'s recognition that a defendant may be liable in fraud even when he "provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return." The followers' receipt of market-value securities did not negate the fraud because the fraud statutes do not require a victim to suffer net economic loss.

The court emphasized that "a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses—regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss." In this case, the indictment alleged that defendants induced their followers to purchase securities through false and misleading social-media posts. The followers parted with their money based on material misrepresentations—that the defendants were holding their positions, that the securities had certain price potential, and so on. That inducement, accomplished through deceit, completed the fraud. The fact that the followers received securities of some market value in return did not undo the fraud, because the statutes do not demand a showing that the exchange left the followers with less than they started.

This principle is important for law students to grasp because it runs counter to a certain intuitive sense of fairness. One might think that if the victim receives something of equal value, no real harm was done and therefore no fraud was committed. But the fraud statutes are concerned with the integrity of transactions, not solely with their economic outcomes. A transaction induced by material misrepresentation is fraudulent even if the victim receives full value, because the victim was deprived of the ability to make an informed economic decision. The statutes protect the victim's right to accurate information in deciding whether to part with money or property—even though, as *Ciminelli* clarified, the "right to valuable economic information" is not itself the property interest protected by the fraud statutes. The property interest is the money or property the victim hands over; the materiality of the misrepresentation is what connects the deceit to the deprivation of that property.

## Rejection of the "Mirror Image" Requirement: Baker

The court reinforced its conclusion by citing the Fifth Circuit's prior decision in *United States v. Baker*, 923 F.3d 390 (5th Cir. 2019), which had explained that the fraud statutes do not impose a "mirror image" requirement. Under such a requirement, the victim's loss of money or property would need to equal—or "mirror"—the defendant's gain for the fraud to be complete. *Baker* rejected this approach, and *Kousisis* confirmed the rejection at the Supreme Court level.

The "mirror image" requirement would have meant that the government could prove fraud only by showing that the defendant's gain came directly and proportionally from the victim's loss. In the context of *Constantinescu*, such a requirement would have posed a problem for the government if the followers received securities of market value roughly equal to the money they spent. Under a mirror-image theory, if the followers lost nothing (because they got value back), then the defendants' gain could not be attributed to the followers' loss, and the fraud element might not be satisfied.

But *Baker* and *Kousisis* established that the fraud statutes do not work this way. The defendant's gain and the victim's loss are not required to be symmetrical. The defendant's gain—here, the $114 million in profits from the pump-and-dump scheme—was obtained through fraudulent inducement. The victims' parting with their money to purchase securities, induced by material misrepresentations, was the deprivation that completed the fraud. Whether the victims ultimately lost money on the securities or received something of equivalent value was not determinative. The court also noted the Seventh Circuit's decision in *United States v. Smith*, which applied *Kousisis* in a similar securities-fraud context, further supporting the conclusion that the fraudulent-inducement theory applies to pump-and-dump schemes.

## Intent to Profit and Intent to Harm: A Distinction Without a Difference

The defendants' final argument was that the indictment showed only an intent to profit, not an intent to harm. The court called this "a distinction without a difference." This phrase captures an important insight about the intent-to-defraud element in the context of fraudulent inducement.

The defendants framed the issue as though "intent to profit" and "intent to harm" were mutually exclusive categories—that a defendant could intend one without the other. But the court's analysis showed that, in a fraudulent-inducement scheme, the two are inextricably linked. The defendants' object was to obtain money from their followers. They accomplished this object by fraudulently inducing the followers to purchase securities. The defendants' intent to profit was realized through the very mechanism that constituted the fraud: deceiving followers into parting with their money. The profit motive did not negate the intent to defraud; it was the purpose for which the fraud was committed.

The court's phrase "a distinction without a difference" thus conveys a legal principle: when the defendant's intended profit is obtained by fraudulently inducing the victim to hand over money or property, the intent to profit satisfies the intent-to-defraud element. The government need not separately prove that the defendant harbored a subjective desire to cause the victim economic harm. The intent to obtain the victim's property through deceit is itself the intent to cause the harm that the fraud statutes prohibit—the deprivation of money or property by fraudulent means.

## "Had Money in Mind": How the Indictment Allegations Satisfy the Intent Element

The court concluded its intent-to-defraud analysis by connecting the specific allegations of the indictment to the *Kousisis* framework. Quoting *Kousisis*, the court noted that the indictment's allegations showed that the defendants "had money in mind." This phrase from *Kousisis* captures the essence of the intent element: the defendant's scheme must be directed at obtaining the victim's money or property. The object of the fraud must be the acquisition of money or property, not merely the deprivation of intangible interests like economic information (which, as *Ciminelli* established, is not a protected property interest).

The indictment here alleged that defendants "post[ed] false and misleading information about the security . . . so that other investors were induced to purchase the security." This allegation directly tied the defendants' deception to their followers' expenditure of money. The indictment further alleged that defendants engaged in a coordinated "pump and dump"—purchasing securities, promoting them with false information to drive up the price, and then selling their own holdings for profit. The pump-and-dump mechanism, as alleged, demonstrated that the defendants' object was to obtain money—their followers' money, channeled through securities purchases that inflated the price, allowing the defendants to sell at a profit.

The court thus held that the indictment sufficiently alleged the intent-to-defraud element. The defendants' scheme was directed at obtaining money from their followers through fraudulent inducement. Their intent to profit, achieved by deception that caused their followers to part with money, satisfied the requirement that the defendant "had money in mind." Because the indictment sufficiently alleged both a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud, the court reversed the district court's dismissal and remanded for further proceedings.

# Disposition and Significance

## The Fifth Circuit's Reversal and Remand

Having concluded that the indictment sufficiently alleged both a scheme to defraud and an intent to defraud, the Fifth Circuit reversed the district court's dismissal of the indictment. The court's disposition was straightforward: "Because the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud, we REVERSE the district court's dismissal of the indictment and REMAND for proceedings consistent with this opinion." The brevity of this conclusion belies its significance. The district court had dismissed the indictment on the ground that it failed to allege deprivation of a traditional property interest, relying on a reading of the Supreme Court's decision in *Ciminelli v. United States*. The Fifth Circuit's reversal rejected that reading, determining that the indictment did not rely on the discredited right-to-control theory at all, but instead alleged a classic fraudulent-inducement scheme—defendants tricked their followers into parting with money to purchase securities through material misrepresentations.

The remand means the case returns to the district court for further proceedings. The defendants, who had successfully moved to dismiss the indictment below, now face the prospect of standing trial on the securities fraud charges alleged in the superseding indictment. One defendant, Daniel Knight, had already pleaded guilty before the remaining defendants moved to dismiss, and his plea is unaffected by this appellate decision. The court's reversal thus restores the indictment as to the remaining defendants and permits the prosecution to proceed.

## The Interplay Between Ciminelli, Kousisis, and Constantinescu

To understand the significance of this case, a law student must grasp how the Fifth Circuit situated the indictment within the framework established by two recent Supreme Court decisions: *Ciminelli v. United States*, 598 U.S. 306 (2023), and *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025). These two cases, decided in succession, defined the boundaries of what constitutes a protected property interest under the federal fraud statutes and clarified the intent required for a fraud conviction.

In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court addressed the "right-to-control theory" of fraud. Under that theory, the government could establish fraud by showing that a defendant schemed to deprive a victim of "potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions." The Court rejected this theory, holding that the federal fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests, and the right to valuable economic information is not such an interest. The *Ciminelli* decision was significant because it narrowed the scope of federal fraud law, eliminating a prosecutorial theory that had allowed convictions based solely on a victim's loss of information relevant to decision-making, without any corresponding loss of money or property.

The following year, in *Kousisis*, the Supreme Court addressed a different theory—the "fraudulent-inducement theory." There, the defendant had lied to obtain government contracts by falsely representing that his company would use a disadvantaged-business subcontractor, when in fact he used the subcontractor as a mere pass-through entity. The Court upheld the fraud conviction, confirming that a defendant commits fraud when he "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property—regardless of whether the fraudster, who often provides something in return, seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss." The Court was careful to distinguish the fraudulent-inducement theory from the right-to-control theory rejected in *Ciminelli*, noting that the fraudulent-inducement theory "protects money and property" rather than intangible information rights.

The Fifth Circuit in *Constantinescu* used these two decisions as complementary guideposts. The district court had essentially treated the indictment as if it rested on the right-to-control theory—the theory *Ciminelli* rejected. The Fifth Circuit corrected this characterization, noting that "the indictment does not mention the right-to-control theory." Instead, the indictment alleged that defendants induced their followers to purchase securities—to part with their money—through misrepresentations. This placed the indictment squarely within the fraudulent-inducement theory that *Kousisis* upheld. The Fifth Circuit thus read *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* together as drawing a clear line: fraud convictions cannot rest solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information, but they can rest on fraudulent inducement that causes victims to hand over money or property, even if the victims receive something of equal value in return.

## Implications for Securities Fraud Prosecutions Involving Social Media

The facts of *Constantinescu* reflect a modern iteration of securities fraud that leverages social media platforms rather than traditional channels of communication. The defendants each had large social media followings and held themselves out as skilled stock traders. The alleged scheme—purchasing a security, promoting it to followers through false and misleading posts, and then secretly selling for a profit—is a technologically updated version of the classic pump-and-dump scheme. The indictment alleged that defendants profited $114 million from this conduct.

The Fifth Circuit's decision has several implications for prosecutions of social-media-based securities fraud. First, it confirms that the securities fraud statute, 18 U.S.C. § 1348, reaches schemes conducted through social media just as it reaches schemes conducted through other means. The court noted that § 1348 "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes," and courts have given the terms similar treatment. The underlying fraud concepts—material misrepresentation, scheme to defraud, and intent to defraud—translate directly to the social media context. The medium of the fraud does not change the legal analysis.

Second, the decision provides a roadmap for prosecutors charging social media influencers who use their platforms to manipulate securities markets. The key is that the indictment must allege that the defendant sought to obtain money or property from victims through material misrepresentations—not merely that victims were deprived of information. An indictment alleging that an influencer falsely represented his trading position to induce followers to buy securities, with the intent to profit from the artificially inflated price, satisfies the elements of securities fraud under § 1348. The court's analysis confirms that followers who purchased securities at the influencer's urging were deprived of money or property—namely, the funds they used to purchase the securities—through fraudulent inducement.

Third, the decision highlights the distinction between information that victims might have found valuable and the property that victims actually parted with. The district court dismissed the indictment because it understood the indictment to allege only that defendants sought to deprive followers of "potentially valuable economic information." The Fifth Circuit rejected this framing. While the defendants' followers may have been deprived of accurate information about the defendants' trading positions, the indictment alleged more: it alleged that defendants used that misinformation to induce followers to purchase securities, thereby parting with their money. The property interest at stake was the followers' money, not their right to accurate information.

## Fraud Liability Where Victims Receive Something of Value

One of the most significant doctrinal points in the Fifth Circuit's decision is its confirmation that fraud liability can attach even when victims receive something of value in return for their money or property. This principle, drawn directly from *Kousisis*, has broad implications beyond the securities fraud context.

The defendants argued that the indictment failed to allege an intent to defraud because it alleged only that they intended to profit from their scheme, not to harm any victim. Their followers, after all, did receive securities in exchange for their money—potentially securities of equal market value at the time of purchase. The Fifth Circuit rejected this argument, citing *Kousisis* for the proposition that "a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he 'schem[es] to "obtain" the victim's "money or property," regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.'" The court further noted that a "fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses—regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss."

This principle is significant because it forecloses a potential defense that might seem intuitive: the argument that no fraud occurred because the victim received fair value in exchange. The Fifth Circuit's decision confirms that the focus of fraud law is on the defendant's deceptive conduct and the victim's resulting deprivation of money or property, not on whether the victim ultimately suffered an economic loss. As the court explained, the defendants' object was to obtain money, and they accomplished this by fraudulently inducing followers to purchase securities. The fact that followers received securities in return does not negate the fraud. The court put this bluntly: "Defendants argue they intended to profit—not harm any victim. But here, that is a distinction without a difference."

The court also cited *United States v. Baker*, 923 F.3d 390 (5th Cir. 2019), for the proposition that the fraud statutes do not impose a "mirror image" requirement where the victim's loss of money or property must equal the defendant's gain. This further reinforces the principle that fraud law targets the deceptive acquisition of money or property, not the economic imbalance between victim and fraudster.

## The Confirmatory Role of Seventh Circuit Precedent

The Fifth Circuit concluded its intent-to-defraud analysis by citing a recent Seventh Circuit decision, *United States v. Smith*, Nos. 23-2840, 23-2846, & 23-2849, 2025 WL 2406104 (7th Cir. Aug. 20, 2025), which applied *Kousisis* "in a similar context." While the Fifth Circuit did not extensively discuss *Smith*, its citation serves an important function. By noting that another circuit court had already applied *Kousisis* to similar facts, the Fifth Circuit signaled that its reading of *Kousisis* was not an outlier or a novel extension but rather consistent with how other courts were interpreting the Supreme Court's fraudulent-inducement holding. This cross-circuit confirmatory citation strengthens the precedential weight of the *Constantinescu* decision and suggests that the principles it articulates are likely to be followed in other circuits facing similar social-media-based securities fraud cases.

The citation to *Smith* also underscores the speed with which lower courts have begun to apply the *Kousisis* framework. *Kousisis* was decided in 2025, and within months, both the Seventh and Fifth Circuits had applied its fraudulent-inducement theory to reverse dismissals of indictments in fraud cases. For law students, this illustrates how Supreme Court precedents in the fraud context are operationalized by lower courts—often quickly and with practical consequences for ongoing prosecutions.

Taken together, the Fifth Circuit's decision in *Constantinescu* occupies an important place in the post-*Ciminelli*/*Kousisis* landscape of federal fraud law. It confirms that fraudulent-inducement theory remains a viable basis for fraud convictions, that victims' receipt of value does not negate fraud liability, and that social-media-based pump-and-dump schemes fall squarely within the reach of the securities fraud statute. The reversal and remand ensure that the defendants will face trial on the charges alleged, and the opinion provides guidance for prosecutors and courts confronting similar schemes in the future.
