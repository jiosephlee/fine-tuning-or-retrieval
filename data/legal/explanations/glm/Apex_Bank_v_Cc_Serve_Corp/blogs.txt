Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# "Similarity" in the Eye of the Beholder: The Federal Circuit Demands Doctrinal Consistency Across DuPont Factors

When a multi-factor test has been around for over fifty years, you might expect its components to operate in well-settled, independent lanes. The Federal Circuit's recent decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* pushes back against that assumption, at least in the trademark context. The case concerns a seemingly technical question—how the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board defines "similarity" when assessing third-party marks under the sixth DuPont factor—but the court's reasoning has broader implications for how administrative tribunals must maintain consistency across the factors of a multi-part legal test.

## The Dispute

CC Serve owns a 1998 registration for the word mark ASPIRE, used in connection with credit card services. CC Serve does not issue cards directly; it partners with banks that issue ASPIRE-branded credit cards, which CC Serve then services. Apex Bank, a Tennessee-chartered retail bank with eighteen branches, does not currently offer credit cards. It filed intent-to-use applications to register ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for "banking and financing services" in connection with a planned online banking platform. CC Serve opposed, and the Board sustained the opposition under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, finding a likelihood of confusion.

On appeal, Apex challenged the Board's analysis of three DuPont factors: the second (similarity of the goods or services), the sixth (number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods), and the first (similarity of the marks in their entireties). The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board's second-factor analysis but vacated its sixth- and first-factor analyses, remanding for reconsideration.

## The Second Factor: A Finding That Stood

The Board's second-factor analysis is straightforward and unsurprising. The Board found that "credit card services" and "banking and financing services" are "legally identical, in part," relying on dictionary definitions showing that banking and finance encompass extending credit through credit cards, supplemented by third-party registrations covering both categories. Apex argued that CC Serve was merely partnering with banks to manage credit card programs rather than providing banking services itself, but the court found this argument "unavailing." The Board's careful comparison of the service descriptions, supported by substantial evidence, was enough to sustain the finding.

This portion of the opinion is unremarkable, but it sets the stage for the more interesting ruling that follows.

## The Sixth Factor: Where Consistency Breaks Down

The sixth DuPont factor asks about "[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." Evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant because it can show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection. The logic is straightforward: when a field is crowded with similar marks, consumers learn to distinguish among them, reducing the likelihood of confusion.

Apex submitted evidence of numerous third-party uses of marks incorporating "Aspire" in the financial services industry. The Board, however, limited its consideration to marks used specifically for credit card services—nine in total—and excluded marks for other banking and financial services. The Board concluded that nine marks did not rise to the level of "considerable" or "ubiquitous" use sufficient to demonstrate weakness, citing the Federal Circuit's prior decisions in *Jack Wolfskin* (fourteen "notable examples") and *Juice Generation* (twenty-six marks as "a considerable number").

The Federal Circuit held this was legal error. The problem was not the Board's ultimate conclusion about whether nine marks suffice to show weakness—that is a factual question reviewed for substantial evidence. The problem was the Board's *scope of inquiry*. Having already found under the second factor that the parties' services were "highly similar" and even "legally identical, in part," the Board then narrowed the universe of third-party marks to only those for credit card services. The court saw no reason to "impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor" than the Board applied under the second.

This is the analytical heart of the opinion, and it is worth pausing to consider what the court is really saying. The Federal Circuit is not merely correcting a factual oversight; it is articulating a doctrinal constraint. Once the Board makes a factual finding about the degree of similarity between the parties' services, that finding must carry forward into the Board's analysis of what counts as a "similar" good or service under the sixth factor. The Board cannot expand similarity when it works in favor of a confusion finding and then contract it when similarity would cut the other way.

## The First Factor: A Cascade Effect

The court's vacatur of the first DuPont factor follows logically from its sixth-factor ruling. The first factor examines the similarity of the marks in their entireties, including their commercial impression. Commercial strength—or weakness—directly informs commercial impression. If the sixth-factor analysis changes on remand—say, the Board now considers forty-plus third-party ASPIRE-formative marks in the financial services industry and concludes that CC Serve's mark is relatively weak—that determination could alter the mark's commercial impression and, by extension, the first-factor analysis. The court accordingly remanded the first factor for reconsideration as well.

This cascading effect illustrates the interdependence of the DuPont factors in a way that the Federal Circuit has not previously made so explicit. The court's willingness to vacate a factor based on the anticipated effects of reconsidering another factor suggests a view of the DuPont framework as a system of interacting analytical components rather than a checklist of independent inquiries.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The most immediate implication is practical: the Board must be more careful about defining the scope of "similarity" consistently across factors. But the deeper question the opinion raises is whether the Federal Circuit views the DuPont factors as more interdependent than previously understood—or at least more interdependent than the Board has been treating them.

On one reading, the decision is narrow. The court did not hold that every factual finding under one factor must be mechanically transposed into every other factor. It held only that the Board's *threshold definition of similarity* must remain consistent between the second and sixth factors. That is a relatively modest constraint, and it leaves the Board substantial room to calibrate its analysis under each factor in other respects.

On another reading, however, the opinion signals a more structural view. The court's reasoning—that the Board "should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors"—could be invoked in future cases to challenge inconsistencies across other pairs of factors. If the Board finds goods to be complementary under the second factor, must it consider trade dress similarities under the third factor with the same understanding of the commercial context? If the Board finds that consumers are sophisticated under the fourth factor, must that finding inform the weight given to third-party marks under the sixth? The opinion does not answer these questions, but it provides a doctrinal foothold for litigants to raise them.

There is also a question of whether this consistency requirement cuts in only one direction. The court's framing suggests that the Board's second-factor finding of broad similarity should expand the sixth-factor inquiry. But the logic works the other way too: if a Board were to find services only tangentially related under the second factor, a litigant could argue that the sixth-factor inquiry should correspondingly narrow. The opinion's reasoning is doctrinally symmetric even if the facts presented a one-directional case.

Finally, the opinion's treatment of the first factor suggests that the Federal Circuit may be willing to revisit not just individual factor analyses but the *relationships* among them. If a change in one factor's analysis can cascade into another, parties on both sides of future oppositions and cancellations will have strategic incentives to litigate the factors in a particular sequence—or to argue that the Board's sequencing of its own analysis produced internal inconsistencies. The Board may need to develop a more self-conscious methodology for ensuring that its factor-by-factor findings are mutually coherent.

## The Bottom Line

*Apex Bank* is not a landmark case, but it is a telling one. The Federal Circuit's core holding—that the Board cannot apply inconsistent definitions of "similarity" across DuPont factors—reflects a concern with the internal logic of multi-factor tests that goes beyond trademark law. Any tribunal applying a structured balancing test should take note: factual findings under one factor may constrain the analytical framework of another, and a failure to maintain consistency across factors is not just a weak opinion but legal error. Whether this principle extends beyond the specific second-to-sixth-factor link at issue here is a question future cases will have to answer. But the door is now open.



# The Cascade Effect: How Errors in DuPont Factor Six Can Reshape Factor One's Commercial Impression Analysis

When the Federal Circuit vacates a likelihood-of-confusion determination, it usually does so because the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board got the facts wrong on a single factor or misapplied the legal standard in isolation. But last week's decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* presents something different: a cascading doctrinal relationship between two DuPont factors that practitioners and the Board itself may not have fully appreciated. The court held that an error in the sixth DuPont factor—third-party marks and mark weakness—requires reconsideration not only of that factor but also of the first DuPont factor—similarity of the marks in their entireties—as to appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. The reason? Commercial strength, evaluated under factor six, may be "a thread that runs through" the commercial impression analysis under factor one. The decision deserves close attention from trademark practitioners, because it clarifies—perhaps more than any prior Federal Circuit decision—that the DuPont factors are not hermetically sealed analytical silos.

## The Dispute and the Board's Decision

The facts are straightforward. CC Serve owns a registration for the word mark ASPIRE, used in connection with credit card services since 1996. CC Serve does not issue cards directly; it partners with banks that issue ASPIRE-branded credit cards, which CC Serve then services. Apex Bank, a Tennessee retail bank with eighteen branches, sought to register ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for "banking and financing services" in connection with a planned internet bank. Apex does not currently offer credit cards. CC Serve opposed registration under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, and the Board sustained the opposition, finding a likelihood of confusion.

The Board's analysis turned on three DuPont factors. On the second factor—similarity of the goods and services—the Board found the parties' services "legally identical, in part," reasoning that dictionary definitions of "banking" and "finance" encompass extending credit through credit card issuance, and citing third-party registrations covering both credit card and banking services. On the sixth factor—number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods—the Board limited its analysis to marks used specifically for credit card services, finding nine such ASPIRE-formative marks and concluding that this number did not rise to the level of "considerable" or "ubiquitous" use sufficient to demonstrate weakness. The Board thus accorded CC Serve's mark the "normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks." On the first factor—similarity of the marks in their entireties—the Board found the marks similar in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression, weighing in favor of confusion.

## The Sixth Factor Error: Inconsistent Standards of Similarity

The Federal Circuit's vacatur of the sixth factor is rooted in a simple but important point of analytical consistency. Having already found under the second factor that the parties' services are "highly similar"—indeed, "legally identical, in part"—the Board then applied a narrower standard of similarity when it came to the sixth factor, considering only third-party marks used for credit card services and excluding marks used for other banking and financing services. The court found this inconsistency legally erroneous.

The sixth DuPont factor, the court reminded, "does not require identical goods—only similar ones," citing *Olde Tyme Foods* and *Juice Generation*. The court's reasoning is compelling. If banking and financing services are similar enough to credit card services to support a finding of likely confusion under factor two, they must also be similar enough to bring third-party ASPIRE marks used in the broader financial services space within the scope of factor six. The Board cannot have it both ways: a broad definition of similarity when it favors the opposer under factor two, and a narrow definition when it might favor the applicant under factor six. The court thus remanded for the Board to reconsider the universe of third-party marks eligible for consideration "in view of the Board's factual finding that the parties' services are highly similar."

This is a clean application of a consistency principle, and it is hard to quarrel with. The Board's finding that nine ASPIRE marks for credit card services alone did not demonstrate weakness may well have been different if the Board had considered the 42 additional ASPIRE-formative marks in the broader financial services industry that Apex put forward. The court did not hold that those marks necessarily establish weakness—only that the Board was legally required to consider them.

## The Cascade: From Factor Six to Factor One

The more interesting—and doctrinally novel—aspect of the decision is what the court did next. Having vacated the sixth factor, the court proceeded to vacate the first factor as well. The court's reasoning is brief but loaded with implications: "Because commercial impression informs the analysis under the first DuPont factor, we must similarly vacate the Board's analysis here because reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark's commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression."

This is the cascade effect. The sixth factor asks whether the marketplace is crowded with similar marks on similar goods, which speaks to whether the senior mark is commercially weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection. The first factor asks whether the marks are similar in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. The court appears to treat commercial strength as a variable that, once changed under factor six, must be reentered into the equation under factor one. If the Board on remand finds that CC Serve's mark is weaker than initially determined—because the field is more crowded than the Board previously recognized—then the commercial impression of the mark, and the likelihood that consumers will be confused by a similar mark, may shift as well.

This is a significant doctrinal move. The Federal Circuit has long recognized that the DuPont factors are interrelated and that the weight given to each factor may vary depending on the facts. But *Apex Bank* goes further: it treats the commercial strength finding under factor six as an input that can directly reshape the analysis under factor one. The court does not elaborate extensively on the mechanics of this relationship, but the logic seems to be that a mark's commercial strength affects the range of similar marks that would be likely to cause confusion. A strong mark casts a wider net; a weak mark casts a narrower one. If the sixth factor is reconsidered and the mark is found weaker, the first factor's commercial impression analysis must be recalibrated accordingly.

## Circularity and the Risk of Double-Counting

The cascade effect raises a legitimate concern about circularity. If commercial strength is relevant to both factor six and factor one, evidence of third-party use could effectively be counted twice: once to establish weakness under factor six, and again to narrow the scope of protection under factor one's commercial impression analysis. This is not necessarily a problem—the DuPont factors are not required to be mutually exclusive, and some overlap is inherent in a multifactor test. But it does create a risk that the same evidence will have an outsized effect on the ultimate likelihood-of-confusion determination, particularly in close cases.

The court's opinion does not address this concern directly, and one wishes the court had been more explicit about how the Board should manage the relationship between the two factors on remand. Should the Board's first-factor analysis explicitly incorporate the commercial strength finding from factor six, or should the two factors remain analytically distinct, with commercial strength as merely a background consideration? The opinion's language—"reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark's commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression"—suggests the former: the Board should affirmatively reconsider the commercial impression in light of whatever it finds on commercial strength. But the court leaves the precise mechanics to the Board.

## Practical Implications for Trademark Practitioners

For practitioners, *Apex Bank* offers several takeaways. First, it underscores the importance of consistency in defining the relevant universe of similar goods and services across all DuPont factors. If an applicant argues that goods are dissimilar under factor two, it cannot then argue that a broad range of third-party marks should be considered under factor six without explaining the apparent inconsistency—and the same is true for opposers. The court's opinion effectively requires parties and the Board to maintain a coherent definition of "similarity" throughout the analysis, or to justify any departure from it.

Second, the decision should encourage applicants opposing a likelihood-of-confusion finding to invest heavily in sixth-factor evidence. The payoff is no longer limited to narrowing the scope of protection under that factor alone; a robust showing of third-party use may now cascade into the first factor and alter the overall commercial impression analysis. Applicants who previously might have treated third-party mark evidence as a secondary line of defense should consider elevating it in their strategy, particularly in crowded fields where the senior mark is not commercially strong.

Third, the opinion may give the Board pause before limiting the scope of third-party marks it considers under factor six. The Board has sometimes been criticized for narrowly construing the universe of relevant third-party marks, effectively shielding senior marks from weakness findings. *Apex Bank* gives applicants a clear doctrinal hook to challenge such narrowing, at least where the Board has already found the parties' goods or services to be similar under other factors.

## A Thread Worth Pulling

The Federal Circuit's opinion is relatively brief, and the cascade analysis occupies only a few sentences. But those sentences may prove more consequential than the court anticipated. By explicitly linking commercial strength under factor six to commercial impression under factor one, the court has identified a doctrinal relationship that, while logically sound, has not been clearly articulated in prior Federal Circuit precedent. Whether this cascade effect will be limited to the specific relationship between factors one and six or will be extended to other interfactor relationships remains to be seen. But for now, *Apex Bank* stands as a reminder that the DuPont factors are not a checklist to be completed in isolation—they are an interconnected analytical framework in which a change in one factor can ripple through the others.



# How Many Marks Make a Crowd? The Elusive Quantum for Demonstrating Mark Weakness Under DuPont Factor Six

When does a trademark become so crowded with similar third-party uses that consumers learn to distinguish them, narrowing the mark's scope of protection? The Federal Circuit's recent decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* doesn't answer that question definitively, but it exposes a persistent and important gap in likelihood-of-confusion doctrine: the absence of any clear threshold for how much third-party use suffices to show that a mark is weak. The case also reveals a doctrinal tension that the Board has struggled to resolve—what happens when a mark is neither demonstrably strong nor demonstrably weak? The court's remand may push the Board toward confronting that question more directly, and the answer could reshape how trademark practitioners assess vulnerability in crowded fields.

## The Board's Narrow Window on a Crowded Field

The dispute arose when Apex Bank, a Tennessee-chartered retail bank with eighteen branches, sought to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services. CC Serve, which owns a 1998 registration for ASPIRE in connection with credit card services, opposed the application. The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board sustained the opposition under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, finding likelihood of confusion.

Central to the Board's analysis was the sixth DuPont factor, which considers "the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." The doctrinal logic is straightforward: when a marketplace is crowded with similar marks on similar goods, consumers become more discerning, and the mark at issue is entitled to only a narrow scope of protection. Apex had submitted evidence of numerous third-party uses of "Aspire" and Aspire-formative marks in the financial services industry. But the Board narrowed the universe it would consider.

Because the Board had determined that the relevant public consisted of "consumers of credit card services," it looked only at third-party marks used in connection with credit card services and deemed marks using "Aspire" for other financial services "essentially irrelevant." Within that restricted set, the Board identified nine Aspire-formative marks for credit card services. It concluded that nine uses did not rise to the level of "considerable" or "ubiquitous" use necessary to demonstrate weakness, citing the Federal Circuit's prior decisions in *Jack Wolfskin* and *Juice Generation*—cases that involved fourteen and twenty-six third-party marks, respectively.

## The Quantum Problem

The Board's reasoning implicitly raises a question the Federal Circuit has never squarely answered: Is there a numerical threshold below which third-party use cannot establish weakness? The opinion in *Apex Bank* doesn't resolve this, but it makes the question more urgent.

The Board cited *Jack Wolfskin* for the proposition that "voluminous evidence" of third-party registration and use can demonstrate weakness, noting that the court there highlighted fourteen "notable examples." In *Juice Generation*, the Federal Circuit referred to twenty-six third-party marks as "a considerable number." The Board read these cases as establishing a kind of soft minimum—nine marks, in its view, simply wasn't enough.

But neither *Jack Wolfskin* nor *Juice Generation* announced a numerical floor. In both cases, the Federal Circuit's language was descriptive rather than prescriptive. The court in *Jack Wolfskin* emphasized the "voluminous" nature of the evidence, which included not just registrations but also extensive marketplace use, before concluding that the mark at issue was weak. In *Juice Generation*, the twenty-six marks were part of a broader evidentiary record showing widespread third-party use of the term "HUNGRY" in restaurant services. The court's characterization of that number as "considerable" was a contextual judgment, not a rule.

This raises a critical analytical question. If fourteen marks sufficed in *Jack Wolfskin* and twenty-six in *Juice Generation*, but nine did not in *Apex Bank*, where exactly is the line? The honest answer is that there isn't one—and that's a problem. Trademark practitioners advising clients on whether to invest in prosecuting a mark, or whether to oppose a competitor's application, need some predictable standard for evaluating the strength of a crowded-field argument. The current jurisprudence offers only a fuzzy sense that somewhere between nine and fourteen third-party uses might cross an invisible threshold, with no guidance on what contextual variables matter beyond raw numbers.

The Federal Circuit has consistently framed the inquiry as context-dependent, and that framing is defensible. The number of marks needed to demonstrate weakness should plausibly vary with factors like the prominence of the third-party uses, the degree of similarity to the challenged mark, the nature of the goods or services, and the geographic scope of the use. Nine instances of widespread, prominent use by major financial institutions might be more probative of weakness than twenty-six obscure registrations that never translated into marketplace reality. But the court has never articulated which contextual variables matter or how they interact with the raw count. Without that framework, the quantum question remains effectively unanswerable ex ante.

## The Doctrinal Middle Ground

Perhaps the most puzzling aspect of the Board's decision was its treatment of the mark's commercial strength. The Board acknowledged that CC Serve "has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength." At the same time, it concluded that Apex had not shown the mark to be "commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection." Having found neither strength nor weakness, the Board placed the ASPIRE mark in a doctrinal limbo: entitled to the "normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks."

This is a familiar move by the Board, but it deserves closer scrutiny. The premise behind the sixth DuPont factor is that marketplace evidence of third-party use is relevant precisely because it bears on the commercial strength of the mark. When the Board concedes that the senior user has failed to demonstrate commercial strength but simultaneously refuses to find weakness based on third-party use, it effectively shields the mark from the evidentiary consequences of its own concession. The mark gets the benefit of inherent distinctiveness without any discount for the marketplace reality that consumers may already encounter multiple "Aspire" brands in financial services.

This isn't necessarily wrong as a matter of doctrine—inherently distinctive marks do start with a presumption of normal protection, and the burden of demonstrating weakness falls on the junior user. But it creates a practical asymmetry. The senior user can prevail by pointing to the inherent distinctiveness of its mark and the similarity of the parties' services without ever proving that consumers actually associate the mark with a single source. Meanwhile, the junior user must clear an evidentiary bar whose height is undefined. The result is that marks in moderately crowded fields—neither ubiquitous nor unique—receive protection that may exceed what consumer perception actually supports.

## The Remand's Potential to Reshape the Calculus

The Federal Circuit's most consequential holding in *Apex Bank* was its determination that the Board erred in restricting the sixth-factor analysis to credit card services. The court's reasoning was elegantly simple: the Board had already found, under the second DuPont factor, that the parties' services were "highly similar" and even "legally identical, in part." Having made that factual finding, the Board could not then impose a more stringent similarity standard under the sixth factor by limiting the universe of relevant third-party marks to credit card services alone. As the court put it, "we see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor."

This holding is doctrinally significant because it ties the scope of the sixth-factor inquiry to the Board's own findings on the second factor. Going forward, the Board cannot narrowly cabin third-party use evidence to the precise category of the senior user's services when it has already found broader similarity. The consistency principle the Federal Circuit articulates here may well push the Board toward more expansive sixth-factor analyses in cases involving related but not identical services—which describes a substantial portion of Section 2(d) oppositions.

On remand, the Board must now consider the additional forty-two third-party marks using "Aspire" in the broader financial services industry that it previously excluded. If the Board counts those marks, the total universe of relevant third-party uses could exceed fifty—well above the thresholds cited in *Jack Wolfskin* and *Juice Generation*. That doesn't automatically mean the Board will find the mark weak; the contextual variables still matter. But it means the Board can no longer avoid the quantum question by defining the relevant field so narrowly that the number stays small.

## Implications for Practitioners and the Board

The remand thus sets up a potentially significant doctrinal moment. If the Board, considering the expanded universe of marks, still finds ASPIRE entitled to normal protection, it will need to explain why fifty-plus third-party uses are insufficient to demonstrate weakness—or articulate what contextual variables outweigh the raw count. That explanation could provide the first real guidance on the quantum question since *Jack Wolfskin* and *Juice Generation*. Conversely, if the Board finds the mark weak, it will signal that the Federal Circuit's consistency principle meaningfully expands the evidentiary toolkit for junior users in oppositions involving related services.

Either way, the case underscores a broader point. The sixth DuPont factor is supposed to be about marketplace reality—whether consumers have been educated to distinguish similar marks. But the Board's analysis often reduces to a counting exercise conducted within artificially drawn categorical boundaries. The Federal Circuit's insistence on consistency between the second and sixth factors is a step toward grounding the analysis in how consumers actually encounter marks in the marketplace, rather than in how trademark classification systems organize them.

For practitioners, the takeaways are concrete. First, when assembling third-party use evidence, think broadly about the range of services the Board has found or might find similar under the second factor—the same evidence may become admissible under the sixth. Second, don't assume that single-digit numbers of third-party marks are categorically insufficient; the Board will need to explain its reasoning, and the absence of a clear threshold means creative contextual arguments have room to operate. And third, the doctrinal middle ground—where a mark is neither proven strong nor proven weak—is increasingly unstable. The Federal Circuit's decision suggests an implicit expectation that the Board will follow the evidence wherever it leads, rather than defaulting to normal protection when both parties fall short of their respective burdens.

The quantum question remains open. But *Apex Bank* has ensured that on remand, the Board will have to face it with a fuller evidentiary record and a more coherent analytical framework. Whether that produces a clearer standard or merely a more elaborate version of the same indeterminacy remains to be seen.
