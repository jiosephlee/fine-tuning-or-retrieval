Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Why does the Federal Circuit have jurisdiction over a TTAB opposition appeal instead of the district court?
Question:
I'm reading Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., and the court says it has jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B) to review a Trademark Trial and Appeal Board decision. I thought trademark disputes typically go to federal district court. Can someone explain why TTAB opposition decisions are appealed directly to the Federal Circuit rather than a district court, and what types of TTAB decisions fall under this jurisdictional statute?
Answer:
The Federal Circuit has jurisdiction here because 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B) grants it exclusive jurisdiction over appeals from TTAB decisions "with respect to applications for registration of trademarks." An opposition is precisely that — a challenge to a pending application for registration.

In this case, Apex filed intent-to-use applications with the PTO to register the ASPIRE BANK marks. CC Serve then initiated an opposition before the TTAB, arguing that Apex's marks were confusingly similar to CC Serve's registered ASPIRE mark. The Board sustained the opposition under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, effectively refusing to register Apex's marks. When Apex appealed, the Federal Circuit had jurisdiction because the Board's decision was one "with respect to applications for registration" — Apex's pending applications were the subject of the proceeding.

Your confusion likely stems from conflating two distinct categories of trademark disputes:

1. **TTAB administrative proceedings** — These are proceedings within the PTO about whether a mark should be registered. An opposition is one type: a third party challenges an applicant's pending application before it matures into a registration. The TTAB's decision is an administrative determination about registrability.

2. **Trademark infringement litigation** — These are civil actions in federal district court where a trademark owner alleges that another party's use of a mark infringes the owner's rights. This is what people typically mean when they say trademark disputes go to district court.

The opinion involves the first category. CC Serve did not sue Apex for trademark infringement. It filed an opposition with the TTAB — an administrative proceeding to block registration of Apex's marks. The appeal is from an agency decision about registrability, not from a district court judgment about infringement.

As for what types of TTAB decisions fall under § 1295(a)(4)(B), the opinion establishes that at least oppositions qualify. The statutory basis the opinion cites — decisions "with respect to applications for registration of trademarks" — encompasses TTAB proceedings where the subject is an application for registration. An opposition fits this description because it challenges a pending application, as the facts here demonstrate: CC Serve opposed Apex's "intent-to-use applications" to register the ASPIRE BANK marks, and the Board's decision refusing those applications falls squarely within the Federal Circuit's jurisdiction.

### Does the TTAB need to analyze all 13 DuPont factors in a likelihood-of-confusion determination?
Question:
In Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., the court quotes M2 Software for the proposition that the Board need not consider every DuPont factor, only those 'that are relevant and of record.' But the opinion also says likelihood of confusion is a question of law based on underlying factual findings under the DuPont factors. If the Board can skip factors, how does an appellant know which factors will be considered? And does the Board have an obligation to explain why certain factors are irrelevant, or can it just silently omit them? In this case, the court only discusses factors 1, 2, and 6—does that mean the Board only considered those three, or did it consider others that weren't challenged on appeal?
Answer:
The short answer is no—the Board does not need to analyze all thirteen DuPont factors. But the fact that the Federal Circuit's opinion only discusses three factors tells you more about the scope of appellate review than about what the Board did below.

**The Board's obligation: only relevant factors of record**

The opinion quotes the established standard from *M2 Software*: "The Board need not consider every DuPont factor, only those 'that are relevant and of record.'" This is not a license to silently skip factors at will. The standard implicitly requires the Board to make a judgment about which factors are relevant to the particular case before it and which have evidence in the record supporting them. A factor with no record evidence—say, simultaneous use of a mark (factor 12) where no such use is alleged—need not be belabored. But the Board's job is to conduct the likelihood-of-confusion analysis using the factors that matter given the facts and arguments presented.

**Why the opinion only discusses three factors**

The opinion tells you directly why only factors 1, 2, and 6 appear: "On appeal, Apex argues that the Board erred in its likelihood-of-confusion analysis, specifically with respect to its analysis of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors." The Federal Circuit's review is responsive to the issues the parties raise. As the court states, quoting *Bose*, "Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence **when called into question on appeal**."

So the court discusses only those three because those are the ones Apex challenged. The court's silence on the other factors does not mean the Board never considered them—only that they were not "called into question." The Board's actual decision appears at J.A. 24–63, and the opinion references the Board's analysis of factors 1, 2, and 6 with specific record citations, suggesting the Board did address at least those three. Whether the Board addressed additional factors below is not something the opinion reveals, because the court had no reason to discuss unchallenged findings.

**Does the Board need to explain why it skips a factor?**

The opinion doesn't directly address a duty to explain omissions. But the *M2 Software* standard—"relevant and of record"—gives you the practical answer. If a party puts a factor in dispute by arguing it below or introducing evidence on it, the Board must address it. If neither party raises a factor and no evidence in the record bears on it, the Board's failure to discuss it is generally not reversible error. The risk for the Board is in being too narrow in what it treats as relevant, which is exactly what happened here: the court found the Board erred in limiting its sixth-factor analysis to credit card services only, when the Board had already found the parties' services "highly similar" under factor 2. The lesson is that the Board's relevance determination is itself subject to review, and an unduly narrow conception of what's relevant can be a legal error.

**Practical takeaway for an appellant**

You cannot know with certainty before the Board's decision which factors it will find relevant, but you can predict based on the nature of the opposition and the evidence you put in. The more strategic concern this case illustrates is internal consistency: the Board's relevance determinations across factors must be coherent. The Federal Circuit vacated factors 6 and 1 precisely because the Board's narrow sixth-factor analysis was inconsistent with its broad second-factor finding. If you are the appellant, your best approach is to argue the factors the Board actually addressed and, where applicable, challenge the Board's implicit relevance determinations—arguing that a factor it ignored should have been considered given its own findings on the factors it did analyze.

### What does 'legally identical, in part' mean in the DuPont factor analysis for similarity of services?
Question:
In Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., the Board found that Apex's 'banking and financing services' and CC Serve's 'credit card services' are 'legally identical, in part' because dictionary definitions of 'banking' and 'finance' encompass extending credit through credit cards. I'm confused about what 'legally identical, in part' means as a legal standard. Does it mean the services overlap but aren't fully identical? And how does this relate to the test from Coach Services that products need only be 'related in some manner'? Is 'legally identical' a higher degree of similarity than 'related,' and does it automatically trigger a finding that the factor weighs in favor of confusion, or is it just one piece of evidence?
Answer:
---

**Short Answer**

"Legally identical, in part" is a factual finding that the parties' services share a common legal scope—here, because the dictionary definitions of "banking" and "finance" encompass the extension of credit through credit cards. It is a higher degree of similarity than merely being "related in some manner" under *Coach Services*, but it does not automatically resolve the DuPont factor; it is evidence that the Board weighs alongside other evidence to determine whether the factor favors confusion.

---

**Explanation**

**1. What "legally identical, in part" means**

The Board used "legally identical, in part" to describe a specific factual finding: that a portion of Apex's identified services—"[b]anking and financing services"—legally encompasses what CC Serve does—issuing credit cards to finance purchases. The Board reached this conclusion by consulting dictionary definitions of "banking," "bank," and "finance," each of which includes extending credit or providing funds through credit cards. Because the *legal scope* of Apex's services overlaps with CC Serve's services in this respect, the services are "legally identical" as to that overlapping part, even though Apex offers a broader array of services (checking accounts, mortgages, loans) and does not itself offer credit cards.

So your intuition is essentially correct: the services overlap but are not fully identical. The phrase captures partial overlap at the level of legal definition rather than complete identity of all services offered.

**2. Relationship to the *Coach Services* "related in some manner" standard**

The *Coach Services* formulation—that products need only be "related in some manner"—sets the threshold for the second DuPont factor: services need not be identical or directly competitive. The Board's finding of "legally identical, in part" is a stronger conclusion than "related." It means that a subset of the services is not merely related but is legally the *same* service. The Federal Circuit affirmed this finding, noting that "substantial evidence supports the Board's finding that the parties' services are highly similar."

So yes, "legally identical" represents a higher degree of similarity than "related." The *Coach Services* standard is the floor; "legally identical, in part" is a finding well above that floor.

**3. Whether it automatically triggers a finding in favor of confusion**

No. The Board did not treat "legally identical, in part" as dispositive. It considered this finding alongside other evidence—specifically, third-party registrations that cover both credit card services and banking/financing services—to support its conclusion that "the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark." Only after weighing this combined evidence did the Board conclude that the second factor "weighed heavily in favor of finding likelihood of confusion."

The "legally identical, in part" finding is thus one important piece of the puzzle, not an automatic trigger. It strengthens the case for confusion, but the Board still needed to assess the totality of the evidence under the factor before reaching its conclusion.

**4. Why the distinction matters on appeal**

The Federal Circuit's disposition of the sixth DuPont factor illustrates why the scope of "similarity" matters. The Board found the services "highly similar—in fact, partially legally identical" under the second factor, but then applied a narrower definition of similarity under the sixth factor, limiting consideration to marks used for credit card services alone. The court held this was error: having already found the services highly similar (and partially legally identical), the Board could not impose "a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity" under the sixth factor. The court required the Board to maintain a consistent scope of similarity across factors on remand.

This confirms that "legally identical, in part" is a factual finding about the degree of overlap that carries forward across the DuPont analysis—it does not automatically resolve any single factor, but it shapes the framework within which each factor is evaluated.

### Why was it inconsistent for the TTAB to use different standards of 'similarity' under DuPont factors 2 and 6?
Question:
In Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., the Federal Circuit vacated the Board's sixth DuPont factor analysis because the Board narrowed the universe of third-party marks to only those for 'credit card services,' excluding marks for other banking and financing services. The court said this was inconsistent with the Board's second factor analysis, where it found the parties' services to be 'highly similar' and even 'legally identical, in part.' I understand the court's reasoning that the Board shouldn't apply a stricter similarity standard under factor 6 than under factor 2, but is the court saying the Board MUST use the exact same scope of similarity across all factors? Aren't the factors asking different questions—factor 2 is about the parties' own services, while factor 6 is about third-party marks on similar goods? Could there be a legitimate reason to define 'similar' differently in each context?
Answer:
## The issue is internal consistency, not identical standards across factors

The Federal Circuit is not requiring the Board to use the "exact same scope of similarity" across all DuPont factors in the abstract. Rather, the court is requiring the Board to remain consistent with **its own factual findings** within a single proceeding. Once the Board determined under factor 2 that credit card services and banking/financing services are "highly similar" and even "legally identical, in part," it could not then, under factor 6, adopt a narrower definition of "similar" that excluded banking and financing services from the universe of relevant third-party marks.

## The factors ask different questions, but "similar goods" means the same thing

You are right that factors 2 and 6 serve different analytical purposes. Factor 2 compares **the parties' own goods or services** to assess whether they are related enough that consumers might assume a common source. Factor 6 looks at **third-party marks on similar goods** to assess whether the field is crowded—which bears on the mark's commercial strength and the scope of protection it deserves.

But the key insight from the opinion is that the concept of "similarity of goods" itself should not shift between the two analyses. The court put it directly:

> When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar—in fact, partially legally identical—in its analysis of the second DuPont factor, the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors. We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor.

The court also cited its precedent confirming that factor 6, like factor 2, "does not require identical goods—only similar ones." *Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy's, Inc.*, 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992). So the legal standard for what counts as "similar" is the same; what differs is what the factor does with that determination.

## Why the Board's approach was problematic

The practical problem was that the Board's inconsistency cut in the same direction—against the applicant—on both factors:

- **Factor 2:** The Board used a broad conception of similarity (banking/financing services are "highly similar" to credit card services) to find a strong likelihood of confusion.
- **Factor 6:** The Board then narrowed the relevant universe to only credit card services, excluding the 42 other Aspire-formative marks used in connection with other banking and financing services. This narrowed the evidence of third-party usage, making CC Serve's mark appear stronger than it might be if the broader field were considered.

This created a double standard: a broad definition of similarity to support confusion, and a narrow one to limit evidence of weakness. The court's concern is not that the factors must always yield the same outcome, but that the Board cannot make a factual finding about the relationship between service categories under one factor and then effectively contradict that finding under another.

## Could there be a legitimate reason to define similarity differently?

The court does not foreclose the possibility that the relevant universe could be defined differently in some cases—it is not announcing a rigid rule that every factor must apply identical parameters. The opinion is specifically tied to the Board's **factual finding** under factor 2. Had the Board found the services only modestly similar, or had it defined the relevant market more carefully under factor 2, it might have had more latitude under factor 6. The problem was not that the Board defined "similar" differently in the abstract; the problem was that the Board's factor 6 definition was inconsistent with its own factor 2 finding.

In short: the court requires consistency with prior factual determinations, not uniformity across factors as a matter of law. The Board cannot have it both ways.

### How does the sixth DuPont factor (third-party marks) affect the first DuPont factor (commercial impression)?
Question:
In Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., the court remanded the first DuPont factor analysis solely because reconsideration of the sixth factor 'may result in a different determination of the mark's commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression.' I don't understand the doctrinal link between these two factors. The first factor is about similarity of the marks themselves (appearance, sound, connotation), while the sixth is about how many similar marks exist in the marketplace. How does the number of third-party marks change the 'commercial impression' of CC Serve's ASPIRE mark? Is the court saying that if there are many ASPIRE marks in financial services, consumers will perceive CC Serve's mark as weaker, and that weaker impression affects the first factor analysis? Is commercial strength part of the first factor, or is it exclusively a sixth factor consideration?
Answer:
---

Your understanding is essentially correct. The court is describing an interdependency between the two factors: the sixth factor's assessment of commercial strength (or weakness) feeds into the first factor's "commercial impression" element.

**The doctrinal link is "commercial strength."**

The first DuPont factor asks whether the marks are similar "as to appearance, sound, connotation and **commercial impression**." The sixth factor examines "[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods," which serves as evidence of whether a mark is commercially strong or weak. As the court explains, evidence of third-party use "is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection," and when a field is crowded with similar marks, "customers will be more adept at distinguishing marks from each other and are less likely to be confused."

The connection is this: a mark's commercial strength—as determined under the sixth factor—shapes its **overall commercial impression** under the first factor. A mark that consumers encounter frequently across multiple sources in the same industry leaves a different (weaker) impression than one that stands alone. Consumers who have been "educated to distinguish between marks" will perceive any single ASPIRE mark differently if there are forty-two similar marks in the financial services industry versus nine.

So the court's logic on remand is straightforward:

1. The Board erred by too narrowly limiting the universe of third-party marks under the sixth factor (only credit card services, excluding broader banking/financing).
2. On remand, the Board must reconsider the sixth factor with a broader set of similar marks.
3. That reconsideration "may result in a different determination of the mark's commercial strength or weakness."
4. Because commercial strength affects "the overall commercial impression," and commercial impression is an element of the first factor, the first factor must also be reconsidered.

**Is commercial strength part of the first factor or exclusively a sixth factor consideration?**

It is assessed primarily under the sixth factor, but it is not *exclusively* a sixth factor consideration. The court's remand makes clear that commercial strength is an input into the first factor's commercial impression analysis. The first factor does not ask about commercial strength in isolation—it asks about the "overall commercial impression" of the marks, which is informed by the marketplace reality that the sixth factor captures. The two factors are analytically distinct but not hermetically sealed from one another.

The Board here had found that CC Serve's mark was neither particularly strong nor weak and was entitled to the "normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks." If, on remand, the broader universe of third-party ASPIRE marks shows the mark to be commercially weak, that determination would narrow the mark's commercial impression and, correspondingly, the scope of protection under the first factor. That is why the court could not simply affirm the first factor while vacating the sixth—the first factor's conclusion may depend on the outcome of the sixth.

### How many third-party uses are needed to show a mark is 'weak' under the sixth DuPont factor?
Question:
In Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., the Board found that nine third-party ASPIRE-formative marks for credit card services did not rise to the level of 'considerable' or 'ubiquitous' use needed to demonstrate weakness. The court cited Jack Wolfskin (14 'notable examples' with 'voluminous evidence') and Juice Generation (26 marks as 'a considerable number'). Is there a minimum threshold number of third-party marks needed to establish weakness? Does the quality or notoriety of the third-party uses matter, or is it purely a numbers game? And if Apex introduces the additional 42 marks on remand, is there a number that would be dispositive, or does the Board still have discretion to weigh the evidence?
Answer:
## Answer

**There is no fixed numerical threshold, and the inquiry is not purely a numbers game.** The sixth DuPont factor expressly considers both "the number *and nature* of similar marks in use on similar goods," meaning the Board evaluates the quality and prominence of the third-party uses alongside the raw count. *DuPont*, 476 F.2d at 1361. The cases the Board cited are illustrative, not prescriptive—*Jack Wolfskin* referenced fourteen "notable examples" supported by "voluminous evidence," and *Juice Generation* described twenty-six marks as "a considerable number"—but neither case establishes a minimum count that must be exceeded. The Federal Circuit has not set such a line, and the *Apex* opinion does not purport to either.

The court's language throughout points to a qualitative assessment. Terms like "considerable" and "ubiquitous" describe the *overall evidentiary picture*, not a bright-line rule. The court's discussion of *Jack Wolfskin* emphasizes not just the number of examples but that they were "notable" and backed by "voluminous evidence," suggesting that the prominence and documentation of the third-party uses matter. The underlying theory of the sixth factor supports this: third-party use is relevant because it shows that "consumers have been educated to distinguish between marks," *Jack Wolfskin*, 797 F.3d at 1374, and that education effect depends on whether consumers actually encounter the third-party marks in the marketplace—not merely on whether the marks exist on a list.

**The Board retains discretion to weigh the evidence, and no number is automatically dispositive.** The Federal Circuit in *Apex* did not say that if Apex introduces enough marks, the Board must find weakness. The court vacated the sixth-factor analysis not because nine marks were too few as a matter of law, but because the Board applied an unduly narrow standard of similarity when deciding *which* marks to count. The Board had already found the parties' services "highly similar" and "partially legally identical" under the second DuPont factor, but then restricted its sixth-factor analysis to marks used only for credit card services—excluding marks for other banking and financing services. The Federal Circuit held this was legally inconsistent: the sixth factor requires only "similar" goods, not identical ones, and the Board "should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors."

So on remand, the Board must broaden the universe of eligible third-party marks to include those used on services similar to the parties' services—not just credit card services narrowly defined. The additional forty-two marks Apex identified may enter the analysis if they meet this similarity standard. But the Board still must assess their "number *and nature*" and determine whether they collectively rise to the level of "considerable" or "ubiquitous" use demonstrating that the mark is weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection. The Board retains its role as fact-finder in weighing this evidence, subject to substantial-evidence review on appeal.

In short: there is no magic number, quality and notoriety matter, and the Board exercises informed discretion—constrained by the requirement that it apply a consistent standard of similarity across the DuPont factors.

### What is a 'letter of protest' in trademark prosecution and how does it differ from a formal opposition?
Question:
In Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., CC Serve submitted a 'letter of protest' to the USPTO during prosecution of Apex's applications, but the examining attorney still approved the marks for publication. CC Serve then filed a formal opposition. I'm confused about the procedural distinction. What is a letter of protest—who can submit one, what is the examining attorney's obligation to consider it, and what happens if it's ignored? How does it differ from an opposition proceeding before the TTAB? Is a letter of protest a prerequisite to filing an opposition, or could CC Serve have skipped it?
Answer:
In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the Federal Circuit reviews a procedural timeline that perfectly illustrates the distinction between a letter of protest and a formal opposition. 

Here is the breakdown of the two mechanisms:

### What is a Letter of Protest and Who Can Submit One?
A letter of protest is an informal procedure that allows a third party to bring evidence to the attention of the USPTO examining attorney during the prosecution of a trademark application. Any third party who believes they would be damaged by the registration of a pending mark can submit one. In this case, CC Serve, as the owner of the existing ASPIRE mark, submitted a letter of protest to assert that Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK marks were confusingly similar to its own.

### The Examining Attorney's Obligation and Consequences of Ignoring It
A letter of protest is advisory. The examining attorney has the discretion to consider the submitted evidence, but is not a formal party to a dispute and is not obligated to refuse the application based on the letter. As the opinion notes, CC Serve submitted its letter of protest, but "Nonetheless, the examining attorney approved the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for publication." 

If the examining attorney ignores the letter or finds it unpersuasive, the application simply proceeds to the next stage of prosecution: publication for opposition. The third party's recourse is then to file a formal opposition.

### How Does it Differ from a Formal Opposition?
The primary differences lie in timing, forum, and procedural formality:

1. **Timing**: A letter of protest is submitted *during prosecution* (before a mark is approved). An opposition is filed *after* the examining attorney has approved the mark for publication. In *Apex Bank*, the marks published on December 17, 2019, and CC Serve initiated its formal opposition in February 2020.
2. **Forum**: A letter of protest is reviewed by the examining attorney assigned to the application. A formal opposition is an adversarial proceeding before the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB).
3. **Procedure**: A letter of protest is an *ex parte* submission; the third party simply provides evidence to the USPTO and does not participate further in the examination. An opposition is an *inter partes* proceeding where both sides litigate the issue. In this case, the TTAB conducted a full likelihood-of-confusion analysis applying the thirteen *DuPont* factors, resulting in a formal Board decision that was then appealed to the Federal Circuit. 

### Is a Letter of Protest a Prerequisite?
No. A letter of protest is an optional, early-step tactic, not a prerequisite to filing an opposition. 

A third party can choose to skip the letter of protest entirely, wait for the examining attorney to approve the mark for publication, and then file an opposition with the TTAB. Here, CC Serve attempted to halt the prosecution early via the letter of protest. When that informal attempt failed, CC Serve pursued its formal statutory right to oppose the mark before the TTAB. The opinion treats the formal opposition as the operative proceeding that resulted in the Board's refusal, entirely independent of the earlier letter of protest.

### What is the practical effect of 'affirm-in-part, vacate-in-part, and remand' on the trademark application?
Question:
In Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., the court affirmed the second DuPont factor finding but vacated the first and sixth factor findings, remanding for the Board to reconsider. I'm trying to understand what happens procedurally. Does Apex's application remain suspended during remand? If the Board reconsiders factors 1 and 6 but reaches the same ultimate conclusion (likelihood of confusion exists), can Apex appeal again? Could the Board's reconsideration of the sixth factor lead to a finding that CC Serve's mark is weak enough that there's no likelihood of confusion, thus allowing Apex's registration? Also, the court awarded costs to appellant—does that mean Apex 'won' even though the Board's ultimate ruling was mostly affirmed?
Answer:
Your question touches on several important aspects of administrative review and TTAB procedure following a partial remand from the Federal Circuit.

### Does the application remain suspended during remand?

Procedurally, yes, the application cannot proceed to registration while the remand is pending. The Board's original decision sustaining the opposition and refusing registration has been vacated in part. The Federal Circuit instructed the Board to "remand for the Board to consider the number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods and the appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression of the marks." Because the Board must issue a new decision after reconsidering DuPont factors 1 and 6, the opposition remains unresolved. The application is effectively held in abeyance until the Board completes its reconsideration and issues a new final order.

### Can Apex appeal again if the Board reaches the same ultimate conclusion?

Yes. The remand requires the Board to conduct a new analysis of the first and sixth DuPont factors. If, after reconsideration, the Board issues a new decision sustaining the opposition and refusing registration, that constitutes a new final decision of the Board. The opinion notes that the Federal Circuit has jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B), which grants the court jurisdiction over appeals from final decisions of the Board. If Apex is dissatisfied with the Board's new findings on remand—whether regarding the strength of the mark or the ultimate likelihood of confusion—it may appeal that new final decision.

### Could reconsideration of the sixth factor lead to a finding of no likelihood of confusion?

Yes, that is a possible outcome. The sixth DuPont factor considers "the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." As the court explained, "Evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection." The court vacated the Board's sixth factor finding because the Board improperly limited its review to only marks related to credit card services, excluding 42 other Aspire-formative marks used in the broader financial services industry.

The court noted that "when a field is crowded with similar marks... customers will be more adept at distinguishing marks from each other and are less likely to be confused." By expanding the universe of third-party marks on remand, the Board could find that CC Serve's ASPIRE mark is commercially weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection. Furthermore, the court explicitly linked the sixth factor to the first, stating that reconsideration of the mark's strength "may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression." If the Board finds the mark is weak and the commercial impression of the parties' marks is different, it could conclude there is no likelihood of confusion, which would result in dismissal of the opposition and allow Apex's registration to proceed.

### Did Apex "win" if it was awarded costs?

Yes, in the context of appellate litigation, obtaining a vacatur and remand constitutes a meaningful "win" for purposes of costs, even though the Board's ultimate ruling on the second factor was affirmed. The court affirmed the Board's finding on the second DuPont factor (similarity of the services) against Apex, but Apex successfully challenged the Board's analysis on two of the three factors it appealed, resulting in a remand for further proceedings.

Under Federal Circuit rules, costs are generally awarded to the prevailing party. In mixed results like this—affirm-in-part, vacate-in-part, and remand—the court has discretion to award costs. By awarding costs to Apex as the appellant, the court acknowledged that Apex prevailed on the most significant issues raised on appeal, specifically by demonstrating that the Board committed legal error in its analysis of the first and sixth DuPont factors. While Apex did not secure a complete reversal of the refusal, it succeeded in forcing the Board to reconsider its ruling, which is a significant procedural victory.
