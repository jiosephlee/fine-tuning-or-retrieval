Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

Chapter 4: # The Sixth DuPont Factor: Number and Nature of Similar Marks in Use on Similar Goods

## The Legal Purpose of the Sixth DuPont Factor

The sixth DuPont factor considers "[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." This factor serves a specific and important function within the likelihood-of-confusion analysis: it helps determine the strength of the senior user's mark. As the Federal Circuit explained, quoting its prior precedent in *Palm Bay Imports, Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin*, "Evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection." The logic is straightforward. When a marketplace is crowded with similar marks used on similar goods, consumers become accustomed to encountering those marks and learn to distinguish among them. As the court noted, citing *Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enterprises LLC*, "customers will be more adept at distinguishing marks from each other and are less likely to be confused by similar marks." This consumer education effect, in turn, tends to indicate that the senior user's mark lacks commercial strength. See *Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U.* The practical consequence is significant: a weak mark is entitled to a narrower scope of protection, making it less likely that a junior user's similar mark would cause consumer confusion. The sixth factor thus operates as a tool that a junior user like Apex Bank can deploy to argue that the senior user's mark deserves only limited protection.

## Apex's Evidence of Third-Party ASPIRE Marks

Before the Board, Apex submitted several exhibits demonstrating third-party uses of marks that included the word "Aspire." These marks were used in connection with credit card-related services and, more broadly, across the financial services industry. The breadth of this evidence mattered because the scope of third-party usage bears directly on whether the senior mark is weak. Apex's strategy was to show that the ASPIRE mark was not unique to CC Serve but was instead one of many similar marks in the financial services marketplace. If Apex could demonstrate that consumers of financial services already encountered numerous ASPIRE-formative marks from different sources, it could argue that consumers had been educated to distinguish among them and would not likely confuse Apex Bank's ASPIRE BANK marks with CC Serve's ASPIRE mark. The number and nature of these third-party marks thus formed the evidentiary foundation of Apex's argument that the sixth DuPont factor should weigh against finding a likelihood of confusion.

## The Board's Restriction to Credit Card Services

The Board's analysis of the sixth factor began with a critical threshold decision: defining the relevant public and the universe of similar marks it would consider. The Board concluded that, because of the overlap between Apex and CC Serve's services, the properly defined relevant public was "consumers of 'credit card services.'" This definitional choice had dramatic consequences. The Board then focused solely on marks identified for credit card services and deemed marks using ASPIRE for other services to be "essentially irrelevant." In effect, the Board excluded from consideration the broader category of banking and financing marks—precisely the category of services that Apex sought to register its marks for. On appeal, Apex argued that the Board erred in limiting the relevant public to consumers of credit card services and that the Board should have considered the 42 other third-party marks that used ASPIRE and ASPIRE-formative marks across the financial services industry. This exclusion became the central legal issue on appeal.

## The Board's Finding on "Considerable" or "Ubiquitous" Use

Even within its narrowed universe of credit card services, the Board found only nine ASPIRE-formative marks. The Board then determined that this number did not rise to the level of "considerable" or "ubiquitous" use sufficient to demonstrate that CC Serve's mark was weak. The court's opinion provides context for this finding by reference to prior cases. In *Jack Wolfskin*, the court had highlighted fourteen "notable examples of third-party registration and use" of paw print design elements, referring to "voluminous evidence." In *Juice Generation*, the court had referred to 26 third-party marks as "a considerable number." Against those benchmarks, nine marks did not satisfy the Board's standard. The Board ultimately concluded that while CC Serve had not shown its mark to have any particular commercial strength, Apex had also not shown CC Serve's mark to be commercially or conceptually weak such that it would be entitled to only a narrow scope of protection. The Board determined that CC Serve's mark was entitled to the "normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks." This finding meant that the sixth DuPont factor did not weigh in Apex's favor.

## The Federal Circuit's Legal Error Analysis

The Federal Circuit agreed with Apex that the Board's analysis was legally flawed. The court's reasoning centered on a critical inconsistency in how the Board defined "similarity" across different DuPont factors. The sixth DuPont factor requires the Board to consider similar marks for *similar* goods and services—not identical ones. The court cited *Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy's, Inc.* and *Juice Generation* for the established principle that the sixth factor does not require identical goods, only similar ones. The problem was that the Board had already made a factual finding, in its analysis of the second DuPont factor, that the parties' services were highly similar—in fact, partially "legally identical." The Board reached this conclusion by determining that dictionary definitions for "banking," "bank," and "finance" encompass extending credit through the issuance of credit cards, and by pointing to third-party registrations covering both credit card and banking and financing services. Yet when the Board turned to the sixth factor, it abruptly narrowed the universe of relevant marks to only those for credit card services, excluding marks for other banking and financing services. The Federal Circuit found this inconsistency to be a legal error. The court stated plainly: "We find the Board's definition of similarity to be too narrow."

## The Consistency Principle Across DuPont Factors

The Federal Circuit's ruling articulates an important principle for likelihood-of-confusion analysis: the Board must apply a consistent standard of similarity across DuPont factors. The court framed the issue with precision: "When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar—in fact, partially legally identical—in its analysis of the second DuPont factor, the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors." The court saw "no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor." This principle flows from the structure of the DuPont analysis itself. The factors are interrelated and together inform the ultimate question of likelihood of confusion. If the Board determines that credit card services and banking and financing services are highly similar for purposes of the second factor—weighing that factor heavily in favor of finding confusion—it cannot then adopt a more restrictive definition of similarity under the sixth factor that excludes banking and financing marks from the universe of relevant third-party uses. Such an approach would artificially insulate the senior mark from evidence of weakness. The consistency principle ensures that the Board's analysis of mark strength under the sixth factor reflects the same understanding of marketplace realities that informed its analysis of service similarity under the second factor.

## Vacation and Remand

Having identified the legal error, the Federal Circuit vacated the Board's finding with respect to the sixth DuPont factor and remanded for reconsideration. The court directed the Board to reconsider "the appropriate scope of third-party marks eligible for consideration in view of the Board's factual finding that the parties' services are highly similar." This remand did not pre-judge the outcome; the court did not hold that the broader universe of marks would necessarily demonstrate that CC Serve's mark is weak. Rather, the court required only that the Board apply the correct legal standard—considering similar marks on similar services, using the same definition of similarity it had already applied under the second factor. The court also vacated the Board's analysis of the first DuPont factor, which addresses the similarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. Because commercial impression is informed by the mark's commercial strength, the court reasoned that reconsideration of the sixth factor "may result in a different determination of the mark's commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression." The interdependence of the factors thus required a fresh analysis of the first factor as well. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board's analysis of the second DuPont factor, where it found no legal error and concluded that substantial evidence supported the Board's finding of high service similarity. But the inconsistency in the Board's treatment of similarity across factors led the court to vacate and remand on factors one and six.
