Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Case Background, Charges, and Procedural Posture

## The November 19-20, 2020 Incident

The facts underlying Justin Cutbank's conviction began on the night of November 19 and early morning of November 20, 2020. After consuming alcohol and methamphetamine, Cutbank awakened his girlfriend, D.F., pointed a gun at her face, and forced her into a closet. Once there, he made paranoid accusations, claiming that "listening devices" had been placed in the home and that people outside were waiting to harm him. He held D.F. in the closet at gunpoint for a period she later testified felt like hours. During this ordeal, Cutbank repeatedly threatened D.F.'s life and struck her across the face with the gun. At some point, he ordered D.F. to wake others sleeping in the house, including her two teenage sons and her housemate, C.F.

For a law student reading this opinion, these facts are not merely background narrative—they are the foundation for both the conviction and the sentencing enhancement that Cutbank challenges on appeal. The use of a firearm during a domestic incident, the physical abuse, and the paranoid behavior all become relevant at multiple stages of the case: at trial to prove possession of the firearm, and at sentencing to support the four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm "in connection with another felony offense." Understanding what happened that night is therefore essential to evaluating the legal issues the Eighth Circuit addresses.

## Cutbank's Flight and D.F.'s Call to Police

Cutbank eventually fled the residence, taking with him both D.F.'s cell phone and the gun. Once he left, D.F. called the police. This flight is significant for several reasons. First, it established that D.F. was able to identify the weapon and describe the events to law enforcement, which formed the basis for the investigation. Second, Cutbank's departure with the gun meant that the firearm was not recovered at D.F.'s home, but rather had to be located along the path he traveled—a fact that becomes important when considering the physical evidence the police later recovered. Third, the fact that Cutbank took D.F.'s cell phone suggests an attempt to prevent her from calling for help, which bears on the dangerousness of his conduct and the seriousness of the offense.

## Police Response, Barricading, and Resistance

While responding to D.F.'s call, police also received a separate call from a residence approximately a half mile from D.F.'s home. The caller reported that a man was in the garage and refused to leave. When officers arrived, Cutbank barricaded himself inside. Over the course of several hours, he made erratic statements and claimed to have a weapon. When officers eventually attempted to detain him, Cutbank resisted violently—he bit an officer in the face and neck and grabbed the officer's gun.

This portion of the encounter is procedurally important because it gave rise to additional facts that the district court relied upon at sentencing. The PSR recommended a two-level sentencing enhancement because Cutbank "recklessly created a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing from police." Although Cutbank does not specifically challenge that enhancement on appeal, the facts of his resistance contextualize the overall dangerousness assessment that informed his 292-month sentence. The barricading and resistance also explain why police were searching the area between D.F.'s home and the garage, which led to the recovery of critical physical evidence.

## Recovery of Physical Evidence

Officers searched the path Cutbank likely traveled between D.F.'s residence and the garage where he barricaded himself. Along that route, they recovered three items: D.F.'s cell phone, a butcher-style knife, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with a sawed-off barrel and stock. The location of these items along the travel route connected Cutbank's conduct at D.F.'s home to his later presence at the garage and helped establish the chain of events.

The recovery of the sawed-off rifle is particularly significant. Under the sentencing guidelines, a sawed-off firearm is treated as a distinct category of dangerous weapon. The PSR recommended a base offense level of 26 in part because the offense involved a sawed-off firearm described in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), which is the statutory provision defining regulated firearms under the National Firearms Act. The sawed-off nature of the rifle thus elevated the base offense level even before other enhancements were applied. Additionally, the PSR recommended a three-level increase because the firearm had an altered or obliterated serial number—a fact consistent with the weapon having been illegally modified.

## DNA Evidence and Witness Identifications

Forensic testing confirmed that DNA on the recovered rifle matched Cutbank. This physical evidence was critical because it directly linked him to the firearm, which was the central element of the charged offense. The charge under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) requires the government to prove that the defendant, a convicted felon, knowingly possessed a firearm. The DNA evidence provided scientific corroboration that Cutbank had handled the gun.

At trial, D.F. and C.F. both identified the weapon as one they had seen Cutbank with before the night of the incident. D.F. testified that she had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle a "handful of times" previously, and C.F. testified that she had seen the gun "once or twice" before. These identifications were important not only to establish that Cutbank possessed the firearm on the night in question, but also to demonstrate what the government characterized as "continuous possession" of the weapon. The admissibility of this prior-possession testimony became one of the central evidentiary issues on appeal, as Cutbank argued it constituted impermissible propensity evidence under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b).

## Cutbank's Criminal History and the ACCA Designation

Cutbank was not a first-time offender. The opinion describes him as "a felon with an extensive criminal history." At sentencing, the district court adopted—without objection—the PSR's factual statements regarding Cutbank's prior felony convictions. The record reflects that Cutbank had nearly a dozen prior felony convictions, many involving violence and firearms. This criminal history placed him in Criminal History Category VI, the highest category under the federal sentencing guidelines.

The Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), codified at 18 U.S.C. § 924(e), mandates enhanced penalties for defendants convicted under § 922(g)(1) who have three or more prior convictions for violent felonies or serious drug offenses. The ACCA sets a minimum sentence of fifteen years and a maximum of life imprisonment. The guideline counterpart, USSG § 4B1.4, provides a corresponding guideline range for armed career criminals. In this case, the jury found that Cutbank had at least three prior violent felony convictions, triggering the ACCA's enhanced penalties. This finding is distinct from the general verdict of guilt on the possession charge; it required the jury to make a separate determination about the nature and number of Cutbank's prior convictions.

The ACCA designation affected Cutbank's sentencing in a concrete way. The Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have produced a total offense level of 34. However, the guideline calculations including the challenged § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement produced a total offense level of 35. Under USSG § 4B1.4(b), the greater offense level applies. This interaction between the ACCA guideline and the firearms-possession guideline enhancements is central to Cutbank's sentencing argument on appeal.

## The Conviction and the § 924(e) Finding

The jury convicted Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm in violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e). Section 922(g)(1) makes it unlawful for a person convicted of a felony to possess a firearm or ammunition. The government must prove three elements: (1) the defendant was previously convicted of a felony; (2) the defendant knowingly possessed a firearm; and (3) the possession was in or affecting interstate commerce. In this case, the first element was established by Cutbank's prior felony convictions, the second by the DNA evidence and witness identifications linking him to the recovered rifle, and the third by the nature of the firearm as an instrumentality of interstate commerce—a element typically satisfied with minimal proof that the firearm crossed state lines at some point.

The jury also found that Cutbank had three or more prior violent felony convictions, triggering the enhanced penalty provisions of § 924(e). This finding transformed the potential sentence from the standard range for a § 922(g)(1) violation—which carries a maximum of ten years—into the ACCA range with its fifteen-year mandatory minimum. The combined verdict thus established both the offense and the sentencing framework under which Cutbank would be punished.

## The 292-Month Sentence and Issues on Appeal

The PSR calculated a total offense level of 35 and a Criminal History Category of VI, producing an advisory guidelines range of 292 to 365 months imprisonment. The district court sentenced Cutbank to 292 months—the bottom of that range. The sentence fell within the advisory guidelines range, which appellate courts presume to be reasonable.

Cutbank raises two categories of issues on appeal. First, he argues that the district court committed multiple evidentiary and procedural errors that deprived him of a fair trial. These include challenges to the admission of prior-possession testimony under Rule 404(b), the district court's refusal to give a limiting instruction regarding D.F.'s statements to police, the admission of testimony about D.F.'s sister being killed by a gun, and the court's restriction on defense counsel's cross-examination of C.F. Second, Cutbank argues that the district court improperly applied the four-level sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing a firearm in connection with another felony offense. His argument on this point is not that the facts fail to support the enhancement, but rather that the resulting within-guidelines sentence "conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)" that a sentence be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary" to achieve the purposes of sentencing.

These two categories of issues structure the remainder of the opinion. The evidentiary issues require the Eighth Circuit to examine whether the district court abused its discretion in its trial rulings and whether any errors were harmless. The sentencing issue requires the court to address the procedural reasonableness of the guidelines calculation and the substantive reasonableness of the resulting sentence. As the following chapters explain, the Eighth Circuit finds none of Cutbank's arguments persuasive and affirms both the conviction and the sentence.

# Evidentiary Issues I — Rule 404(b) and Prior Possession of the Same Firearm

## The Pretrial Motion to Exclude Prior Possession Testimony Under Rule 404(b)

Before trial, Cutbank filed a motion in limine seeking to prevent D.F. and C.F. from testifying that they had previously observed him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle—the same firearm recovered along the path between D.F.'s home and the garage where Cutbank barricaded himself. Cutbank argued that such testimony would constitute propensity evidence prohibited by Rule 404(b) of the Federal Rules of Evidence. Rule 404(b) embodies a fundamental principle of American evidence law: evidence of a defendant's prior bad acts may not be introduced to prove the defendant's character and to show that the defendant acted in conformity with that character on a given occasion. The rule rests on the concern that a jury, upon learning of a defendant's prior misconduct, may convict not because the government proved the charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt, but because the defendant is a bad person who probably committed this crime too. Cutbank's argument invoked this protective purpose, framing any testimony about his prior possession of the rifle as an impermissible invitation for the jury to infer guilt from his past behavior rather than from evidence of the charged offense.

## The Government's Counterargument: Intrinsic Evidence of Continuous Possession

The government countered that the prior possession testimony was not propensity evidence at all, but rather intrinsic evidence directly relevant to proving an element of the charged offense. This distinction is central to understanding Rule 404(b)'s scope. Evidence is considered "intrinsic" when it is inextricably intertwined with the charged offense or serves to complete the story of the crime. Intrinsic evidence is not subject to Rule 404(b)'s restrictions because it is not being offered to prove character or conformity; it is part of the factual narrative of the offense itself. The government's argument was more specific, however. It contended that testimony showing Cutbank had possessed the same sawed-off rifle on prior occasions helped establish his ownership or control over the weapon recovered by police on the night of November 19–20, 2020. To convict Cutbank of being a felon in possession of a firearm under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1), the government needed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Cutbank knowingly possessed the firearm. Prior possession of the same weapon, the government argued, was circumstantial evidence from which a jury could reasonably infer that Cutbank possessed the rifle on the night in question—that it was his gun, under his control, and not merely a weapon he encountered by chance.

## The District Court's Pretrial Ruling

The district court agreed with the government, ruling before trial that D.F. and C.F. could testify about having previously observed Cutbank with the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle. The court adopted the government's characterization of this evidence as intrinsic to the possession charge and relevant to demonstrating continuous possession of the firearm before the incident. This pretrial ruling was significant because it resolved the evidentiary question in the government's favor before the trial began, allowing the government to elicit the prior possession testimony from its witnesses without having to navigate Rule 404(b)'s procedural requirements—such as providing reasonable advance notice of the intent to introduce the evidence—because the court determined that Rule 404(b) did not apply to this testimony in the first place. The ruling also meant that Cutbank would need to develop trial strategy anticipating that the jury would hear evidence of his prior possession of the rifle.

## The Eighth Circuit's Precedent in United States v. Battle

On appeal, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court's ruling, grounding its analysis in prior precedent. The court cited United States v. Battle, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014), for the proposition that "prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon." Battle thus establishes an important principle within the Eighth Circuit: when a defendant is charged with unlawful possession of a specific firearm, evidence that the defendant possessed that same firearm on prior occasions is admissible not as propensity evidence under Rule 404(b), but as direct circumstantial evidence of an element of the offense—possession. The logic is straightforward. Possession is not necessarily a momentary act; it can be ongoing. Evidence that a defendant possessed a firearm before the charged date makes it more likely that the defendant possessed the firearm on the charged date, because it links the defendant to the weapon and suggests a relationship of ownership or control that extends over time. This is not the same as arguing that because a defendant possessed firearms in the past, he probably possessed one on the night in question—such an argument would be classic propensity reasoning. Instead, the evidence pertains to the same weapon, and the inference sought is that the defendant's connection to that particular firearm was established before the incident and continued through the time of the charged offense.

## The Distinction Between Rule 404(b) Propensity Evidence and Intrinsic Evidence of Continuous Possession

The line between Rule 404(b) propensity evidence and intrinsic evidence of possession turns on several key distinctions. Propensity evidence under Rule 404(b) typically involves prior acts involving different weapons or different victims, offered to suggest that the defendant has a general propensity to commit the type of crime charged. For example, evidence that Cutbank had previously possessed a different firearm would likely raise Rule 404(b) concerns, because it would invite the jury to reason that Cutbank is the kind of person who possesses firearms and therefore probably possessed one on this occasion. In contrast, evidence of prior possession of the same firearm is offered not to show a general character trait but to establish a specific, concrete fact: that the defendant had a preexisting relationship with this particular weapon. The inference is narrower and more defensible: someone who has possessed a specific firearm before is more likely to have possessed it again than someone with no prior connection to it. The Eighth Circuit's treatment of this evidence as intrinsic also reflects the view that prior possession of the same weapon is part of the narrative of the charged offense—it helps explain how Cutbank came to possess the rifle on the night of November 19–20 and supports the inference that the recovered firearm was his.

## D.F.'s Trial Testimony Regarding Prior Possession

At trial, D.F. testified that she had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle a "handful of times" before the incident at issue. This testimony was consistent with the scope of the district court's pretrial ruling. It did not suggest a general propensity to possess firearms; it identified a specific weapon and a limited pattern of prior possession. The defense renewed its objection to this testimony, preserving the issue for appellate review. D.F.'s testimony was important to the government's case because it connected Cutbank to the rifle through more than just the DNA evidence found on the gun. While DNA established physical contact with the weapon, D.F.'s testimony established a history of possession and control, supporting the inference that Cutbank knowingly possessed the rifle on the night in question and that the DNA was not the result of incidental or momentary contact.

## C.F.'s Trial Testimony and the Renewed Objections

C.F., the housemate, also testified as a government witness that she had seen the gun used during the incident "once or twice" before. The district court overruled the defense's objection to this testimony, consistent with its pretrial ruling that such evidence was intrinsic and admissible. C.F.'s testimony provided independent corroboration of D.F.'s account, strengthening the government's claim that Cutbank had an established connection to the rifle. The defense's objections at trial, like the pretrial motion, were rooted in the Rule 404(b) framework, but the district court's consistent rulings reflected its view that the testimony fell outside that framework entirely. From the court's perspective, the testimony was not about Cutbank's character; it was about his possession of a specific weapon.

## The Eighth Circuit's Affirmation on Appeal

On appeal, Cutbank did not cite any authority refuting the district court's pretrial ruling or the Eighth Circuit's prior holding in Battle. The court of appeals noted this absence of contrary authority and affirmed the district court's rulings on this issue. The court's analysis was concise: the precedent was clear, the district court had followed it, and Cutbank had offered no reason to depart from it. The affirmance underscores an important lesson about appellate advocacy. The strength of an evidentiary argument on appeal often depends not only on the trial record but also on the legal authority marshaled in support. Cutbank's failure to provide cases or meaningful analysis—repeated throughout his appellate briefs—substantially weakened his challenges. The Eighth Circuit's citation to Battle and its observation that Cutbank offered no contrary authority illustrate how precedent functions in appellate review: when a circuit court has established a clear rule, the burden falls on the party seeking to challenge it to demonstrate why the rule should not apply or should be reconsidered. Cutbank did not carry that burden, and the district court's evidentiary rulings on the prior possession testimony were affirmed.

# Evidentiary Issues II — Hearsay, Confrontation Clause, Impeachment, and Harmless Error

## The Pretrial Request for a Limiting Instruction

Before trial, Cutbank's defense counsel anticipated that police officers would testify about statements D.F. made to them when she called 911 after Cutbank fled her home. Concerned that the jury might treat these out-of-court statements as substantive evidence of Cutbank's actions, Cutbank requested a limiting instruction. Specifically, he asked the court to instruct the jury that it could not consider D.F.'s statements to police as substantive evidence of what Cutbank did until D.F. herself testified to the events in court. The defense framed this request as necessary to avoid two distinct problems: hearsay and Confrontation Clause violations.

This request reflects a common defensive strategy in criminal trials. When a witness gives an out-of-court statement to law enforcement, that statement is hearsay if offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted. Under the Federal Rules of Evidence, hearsay is generally inadmissible unless it falls within a recognized exception. A police officer testifying about what a victim told them could run afoul of this rule if the prosecution intends the statement as substantive evidence of the defendant's guilt rather than merely as background explaining why the officer took certain investigative steps. The Confrontation Clause of the Sixth Amendment raises a related but distinct concern: it guarantees a criminal defendant the right to confront the witnesses against him. When the prosecution seeks to introduce a testimonial out-of-court statement, the Clause generally requires that the declarant be available for cross-examination at trial.

## The Government's Counterargument

The government responded that the officers' testimony about D.F.'s statements would not overstep hearsay rules because the statements would be offered not for their truth but to establish the context for the officers' actions and their knowledge at the time they responded. This is a classic non-hearsay purpose: an out-of-court statement offered to show its effect on the listener—here, what the officers knew and why they acted as they did—is not offered for the truth of the matter asserted and therefore does not constitute hearsay. The government also argued that if any limiting instruction were needed, a later instruction from the bench at the close of trial would be customary and sufficient to address any concern.

## The Standard of Review for Refusing a Limiting Instruction

The Eighth Circuit reviews a district court's decision not to give a limiting instruction for abuse of discretion. This standard, articulated in *United States v. Bennett*, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014), gives substantial deference to the trial judge. An appellate court will not reverse simply because it might have chosen to give the instruction; it must find that the district court's decision was a clear and prejudicial abuse of its discretion. This deferential posture reflects the reality that trial judges are best positioned to assess the flow of evidence and the likely impact of instructions on the jury in real time.

## Contemporaneous Instructions Versus Closing Instructions

A key principle in this area is that a district court need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted, so long as the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial. As the *Bennett* court noted, the timing of a limiting instruction is within the trial court's discretion. This rule recognizes that interrupting the presentation of evidence to give instructions can disrupt the trial's flow and may actually confuse jurors by emphasizing specific pieces of evidence. A single, well-crafted instruction at the close of trial can adequately cabin the jury's use of the evidence. In Cutbank's case, the Eighth Circuit found that the record demonstrated the court did not admit hearsay during the trial, rendering the timing question largely academic. Because the officers' testimony was offered for the permissible non-hearsay purpose of establishing context for their actions, no limiting instruction was needed at all.

## Confrontation Clause Satisfaction Through In-Court Testimony

The Confrontation Clause dimension of Cutbank's argument was resolved by a straightforward principle: the Clause is satisfied when hearsay declarants actually appear in court and testify in person. The Eighth Circuit had established this rule in *United States v. Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010). The rationale is intuitive. The Confrontation Clause guarantees the right to cross-examine adverse witnesses. If the declarant of the out-of-court statement takes the stand and is subject to cross-examination, the defendant has enjoyed the confrontation the Clause requires. Here, D.F. testified at trial and was available for cross-examination. Whatever statements she had made to police, the defense had the opportunity to confront her about them directly. There was, accordingly, no Confrontation Clause violation and no abuse of discretion in the court's handling of the issue.

## The Testimony About D.F.'s Sister Being Killed by a Gun

During cross-examination, the defense explored prior inconsistent statements D.F. had made, including a statement that she did not remember seeing the gun on the night in question. In response to questions about why she had made inconsistent statements, D.F. explained that some of her statements reflected the fact that she was "not familiar with guns" and "did not like guns . . . [a]s [her] sister was killed by a gun." The defense moved to strike the testimony about D.F.'s sister, but the district court denied the motion.

The Eighth Circuit upheld this ruling on two grounds. First, the testimony was responsive to the defense's own cross-examination. Having opened the door to why D.F. made prior inconsistent statements, the defense could not object when she provided an answer that explained the inconsistency. Second, the court applied the balancing test of Federal Rule of Evidence 403, which permits a court to exclude relevant evidence only if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusion, or other enumerated harms. The court of appeals noted that Cutbank pointed to no unfair prejudice or confusion resulting from the testimony, and none was apparent. The testimony explained D.F.'s prior statements and provided context for her perception of the events; it did not inflame the jury with irrelevant emotional content unrelated to the issues in the case.

The Eighth Circuit reinforced its conclusion by citing *Sprint/United Mgmt. Co. v. Mendelsohn*, 552 U.S. 379, 384 (2008), for the broad evidentiary discretion trial courts enjoy. The Supreme Court has consistently recognized that trial judges are in the best position to make case-by-case determinations about the admissibility of evidence, weighing probative value against potential prejudice in light of the specific trial context. Appellate courts will disturb such rulings only when the trial court has clearly abused that discretion. Additionally, C.F., testifying as a government witness, mentioned D.F.'s sister being killed by a gun without any defense objection, further undermining the claim that this evidence was improperly admitted.

## Authentication Requirements for Impeachment by Prior Inconsistent Statement

The defense also sought to undermine C.F.'s testimony that she had seen the gun before the incident. On cross-examination, the defense questioned C.F. about prior statements she made to a police investigator. The district court permitted questions about what C.F. said to police but sustained the government's objections when defense counsel attempted to read portions of the resulting police report into the record. The basis for the objection was that the report was not authored by C.F. and therefore lacked proper foundation.

The Eighth Circuit's decision in *United States v. Cumbie*, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022), sets out the governing principle: although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated. Authentication requires evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it is. A police report is hearsay if offered for the truth of its contents, and when offered to establish what C.F. previously said, it must be properly connected to the witness whose statements it purports to record. Because C.F. did not author the report, reading it into the record without additional foundation would have been improper. The court did not abuse its discretion by requiring proper authentication before allowing the report to be used. The defense was free to ask C.F. directly what she told the investigator—which the court permitted—but it could not circumvent the authentication requirement by reading a third-party document into the record.

## The Harmless Error Standard

The Eighth Circuit addressed one additional principle relevant to all of Cutbank's evidentiary claims: the harmless error doctrine. Citing *United States v. DeMarce*, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009), the court reiterated that it will not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless. Reversal is warranted only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence. This standard reflects the fundamental distinction between errors that affect a defendant's substantial rights and those that do not. Even if one or more of the district court's evidentiary rulings had been erroneous, the appellate court asks whether that error, in the context of the entire trial, likely influenced the verdict.

In Cutbank's case, the government's evidence was overwhelming. D.F. and C.F. identified the weapon as one they had seen Cutbank with before the incident. DNA on the gun matched Cutbank. The gun was found along the path Cutbank likely traveled between D.F.'s home and the garage where he barricaded himself. The physical and testimonial evidence of Cutbank's possession of the firearm was substantial. The Eighth Circuit concluded that the district court's evidentiary rulings, even if any had been erroneous, "did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict." The errors, if any, were harmless.

## Cumulative Error and Substantial Evidence of Guilt

Finally, the court addressed the cumulative error doctrine. Citing *United States v. Keys*, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019), the Eighth Circuit explained that it will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is substantial prejudice to the defendant. This doctrine recognizes that while individual errors may each be harmless, their combined effect might collectively deprive a defendant of a fair trial. However, the doctrine requires more than simply tallying the number of alleged errors; the court must assess whether the aggregate of the errors undermines confidence in the verdict.

Here, Cutbank raised a series of evidentiary and procedural challenges but provided no case law and almost no analysis to support his arguments. The court found that none of the contentions had merit individually, and the cumulative error doctrine did not rescue them. The government's evidence—both testimonial and physical—was substantial, and the district court's evidentiary rulings did not, individually or collectively, substantially prejudice Cutbank's defense. The conviction stood.

# Sentencing — The § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Enhancement and Sentence Reasonableness

## The Presentence Investigation Report's Offense Level Calculations

After the jury convicted Cutbank of being a felon in possession of firearm under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e), the case proceeded to sentencing. The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) served as the foundational document for the sentencing proceedings, setting forth both the factual basis for the offense and the recommended advisory guideline calculations. Understanding the PSR's calculations is essential to understanding the sentencing issues on appeal.

The PSR began with a base offense level of 26. This starting point reflected two factors identified in the guidelines: first, that the offense involved a sawed-off firearm of the type described in 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), and second, that Cutbank had at least two prior convictions for crimes of violence. The sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle recovered along the path between D.F.'s home and the garage where Cutbank barricaded himself satisfied the first factor. The jury's finding that Cutbank had three or more prior violent felony convictions, triggering Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) penalties under § 924(e), supplied the second.

On top of the base offense level, the PSR applied three separate enhancements. First, a three-level increase was applied because the firearm had an altered or obliterated serial number. Second, a two-level increase was applied because Cutbank recklessly created a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing from police—conduct vividly illustrated by the record of his barricading himself in the garage, making erratic statements about having a weapon, biting an officer in the face and neck, and grabbing an officer's gun during the struggle to detain him. Third, and most central to the appeal, a four-level increase was applied under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) because Cutbank possessed the firearm in connection with another felony offense.

These enhancements brought the total offense level to 35. Combined with a Criminal History Category of VI—the most serious category—the PSR calculated an advisory guidelines sentencing range of 292 to 365 months of imprisonment. The district court sentenced Cutbank to 292 months, the bottom of that range.

## The § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Enhancement Standard

The four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) was the primary sentencing issue Cutbank raised on appeal. The provision applies when a defendant used or possessed any firearm or ammunition "in connection with another felony offense," regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought or a conviction was obtained. This last point is significant: the enhancement does not require that the defendant be separately charged with, tried for, or convicted of the underlying felony. The sentencing court need only find, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the defendant possessed the firearm in connection with another felony offense. The commentary to § 2K2.1, specifically note 14(C), makes this clear.

In Cutbank's case, the "other felony offense" was readily identifiable from the facts of the incident itself. The PSR detailed how Cutbank, after drinking and using methamphetamine, awakened his girlfriend D.F., pointed a gun at her face, forced her into a closet, held her there at gunpoint for what felt like hours, threatened her life, and struck her across the face with the gun. These facts describe conduct that constitutes multiple felony offenses—among them domestic assault, kidnapping, and assault with a dangerous weapon. Cutbank possessed the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle during the commission of these offenses. The firearm was not merely present; it was integral to the criminal conduct, used to coerce, threaten, and assault the victim.

## The District Court's Adoption of the PSR and the Clearly Erroneous Standard

At sentencing, the district court adopted the PSR's factual statements regarding Cutbank's prior felony convictions without objection. This is a critical procedural point. When neither party objects to the factual statements in the PSR, the district court may adopt those facts as its own findings. The court's factual findings at sentencing are then reviewed on appeal for clear error. Under this standard, as the Eighth Circuit explained in *United States v. Dunn*, 723 F.3d 919, 929-30 (8th Cir. 2013), the appellate court will not reverse unless left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake was made. The clearly erroneous standard is highly deferential to the district court, particularly when the findings are based on unobjected-to PSR facts.

Because the district court adopted the PSR's factual statements without objection, the court of appeals had no difficulty concluding that the district court did not err—much less clearly err—in finding that the facts established the gun was used in connection with another felony. The facts were not in dispute, and they plainly supported the enhancement.

## Cutbank's § 3553(a) Argument and the Interaction with the Armed Career Criminal Guideline

Cutbank's argument on appeal was not that the district court's factual findings were wrong, nor that the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement was technically inapplicable. Instead, he argued that application of the four-level enhancement produced a within-guidelines sentence that "conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)" that a sentence be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary, to comply with the purposes set forth in paragraph (2) of this subsection." In other words, Cutbank accepted that the guidelines calculation produced a total offense level of 35 but argued that the resulting sentence was too long to satisfy the statutory sentencing goals.

This argument required the court of appeals to navigate the interaction between two sentencing frameworks. On one hand, the Armed Career Criminal Guideline, USSG § 4B1.4, would have produced a total offense level of 34 based on the jury's finding that Cutbank had three or more prior violent felony convictions triggering ACCA penalties. On the other hand, the § 2K2.1 enhancements produced a total offense level of 35. The guidelines resolve this overlap with a straightforward rule: the greater offense level applies. See USSG § 4B1.4(b). Thus, the § 2K2.1 calculation at level 35 governed, and the advisory range was 292 to 365 months rather than the somewhat lower range that a level 34 would have produced.

Cutbank's argument, in substance, was that this mechanical application of the greater-offense-level rule produced a sentence inconsistent with the parsimony provision of § 3553(a). The court of appeals treated this argument as a challenge to the substantive reasonableness of the sentence rather than as a procedural error in calculating the guidelines range.

## Procedural Reasonableness: Correct Calculation of the Guidelines Range

Before reaching the substantive question of whether the sentence was too long, the court of appeals first had to satisfy itself that the district court committed no significant procedural error. This sequential approach follows the Supreme Court's framework established in *Gall v. United States*, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007), which directs that a district court should begin all sentencing proceedings by correctly calculating the applicable Guidelines range. As the Court reiterated in *Rosales-Mireles v. United States*, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018), before a court of appeals can consider the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, it must first ensure that the district court committed no significant procedural error, such as failing to calculate—or improperly calculating—the Guidelines range.

In Cutbank's case, the procedural threshold was met. The district court correctly calculated the advisory guidelines range by applying the § 2K2.1 enhancements, arriving at a total offense level of 35, and using the greater-offense-level rule to resolve the overlap with the Armed Career Criminal Guideline. The court then considered whether the § 3553(a) factors warranted a departure or variance from the advisory range. Because the guidelines calculation was correct and the district court followed the procedural sequence, the court of appeals could proceed to the substantive question.

## The Presumption of Reasonableness for Within-Guidelines Sentences

A central principle in the court's substantive analysis was the presumption of reasonableness that attaches to within-guidelines sentences on appeal. As the Eighth Circuit explained in *United States v. Goodhouse*, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8 Cir. 2023), when a district court imposes a sentence within the applicable guidelines range, the appellate court presumes that sentence is reasonable. This presumption is not binding—it does not shift the burden of proof or preclude the defendant from arguing that the sentence is nonetheless too long—but it reflects the appellate view that a sentence reflecting a properly calculated guidelines range, arrived at through the procedures the guidelines contemplate, is generally appropriate.

Cutbank's 292-month sentence was at the bottom of the 292-to-365-month advisory range. Because it was a within-guidelines sentence, the presumption of reasonableness applied. To overcome the presumption, Cutbank bore the burden of demonstrating that the sentence was unreasonable in light of the § 3553(a) factors and the totality of the circumstances.

## The District Court's Consideration of § 3553(a) Factors

The record confirmed that the district court considered the § 3553(a) factors before imposing sentence. At the sentencing hearing, the court noted its review of the PSR and the sentencing memoranda and reviewed the § 3553(a) factors that could warrant a variance. The court did not, however, provide a mechanical recitation of each factor and its application to Cutbank's case. The court of appeals found this sufficient.

Relying on *United States v. Masood*, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025), the court reasoned that because the district court was aware of Cutbank's arguments for a variance, the appellate court presumed that the district court considered and rejected them. The court further cited *United States v. Maluoth*, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024), for the principle that the law does not require a district court to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence. Here, the court's acknowledgment of the PSR, the sentencing memoranda, and the § 3553(a) factors satisfied this standard.

## Substantive Reasonableness and Affirmance

The substantive reasonableness inquiry asks whether the sentence is appropriate in light of the totality of the circumstances, giving due deference to the district court's substantial sentencing discretion. The court of appeals reviewed the aggravating features of Cutbank's offense and criminal history, which provided powerful support for the within-guidelines sentence.

Cutbank had nearly a dozen prior felony convictions, many involving violence and firearms. The offense of conviction itself was not a simple possession case. It involved domestic abuse—Cutbank awakened D.F., pointed a gun at her face, and forced her into a closet. It involved kidnapping—D.F. was held at gunpoint for what felt like hours. It involved assault in the victim's home and in the presence of her teenage children, whom Cutbank ordered D.F. to rouse. And it involved injuring a police officer during the struggle to detain him—Cutbank bit an officer in the face and neck and grabbed the officer's gun. These facts placed the offense at the serious end of the felon-in-possession spectrum and amply justified the district court's sentence.

The court of appeals concluded that the district court did not abuse its substantial sentencing discretion and did not impose a substantively unreasonable sentence when it sentenced Cutbank to 292 months of imprisonment. The judgment of the district court was affirmed.
