Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Why is prior possession of the same firearm considered "intrinsic evidence" rather than 404(b) propensity evidence?
Question:
In United States v. Cutbank, the defendant filed a pretrial motion to exclude testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had previously seen him with the sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle, arguing this was propensity evidence barred by Rule 404(b). The district court ruled it was "intrinsic evidence" relevant to proving the possession charge and demonstrating continuous possession. The Eighth Circuit agreed, citing United States v. Battle for the principle that "prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control."

I'm confused about the line between intrinsic evidence and 404(b) propensity evidence. I thought Rule 404(b) bars evidence of prior bad acts used to show propensity, but here the witnesses are describing seeing the defendant with the same gun on prior occasions. How does the fact that it's the *same weapon* make this intrinsic rather than propensity evidence? If the witnesses had said they saw him with a *different* gun on prior occasions, would that analysis change? What is the test for determining whether evidence is "intrinsic" versus requiring 404(b) analysis?
Answer:
## The "same weapon" distinction is doing the work here

The key to understanding this case is recognizing what the government had to prove and how the testimony related to that burden.

### What Rule 404(b) actually bars

Rule 404(b)(1) bars evidence of prior bad acts **"to prove a person's character in order to show that on a particular occasion the person acted in accordance with the character."** The concern is the inferential chain: the defendant did bad thing X in the past, therefore he has a propensity to do things like X, therefore he probably did the charged conduct. That reasoning is forbidden.

But Rule 404(b) is not a general bar on mentioning anything the defendant did before the charged conduct. It applies to acts that are **separate from the charged offense** and offered for some non-propensity purpose. Evidence that directly proves an element of the charged offense is a different matter entirely.

### Why prior possession of the *same* firearm is intrinsic

Cutbank was charged with being a felon in possession of a firearm. An element the government had to prove was that Cutbank **possessed** the sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle found along his flight path. The testimony from D.F. and C.F. that they had seen Cutbank with that *same rifle* before November 19–20 was offered to prove that element—specifically, that Cutbank owned or controlled the weapon, rather than just happening to pick it up that night.

As the Eighth Circuit explained, quoting *United States v. Battle*: **"Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon."** 774 F.3d at 511. The testimony wasn't being used to say "Cutbank is a gun guy, so he probably had a gun that night." It was being used to say "this specific weapon belonged to or was controlled by Cutbank," which is a direct element of the offense. That makes it intrinsic evidence, not 404(b) other-acts evidence.

### Why a *different* weapon would change the analysis

If the witnesses had testified that they previously saw Cutbank with some *other* firearm—a different rifle, a handgun, anything other than the sawed-off Marlin .22—that testimony would not directly prove Cutbank possessed the charged weapon. It would only be relevant through the propensity inferential chain: Cutbank has a history of possessing guns, therefore he probably possessed *this* gun. That is precisely the reasoning Rule 404(b)(1) prohibits.

A different-weapon scenario could potentially come in under a 404(b)(2) purpose—knowledge, intent, or absence of mistake, for example—but it would require the 404(b) analysis the defendant sought here: notice, a proper purpose, 403 balancing, and a limiting instruction. The "same weapon" fact is what keeps this testimony on the intrinsic side of the line.

### The test

The distinction turns on whether the evidence is offered to prove an element of the charged offense through direct inference, or whether it requires a propensity inference to connect the prior act to the charged conduct. When the prior act involves the **same instrumentality** as the charged offense and directly bears on an element (here, possession and control), it is intrinsic. When the prior act is separate and can only be connected to the charged conduct through a character-based inference, 404(b) governs.

This is why the district court's framing—"intrinsic evidence relevant to proving the possession charge and demonstrating continuous possession"—was accurate. The testimony about seeing Cutbank with the same rifle on prior occasions wasn't painting a picture of bad character; it was establishing his ongoing dominion and control over the very weapon the indictment charged him with possessing.
