Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### When can extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement be excluded for lack of authentication?
Question:
In Cutbank, the defense wanted to cross-examine C.F. about prior statements she made to a police investigator that were inconsistent with her trial testimony. The court allowed questions about what C.F. said but sustained the government's objection to defense counsel reading portions of the police report into the record, because the report was not authored by C.F. and lacked proper foundation. The Eighth Circuit cited United States v. Cumbie for the principle that "extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated."

I'm confused about the authentication requirement here. If the defense is trying to impeach C.F. with her own prior inconsistent statements, why does it matter who authored the police report? Is the issue that a police report containing a witness's statements is hearsay within hearsay? Could the defense have called the officer who wrote the report to authenticate it, or would that still be improper because the officer didn't personally witness C.F.'s statements? What would have been the proper way to introduce C.F.'s prior inconsistent statements from the police report?
Answer:
## Authentication is a threshold requirement for extrinsic evidence, regardless of the impeachment purpose

The short answer is that authentication and hearsay are distinct requirements, and authentication comes first. Even when extrinsic evidence is offered for a permissible non-hearsay purpose—impeachment by prior inconsistent statement—it still must be authenticated. The defense was not trying to introduce the police report for its truth; it was trying to use the report to prove that C.F. previously made inconsistent statements. That impeachment purpose doesn't bypass the authentication requirement.

### Why authorship matters for authentication

Authentication under Federal Rule of Evidence 901 requires the proponent to "produce evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it is." Here, the defense was offering the police report as evidence of what C.F. actually said to the investigator. But the report was authored by the investigator, not by C.F. When defense counsel simply read portions of the report to C.F. on cross-examination, there was no foundation establishing that the report accurately recorded C.F.'s statements—or even that C.F. had made any statements at all. The document itself had not been authenticated as a reliable record of C.F.'s words.

This is why the court allowed defense counsel to question C.F. directly about what she said to police but drew the line at reading the report into the record. Asking the witness "Did you tell the investigator X?" is examination of the witness about her own prior statements. Reading a third-party document that purportedly records those statements is introducing extrinsic evidence, and extrinsic evidence must be authenticated.

### Why this is not primarily a hearsay-within-hearsay problem

The student asks whether the issue is hearsay within hearsay. That is a related concern but not the one the Eighth Circuit identified. The opinion cites *United States v. Cumbie* for the proposition that extrinsic evidence used for impeachment "must still be authenticated." The court framed the issue as authentication, not hearsay.

This makes sense because impeachment by prior inconsistent statement is generally a non-hearsay use of the statement—the statement is offered not for its truth but to show the inconsistency. However, authentication is a separate threshold requirement. A document offered for a non-hearsay purpose still must be shown to be what the proponent claims it is. The defense claimed the report contained C.F.'s prior statements, but without authentication, there was no basis for that claim in the record.

### Could the defense have called the officer?

Yes. The officer who interviewed C.F. and wrote the report could have authenticated it. The officer would not need to have witnessed the underlying events—the relevant authentication question is whether the officer personally conducted the interview and accurately recorded C.F.'s statements. An officer who interviewed C.F. can testify that C.F. made specific statements to them and that the report reflects those statements. That testimony authenticates both the report and, more directly, the prior inconsistent statements themselves.

In fact, calling the officer would have been cleaner than trying to introduce the report as a document. The officer's testimony that "C.F. told me she did not see the gun that night" is extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement, authenticated by the witness who heard it firsthand. The report itself might raise additional hearsay concerns if offered as a document, but the officer's testimony about what C.F. said would be admissible for impeachment purposes.

### The proper procedure

The proper approach under Federal Rule of Evidence 613 would have been:

1. **Examine C.F. on cross-examination** about the specific prior statements, giving her an opportunity to explain or deny them. The court permitted this.
2. **If C.F. denied or did not distinctly admit making the statements**, introduce extrinsic evidence through the officer who conducted the interview, authenticating the statements by firsthand testimony.
3. **If the defense wanted to introduce the report itself**, lay a foundation through the officer—establishing that the officer wrote the report, interviewed C.F., and accurately recorded her statements—or through another authentication method under Rule 901.

The district court's ruling was consistent with this framework. It allowed the first step and required proper foundation for the second. As the Eighth Circuit noted, this was not an abuse of discretion, and in any event, any error would have been harmless given the substantial evidence of Cutbank's guilt.
