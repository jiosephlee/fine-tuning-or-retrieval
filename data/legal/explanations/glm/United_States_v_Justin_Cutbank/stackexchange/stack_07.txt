Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Can a defendant challenge a within-guidelines sentence by arguing the guidelines calculation itself violates § 3553(a)?
Question:
In Cutbank, the defendant argued that applying the four-level § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement resulted in a within-range sentence that "conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)" that a sentence "be sufficient, but not greater than necessary." The Eighth Circuit rejected this, holding that the district court correctly calculated the guidelines range, considered the § 3553(a) factors, and imposed a within-range sentence that is presumed reasonable.

I'm confused about the boundaries between procedural and substantive reasonableness challenges. Cutbank seems to be arguing that the guidelines enhancement itself produces a sentence that is greater than necessary—this sounds like a substantive challenge to the reasonableness of the sentence. But the court treats it as if the guidelines calculation was proper and the sentence is presumptively reasonable. Can a defendant ever argue that a correctly calculated guidelines range is substantively unreasonable because the enhancements stack in a way that violates § 3553(a)? Or is the proper procedure to argue for a variance under § 3553(a) after accepting the guidelines calculation? What would Cutbank have needed to show to successfully challenge the sentence as substantively unreasonable?
Answer:
## Can a defendant challenge a within-guidelines sentence by arguing the guidelines calculation violates § 3553(a)?

**No—not in the way Cutbank framed it.** A correctly calculated guidelines range does not "violate" § 3553(a). The guidelines calculation is a procedural step governed by the Sentencing Guidelines themselves; § 3553(a) governs the sentence the court ultimately imposes. The proper path is to accept the guidelines calculation (or challenge it as procedurally wrong) and then argue for a variance under § 3553(a).

### The sequential framework

*Gall* establishes a specific sequence: the district court must first "correctly calculat[e] the applicable Guidelines range," then consider the § 3553(a) factors, then consider arguments for departure or variance, and finally impose a sentence. Appellate review follows the same order. As the Eighth Circuit noted, quoting *Rosales-Mireles*, "Before a court of appeals can consider the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, it must first ensure that the district court committed no significant procedural error, such as failing to calculate (or improperly calculating) the Guidelines range."

This means a defendant's challenge to the guidelines calculation is always a **procedural** challenge—either the court applied the right enhancements to the right facts or it didn't. Cutbank's argument that the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement "conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)" conflates the guidelines calculation (step one) with the § 3553(a) analysis (step two). The guidelines math is either correct or incorrect; it doesn't "violate" § 3553(a) because § 3553(a) doesn't govern how guidelines are calculated. It governs what sentence the court imposes after the range is determined.

### What the Eighth Circuit actually did in Cutbank

The court followed the *Gall* sequence precisely:

1. **Procedural check:** The court confirmed the district court "correctly calculated" the guidelines range. The PSR's factual statements were adopted without objection, and the facts established the gun was used in connection with another felony, so the four-level enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) was properly applied. No procedural error.

2. **§ 3553(a) consideration:** The court noted the district court "reviewed § 3553(a) factors that could warrant a variance." Because the district court was aware of Cutbank's arguments, the appellate court "presume[d] that the district court considered and rejected them."

3. **Substantive reasonableness:** The within-range sentence is "presume[d] ... reasonable on appeal." The court then examined whether the presumption was overcome given Cutbank's extensive criminal history (nearly a dozen prior felonies, many involving violence and firearms) and the seriousness of the offense (domestic abuse, kidnapping, assault in the victim's home in the presence of her children, and injuring a police officer). The court concluded the sentence was not substantively unreasonable.

### Can a within-range sentence ever be substantively unreasonable? Yes, but the burden is steep.

A defendant can argue that a within-range sentence is substantively unreasonable under § 3553(a)—that's exactly what substantive reasonableness review is for. But the defendant must **rebut the presumption of reasonableness** that attaches to within-range sentences. The way to do this is not by arguing that the guidelines calculation "violates" § 3553(a), but by pointing to specific § 3553(a) factors that make the within-range sentence greater than necessary—factors the district court failed to adequately account for.

The practical vehicle for this is a **variance request**. The defendant argues: "The guidelines range is calculated correctly, but given the § 3553(a) factors—here, the nature and circumstances of the offense, my history and characteristics, and the need for the sentence—the court should vary below the range." If the district court rejects the variance and imposes a within-range sentence, the defendant can then challenge that sentence as substantively unreasonable on appeal.

### What would Cutbank have needed to show?

To succeed, Cutbank would have needed to:

1. **Concede or properly challenge the guidelines calculation.** If the calculation is correct (as it was here), procedurally challenging it will fail. The opinion makes clear the facts in the PSR established the gun was used in connection with another felony, and the court adopted those facts without objection.

2. **Make a developed variance argument under § 3553(a).** Cutbank needed to identify specific § 3553(a) factors showing why a below-range sentence was sufficient—but not greater than necessary—despite the correctly calculated range. Vague invocations of the "not greater than necessary" principle are not enough.

3. **Rebut the presumption of reasonableness on appeal.** Even if the district court rejected a variance argument, the appellate court presumes a within-range sentence is reasonable. Cutbank would have needed to show that the § 3553(a) factors, taken together, render the within-range sentence unreasonable. Given his extensive criminal history and the violent nature of the offense, this would have been extraordinarily difficult.

The opinion's recitation of the facts underscores why Cutbank's challenge failed on the merits: nearly a dozen prior felony convictions, many involving violence and firearms; domestic abuse with a firearm pointed at the victim's face; holding the victim at gunpoint in a closet; committing the offense in the presence of her children; and biting a police officer while resisting arrest. These facts make the within-range sentence difficult to characterize as "greater than necessary."

### Bottom line

The guidelines calculation and the § 3553(a) analysis are sequential, not overlapping. A guidelines enhancement either was or was not properly applied as a matter of Guidelines interpretation and factfinding—that's procedural. Whether the resulting sentence is reasonable in light of all the § 3553(a) factors is substantive. Cutbank's mistake was framing a substantive challenge (the sentence is too long) as a procedural one (the guidelines calculation "conflicts with" § 3553(a)). The proper approach is to accept the correctly calculated range and argue for a variance, then challenge the substance of the within-range sentence if the district court rejects the variance. But overcoming the presumption of reasonableness for a within-range sentence requires far more than a general assertion that the sentence is "greater than necessary."
