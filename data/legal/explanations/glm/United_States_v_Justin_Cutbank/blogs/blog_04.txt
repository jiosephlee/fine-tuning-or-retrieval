Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# When Guidelines Enhancements Clash with the Parsimony Principle: The § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Problem in Armed Career Criminal Cases

The Armed Career Criminal Act is one of the most powerful sentencing tools the federal government possesses. It transforms a felon-in-possession offense carrying a ten-year maximum into one with a fifteen-year minimum and a maximum of life. But what happens when guidelines enhancements stack on top of ACCA's already severe penalties to produce an advisory range that dwarfs what the statute alone would require? That was the question Justin Cutbank raised in *United States v. Cutbank*, No. 24-1225 (8th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025), and the Eighth Circuit's answer reveals a structural tension in federal sentencing that deserves more attention than the court gave it.

## The Factual Backdrop and the Evidentiary Issues

The facts of Cutbank's offense are grim. After a night of drinking and methamphetamine use, Cutbank held his girlfriend at gunpoint in a closet for hours, beat her with the sawed-off rifle, terrorized her teenage sons and housemate, and then fled. He barricaded himself in a nearby garage, resisted arrest, and bit a police officer in the face and neck. Police recovered the sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle along the likely flight path, and DNA tied Cutbank to the weapon. A jury convicted him under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e).

Cutbank raised a series of evidentiary challenges on appeal — all of which the Eighth Circuit dispatched with little ceremony. The most interesting of these involved testimony from the victim and her housemate that they had seen Cutbank with the same sawed-off rifle on prior occasions. Cutbank argued this was inadmissible propensity evidence under Rule 404(b). The court correctly rejected this argument, relying on *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504 (8th Cir. 2014), which holds that prior possession of the same firearm is intrinsic evidence relevant to proving ownership and control. This is a well-established principle in the Eighth Circuit and elsewhere, and the panel was right to affirm it without much fanfare.

The remaining evidentiary arguments — involving a denied request for a contemporaneous limiting instruction on hearsay, testimony about the victim's sister being killed by a gun offered in response to cross-examination about prior inconsistent statements, and the authentication requirement for a police report used to impeach a witness — were all resolved in the district court's favor under deferential abuse-of-discretion review. The court also applied the cumulative-error doctrine and found that even assuming arguendo that any individual ruling was erroneous, the evidence of guilt was so overwhelming that no error could have substantially swayed the verdict. This section of the opinion is straightforward and unlikely to trouble future litigants.

## The Sentencing Question: § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) Meets the Parsimony Principle

The sentencing issue is where this case gets interesting, even though the court treated it almost as an afterthought.

The Presentence Investigation Report calculated Cutbank's total offense level at 35. This figure reflected a base offense level of 26 (for a sawed-off firearm with two prior crime-of-violence convictions), plus a three-level enhancement for an obliterated serial number, a two-level enhancement for recklessly creating a risk of death or serious bodily injury during flight, and a four-level enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) for possessing the firearm "in connection with another felony offense." With a Criminal History Category of VI, the advisory range was 292 to 365 months. The district court sentenced Cutbank to 292 months — the bottom of the range.

Cutbank did not dispute the factual basis for the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement. The PSR detailed his conduct — holding his girlfriend at gunpoint, assaulting her, preventing her from leaving — which plainly constitutes state felony offenses of domestic assault and false imprisonment. The enhancement applies regardless of whether charges are brought or convictions obtained. USSG § 2K2.1, comment. (n.14(C)). On the facts, the four-level bump was unassailable.

Instead, Cutbank made a more structural argument. He contended that the enhancement produced a within-guidelines sentence that "conflicts with the core principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)" — the parsimony provision — which requires that a sentence be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary" to achieve the purposes of sentencing. The key to understanding this argument is the interaction between the ACCA guideline and the firearms guideline.

The Armed Career Criminal Guideline, USSG § 4B1.4, produces its own offense level calculation for ACCA-eligible defendants. In Cutbank's case, § 4B1.4 would have yielded a total offense level of 34, producing an advisory range of 262 to 327 months at Criminal History Category VI. But because the § 2K2.1 enhancements pushed the offense level to 35, the greater offense level governed. See USSG § 4B1.4(b). The four-level § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement was thus the mechanism by which Cutbank's advisory range exceeded what the ACCA guideline alone would have produced — by approximately thirty months at the bottom of the range. Cutbank's argument, in essence, was that the ACCA already accounts for his dangerousness and criminal history through its enhanced statutory penalties and corresponding guideline, and that the additional four-level bump for the same underlying conduct that triggered ACCA in the first place produces a range that is "greater than necessary."

This is not a frivolous observation. The parsimony principle is, after all, the overarching constraint that Congress imposed on federal sentencing. It is the one statutory command that applies to every sentence, in every case, regardless of the guideline calculation. And stacked enhancements — particularly when they layer on top of an already-enhanced statutory scheme like ACCA — can produce advisory ranges that seem to exceed what any reasonable person would consider the minimum necessary sentence. Cutbank's fifteen-year statutory minimum was already a severe consequence for firearm possession. His 292-month sentence — nearly twenty-five years — is approaching a level that many would consider more retributive than necessary, particularly given that the underlying conduct involved no fatality.

## The Eighth Circuit's Response: Procedural Correctness Subsuming Substantive Challenge

The Eighth Circuit's treatment of this argument is revealing, though ultimately unsatisfying. The panel did not engage with the substance of Cutbank's parsimony argument at all. Instead, it recited the familiar procedural framework: the district court correctly calculated the guidelines range, considered the § 3553(a) factors, and imposed a within-range sentence. Such sentences are presumed reasonable on appeal. *United States v. Goodhouse*, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023). The district court "was aware of [Cutbank's] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them." *United States v. Masood*, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025) (quotation omitted). The court did not need to provide a "mechanical recitation" of the factors it weighed. *United States v. Maluoth*, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024). And given Cutbank's extensive criminal history and the violent circumstances of the offense, the sentence was substantively reasonable.

This approach illustrates a structural feature of post-Booker sentencing that merits scrutiny. The parsimony principle is supposed to function as an independent constraint on sentence length — a ceiling beyond which a sentence becomes "greater than necessary" regardless of what the guidelines say. But the Eighth Circuit's analytical framework effectively subsumes the parsimony inquiry into the presumption of reasonableness. If the guidelines range is correctly calculated and the sentence falls within it, the parsimony argument is never reached on its merits. The court treats the guidelines calculation as the starting and ending point of the substantive reasonableness inquiry, with the presumption of reasonableness doing all the heavy lifting.

This is not unique to the Eighth Circuit. The presumption of reasonableness for within-range sentences has been endorsed by the Supreme Court and adopted across the circuits. But Cutbank's case exposes the cost of this approach: a defendant who argues that a correctly calculated guidelines range is itself "greater than necessary" faces a nearly insurmountable burden. He must overcome not only the district court's discretionary sentencing determination but also an appellate presumption that the range itself is reasonable. The parsimony principle, in practice, functions less as an independent constraint and more as a rhetorical flourish — a statutory aspiration that is always acknowledged and never applied.

## The Broader Problem of Stacked Enhancements in ACCA Cases

There is also a deeper structural issue that the panel did not address. The ACCA and the guidelines enhancements under § 2K2.1 are not fully independent calibrations of sentencing severity. The ACCA already reflects a congressional judgment that certain recidivist firearm offenders are especially dangerous and warrant dramatically enhanced penalties. The § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement then adds another layer of severity for the same underlying conduct — the defendant's use of the firearm in connection with a felony — that may have contributed to the ACCA triggering analysis or that reflects the same dangerousness that ACCA already punishes. When the guidelines then instruct that the greater of the two offense levels governs, the result is that the firearms guideline's enhancement effectively overrides the ACCA guideline's own calibration.

This is not technically double-counting in the conventional sense, because the ACCA and § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) serve distinct purposes — one targets recidivism, the other targets the connection between the firearm and the underlying felony. But the practical effect is cumulative punishment for overlapping aspects of the same conduct, and the guidelines provide no mechanism for assessing whether the combined result is proportional. The parsimony principle could, in theory, serve as that mechanism — but only if courts are willing to take it seriously as an independent constraint on advisory ranges.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The Eighth Circuit's opinion in Cutbank is unlikely to change sentencing practice in any direct way. It applies well-established precedent to routine facts and reaches a predictable result. But it highlights a question that will continue to arise as long as the guidelines permit stacking: can a defendant ever successfully argue that a correctly calculated within-range sentence exceeds what the parsimony principle permits?

The answer, at least in the Eighth Circuit, appears to be no — or at least not in a case like Cutbank's, where the underlying conduct is violent and the criminal history is extensive. The presumption of reasonableness, combined with the deferential abuse-of-discretion standard for sentencing determinations, creates a formidable barrier. A defendant might have a better argument in a case where the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement is based on a non-violent felony, where the ACCA predicate offenses are less serious, or where the guidelines range dramatically exceeds the ACCA's statutory minimum. But even then, the court's framework — treating correct calculation as the prerequisite for the presumption, and the presumption as the practical end of the substantive inquiry — leaves little room for a successful parsimony challenge.

For defense attorneys in the Eighth Circuit and similar jurisdictions, the takeaway is sobering. The parsimony argument in the context of stacked enhancements is theoretically alive but practically moribund. The most promising path may be to press the argument at sentencing rather than on appeal — asking the district court to vary downward based on the cumulative effect of enhancements that produce a range exceeding what § 3553(a) requires — and to build a record that specifically addresses the proportionality concerns that the guidelines do not. Whether any district court will be willing to grant such a variance in an ACCA case remains an open question, but Cutbank suggests that the alternative — winning the argument on appeal — is a decidedly uphill battle.
