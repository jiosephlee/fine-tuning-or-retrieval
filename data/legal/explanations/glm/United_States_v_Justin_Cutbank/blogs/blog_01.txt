Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Same Gun, Different Night: The Intrinsic Evidence Doctrine and Rule 404(b) in Felon-in-Possession Cases

When a defendant is charged with being a felon in possession of a firearm, the government must prove that he knowingly possessed the weapon. But what if witnesses testify that they saw him with the same gun days, weeks, or even months before the charged offense? Is that proof of a course of possession—or is it propensity evidence that the defendant is a gun-toting criminal, introduced to prejudice the jury? The Eighth Circuit's recent decision in *United States v. Cutbank* illustrates a doctrinal fault line that circuits have papered over with relatively little scrutiny: the so-called "same weapon" rule, under which prior possession of the firearm charged in the indictment is classified not as Rule 404(b) other-acts evidence but as intrinsic evidence of the offense itself.

The classification matters enormously. Evidence designated as intrinsic escapes the procedural safeguards that Rule 404(b) imposes on prosecutions seeking to introduce prior bad acts. The government need not provide pretrial notice under Rule 404(b)(2)(A). The defense cannot demand a pretrial hearing on whether the probative value of the evidence is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice under Rule 403. And perhaps most importantly, the jury receives no limiting instruction explaining that it may consider the evidence only for the permitted purpose and not to infer criminal disposition. The "intrinsic" label is, in practical terms, a free pass.

## The Cutbank Facts and the Eighth Circuit's Reasoning

Justin Cutbank was convicted of being a felon in possession of a firearm after a harrowing night in which he held his girlfriend at gunpoint in a closet for hours, threatened her life, struck her with the gun, and then fled—leading to a standoff with police during which he bit an officer and attempted to grab the officer's weapon. Police recovered a sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle along the path between the girlfriend's home and the garage where Cutbank barricaded himself. DNA on the gun matched Cutbank.

Before trial, Cutbank moved to exclude testimony from his girlfriend, D.F., and her housemate, C.F., that they had previously observed him with the same sawed-off rifle. He argued this was propensity evidence barred by Rule 404(b). The district court rejected the argument, classifying the testimony as intrinsic evidence relevant to proving the possession charge and demonstrating continuous possession. The Eighth Circuit affirmed, citing its prior decision in *United States v. Battle*, 774 F.3d 504, 511 (8th Cir. 2014), for the proposition that "prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon."

At trial, D.F. testified that she had seen Cutbank with the sawed-off rifle a "handful of times" before the incident. C.F. testified that she had seen the gun used during the incident "once or twice" before. The panel found this testimony squarely within the *Battle* rule and noted that Cutbank cited no authority to the contrary.

## The Doctrinal Boundary and Its Problems

The distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic evidence is deceptively simple in formulation and maddeningly imprecise in application. Intrinsic evidence is evidence of acts that are "part of the same transaction" as the charged offense. Extrinsic evidence—governed by Rule 404(b)—is evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts offered to prove propensity. The classic formulation comes from *United States v. Williams*, 900 F.2d 823, 825 (5th Cir. 1990), which describes intrinsic evidence as "evidence concerning the background, circumstances, and details of the charged offense." But courts have stretched this concept considerably in the firearms context.

The "same weapon" rule as articulated in *Battle* and now applied in *Cutbank* treats prior possession of the identical firearm charged in the indictment as intrinsic to the offense of possession. The logic is that possession is not a discrete instant but a continuing state; evidence that a defendant possessed the gun on prior occasions helps establish that he possessed it on the charged date. This reasoning has surface plausibility. If the government must prove knowing possession and the defendant's defense is that he merely stumbled upon the weapon or was framed, evidence that he possessed the same gun previously is probative of ownership and control.

But the doctrine's application in cases like *Cutbank* reveals two significant problems.

The first is temporal indeterminacy. The "handful of times" and "once or twice" that the witnesses described are untethered from any specific date. The testimony establishes only that Cutbank had possessed the rifle at some unspecified points before November 2020. If the prior possession occurred days or even weeks before the charged offense, the inference of continuous possession is reasonably strong. But if the prior observations were months or years earlier, the chain of inference becomes considerably more attenuated. The doctrine as articulated in *Battle* and now *Cutbank* contains no temporal limitation—no outer boundary beyond which prior possession is too remote to qualify as intrinsic. This is a striking omission, given that Rule 404(b) case law routinely treats temporal proximity as a key factor in the balancing analysis. By classifying prior possession as intrinsic regardless of how remote in time, courts effectively immunize the evidence from the very scrutiny that remoteness would ordinarily invite.

The second problem is the evasion of Rule 403 balancing. When evidence is classified as intrinsic, it is generally not subject to the prejudice-versus-probative-value analysis that Rule 403 demands for 404(b) evidence. Yet the concerns that animate Rule 403 are fully present. Testimony that a defendant possessed a sawed-off rifle—a particularly menacing weapon—"a handful of times" before the charged offense invites the jury to infer exactly what Rule 404(b) forbids: that the defendant is the sort of person who carries dangerous weapons and therefore probably committed the charged offense. The probative value of this evidence—establishing ownership and control—is often substantial, but so is the prejudicial effect. In a case like *Cutbank*, where the government also had DNA evidence linking the defendant to the gun and two eyewitnesses who identified the weapon, the marginal probative value of the prior-possession testimony was likely modest. The prejudice, by contrast, was considerable: the jury heard that Cutbank was not merely a felon caught with a gun but a repeat sawed-off-rifle possessor.

## The Harmless Error Safety Valve and Its Limits

The Eighth Circuit's affirmance of the evidentiary rulings did not rest solely on the intrinsic evidence doctrine. The panel also invoked the harmless error doctrine, noting that the government's evidence was "substantial"—DNA on the gun, eyewitness identification, recovery of the weapon along the flight path—and that any error "did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict." This alternative ground for affirmance is common in cases involving evidentiary challenges, and it underscores a structural feature of appellate review in felon-in-possession cases: when the government's physical evidence is strong, courts have little incentive to police the boundary between intrinsic and extrinsic evidence carefully. The defendant would have been convicted regardless, so the doctrinal error—if error there was—becomes harmless.

But harmless error is not a substitute for sound doctrine. The rule announced in *Battle* and applied in *Cutbank* shapes pretrial practice in every felon-in-possession case in the Eighth Circuit, not just those with overwhelming physical evidence. In closer cases—where the gun is recovered without fingerprints, where eyewitness identification is shaky, or where the defense theory is accidental or momentary possession—the automatic admission of prior-possession testimony as intrinsic evidence could tip the balance. And because the evidence is classified as intrinsic, the defense has no meaningful pretrial mechanism to exclude it or to obtain a limiting instruction tailored to its actual probative function.

## Downstream Implications

The *Cutbank* decision, while unpublished in effect if not in form, reinforces a trend that should give defense counsel and district judges pause. The "same weapon" rule as currently articulated treats temporal remoteness as irrelevant, conflates possession with ownership, and bypasses the careful balancing that the Rules of Evidence were designed to ensure. Several circuits have recognized versions of this doctrine, though the precise formulations vary. The Fifth Circuit, for instance, has suggested that prior possession of the same weapon is admissible when it is "not too distant in time," implicitly acknowledging that remoteness matters. The Eighth Circuit's broader formulation—that prior possession is "directly relevant" regardless of temporal proximity—leaves district courts with no analytical tools to exclude even decades-old prior possession of the same firearm.

The better approach would be for courts to treat prior possession of the same weapon as 404(b) evidence subject to a context-sensitive analysis, or at minimum to require district courts to make a finding of temporal proximity before classifying such evidence as intrinsic. The continuous possession theory has genuine evidentiary value, but it should be tested against the realities of each case rather than applied as a categorical rule. When prior possession is close in time to the charged offense, the intrinsic label may well be appropriate. When it is remote, the evidence should pass through the filter of Rule 403 and the notice requirements of Rule 404(b)—not because the evidence is irrelevant, but because the procedures that surround 404(b) exist precisely to ensure that relevant evidence does not become a vehicle for improper propensity reasoning.

None of this changes the outcome in *Cutbank*. The evidence against him was overwhelming, and the within-guidelines sentence of 292 months—driven by his extensive violent history and the circumstances of the offense—was amply supported. But the doctrinal shortcut the panel endorsed is worth watching. In the Eighth Circuit and beyond, the "same weapon" rule has become a settled feature of felon-in-possession prosecutions. The question is whether courts will continue to apply it categorically or begin to ask the harder questions about remoteness, prejudice, and the purpose for which prior-possession evidence is actually offered. *Cutbank* suggests the categorical approach remains very much in force.
