Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Reading Police Reports During Cross-Examination: Authentication Requirements for Impeachment by Prior Inconsistent Statement

When defense counsel cross-examines a witness about prior statements to law enforcement, a natural instinct is to reach for the police report itself—to read the relevant portions into the record and force the witness to reconcile her trial testimony with what she previously said. The Eighth Circuit's recent decision in *United States v. Cutbank* serves as a useful reminder that this instinct can run aground on authentication requirements, and that the rules governing impeachment by prior inconsistent statement do not carve out a special exception for law enforcement reports.

## The Factual and Procedural Background

Justin Cutbank was convicted of being a felon in possession of a firearm and sentenced to 292 months under the Armed Career Criminal Act. The charges stemmed from a harrowing November 2020 incident in which Cutbank, after drinking and using methamphetamine, held his girlfriend at gunpoint in a closet for hours, struck her with the gun, and later barricaded himself in a garage a half mile away, where he bit a police officer and grabbed the officer's weapon during the struggle. DNA on a sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle found along the route between the two locations matched Cutbank.

At trial, the government called C.F., a housemate of the victim, who testified that she had seen Cutbank with the gun used during the incident "once or twice" before. On cross-examination, defense counsel sought to undermine this testimony by confronting C.F. with prior statements she had made to a police investigator. The district court allowed questions about what C.F. said to police but sustained the government's objections when defense counsel attempted to read portions of the resulting police report into the record without proper foundation, because the report was not authored by C.F.

## The Authentication Requirement Under *Cumbie*

The Eighth Circuit affirmed, relying on *United States v. Cumbie*, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022), which holds that "although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated." The panel reviewed the claim—despite some uncertainty about whether it was preserved—and found it lacking "whether preserved or not."

The court's reasoning tracks the structure of the Federal Rules of Evidence. Rule 613(b) permits extrinsic evidence of a witness's prior inconsistent statement, but only if the witness is given an opportunity to explain or deny the statement and an adverse party is given an opportunity to examine the witness about it. Rule 901, however, imposes a separate authentication requirement: to introduce extrinsic evidence, the proponent must produce sufficient evidence to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it is. A police report, as a document authored by a law enforcement officer based on statements made by the witness, does not authenticate itself simply because the witness made the underlying statements. The report is hearsay as to the witness's statements—or at minimum, it is a document whose authorship and accuracy must be established before it can be read to the jury as extrinsic evidence of what the witness previously said.

The distinction the panel draws is important and often overlooked in practice. There is a meaningful difference between asking a witness, "Did you tell Officer Smith on November 20 that you had never seen the gun before?" and reading to the jury from a police report: "The witness stated she had never seen the firearm." The former is classic cross-examination on prior inconsistent statements, testing the witness's credibility through her own responses. The latter introduces a document as extrinsic evidence—and that document must be authenticated, whether through the testimony of its author, a stipulation, or another method satisfying Rule 901.

## The Panel's Criticism of the Government

Worth noting is the panel's pointed rebuke of the government's briefing. The court observed that Cutbank "did not refer to this ruling" in his appellate brief, yet the government's brief argued that Cutbank's "next claim is that the district court 'thwarted' his efforts to cross-examine C.F. on her testimony regarding the gun possession." The panel responded: "We do not appreciate appellees who unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal."

This is a welcome reminder that appellees should resist the temptation to address arguments the appellant did not pursue. The instinct to get ahead of potential objections or to preemptively characterize an appellant's position is understandable, but the Eighth Circuit's reaction illustrates the risks. Injecting issues that the appellant has abandoned or never raised can clutter the appellate record, irritate the panel, and potentially prejudice the appellee's own cause by drawing the court's attention to unfavorable rulings that might otherwise have passed without comment. The better practice is to address the arguments the appellant actually makes and let the court decide what issues are properly before it.

## Doctrinal Implications and Practical Takeaways

The authentication requirement for police reports used in impeachment is not new, but *Cutbank* reinforces it in a context where the temptation to cut corners is strong. Defense counsel frequently encounter situations where a witness's trial testimony differs from what appears in a police report, and the report seems like the most efficient way to establish the inconsistency. But *Cumbie* and *Cutbank* make clear that efficiency cannot substitute for foundation.

Several practical lessons follow. First, defense counsel who wish to use a police report for impeachment should plan their authentication strategy before trial. If the report's author is available, calling the author as a witness—or at least securing a stipulation from the government—can satisfy Rule 901. Second, counsel can often achieve the same impeachment effect through careful questioning of the witness about her prior statements, without introducing the report at all. This approach sidesteps authentication issues entirely and may be more effective, as it forces the witness to confront her own words rather than relying on an officer's recording of them. Third, if the witness denies making the prior statement, Rule 613(b)'s extrinsic evidence door opens—but the authentication requirement still must be met before the report itself comes in.

The broader doctrinal point is that the rules of evidence apply with equal force to impeachment evidence as to substantive evidence. Courts have long recognized that the impeachment context does not create a free-floating exception to hearsay, authentication, or other evidentiary requirements. The Sixth Amendment's Confrontation Clause guarantees the right to cross-examine, but it does not guarantee the right to introduce unauthenticated documents during that cross-examination. Defense counsel must navigate both sets of constraints simultaneously.

## Conclusion

*Cutbank* is unlikely to reshape evidentiary doctrine, but it is a clean illustration of a recurring problem in firearms and domestic violence trials, where witness recollections shift and police reports become the critical point of reference. The Eighth Circuit's message is straightforward: if you want to read from a police report during cross-examination, authenticate it first. And if you are the government, do not manufacture issues the defendant did not raise. Both pieces of advice are sound, and both are too often ignored.
