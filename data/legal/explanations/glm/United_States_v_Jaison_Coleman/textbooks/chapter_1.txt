Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

Chapter 1: # Factual Background and Procedural Posture

## The 911 Domestic Violence Call and Officer Kizer's Arrival at the Coleman Home

In the early morning hours of April 20, 2023, the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department received a 911 call reporting domestic violence at a private residence. The caller was Lisa Coleman's stepdaughter, who reported that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and that he remained inside the family home. The call placed officers in the kind of volatile, unpredictable situation that characterizes domestic violence responses—a context the court itself acknowledged at the outset of its opinion as among the most difficult responsibilities police officers face.

Officer Jamie Kizer was dispatched to the Coleman home. The court's account of what happened next draws from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues, a point the court emphasized by citing *United States v. Norville* for the proposition that video evidence can resolve factual disputes that might otherwise cloud the record. This reliance on body camera footage matters for the court's analysis because it means the court is not merely accepting one party's characterization of events but is reviewing an objective visual and audio record of the encounter.

Upon arriving at the home at approximately 4:00 a.m., Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa Coleman stepped onto the porch to speak with him. She told Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside the home. This initial contact set the stage for a series of exchanges that would ultimately determine whether the officers lawfully entered the home—a question that sits at the center of this case.

## Lisa's Initial Responses and Her First Refusal of Entry

Once on the porch, Lisa's responses to Officer Kizer's questions were marked by hesitation and ambivalence, a pattern the court noted with care. When Officer Kizer asked whether her children were safe, Lisa initially said only that they were in her bedroom. Pressed further—asked whether her children were going to be okay—Lisa responded that she "hope[d] so." These equivocal answers signaled to Officer Kizer that the situation inside the home remained uncertain and potentially dangerous.

Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer then asked where Lisa's husband was located inside the home. Lisa responded that she did not know. At that point, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter the home. Lisa, who had just turned back toward the door, paused and then said, "um … I'd prefer you not."

This initial refusal of entry is significant for two reasons. First, it demonstrates that Lisa understood she had the authority to grant or deny permission to enter—she exercised that authority by declining the officer's first request. Second, it establishes a baseline against which the court later measures the voluntariness of her eventual consent. The court would rely on this moment to conclude that Lisa possessed the capacity to say no and that her later consent was therefore not the product of coercion but a deliberate choice.

After Lisa's refusal, a break in the conversation followed. Lisa told Officer Kizer she needed to go inside to get her shoes. She went inside, and after some time passed without her returning, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times to ask whether she was okay. Approximately a minute later, Lisa returned to the doorway and stepped back outside. Officer Kizer then asked again how things were going inside and, more specifically, asked for the name of her husband—the person he understood had threatened her. Lisa identified him as Jaison, whom the opinion thereafter refers to as Coleman.

## The Critical Second Exchange: Officer Kizer's Statement of Need and Lisa's Consent

The pivotal exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa followed directly. Officer Kizer asked whether Coleman was "more calm right now" and whether the children were okay. Lisa responded haltingly: "I … I … I don't … They're … They're okay right this second … [unintelligible]." Her answer, while tentatively reassuring, conveyed ongoing uncertainty.

Officer Kizer then made the statement that would become the focal point of the legal dispute on appeal: "Okay, um, I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that." Lisa responded, "Okay." Officer Kizer continued, and Lisa volunteered additional information, telling him that the children were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then asked whether the officers would be bitten by dogs if they entered, to which Lisa assured him that the dogs would jump on them but would not bite. Lisa also told Officer Kizer that she believed Coleman was in the back bedroom.

The court understood this exchange as constituting voluntary consent to enter the home. Two features of the interaction proved decisive for the court's analysis. First, the court characterized Officer Kizer's tone and demeanor throughout the encounter as that of someone expressing concern, not someone exerting coercion. Second, the court pointed to Lisa's earlier refusal as evidence that she retained the ability to deny consent and that her subsequent "Okay" represented a genuine choice rather than submission to pressure.

The defendant, Coleman, would later argue on appeal that Officer Kizer's use of the word "need"—in the phrase "we almost need to come inside"—transformed what should have been a request into a directive, thereby rendering Lisa's consent involuntary. The court acknowledged this as a "plenty fair argument" but concluded that it was "too narrow" when viewed against the totality of the circumstances. The court found that Officer Kizer expressed concern for the safety of the children with some urgency but did not cross the line into coercion. Lisa's own responses reinforced this understanding: after saying "Okay," she provided the officers with practical information about the layout of the home and the dogs, conduct consistent with someone facilitating entry rather than acquiescing under duress.

## The Officers' Entry, Their Actions Inside the Home, and Discovery of Information from the Children

Following Lisa's consent, she opened the door for Officer Kizer and his colleagues and actively facilitated their entry by brushing the dogs away from the entrance. She then led the officers to one of the back bedrooms and pointed out where the children were located.

Not seeing Coleman in that bedroom, Officer Kizer asked Lisa where he was. Lisa again responded that she did not know. A quick search of the home resulted in Officer Kizer locating Coleman in a separate bedroom. A brief discussion followed, during which Coleman permitted Officer Kizer to conduct a protective pat-down search of his person.

The officers then spoke with the two children. What the children told the officers would prove critical to the subsequent investigation. The children reported that Lisa and Coleman had been fighting. They described Coleman as loud, aggressive, and mean. More significantly, the children stated that their father had pointed a gun at their mother and had threatened to shoot her and to set the house on fire. In response to the officers' questions, the children confirmed that they had seen the gun, both that night and on prior occasions, but they did not know where the gun was at that moment.

The court would later hold that the officers' conduct inside the home remained within the scope of Lisa's consent. The officers' stated purpose for entering was to check on the welfare of the children and to confirm that Coleman presented no danger to them or anyone else. The court reasoned that accomplishing this purpose necessarily required the officers to scan the home, locate everyone present, and ask basic questions—all of which fell within the boundaries of the consent Lisa had given. The information the officers gathered during this lawful entry supplied the foundation for the next steps in the investigation.

## The Search Warrant, Recovery of Firearms, and Federal Indictment Under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1)

The information the officers obtained after entering the home—particularly the children's accounts of Coleman pointing a gun at Lisa and threatening to shoot her and burn the house—provided the basis for securing a search warrant for the Coleman residence. The officers sought and obtained this warrant, and the subsequent search of the home turned up several firearms.

The recovery of firearms led to a federal indictment charging Coleman with possessing a firearm as a convicted felon, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Section 922(g)(1) is a federal statute that prohibits any person who has been convicted of a felony from possessing a firearm or ammunition that has traveled in or affected interstate commerce. The charge placed Coleman's case in federal court and set the stage for the pretrial motion practice that would determine the admissibility of the firearms recovered from his home.

## Coleman's Motion to Suppress, the Magistrate Judge's Report, and the District Court's Ruling

Before trial, Coleman moved to suppress the firearms recovered from his home. His central argument was that his wife, Lisa, had never consented to Officer Kizer and his colleagues entering the home in the first instance. If the initial entry was unlawful, Coleman contended, then the information the officers gathered inside—including the children's statements about the gun—was the fruit of an unconstitutional search and could not supply probable cause for the warrant. Suppressing the firearms thus depended on establishing that the officers' warrantless entry violated the Fourth Amendment.

The case proceeded to a suppression hearing. A magistrate judge conducted the hearing and issued a report recommending that Coleman's motion be denied. The magistrate judge's key factual finding was that Lisa had consented to the officers' entry into the home. This finding rested on the body camera footage capturing the exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, particularly the moment when Officer Kizer said, "I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that," and Lisa responded, "Okay."

The district court adopted the magistrate judge's recommendation. In doing so, the district court made two key rulings. First, it found that Lisa had consented to the officers' entry, adopting the magistrate judge's factual conclusion. Second, the district court separately rejected Coleman's argument that the officers' conduct inside the home exceeded the scope of the consent Lisa had given. With both the existence and the scope of consent established, the district court denied the motion to suppress, allowing the firearms to be used as evidence against Coleman.

## The Conditional Guilty Plea Preserving the Right to Appeal the Suppression Rule

Following the district court's denial of his motion to suppress, Coleman faced a strategic decision. Rather than proceed to trial and risk conviction on all counts, Coleman chose to plead guilty. Importantly, however, he did so conditionally—specifically, he reserved his right to appeal the district court's suppression ruling.

This procedural device allows a defendant to plead guilty while preserving a specific legal issue for appellate review. By entering a conditional plea, Coleman accepted conviction but maintained his right to challenge the district court's determination that Lisa's consent was voluntary and that the officers stayed within the scope of that consent once inside the home. If the Seventh Circuit were to reverse the suppression ruling, Coleman's plea could be withdrawn and the firearms excluded from evidence, potentially undermining the government's case.

The appeal thus presented the Seventh Circuit with two intertwined questions: whether the district court committed clear error in finding that Lisa voluntarily consented to the officers' entry, and whether the district court likewise erred in finding that the officers' actions inside the home remained within the scope of that consent. The court of appeals would review both findings under the deferential clear-error standard, a point that shaped the entire analytical framework the court applied in reaching its decision to affirm.
