Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

Chapter 3: # Applying the Voluntariness Standard to Lisa's Consent

## The Legal Framework and the Question Presented

Consent is a well-established exception to the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement for searching a home. For consent to be valid, however, it must be "freely and voluntarily" given. Courts assess voluntariness by considering the totality of the circumstances, examining factors such as the person's age, intelligence, and education; whether the person was advised of constitutional rights; the length of detention preceding consent; whether consent was immediate or prompted by repeated requests; whether physical coercion was used; and whether the individual was in police custody at the time. The Seventh Circuit reviews a district court's finding of voluntary consent for clear error—a deferential standard that asks only whether the lower court's factual assessment was plausible, not whether the reviewing court would have reached the same conclusion.

In this case, the critical question was whether Lisa Coleman's "Okay" in response to Officer Kizer's statement that he was "sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure" the children were okay constituted voluntary consent. Jaison Coleman argued it did not, contending that the officer's use of the word "need" transformed the encounter into a coercive one. The court rejected this argument, grounding its analysis in the totality of circumstances visible on the body camera footage.

## Officer Kizer's Tone and Demeanor: Concerned Rather Than Coercive

The court's voluntariness analysis began with a foundational observation drawn directly from the body camera footage: Officer Kizer's tone and demeanor throughout the encounter were those of someone expressing concern, not someone exerting coercion. This distinction matters because the voluntariness inquiry is not a mechanical application of factors but a holistic assessment of whether the interaction was, in substance, a request that the person was free to decline or an assertion of authority that left no real choice.

The court noted that from the moment Officer Kizer arrived at the Coleman residence, he spoke calmly and respectfully to Lisa. His approach reflected the inherently difficult nature of responding to domestic violence calls, where officers must balance genuine safety concerns against constitutional protections. The court recognized that Officer Kizer conveyed his concern for the children's welfare with "some urgency and intensity," but concluded that doing so did not cross the line into coercion. This observation underscores an important principle: an officer's expression of urgency, standing alone, does not invalidate consent. The question is whether the officer's conduct, viewed in context, overbore the individual's free will.

## Lisa's Demonstrated Capacity to Refuse Consent

A cornerstone of the court's analysis was its observation that Lisa had already demonstrated her ability to deny consent to entry. When Officer Kizer first asked if he could enter the home, Lisa responded, "um … I'd prefer you not." This initial refusal was significant to the court for two reasons. First, it showed that Lisa understood she had the power to say no—her will had not been overborne from the outset of the encounter. Second, it established a baseline against which to measure her subsequent consent. The court reasoned that a person who has already declined a request and later accedes to it, without any change in the coercive environment, has made a voluntary choice.

This aspect of the opinion highlights an important doctrinal point: evidence that a person exercised the right to refuse is strong indicia of voluntariness. The court implicitly rejected the notion that an initial refusal, followed by consent, suggests that the second request was inherently more coercive. Instead, the sequence demonstrated Lisa's agency throughout the interaction.

## The Second Request and the Absence of Coercion

The court paid close attention to the dynamics of Officer Kizer's second request for entry. After Lisa's initial refusal, the conversation continued, and the topic arose again when Lisa expressed uncertainty about whether her children were safe. Officer Kizer responded by saying he was "sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that." The court acknowledged that Officer Kizer asked for permission twice, but emphasized that the second request was "unaccompanied by any coercion."

This finding is significant because the voluntariness factors include whether consent was "prompted by repeated requests by the authorities." Repeated requests can, in some circumstances, become so persistent that they effectively wear down a person's resistance. The court, however, drew a line between persistence and coercion. Officer Kizer's second request arose naturally from the flow of conversation—it was prompted by Lisa's own equivocation about her children's safety, not by an officer badgering her for entry. The court noted that while Officer Kizer did not explicitly tell Lisa that she could refuse consent, "the fact that he asked for permission implied as much, as did the fact that she initially denied his request."

## Duration of the Encounter and Lisa's Non-Custodial Status

Two additional factors weighed in favor of voluntariness. First, the entire conversation between Officer Kizer and Lisa lasted only "but a few minutes." The brevity of the encounter undermined any argument that Lisa's consent was the product of a prolonged or grinding interrogation. Second, "at no point was Lisa in custody." Custodial settings carry inherent pressures that can color the voluntariness of consent, but Lisa remained free throughout the interaction—a fact reinforced by her own movements, including stepping inside to get her shoes and returning to the porch.

The court acknowledged that Lisa "may have felt some degree of pressure to let the police enter her home," particularly given the domestic violence context and the early morning hour. But the court drew a critical distinction between pressure and coercion. Some pressure is an inherent feature of any police-citizen encounter; the voluntariness standard requires not the absence of all pressure, but the absence of coercion sufficient to overbear the individual's free will. The court concluded that "at no point did Officer Kizer exert coercion."

## Coleman's Argument: The Word "Need" and the Court's Rejection

Coleman's challenge on appeal focused tightly on Officer Kizer's use of the word "need" in the critical second exchange. The argument was that by saying the officers "almost need to come inside," Officer Kizer effectively communicated that entry was not optional—that it was required, regardless of Lisa's wishes. On this view, Lisa's "Okay" was not consent but submission to an assertion of authority.

The court characterized this as a "plenty fair argument" but ultimately found it "too narrow." The court's reasoning illustrates the difference between parsing a single word in isolation and evaluating consent under the totality of circumstances. While the word "need" could, in some contexts, convey a command, the court examined the word in its full context: Officer Kizer's calm and concerned tone, Lisa's demonstrated ability to refuse, the brevity of the encounter, her non-custodial status, and her responsive conduct. In light of these circumstances, the district court could reasonably find that the word "need," as used by Officer Kizer, did not make his request coercive.

The court cited *United States v. Hicks*, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008), in support of this conclusion, reinforcing the principle that a single word or phrase must be evaluated against the backdrop of the entire interaction. The court acknowledged that Officer Kizer "relayed that concern with some urgency and intensity," but held that the district court "did not clearly err by finding that he did so without overstepping and coercing her consent."

## Lisa's Responsive Conduct as Confirmation

The court found powerful corroborating evidence of voluntariness in Lisa's conduct immediately after saying "Okay." Rather than passively standing aside or appearing resigned, Lisa engaged with Officer Kizer's request in ways that demonstrated she understood it as a request for permission—and that she was granting it. She told the officers where her children were located inside the home. She proactively warned them about her dogs, assuring them that the dogs would jump on them but would not bite. She then opened the door and facilitated the officers' entry by brushing her dogs away. She led the officers to one of the back bedrooms and pointed out where the children were.

The court viewed this sequence of actions as confirming that Lisa heard Officer Kizer's statement as a request—one she chose to grant—rather than as a command she felt compelled to obey. Her conduct was consistent with someone cooperating with a consensual entry, providing practical information to help the officers carry out the purpose for which she had admitted them. This behavioral evidence strengthened the court's conclusion that Lisa's consent was knowing and voluntary.

## The Totality of Circumstances and the Role of Appellate Review

Pulling these threads together, the court affirmed the district court under the deferential clear-error standard. The totality of circumstances—Officer Kizer's concerned demeanor, Lisa's initial refusal demonstrating her capacity to say no, the brief and non-custodial nature of the encounter, the absence of physical coercion, and Lisa's cooperative conduct after granting consent—provided a sufficient basis for the district court's finding of voluntary consent. The court did not hold that the word "need" could never be coercive, nor that every domestic violence encounter yields voluntary consent as a matter of course. Rather, the court held that on this specific record, the district court's finding was not clearly erroneous.

This conclusion also eliminated any need for the court to address the government's alternative arguments—exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery—which would have independently supported the officers' entry. The court's reliance on consent alone, without resort to these doctrines, underscores the strength of its voluntariness finding. The case thus stands as an illustration of how courts apply the totality-of-circumstances test in a nuanced, fact-specific manner, resisting the temptation to resolve the voluntariness question based on a single word or moment, and instead evaluating the full arc of the encounter as captured by the body camera footage.
