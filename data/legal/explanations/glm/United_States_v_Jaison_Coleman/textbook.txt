Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

# Factual Background and Procedural Posture

## The 911 Domestic Violence Call and Officer Kizer's Arrival at the Coleman Home

In the early morning hours of April 20, 2023, the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department received a 911 call reporting domestic violence at a private residence. The caller was Lisa Coleman's stepdaughter, who reported that her stepfather, Jaison Coleman, had threatened to kill her mother, Lisa Coleman, and that he remained inside the family home. The call placed officers in the kind of volatile, unpredictable situation that characterizes domestic violence responses—a context the court itself acknowledged at the outset of its opinion as among the most difficult responsibilities police officers face.

Officer Jamie Kizer was dispatched to the Coleman home. The court's account of what happened next draws from body camera footage recorded by Officer Kizer and his colleagues, a point the court emphasized by citing *United States v. Norville* for the proposition that video evidence can resolve factual disputes that might otherwise cloud the record. This reliance on body camera footage matters for the court's analysis because it means the court is not merely accepting one party's characterization of events but is reviewing an objective visual and audio record of the encounter.

Upon arriving at the home at approximately 4:00 a.m., Officer Kizer knocked on the door and announced himself. Lisa Coleman stepped onto the porch to speak with him. She told Officer Kizer that her son and daughter were inside the home. This initial contact set the stage for a series of exchanges that would ultimately determine whether the officers lawfully entered the home—a question that sits at the center of this case.

## Lisa's Initial Responses and Her First Refusal of Entry

Once on the porch, Lisa's responses to Officer Kizer's questions were marked by hesitation and ambivalence, a pattern the court noted with care. When Officer Kizer asked whether her children were safe, Lisa initially said only that they were in her bedroom. Pressed further—asked whether her children were going to be okay—Lisa responded that she "hope[d] so." These equivocal answers signaled to Officer Kizer that the situation inside the home remained uncertain and potentially dangerous.

Taking a different approach, Officer Kizer then asked where Lisa's husband was located inside the home. Lisa responded that she did not know. At that point, Officer Kizer asked if he could enter the home. Lisa, who had just turned back toward the door, paused and then said, "um … I'd prefer you not."

This initial refusal of entry is significant for two reasons. First, it demonstrates that Lisa understood she had the authority to grant or deny permission to enter—she exercised that authority by declining the officer's first request. Second, it establishes a baseline against which the court later measures the voluntariness of her eventual consent. The court would rely on this moment to conclude that Lisa possessed the capacity to say no and that her later consent was therefore not the product of coercion but a deliberate choice.

After Lisa's refusal, a break in the conversation followed. Lisa told Officer Kizer she needed to go inside to get her shoes. She went inside, and after some time passed without her returning, a concerned Officer Kizer cracked the door and called into the house several times to ask whether she was okay. Approximately a minute later, Lisa returned to the doorway and stepped back outside. Officer Kizer then asked again how things were going inside and, more specifically, asked for the name of her husband—the person he understood had threatened her. Lisa identified him as Jaison, whom the opinion thereafter refers to as Coleman.

## The Critical Second Exchange: Officer Kizer's Statement of Need and Lisa's Consent

The pivotal exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa followed directly. Officer Kizer asked whether Coleman was "more calm right now" and whether the children were okay. Lisa responded haltingly: "I … I … I don't … They're … They're okay right this second … [unintelligible]." Her answer, while tentatively reassuring, conveyed ongoing uncertainty.

Officer Kizer then made the statement that would become the focal point of the legal dispute on appeal: "Okay, um, I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that." Lisa responded, "Okay." Officer Kizer continued, and Lisa volunteered additional information, telling him that the children were in her bedroom. Officer Kizer then asked whether the officers would be bitten by dogs if they entered, to which Lisa assured him that the dogs would jump on them but would not bite. Lisa also told Officer Kizer that she believed Coleman was in the back bedroom.

The court understood this exchange as constituting voluntary consent to enter the home. Two features of the interaction proved decisive for the court's analysis. First, the court characterized Officer Kizer's tone and demeanor throughout the encounter as that of someone expressing concern, not someone exerting coercion. Second, the court pointed to Lisa's earlier refusal as evidence that she retained the ability to deny consent and that her subsequent "Okay" represented a genuine choice rather than submission to pressure.

The defendant, Coleman, would later argue on appeal that Officer Kizer's use of the word "need"—in the phrase "we almost need to come inside"—transformed what should have been a request into a directive, thereby rendering Lisa's consent involuntary. The court acknowledged this as a "plenty fair argument" but concluded that it was "too narrow" when viewed against the totality of the circumstances. The court found that Officer Kizer expressed concern for the safety of the children with some urgency but did not cross the line into coercion. Lisa's own responses reinforced this understanding: after saying "Okay," she provided the officers with practical information about the layout of the home and the dogs, conduct consistent with someone facilitating entry rather than acquiescing under duress.

## The Officers' Entry, Their Actions Inside the Home, and Discovery of Information from the Children

Following Lisa's consent, she opened the door for Officer Kizer and his colleagues and actively facilitated their entry by brushing the dogs away from the entrance. She then led the officers to one of the back bedrooms and pointed out where the children were located.

Not seeing Coleman in that bedroom, Officer Kizer asked Lisa where he was. Lisa again responded that she did not know. A quick search of the home resulted in Officer Kizer locating Coleman in a separate bedroom. A brief discussion followed, during which Coleman permitted Officer Kizer to conduct a protective pat-down search of his person.

The officers then spoke with the two children. What the children told the officers would prove critical to the subsequent investigation. The children reported that Lisa and Coleman had been fighting. They described Coleman as loud, aggressive, and mean. More significantly, the children stated that their father had pointed a gun at their mother and had threatened to shoot her and to set the house on fire. In response to the officers' questions, the children confirmed that they had seen the gun, both that night and on prior occasions, but they did not know where the gun was at that moment.

The court would later hold that the officers' conduct inside the home remained within the scope of Lisa's consent. The officers' stated purpose for entering was to check on the welfare of the children and to confirm that Coleman presented no danger to them or anyone else. The court reasoned that accomplishing this purpose necessarily required the officers to scan the home, locate everyone present, and ask basic questions—all of which fell within the boundaries of the consent Lisa had given. The information the officers gathered during this lawful entry supplied the foundation for the next steps in the investigation.

## The Search Warrant, Recovery of Firearms, and Federal Indictment Under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1)

The information the officers obtained after entering the home—particularly the children's accounts of Coleman pointing a gun at Lisa and threatening to shoot her and burn the house—provided the basis for securing a search warrant for the Coleman residence. The officers sought and obtained this warrant, and the subsequent search of the home turned up several firearms.

The recovery of firearms led to a federal indictment charging Coleman with possessing a firearm as a convicted felon, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Section 922(g)(1) is a federal statute that prohibits any person who has been convicted of a felony from possessing a firearm or ammunition that has traveled in or affected interstate commerce. The charge placed Coleman's case in federal court and set the stage for the pretrial motion practice that would determine the admissibility of the firearms recovered from his home.

## Coleman's Motion to Suppress, the Magistrate Judge's Report, and the District Court's Ruling

Before trial, Coleman moved to suppress the firearms recovered from his home. His central argument was that his wife, Lisa, had never consented to Officer Kizer and his colleagues entering the home in the first instance. If the initial entry was unlawful, Coleman contended, then the information the officers gathered inside—including the children's statements about the gun—was the fruit of an unconstitutional search and could not supply probable cause for the warrant. Suppressing the firearms thus depended on establishing that the officers' warrantless entry violated the Fourth Amendment.

The case proceeded to a suppression hearing. A magistrate judge conducted the hearing and issued a report recommending that Coleman's motion be denied. The magistrate judge's key factual finding was that Lisa had consented to the officers' entry into the home. This finding rested on the body camera footage capturing the exchange between Officer Kizer and Lisa, particularly the moment when Officer Kizer said, "I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that," and Lisa responded, "Okay."

The district court adopted the magistrate judge's recommendation. In doing so, the district court made two key rulings. First, it found that Lisa had consented to the officers' entry, adopting the magistrate judge's factual conclusion. Second, the district court separately rejected Coleman's argument that the officers' conduct inside the home exceeded the scope of the consent Lisa had given. With both the existence and the scope of consent established, the district court denied the motion to suppress, allowing the firearms to be used as evidence against Coleman.

## The Conditional Guilty Plea Preserving the Right to Appeal the Suppression Rule

Following the district court's denial of his motion to suppress, Coleman faced a strategic decision. Rather than proceed to trial and risk conviction on all counts, Coleman chose to plead guilty. Importantly, however, he did so conditionally—specifically, he reserved his right to appeal the district court's suppression ruling.

This procedural device allows a defendant to plead guilty while preserving a specific legal issue for appellate review. By entering a conditional plea, Coleman accepted conviction but maintained his right to challenge the district court's determination that Lisa's consent was voluntary and that the officers stayed within the scope of that consent once inside the home. If the Seventh Circuit were to reverse the suppression ruling, Coleman's plea could be withdrawn and the firearms excluded from evidence, potentially undermining the government's case.

The appeal thus presented the Seventh Circuit with two intertwined questions: whether the district court committed clear error in finding that Lisa voluntarily consented to the officers' entry, and whether the district court likewise erred in finding that the officers' actions inside the home remained within the scope of that consent. The court of appeals would review both findings under the deferential clear-error standard, a point that shaped the entire analytical framework the court applied in reaching its decision to affirm.

# Legal Framework: The Fourth Amendment Consent Doctrine

## Consent as a Well-Established Exception to the Fourth Amendment's Warrant Requirement

The Fourth Amendment generally requires police to obtain a warrant before searching a home. But the Supreme Court has long recognized that consent is an exception to that warrant requirement. When a person voluntarily agrees to let officers enter or search, no warrant is needed. In *Coleman*, both parties accepted this basic proposition, and the court treated it as settled law. The dispute was not about whether consent *can* function as an exception to the warrant requirement, but about whether Lisa Coleman's interactions with Officer Kizer amounted to valid consent under the particular circumstances of this case. This distinction matters: the consent exception is not itself controversial in this opinion. The analytical work is done at the next level—determining whether consent was actually given, whether it was voluntary, and whether officers stayed within its scope.

## Third-Party Consent: A Person with Common Authority May Consent to Search Shared Premises

A critical feature of *Coleman* is that the person who allegedly consented to the officers' entry was not the defendant. Lisa Coleman, not Jaison Coleman, was the one who opened the door and spoke with Officer Kizer. The court explained that this posed no legal obstacle to using Lisa's consent to justify the entry, because a third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy. The court cited *United States v. Matlock*, 415 U.S. 164 (1974), the foundational Supreme Court case establishing this principle. Under the Matlock doctrine, when two or more people share common authority over premises—meaning each has the right to use and permit access to the space—either one may consent to a search, and that consent is valid against the other. Importantly, the defendant may still challenge the validity of the third party's consent. In *Coleman*, this meant Jaison could contest whether Lisa's consent was voluntary and whether officers exceeded its scope, even though it was Lisa—not Jaison—who purported to give permission. The court confirmed this principle by citing *United States v. Cellitti*, 387 F.3d 618, 621 (7th Cir. 2004), which applied Matlock in the Seventh Circuit. The practical effect of third-party consent doctrine here was significant: because Lisa shared the home with Coleman, her consent could authorize the officers' entry, and any evidence discovered as a result could be used against him.

## The Voluntariness Requirement: Consent Must Be Freely and Voluntarily Given

Not every expression of permission constitutes valid consent. The Supreme Court has held that consent must be "freely and voluntarily" given to satisfy the Fourth Amendment. The court in *Coleman* cited *Bumper v. North Carolina*, 391 U.S. 543, 548 (1968), for this standard. The core idea is that consent is not valid if it is the product of coercion—whether explicit or implicit. A person who acquiesces to a claim of lawful authority, for example, has not truly consented; she has merely submitted. In *Coleman*, the voluntariness question was central. Coleman argued that Officer Kizer's statement—"I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that"—was coercive, particularly because of the word "need." The court acknowledged this was a "plenty fair argument" but ultimately concluded that the district court did not clearly err in finding Lisa's consent voluntary. The key was that Officer Kizer's tone conveyed concern, not command, and that Lisa had already demonstrated her ability to refuse when she said "I'd prefer you not" to his earlier request. The voluntariness requirement thus functions as a safeguard: even when consent appears on the surface, courts must look beneath the surface to determine whether it was genuinely freely given.

## The Totality-of-the-Circumstances Test and the Six Illustrative Voluntariness Factors

To assess whether consent was voluntary, courts do not apply a rigid checklist but instead examine the totality of the circumstances. The court in *Coleman* cited *United States v. Han*, 105 F.4th 986, 991 (7th Cir. 2024), for this proposition. Within that totality-of-the-circumstances inquiry, the Seventh Circuit has identified six illustrative factors, drawn from *United States v. Figueroa-Espana*, 511 F.3d 696, 705 (7th Cir. 2007): (1) the person's age, intelligence, and education; (2) whether the person was advised of their constitutional rights; (3) how long the person was detained before giving consent; (4) whether consent was immediate or prompted by repeated requests; (5) whether any physical coercion was used; and (6) whether the individual was in police custody when they gave consent. These factors are illustrative, not exhaustive, and no single factor is dispositive. In *Coleman*, the court's voluntariness analysis did not mechanically march through each factor, but the opinion's reasoning tracked several of them. The court noted that the entire conversation lasted only a few minutes (factor three), that Officer Kizer asked twice but without physical coercion (factors four and five), and that Lisa was never in custody (factor six). The court also emphasized Lisa's demonstrated capacity to refuse as evidence that her consent was not the product of coercion. This illustrates how the factors operate in practice: they guide the court's assessment of the overall situation rather than functioning as a formula.

## Scope of Consent Evaluated Under an Objective Reasonableness Standard

Even when consent is voluntary, it has limits. A search is constitutional only if it remains within the scope of the consent given. The court in *Coleman* cited *United States v. Saucedo*, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012), for this principle. To determine the scope of consent, courts apply an objective reasonableness standard, asking what the typical reasonable person would have understood by the exchange between the officer and the person giving consent. The court cited *Florida v. Jimeno*, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991), for this standard. The inquiry is not subjective—courts do not ask what the officer actually intended or what the person actually meant—but rather what a reasonable person would have understood the consent to encompass. In *Coleman*, the officers' stated purpose for entering was to check on the welfare of Lisa's children and to confirm that Coleman posed no danger. The court found that this purpose necessarily required the officers to scan the home, locate everyone present, and ask basic questions. Lisa had told the officers where the children and Coleman were located, and she facilitated their entry by opening the door and brushing the dogs aside. Under the objective reasonableness standard, the court concluded that the officers' actions inside the home—canvassing the rooms, finding Coleman, conducting a protective pat-down, and speaking with the children—fell within the scope of what Lisa had authorized. The information gathered during this welfare check then formed the basis for the search warrant that ultimately led to the firearms. The scope analysis thus asks whether the officers' actual conduct matched what a reasonable person would have understood they were permitted to do.

## The Role of Body Camera Footage in Resolving Factual Disputes About Consent

A notable feature of *Coleman* is the court's reliance on body camera footage. The court noted that the pertinent facts came from the body cameras worn by Officer Kizer and his colleagues, citing *United States v. Norville*, 43 F.4th 680, 682 (7th Cir. 2022), which in turn cited *Scott v. Harris*, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007), for the proposition that video evidence can "evaporate any factual dispute that would otherwise exist." This observation is significant for consent cases because voluntariness often turns on subtle factors: tone of voice, body language, timing, and the precise sequence of questions and responses. In *Coleman*, the court relied on the footage to make several key factual findings: that Officer Kizer's tone was calm and respectful, that Lisa demonstrated understanding throughout, and that Lisa had refused the initial request for entry. Without the video, these facts might have been contested, and the court's deferential standard of review would have made it harder to second-guess the district court. The video allowed the appellate court to see for itself what happened and to confirm that the district court's findings were not clearly erroneous. Body camera footage thus plays an increasingly important role in consent litigation, providing an objective record that can either support or undermine a district court's factual findings about what occurred during a police-citizen encounter.

## Standard of Review: Clear Error for Findings of Voluntary Consent and Scope

The final piece of the legal framework is the standard of appellate review. The court in *Coleman* stated that it reviews a district court's finding of voluntary consent and any challenge to the scope of consent for clear error, citing *United States v. Jones*, 22 F.4th 667, 675 (7th Cir. 2022). Under the clear-error standard, an appellate court does not reweigh the evidence or substitute its own judgment for that of the district court. A factual finding is clearly erroneous only when the appellate court is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake was made. This deferential standard reflects the principle that the district court, having presided over the suppression hearing and evaluated the evidence firsthand, is in a better position to assess the facts. In *Coleman*, this standard shaped the court's analysis in an important way. The court did not ask whether it would have found Lisa's consent voluntary if it were deciding the question in the first instance. Instead, it asked whether the district court's finding of voluntariness was clearly erroneous. Given the video evidence showing Officer Kizer's calm demeanor, Lisa's ability to refuse, and the short duration of the encounter, the court concluded that the district court's finding was reasonable and supported by the record. The same deference applied to the scope question: the court asked whether the district court clearly erred in finding that the officers stayed within the scope of Lisa's consent, and it concluded that the finding was sound. The clear-error standard thus operates as a structural constraint on appellate review in consent cases, ensuring that the district court's factual determinations are disturbed only when they are demonstrably wrong.

# Applying the Voluntariness Standard to Lisa's Consent

## The Legal Framework and the Question Presented

Consent is a well-established exception to the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement for searching a home. For consent to be valid, however, it must be "freely and voluntarily" given. Courts assess voluntariness by considering the totality of the circumstances, examining factors such as the person's age, intelligence, and education; whether the person was advised of constitutional rights; the length of detention preceding consent; whether consent was immediate or prompted by repeated requests; whether physical coercion was used; and whether the individual was in police custody at the time. The Seventh Circuit reviews a district court's finding of voluntary consent for clear error—a deferential standard that asks only whether the lower court's factual assessment was plausible, not whether the reviewing court would have reached the same conclusion.

In this case, the critical question was whether Lisa Coleman's "Okay" in response to Officer Kizer's statement that he was "sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure" the children were okay constituted voluntary consent. Jaison Coleman argued it did not, contending that the officer's use of the word "need" transformed the encounter into a coercive one. The court rejected this argument, grounding its analysis in the totality of circumstances visible on the body camera footage.

## Officer Kizer's Tone and Demeanor: Concerned Rather Than Coercive

The court's voluntariness analysis began with a foundational observation drawn directly from the body camera footage: Officer Kizer's tone and demeanor throughout the encounter were those of someone expressing concern, not someone exerting coercion. This distinction matters because the voluntariness inquiry is not a mechanical application of factors but a holistic assessment of whether the interaction was, in substance, a request that the person was free to decline or an assertion of authority that left no real choice.

The court noted that from the moment Officer Kizer arrived at the Coleman residence, he spoke calmly and respectfully to Lisa. His approach reflected the inherently difficult nature of responding to domestic violence calls, where officers must balance genuine safety concerns against constitutional protections. The court recognized that Officer Kizer conveyed his concern for the children's welfare with "some urgency and intensity," but concluded that doing so did not cross the line into coercion. This observation underscores an important principle: an officer's expression of urgency, standing alone, does not invalidate consent. The question is whether the officer's conduct, viewed in context, overbore the individual's free will.

## Lisa's Demonstrated Capacity to Refuse Consent

A cornerstone of the court's analysis was its observation that Lisa had already demonstrated her ability to deny consent to entry. When Officer Kizer first asked if he could enter the home, Lisa responded, "um … I'd prefer you not." This initial refusal was significant to the court for two reasons. First, it showed that Lisa understood she had the power to say no—her will had not been overborne from the outset of the encounter. Second, it established a baseline against which to measure her subsequent consent. The court reasoned that a person who has already declined a request and later accedes to it, without any change in the coercive environment, has made a voluntary choice.

This aspect of the opinion highlights an important doctrinal point: evidence that a person exercised the right to refuse is strong indicia of voluntariness. The court implicitly rejected the notion that an initial refusal, followed by consent, suggests that the second request was inherently more coercive. Instead, the sequence demonstrated Lisa's agency throughout the interaction.

## The Second Request and the Absence of Coercion

The court paid close attention to the dynamics of Officer Kizer's second request for entry. After Lisa's initial refusal, the conversation continued, and the topic arose again when Lisa expressed uncertainty about whether her children were safe. Officer Kizer responded by saying he was "sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that." The court acknowledged that Officer Kizer asked for permission twice, but emphasized that the second request was "unaccompanied by any coercion."

This finding is significant because the voluntariness factors include whether consent was "prompted by repeated requests by the authorities." Repeated requests can, in some circumstances, become so persistent that they effectively wear down a person's resistance. The court, however, drew a line between persistence and coercion. Officer Kizer's second request arose naturally from the flow of conversation—it was prompted by Lisa's own equivocation about her children's safety, not by an officer badgering her for entry. The court noted that while Officer Kizer did not explicitly tell Lisa that she could refuse consent, "the fact that he asked for permission implied as much, as did the fact that she initially denied his request."

## Duration of the Encounter and Lisa's Non-Custodial Status

Two additional factors weighed in favor of voluntariness. First, the entire conversation between Officer Kizer and Lisa lasted only "but a few minutes." The brevity of the encounter undermined any argument that Lisa's consent was the product of a prolonged or grinding interrogation. Second, "at no point was Lisa in custody." Custodial settings carry inherent pressures that can color the voluntariness of consent, but Lisa remained free throughout the interaction—a fact reinforced by her own movements, including stepping inside to get her shoes and returning to the porch.

The court acknowledged that Lisa "may have felt some degree of pressure to let the police enter her home," particularly given the domestic violence context and the early morning hour. But the court drew a critical distinction between pressure and coercion. Some pressure is an inherent feature of any police-citizen encounter; the voluntariness standard requires not the absence of all pressure, but the absence of coercion sufficient to overbear the individual's free will. The court concluded that "at no point did Officer Kizer exert coercion."

## Coleman's Argument: The Word "Need" and the Court's Rejection

Coleman's challenge on appeal focused tightly on Officer Kizer's use of the word "need" in the critical second exchange. The argument was that by saying the officers "almost need to come inside," Officer Kizer effectively communicated that entry was not optional—that it was required, regardless of Lisa's wishes. On this view, Lisa's "Okay" was not consent but submission to an assertion of authority.

The court characterized this as a "plenty fair argument" but ultimately found it "too narrow." The court's reasoning illustrates the difference between parsing a single word in isolation and evaluating consent under the totality of circumstances. While the word "need" could, in some contexts, convey a command, the court examined the word in its full context: Officer Kizer's calm and concerned tone, Lisa's demonstrated ability to refuse, the brevity of the encounter, her non-custodial status, and her responsive conduct. In light of these circumstances, the district court could reasonably find that the word "need," as used by Officer Kizer, did not make his request coercive.

The court cited *United States v. Hicks*, 539 F.3d 566, 570–71 (7th Cir. 2008), in support of this conclusion, reinforcing the principle that a single word or phrase must be evaluated against the backdrop of the entire interaction. The court acknowledged that Officer Kizer "relayed that concern with some urgency and intensity," but held that the district court "did not clearly err by finding that he did so without overstepping and coercing her consent."

## Lisa's Responsive Conduct as Confirmation

The court found powerful corroborating evidence of voluntariness in Lisa's conduct immediately after saying "Okay." Rather than passively standing aside or appearing resigned, Lisa engaged with Officer Kizer's request in ways that demonstrated she understood it as a request for permission—and that she was granting it. She told the officers where her children were located inside the home. She proactively warned them about her dogs, assuring them that the dogs would jump on them but would not bite. She then opened the door and facilitated the officers' entry by brushing her dogs away. She led the officers to one of the back bedrooms and pointed out where the children were.

The court viewed this sequence of actions as confirming that Lisa heard Officer Kizer's statement as a request—one she chose to grant—rather than as a command she felt compelled to obey. Her conduct was consistent with someone cooperating with a consensual entry, providing practical information to help the officers carry out the purpose for which she had admitted them. This behavioral evidence strengthened the court's conclusion that Lisa's consent was knowing and voluntary.

## The Totality of Circumstances and the Role of Appellate Review

Pulling these threads together, the court affirmed the district court under the deferential clear-error standard. The totality of circumstances—Officer Kizer's concerned demeanor, Lisa's initial refusal demonstrating her capacity to say no, the brief and non-custodial nature of the encounter, the absence of physical coercion, and Lisa's cooperative conduct after granting consent—provided a sufficient basis for the district court's finding of voluntary consent. The court did not hold that the word "need" could never be coercive, nor that every domestic violence encounter yields voluntary consent as a matter of course. Rather, the court held that on this specific record, the district court's finding was not clearly erroneous.

This conclusion also eliminated any need for the court to address the government's alternative arguments—exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery—which would have independently supported the officers' entry. The court's reliance on consent alone, without resort to these doctrines, underscores the strength of its voluntariness finding. The case thus stands as an illustration of how courts apply the totality-of-circumstances test in a nuanced, fact-specific manner, resisting the temptation to resolve the voluntariness question based on a single word or moment, and instead evaluating the full arc of the encounter as captured by the body camera footage.

# Scope of Consent, Alternative Theories, and Disposition

## The Officers' Stated Purpose: Checking the Welfare of the Children

Once the court concluded that Lisa voluntarily consented to the officers' entry into the Coleman home, it turned to a second question: did the officers stay within the boundaries of that consent once inside? This inquiry is governed by the principle that even voluntary consent has limits. The search that follows must respect "the scope of consent." United States v. Saucedo, 688 F.3d 863, 865 (7th Cir. 2012). The proper measure of that scope is "objective reasonableness"—a court asks what a typical reasonable person would have understood by the exchange between the officer and the person giving consent. Florida v. Jimeno, 500 U.S. 248, 251 (1991).

Here, the court anchored its scope analysis to the officers' stated purpose. Officer Kizer had expressed concern for the safety of Lisa's children, telling her, "I'm sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that." Lisa responded, "Okay," and then provided practical information about the layout of the home, the location of the children, and the temperament of the dogs. The objective, reasonable understanding of this exchange was that the officers were being permitted to enter to check on the children's welfare. That was the purpose Lisa understood and, critically, the purpose the officers articulated. The court's analysis of scope thus began by identifying this specific objective as the yardstick against which the officers' subsequent actions would be measured.

## Whether Scanning the Home, Locating Occupants, and Asking Basic Questions Fell Within the Scope of Consent

The court then evaluated whether the specific actions the officers took inside the home were consistent with that purpose. Upon entry, Lisa led the officers to one of the back bedrooms and pointed out where the children were. Coleman was not immediately visible. Officer Kizer asked where he was, and Lisa said she did not know. The officers then conducted what the court described as a "quick canvas of the home," which resulted in Officer Kizer finding Coleman in a separate bedroom.

The court had little difficulty concluding that these actions fell within the scope of Lisa's consent. Checking on the welfare of the children necessarily required the officers to do more than stand in the doorway. They needed to scan the home to locate everyone present, including Coleman, who was the alleged source of the threat that prompted the 911 call in the first place. Confirming that Coleman presented no danger to the children or anyone else was central to the welfare check that Lisa had authorized. The officers also asked some basic questions—of Lisa, of Coleman, and of the children. The court treated these as natural and limited extensions of the consent entry, not as a general search of the premises.

It is important for students to appreciate what the court was not saying. The court was not holding that consent to enter a home for a welfare check gives officers carte blanche to search wherever they please. The scope of consent is bounded by what a reasonable person would understand the consent to cover. Here, the officers' actions—locating the occupants, confirming their safety, and asking basic questions—were directly tied to the welfare check that Lisa had authorized. Had the officers opened drawers, searched closets, or conducted a room-by-room search for evidence, the analysis would likely have been different. But the record showed a limited, purpose-driven canvas of the home, and the court found no error in the district court's conclusion that the officers respected the scope of Lisa's consent.

## The Protective Pat-Down of Coleman and Information Gathered from the Children

Once the officers located Coleman, a brief discussion followed. Coleman permitted Officer Kizer to conduct a protective pat-down. The opinion does not dwell on the legal basis for this pat-down, noting only that Coleman "permitting" it suggests it, too, rested on consent. For purposes of the scope analysis, the pat-down was not a search of the home but rather a limited physical encounter with a specific individual who was the subject of the domestic violence call and who was located during the welfare check.

More significant was the information the officers gathered from the children. In talking to them, the officers learned that Lisa and Coleman had been fighting, with Coleman reportedly being loud, aggressive, and mean. The children added that their father had pointed a gun at their mother and threatened to shoot her and to light the house on fire. The children confirmed they saw the gun, both that night and before, but did not know where it was at that moment.

This information is critical to the subsequent procedural history. The officers did not find firearms during the welfare check or the protective pat-down. What they found was information—statements from the children describing a gun being pointed at their mother and threats of violence. This information, gathered during a consensual entry that the court upheld, became the foundation for the next step in the investigation.

## How Information Obtained During the Entry Led to the Search Warrant and Recovery of Firearms

The information the officers acquired during their consensual entry—the children's accounts of domestic violence, the sight of a gun, and Coleman's threats—was then used to seek a search warrant. The officers applied for and secured a warrant to search the Coleman home. That warrant-authorized search turned up several guns. In time, the discovery of those firearms led to a federal indictment charging Coleman with possessing a firearm as a convicted felon, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1).

This sequence illustrates an important chain of causation that the suppression motion was designed to test. Coleman's argument was that if Lisa had never validly consented to the officers' entry, then all the information they gathered inside—including the children's statements about the gun—was the fruit of an unlawful entry. Under the exclusionary rule, evidence derived from an unconstitutional search is typically suppressed as "fruit of the poisonous tree." But because the court upheld the district court's finding that Lisa did consent and that the officers stayed within the scope of that consent, the information gathered during the entry was lawfully obtained. That lawfully obtained information provided probable cause for the warrant. The warrant-authorized search of the home, and the firearms recovered pursuant to it, therefore rested on a lawful foundation. The motion to suppress was properly denied.

## The Government's Alternative Arguments: Exigent Circumstances and Inevitable Discovery

The government, as is common in suppression cases, advanced alternative theories in case the court rejected its primary argument on consent. Specifically, the government contended that the doctrines of exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery also supported the officers' entry into and initial sweep of the Coleman residence.

Exigent circumstances constitute an exception to the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement. When law enforcement faces a situation requiring immediate action—such as a risk of physical harm to someone inside a home—officers may enter without a warrant. The classic example is a response to an ongoing emergency, such as a domestic violence call where there is reason to believe someone is in danger. Given the 911 call reporting that Coleman had threatened to kill Lisa, and the officers' observations suggesting possible danger to the children, this theory had surface appeal.

Inevitable discovery is a related but distinct doctrine. Under this rule, evidence obtained through an unlawful search may still be admitted if the government can show that the evidence would have been discovered lawfully anyway, even without the constitutional violation. The doctrine operates as an exception to the fruit-of-the-poisonous-tree rule. The government's theory, presumably, was that even without Lisa's consent, the officers would have entered under exigent circumstances and discovered the same information, or that the firearms would have been found through some other lawful means.

## Why the Court Declined to Reach the Alternative Theories Given Its Ruling on Consent

The court declined to address either alternative theory. Its reasoning was straightforward and reflects a standard principle of judicial restraint: when a court resolves a case on one ground, it need not reach alternative arguments. Because the court affirmed the district court's finding that Lisa consented to the officers' entry and that the officers acted within the scope of that consent, the government's primary argument sufficed. There was no need to consider whether exigent circumstances or inevitable discovery independently supported the entry and sweep.

This is a common and important practice in appellate decision-making. Courts generally avoid deciding constitutional questions or reaching alternative legal theories when the case can be resolved on a narrower basis. The principle conserves judicial resources, avoids unnecessary constitutional rulings, and leaves doctrinal questions for cases where they must be decided. Here, the consent ruling was dispositive: it validated the officers' entry, which in turn validated the information gathering that led to the warrant, which in turn validated the recovery of the firearms. The alternative theories, however plausible, were simply unnecessary to the court's holding.

Students should note that the court's decision not to reach these alternative theories does not mean they lack merit. The court offered no opinion on whether exigent circumstances existed or whether the firearms would inevitably have been discovered. It simply did not need to decide those questions. This leaves those doctrines untested on this particular factual record.

## The Court's Affirmance and Closing Observation on the Officers' Decision-Making in Difficult Circumstances

Having concluded that the district court did not clearly err in finding that Lisa consented to the officers' entry and that the officers did not exceed the scope of that consent, the court affirmed the denial of Coleman's motion to suppress. Because Coleman had pleaded guilty while reserving his right to challenge the suppression ruling, the affirmance of that ruling also affirmed his conviction.

The court closed with a broader observation about the officers' conduct. Judge Scudder noted that "Officer Kizer and his colleagues exhibited sound and balanced decision making in delicate and difficult circumstances." This observation should be read in context. The opinion opened by acknowledging that responding to 911 calls of domestic violence is one of the most difficult responsibilities of police officers. The court's closing remark ties back to that opening, suggesting that the legal outcome in this case was driven not only by doctrine but also by the court's assessment that the officers navigated a volatile situation with appropriate restraint.

For law students, the closing observation offers a reminder that Fourth Amendment doctrine is not applied in a vacuum. Courts evaluate the reasonableness of police conduct against the backdrop of real-world circumstances. The totality-of-the-circumstances test for voluntariness, the objective reasonableness standard for scope, and the clear error standard of review all invite courts to consider the context in which officers operate. Here, the court found that Officer Kizer's tone, his repeated but respectful requests, his concern for the children's safety, and his limited actions inside the home all reflected measured decision-making. The legal conclusion followed from those factual assessments. The court's affirmance, in the end, was both a doctrinal ruling and an implicit endorsement of how the officers handled a genuinely difficult situation.
