Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 4: # The APA Challenge and the Statutory Mandate for Refugee Resettlement Services Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522

## The Statutory Framework of Section 1522(a)(1)(A): Mandatory Language and Appropriations

The central legal question in this portion of the *Pacito v. Trump* order is whether 8 U.S.C. § 1522 creates a mandatory obligation on the federal government to provide refugee resettlement services, or whether it merely authorizes the government to do so at its discretion. The panel majority concluded that the government was not likely to prevail on at least one of the plaintiffs' challenges under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), and the foundation for that conclusion lies in the text of § 1522.

Section 1522(a)(1)(A) states that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement—an office within the Department of Health and Human Services—"to the extent of available appropriations" shall carry out several enumerated functions. The use of the word "shall" is significant. In statutory interpretation, "shall" is generally understood as mandatory, imposing a duty on the actor to whom it is directed, as distinguished from "may," which is permissive. The panel majority's reasoning implicitly treats the "shall" in § 1522(a)(1)(A) as imposing binding obligations on the Director, subject only to the qualifier "to the extent of available appropriations."

That qualifier— "to the extent of available appropriations"—is a standard congressional formulation that limits the scope of a statutory mandate to the funds Congress has actually appropriated for the purpose. It does not, in the panel's view, convert the provision into a discretionary grant of authority. Rather, it cabins the mandate: the government must provide the specified services, but only up to the amount Congress has made available. If appropriations are insufficient to cover all eligible recipients, the government may allocate resources accordingly, but it cannot simply decline to provide the services at all so long as appropriated funds exist.

This interpretive dispute—that is, whether § 1522 is mandatory or permissive—is the fault line between the panel majority and Judge Lee's partial dissent. Judge Lee reads the title of § 1522, "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees," as signaling that the provision is permissive. He argues that "authorization" connotes permission rather than command, and that the specific funding provisions in subsections (b) through (e) use the phrase "the Director is authorized," which he treats as a signal of executive discretion. The panel majority, by contrast, focuses on the "shall" language in subsection (a) and the specific obligations enumerated there, treating the authorization language in the subsections as describing the mechanism for fulfilling the mandatory duties imposed by subsection (a).

## Specific Statutory Obligations Under Section 1522(a)(1)(A)

Section 1522(a)(1)(A) enumerates four specific obligations the Director must fulfill, to the extent of available appropriations. First, the Director must "make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible." This provision reflects Congress's policy goal that refugees should transition to economic independence, and it places on the Director the affirmative responsibility to ensure that adequate employment-related resources are available.

Second, the Director must "provide refugees with the opportunity to acquire sufficient English language training to enable them to become effectively resettled as quickly as possible." This obligation targets linguistic integration, again with the objective of rapid and effective resettlement. Third, the Director must "insure that cash assistance is made available to refugees in such a manner as not to discourage their economic self-sufficiency." This provision addresses direct financial support, but it includes a conditioning principle: cash assistance must be structured so as not to undermine the goal of self-sufficiency. Fourth, the Director must "insure that women have the same opportunities as men to participate in training and instruction," an anti-discrimination provision ensuring gender equity in access to the programs.

The panel majority treated these as concrete, mandatory duties. The opinion lists them in full and then proceeds to analyze the government's conduct against them. The significance of enumerating these specific obligations is that they define the content of the "reception and placement" services that the government is required to provide. The panel's later directive—that the government reinstate cooperative agreements "necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522"—draws its substantive content from these enumerated functions and the related provisions discussed below.

## The Director's Duty to Unaccompanied Minor Refugees Under Section 1522(d)(2)(B)

Section 1522(d)(2)(B) addresses the treatment of unaccompanied minor refugees—children who arrive in the United States without a parent or guardian. The provision states that "[d]uring any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child's immediate care."

The panel majority cited this provision as further evidence of mandatory statutory obligations. The use of "shall" signals a duty, and the provision specifies both legal and financial responsibility for the child. The phrase "if necessary" introduces a degree of conditional discretion—it is only when such responsibility is necessary that the duty attaches—but the majority treated the provision as part of the broader statutory scheme mandating services.

Judge Lee, in dissent, argued that this provision "drips with deference to the executive branch and invokes no firm mandate." He emphasized the word "necessary" and noted that the section also uses the word "authorized," which he reads as permissive. He also observed that it was unclear from the record whether any unaccompanied refugee children were actually at issue in the case. This last point is procedurally significant: if no unaccompanied minor refugees are affected by the government's actions, the provision may not have operative relevance to the plaintiffs' claims, even if it is part of the broader statutory context the majority relies upon.

## The Secretary's Duty Under Section 1522(e)(7)(A) to Develop Alternative Projects for Interim Support

Section 1522(e)(7)(A) imposes a duty on the Secretary of Health and Human Services (rather than the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement) to "develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed." The panel majority cited this provision as another example of mandatory language in § 1522, noting that the Secretary "shall develop and implement" the described projects.

The phrase "as needed" functions similarly to the "if necessary" language in § 1522(d)(2)(B): it introduces a degree of situational discretion into the obligation. The government must develop and implement alternative projects, but the scope and intensity of the services provided—interim support, medical services, support services, and case management—depends on the needs of the refugees. Judge Lee read the "as needed" language as conferring executive discretion over the "metes and bounds" of the programs, arguing that this discretion is inconsistent with a firm congressional mandate. The panel majority, however, appears to have treated "as needed" as qualifying the level of services required, not as eliminating the duty to provide services altogether.

## Section 1523: Congressional Accountability Through Annual Reporting

The panel also referenced 8 U.S.C. § 1523, which requires the Secretary to submit "a detailed report on activities under this subchapter to the Committees on the Judiciary of the House of Representatives and of the Senate not later than January 31 following the end of each fiscal year." This reporting requirement is part of the statutory scheme that the panel invoked to support its characterization of § 1522 as creating binding obligations. The logic is that a reporting requirement presupposes activities to report: if the executive branch were free to simply decline to operate the programs authorized under § 1522, the reporting requirement would have little purpose. While the opinion does not dwell on § 1523 at length, its inclusion reinforces the majority's reading of the overall statutory framework as one in which Congress expected and required the executive branch to carry out refugee resettlement functions, subject to the availability of appropriations.

## Congressional Appropriations for FY 2025

A critical element of the panel's analysis is the existence of appropriated funds. The opinion notes that for FY 2025, Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance. This appropriation was reduced by $800 million under the Rescissions Act of 2025, leaving approximately $2.378 billion. The panel's citation of these figures directly addresses the "to the extent of available appropriations" qualifier in § 1522(a)(1)(A). Because Congress had in fact appropriated substantial funds for refugee assistance, the qualifier does not excuse the government from its statutory obligations on the ground that no funds were available. The appropriated funds exist; the question is whether the government may decline to use them for the purposes Congress designated.

This is a crucial analytical move. If no funds had been appropriated, the government's obligation under § 1522 might be purely theoretical—an unfunded mandate with no practical consequence. But the existence of a substantial appropriation makes the statutory duty operative. The government cannot invoke the appropriations qualifier as a reason to suspend services when Congress has already provided the funding. The panel's recitation of the appropriation figures thus closes a potential gap in the plaintiffs' claim and supports the conclusion that the government is not likely to prevail on this APA challenge.

## Cooperative Agreements as the Mechanism for Providing Reception and Placement Services

Section 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) provides that the resettlement services required under § 1522 may be provided "through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies." The panel noted that the United States has historically entered into cooperative agreements with various resettlement organizations to provide these services. The organizational plaintiffs in this case are three such organizations, each of which had a cooperative agreement with the Department of State.

The concept of a cooperative agreement is a specific federal funding instrument. Unlike a procurement contract, which is primarily used when the government is acquiring goods or services for its own use, a cooperative agreement is used when the government is providing financial assistance to a recipient to carry out a public purpose authorized by law. The cooperative agreement mechanism reflects the statutory design: Congress authorized the government to fund nonprofit organizations to provide resettlement services directly to refugees. The government's ability to fulfill its statutory obligations under § 1522 thus depends, in practice, on the existence of functioning cooperative agreements with organizations capable of delivering those services.

This practical dependency is central to the panel's analysis. The statutory mandate under § 1522 is not self-executing; the government cannot provide employment training, English language classes, cash assistance, and case management directly to every refugee. It relies on cooperative agreements with specialized nonprofit organizations to do so. When the government terminated these agreements, it effectively dismantled the only mechanism through which it could fulfill its statutory obligations.

## The Government's Termination of Cooperative Agreements and Its March 10 Representation

The factual sequence is important to the panel's reasoning. On February 26, 2025—one day after the district court orally issued its preliminary injunction—the government terminated every cooperative agreement to provide reception and placement services for refugees in the United States, as well as all but one of the cooperative agreements to provide USRAP processing support abroad. The timing suggests that the terminations were part of the government's implementation of Executive Order 14163, though the opinion does not explicitly characterize the motive.

On March 10, 2025, the government advised the district court that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services." This representation is significant because it constitutes a acknowledgment by the government that the need for reception and placement services persists and that some mechanism for providing them must exist. In other words, the government did not argue that it had no obligation to provide such services; instead, it represented that it was working to establish a new mechanism for doing so. This representation is consistent with the panel majority's reading of § 1522 as imposing a mandatory obligation—had the government believed the services were purely discretionary, there would have been no need to prepare a request for proposals.

## The Government's Uncertainty at Oral Argument

The panel's oral argument on September 3, 2025, appears to have been a turning point. Despite the government's March 10 representation that it was actively preparing a request for proposals, at oral argument the government could not confirm whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency or agencies to provide reception and placement services. The opinion states that the government "advised the panel at oral argument that it was unaware whether any progress had been made."

This uncertainty is critical to the panel's decision to direct the reinstatement of cooperative agreements. The government had represented to the district court that it was working toward a solution, but it could not demonstrate to the court of appeals that any concrete steps had been taken. In the context of a stay pending appeal, the panel needed to assess whether the government was likely to comply with its statutory obligations during the pendency of the appeal. The government's uncertainty about its ability to provide the statutorily mandated services meant that refugees already admitted to the United States might be left without the support that Congress required the government to provide. This practical gap—between the statutory mandate and the government's actual capacity to fulfill it—provided the basis for the panel's directive.

## The Panel's Directive to Reinstate Cooperative Agreements

The culmination of the panel's analysis of the APA challenge is its directive to the government. The panel ordered the government to "reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States." The panel further specified that such services "shall include the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements."

Several features of this directive are noteworthy. First, it is limited to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States. The panel did not require the government to provide resettlement services to refugees who have not yet been admitted, because the President's authority to suspend admissions under § 1182(f) was upheld. Second, the directive references "the usual and customary services" provided under prior cooperative agreements, grounding the scope of the required services in the established practice that existed before the government's terminations. Third, the directive uses the cooperative agreement mechanism—that is, the same statutory framework Congress established under § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)—as the means of compliance. The panel did not order the government to create a new program or invent a new delivery mechanism; it ordered the reinstatement of the existing one.

The legal significance of the directive is that it treats the APA challenge to the defunding of resettlement services differently from the challenge to the suspension of refugee admissions. On the admissions question, the panel found the government likely to succeed and stayed the district court's injunction. On the resettlement services question, the panel found the government not likely to succeed and declined to stay the district court's injunction insofar as it required the provision of services to already-admitted refugees. The stay is partial: the district court's injunctions are stayed "in their entirety, except as noted," and the reinstatement directive constitutes the exception. This bifurcation reflects the panel's view that the President's § 1182(f) authority over admissions is broad and deferentially reviewed, but that the statutory mandate for services to already-admitted refugees under § 1522 is a separate legal question governed by ordinary principles of statutory interpretation and administrative law.

Judge Lee's dissent takes the opposite view on the services question. He would stay the district court's injunctions in their entirety, reading § 1522 as permissive rather than mandatory and arguing that "the law does not demand the executive branch provide these services." His analysis highlights the interpretive ambiguity in § 1522's text—the tension between the "shall" language in subsection (a) and the "authorized" language in the funding subsections—and illustrates the competing readings that the panel's forthcoming merits opinion will need to resolve.
