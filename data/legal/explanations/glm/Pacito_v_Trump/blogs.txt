Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Section 1182(f) as Black Box: The Ninth Circuit Doubles Down on Trump v. Hawaii's Extreme Deference

When the Supreme Court upheld President Trump's travel ban in *Trump v. Hawaii*, it described 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) as a provision that "exudes deference" to the President on matters of immigration and national security. The Court's opinion left open a host of questions about how far that deference extends—whether it can swallow entire statutory schemes, whether courts will ever probe the factual basis for a presidential finding of "detriment," and whether Congress's own carefully constructed immigration frameworks impose any meaningful limits on the President's suspension power. The Ninth Circuit's September 12 order in *Pacito v. Trump* offers a sobering answer: not much, and perhaps not at all.

The case arises from President Trump's January 20 executive order suspending the entire U.S. Refugee Admissions Program. The President invoked § 1182(f)—the same provision at issue in *Hawaii*—finding that the entry of refugees "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." The district court in the Western District of Washington issued preliminary injunctions blocking implementation of the suspension and ordering the reinstatement of cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations that the State Department had terminated. The Ninth Circuit's panel—acting before issuing a full merits opinion—stayed those injunctions almost entirely, permitting the suspension of refugee admissions to take effect while requiring only that the government maintain funding for reception and placement services to refugees already admitted into the United States.

## The Merits: A Foreclosed Inquiry

What is most striking about the panel's order is not its bottom line on the stay but the casual confidence with which it resolves the merits question. The panel concludes that the government is "likely to prevail" on the plaintiffs' challenge to the executive order's suspension of refugee admissions, and it does so in a single paragraph. The reasoning tracks *Hawaii* almost mechanically: § 1182(f) "exudes deference," its "sole prerequisite" is that the President "find" that entry would be detrimental, and the President has "undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here." The panel then quotes *Hawaii* for the proposition that courts "cannot engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications."

This framing effectively transforms the Refugee Act of 1980 into a suggestion rather than a statutory command. The Refugee Act established an ongoing, detailed framework for refugee admissions—setting annual consultation requirements between the President and Congress, defining eligibility criteria, and creating an elaborate processing infrastructure. As the Supreme Court noted in *Negusie v. Holder*, the Act was designed as "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons." Yet the panel's reasoning suggests that this entire apparatus operates at the President's sufferance, because § 1182(f)—enacted as part of the original Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952 and left largely untouched by the Refugee Act—allows the President to suspend the entry of "any class of aliens" upon making a finding of detriment.

The doctrinal tension is stark. Congress in 1980 enacted a comprehensive scheme that presupposes the ongoing admission of refugees. The President, invoking a statute predating that scheme by nearly three decades, can effectively switch it off by reciting the magic words "detrimental to the interests of the United States." The panel's order does not grapple with whether the Refugee Act's specific framework implicitly narrows the reach of § 1182(f)'s general suspension authority—a plausible reading given that later, more specific statutes are typically understood to modify earlier, more general ones. Instead, the panel treats *Hawaii* as having settled the question, even though *Hawaii* involved a suspension of entry from designated countries, not the wholesale shuttering of a statutorily mandated program that Congress has funded and maintained for forty-five years.

## The Limits (Such as They Are) on Section 1182(f)

The *Hawaii* Court did identify one textual limit: the President's proclamation must fall within the "textual limit on the President's authority." The Ninth Circuit quotes this language but does not interrogate what it might mean in this context. In *Hawaii*, the Court noted that § 1182(f) does not allow the President to "override other limits" on his authority set forth in the INA. But what counts as an "other limit"? The Refugee Act's detailed scheme—its congressional consultation requirements, its annual determination process, its categories of priority refugees—could plausibly be read as precisely the kind of "other limit" that *Hawaii* acknowledged. The panel does not address this argument, and its brevity on the merits suggests it sees no need to.

This is where the "black box" concern becomes most acute. If § 1182(f) permits the President to suspend an entire statutory program that Congress has repeatedly funded and reauthorized, simply by invoking the word "detrimental," then it is difficult to identify any immigration program that operates outside the President's reach under this provision. The President could, on this reading, suspend all family-based immigration, all employment-based immigration, or the diversity visa program—the list is potentially endless. The only constraint *Hawaii* recognized was the prohibition on overriding other provisions of the INA, but the Ninth Circuit's application of that constraint here is so formalistic as to be nearly illusory.

## The Split on Section 1522: A Rare Restraining Note

The panel's most interesting move is its refusal to stay the district court's injunction with respect to reception and placement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The panel concludes that the government is "not likely to prevail" on at least one APA challenge to the defunding of resettlement services, reasoning that § 1522 imposes mandatory obligations on the government to provide certain services to refugees already admitted to the United States. The panel cites specific subsections—§ 1522(a)(1)(A)'s directive that the Director "shall" make available resources for employment training, English language training, and cash assistance; § 1522(d)(2)(B)'s requirement that the Director "shall assume legal responsibility" for unaccompanied refugee children; and § 1522(e)(7)(A)'s mandate that the Secretary "shall develop and implement" support projects for recently resettled refugees.

Judge Lee's partial dissent pushes back forcefully. He argues that § 1522, as its title—"Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees"—suggests, is permissive rather than mandatory. In his reading, the word "authorized" in subsections (b) through (e) signals congressional permission, not congressional command. The "shall" language in § 1522(a), he argues, operates only as a conditional mandate—if the government chooses to fund refugee programs, it must do so subject to the enumerated conditions. And the "necessary" language in subsections (d) and (e), he contends, drips with deference to executive discretion.

Judge Lee has the better of the textual argument on at least one point: the statute's title does say "Authorization," and the operative funding provisions use "authorized" rather than "shall." But the panel's reading is not without support. The conditional language "to the extent of available appropriations" in § 1522(a)(1)(A) suggests that Congress contemplated a spending floor, not a ceiling—services shall be provided so long as funds are available. And Congress did appropriate substantial funds for refugee assistance in FY 2025, though the Rescissions Act clawed back $800 million. The panel's concern about the government's uncertainty regarding its ability to provide mandated services—its observation that the government represented it was "preparing a request for proposals" for a new resettlement agency but could not confirm any progress—suggests a practical worry that the executive branch is evading statutory obligations through procedural foot-dragging.

## Downstream Doctrinal Effects

The panel's order, though interim, sends a clear signal to district courts and to the government. On the admissions side, the message is that § 1182(f) remains an essentially unreviewable power, and that *Hawaii*'s deference framework operates as a near-absolute shield against judicial scrutiny of presidential motivation, factual basis, or consistency with other statutes. This will embolden future administrations of either party to invoke § 1182(f) for broad categorical suspensions, confident that courts will not look behind the President's finding of "detriment."

On the services side, the order preserves a narrow zone of judicial intervention: once refugees are admitted, the government may have statutory obligations it cannot simply abandon. But even this narrowing depends on the full merits opinion, which the panel has not yet issued. Judge Lee's dissent signals that the panel's reading of § 1522 is contestable, and the government will surely press the argument that spending decisions within "available appropriations" are inherently discretionary.

The deeper question—one the panel does not address but that the full merits opinion cannot avoid—is whether the Refugee Act of 1980 means anything at all when § 1182(f) is in play. If the President can suspend the entire refugee program by invoking a 1952 statute, the 1980 Act's elaborate framework becomes a nullity at the moment the President decides he disagrees with it. That is a remarkable conclusion, and one that sits uneasily with the standard canon that later, more specific statutes modify earlier, more general ones. The panel's order reaches this conclusion without confronting it. The forthcoming opinion should.



# 'Shall' or 'May'? The Intra-Panel Split Over Whether Section 1522 Mandates Refugee Resettlement Funding

When a federal statute says the government "shall" provide certain services, but the section's title says "Authorization for programs," which signal controls? That question sits at the heart of the Ninth Circuit's September 12 order in *Pacito v. Trump*, and the panel's answer—or rather, its two competing answers—has immediate consequences for refugees already admitted to the United States and for the broader architecture of executive discretion over immigration and resettlement programs.

The case challenges President Trump's Executive Order 14,163, which suspended the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), and the State Department's subsequent termination of cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations. The panel unanimously concluded that the President likely acted within his statutory authority to suspend refugee admissions, invoking the Supreme Court's decision in *Trump v. Hawaii* for the proposition that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and requires nothing more than a presidential "finding" of detriment. On that issue, the court stayed the district court's preliminary injunctions in full.

But the panel fractured over a separate question: whether 8 U.S.C. § 1522 requires the government to provide "reception and placement" services to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States. The majority ordered the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide those services. Judge Lee would have stayed the injunctions in their entirety. The disagreement turns on a classic problem of statutory interpretation: what do we make of a statute that uses both permissive and mandatory language, sometimes in the same subsection?

## The Majority's Reasoning: "Shall" Means "Shall"

The majority's reasoning, though compressed in this interim order, rests on several textual anchors. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) states that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, "to the extent of available appropriations," "shall" make available resources for employment training, English language instruction, cash assistance, and equal opportunity participation. The majority reads this as a mandatory obligation—subject only to the appropriations qualifier. The word "shall," after all, normally carries an imperative force.

The majority also points to two specific subsections that it reads as imposing concrete duties. Section 1522(d)(2)(B) provides that for unaccompanied refugee children, "the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child's immediate care." Section 1522(e)(7)(A) states that the Secretary of Health and Human Services "shall develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months" providing interim support, medical services, and case management "as needed." Both provisions use "shall," and the majority treats this as dispositive of congressional intent to mandate the described services.

Critically, the majority's decision is reinforced by the government's own uncertainty about its capacity to provide these services. At oral argument, the government could not confirm whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency to deliver reception and placement services—despite having told the district court in March that it was "actively preparing" a request for proposals. The majority appears to have viewed this admission as evidence that the government was not merely exercising discretion over how to provide services but was effectively ceasing to provide them at all, contrary to what the statute requires.

## Judge Lee's Counter: "Authorization" Means Permission, Not Command

Judge Lee's partial dissent advances a coherent structural argument. He begins where the majority does not—with the title of the section: "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." "Authorization," he observes, is permissive. It grants permission; it does not impose obligation. This is a textual signal that Congress was enabling executive action, not compelling it.

Lee then notes that the operative funding provisions in subsections (b) through (e) consistently use the word "authorized." The Director "is authorized" to provide initial resettlement services. The Director "is authorized" to make cash and medical assistance available. This repeated use of permissive language, Lee argues, reflects Congress's intent to vest the executive with discretion over whether and how to fund these programs.

The majority's reliance on the "shall" language in § 1522(a), Lee contends, misreads the provision's structure. Subsection (a) is titled "Conditions and consideration." It begins with the qualifying phrase "In providing assistance under this section"—meaning, Lee argues, that the mandatory conditions it imposes (employment training, English language instruction, cash assistance) are triggered only if the government chooses to provide assistance in the first place. The "shall" in (a) operates as a conditional mandate: if the Director exercises the authority granted in (b)–(e), then the Director shall do so in a manner consistent with the enumerated conditions. It does not independently require the Director to exercise that authority.

As for the specific subsections the majority relies on, Lee reads them as shot through with executive discretion. The duty to assume responsibility for unaccompanied children in (d)(2)(B) arises only "if necessary," and the very next clause authorizes the Director "to make necessary decisions"—language that, Lee argues, "drips with deference to the executive branch." Similarly, (e)(7)(A)'s requirement that the Secretary "shall develop and implement" projects is qualified by "as needed," leaving the scope and substance of those projects to executive judgment. Neither provision, in Lee's view, counteracts the overall permissive architecture of the section.

## Competing Canons and the Architecture of Statutory Meaning

The split reflects a genuine tension in the law of statutory interpretation, and neither position is frivolous. The majority leans on the presumption that "shall" is imperative—the canon, articulated in cases like *Barnhart v. Sigmon Coal Co.*, that "shall" creates an obligation unless context clearly suggests otherwise. This is a strong default, and the majority applies it straightforwardly: where Congress used "shall," courts should enforce the duty.

Lee's approach is more structural, drawing on the principle that statutory language must be read in context and that individual words cannot be enlisted to override the evident design of the larger provision. His argument from the section title invokes a modest version of the canon that titles and headings may aid in resolving ambiguity—though, as the Supreme Court has cautioned, titles are not controlling. *Almendares-Torres v. Sessions* acknowledged that while titles can be informative, they cannot overcome plain statutory text. Lee's stronger argument is not the title alone but the systematic contrast between the permissive "authorized" in the funding subsections and the mandatory "shall" in the conditions subsection—a contrast that, he argues, makes sense only if the "shall" operates conditionally.

The "to the extent of available appropriations" language cuts in both directions. The majority reads it as a funding limitation on an otherwise mandatory duty: the government must provide these services, but only up to what Congress has appropriated. Lee reads it more broadly, as reinforcing the permissive character of the section—if there are no appropriations, there is no obligation, and the executive retains discretion to decide whether and how to deploy the funds that are available.

## The Deeper Stakes: When Does Congress Mandate Executive Action?

The disagreement has implications well beyond refugee resettlement. The federal code is replete with statutes that authorize executive agencies to provide services, impose conditions on how those services are provided, and use both "shall" and "may" within the same section. If the majority's approach prevails, courts will have a powerful tool for compelling executive action whenever a statute uses "shall" in describing the conditions attached to a discretionary program. Agencies that seek to defund or discontinue authorized programs could face judicial mandates to continue them, at least where the statute's language can be read as imposing a duty.

Lee's approach, by contrast, would preserve broader executive discretion to defund authorized programs, so long as the operative funding provisions use permissive language. This reading would make it harder for plaintiffs to challenge program suspensions as statutory violations, and would shift the battleground to Congress—where, arguably, the decision about whether to fund resettlement services properly belongs.

The majority's concern about the government's apparent inability to provide the mandated services through alternative channels suggests a pragmatic dimension to the split. The government represented to the district court that it was preparing a new request for proposals, but at oral argument could not confirm any progress. The majority's order effectively calls that bluff: if the government claims the authority to terminate existing cooperative agreements, it must have a plan to fulfill its statutory duties through other means. Judge Lee's dissent would not impose that requirement, trusting that the statute's permissive language gives the executive latitude to wind down services entirely.

The panel promises a full opinion "in due course." When it arrives, the reasoning on this point will be closely parsed—not only by refugee advocates and the government, but by administrative law scholars watching to see how the Ninth Circuit navigates the perennial question of when Congress has mandated executive action and when it has merely allowed it. The answer may turn on something as modest as the difference between a title and a subsection—but the consequences will be anything but modest for the refugees who have already arrived and the organizations that have long helped them resettle.



# Terminated Contracts and Statutory Obligations: The APA Challenge That Survived the Stay

## A Split Decision with Significant Implications

The Ninth Circuit's September 12 order in *Pacito v. Trump* is, at first glance, a familiar story: a panel largely defers to presidential authority over immigration admissions, staying a district court's injunction against Executive Order 14163's suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program. But buried within the order is a more interesting and potentially significant ruling—one that separates this case from the now-routine litigation over presidential suspension powers. The panel concluded that the government is likely to lose on at least one APA claim, and it directed the reinstatement of cooperative agreements with refugee resettlement organizations necessary to provide statutorily mandated services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. That ruling, and the reasoning that animates it, raises questions that extend well beyond refugee policy to the broader relationship between statutory mandates and the contractual infrastructure agencies use to fulfill them.

## The President's Admission Power and the Stay

On the central question of whether the President may suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the panel's reasoning tracks *Trump v. Hawaii* closely. Section 1182(f), the panel observed, "exudes deference" to the President and requires only that the President "find" that the entry of a class of aliens would be detrimental to the interests of the United States. The President made that finding. The panel declined to engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications," quoting *Hawaii* for the proposition that the statute's sole prerequisite is the presidential finding itself. On this basis, the court stayed the district court's injunctions in their entirety—vacating both its prior stay orders and the administrative stay issued in July.

This portion of the ruling is unlikely to surprise close observers of immigration law. *Hawaii* established a remarkably low bar for § 1182(f) challenges, and the panel applied it straightforwardly. The plaintiffs' argument that the Refugee Act of 1980 cabin's the President's § 1182(f) authority did not persuade the majority, at least at this preliminary stage.

## The APA Claim That Survived

What makes the order noteworthy is the panel's conclusion that, despite the government's likely success on the admission suspension question, it is likely to fail on at least one APA challenge—specifically, the challenge to the State Department's termination of cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations that provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States.

The panel's reasoning here proceeds through 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which governs domestic resettlement programs. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) states that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, "to the extent of available appropriations," "shall" make available resources for employment training, English language training, cash assistance, and ensure equal participation opportunities for women. The panel further cited § 1522(d)(2)(B), which provides that the Director "shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility)" for unaccompanied refugee children when necessary, and § 1522(e)(7)(A), which requires the Secretary to "develop and implement alternative projects" for interim support and medical services for recently resettled refugees. These provisions, the panel concluded, impose obligations that the government cannot simply ignore by terminating the cooperative agreements through which those services have historically been delivered.

The statutory architecture supports this reading. Congress specified that these services "may be provided through the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies" under § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii), but the panel viewed the provision of services themselves as mandatory where appropriations are available. And Congress did appropriate funds—for FY 2025, $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, though the Rescissions Act of 2025 reduced that by $800 million. The panel's order thus distinguishes between the mechanism of service delivery (cooperative agreements, which are discretionary instruments) and the obligation to provide services (which the statute mandates).

## The Government's Own Representations and the Panel's Frustration

The factual backdrop to the panel's ruling is as important as the legal analysis. The government represented to the district court on March 10 that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services." Yet at oral argument before the Ninth Circuit on September 3—nearly six months later—the government advised the panel that it was "unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency or agencies."

This representation, or lack thereof, appears to have been pivotal. The panel framed its order not merely as a ruling on the legal merits but as a practical response to the government's demonstrated inability to confirm that it could meet its statutory obligations without the existing cooperative agreements. The order directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements "necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States," including "the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements."

One can read this as the panel calling the government's bluff. If the executive branch intends to comply with § 1522's mandates through alternative contractual arrangements, it should be able to demonstrate progress toward that end. When it could not, the panel was left with a statutory obligation unmet and no alternative infrastructure on the horizon. The order effectively says: if you cannot show us how you will fulfill the statute, you must restore the mechanism that already worked.

## The Lee Dissent: Authorization or Mandate?

Judge Lee's partial dissent raises a serious textual challenge to the majority's reading of § 1522. Judge Lee emphasizes that the statute's title—"Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees"—signals permissive rather than mandatory action. He notes that the specific funding provisions in subsections (b) through (e) consistently state that the "Director is authorized" to provide assistance, and he reads § 1522(a)'s use of "shall" as imposing conditions on the discretionary funding decisions, not as independently mandating that funding occur.

There is genuine textual force to this argument. The word "authorization" in the section title, combined with the repeated use of "authorized" in the operative subsections, could reasonably be read as granting discretion to the executive branch rather than imposing obligations. Judge Lee's reading also draws support from the "to the extent of available appropriations" language, which he interprets as further underscoring executive discretion over whether and how much to spend.

The majority's counter, though not fully developed in this interim order, appears to rest on several propositions: that "shall" in § 1522(a) carries its ordinary mandatory force, that the specific obligations regarding unaccompanied minors in § 1522(d)(2)(B) and alternative projects in § 1522(e)(7)(A) are framed in mandatory terms, and that the overall statutory scheme envisions services being provided when appropriations are available. The panel's forthcoming merits opinion will need to address Judge Lee's arguments more fully, but the interim order suggests the majority sees § 1522 as creating enforceable obligations that the APA can police when agencies act to dismantle the infrastructure for fulfilling them.

## The Broader Doctrinal Question

The panel's ruling, if sustained in the merits opinion, implicates a question of increasing importance in administrative law: can an agency effectively evade statutory mandates not by repealing the mandate but by declining to maintain the contractual or administrative infrastructure necessary to carry it out?

This question is not unique to refugee resettlement. Across the federal government, statutory obligations are fulfilled through contracts, grants, and cooperative agreements with outside entities. When an agency terminates those arrangements, the statutory mandate does not disappear—but the practical capacity to fulfill it may. The panel's order suggests that courts may be willing to look through the formal act of contract termination to ask whether the agency has a plan for continued statutory compliance. Where the agency cannot articulate such a plan, courts may be prepared to order reinstatement of prior arrangements, at least as an interim measure.

This is a meaningful doctrinal development. It cabins the practical freedom agencies have to reshape statutory programs through contract management rather than rulemaking or statutory amendment. And it reflects a judicial willingness to treat the operational infrastructure of statutory compliance as itself legally significant—not merely a matter of administrative discretion that courts are powerless to examine.

## Practical Consequences and What Comes Next

The practical consequences of the panel's order are significant for the organizational plaintiffs and the refugees they serve. The three resettlement organizations whose cooperative agreements were terminated in February will see those agreements reinstated, at least pending the merits ruling. Refugees already admitted to the United States will continue to receive reception and placement services. The government, meanwhile, faces the prospect of maintaining contractual arrangements it sought to terminate while simultaneously pursuing a policy of suspending new refugee admissions—a posture that may prove administratively and politically awkward.

The panel's forthcoming merits opinion will be closely watched. If the majority holds that § 1522 creates mandatory service obligations enforceable under the APA, it will establish a notable limit on the executive's ability to unwind statutory programs through contract termination. If Judge Lee's permissive reading prevails, it will reinforce broad executive discretion over the operational details of statutory programs. Either way, the opinion will engage a question that extends beyond refugee policy to the basic architecture of administrative governance: when Congress enacts a statutory mandate and appropriates funds to fulfill it, what exactly must the executive do—and what can a court require—when the agency pulls the plug on the contracts that make compliance possible?
