Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Background and Procedural Posture

## Executive Order No. 14163: Suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program

The dispute at the center of *Pacito v. Trump* began on January 20, 2025, when President Trump signed Executive Order No. 14163, entitled "Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program." The order invoked two provisions of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) as its statutory authority: 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a). Section 1182(f) is the broader of the two provisions, and it occupies a central role throughout this litigation. It provides that "[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate." In other words, § 1182(f) delegates to the President sweeping authority to suspend the entry of noncitizens whenever the President makes a "finding" that their entry would be "detrimental" to the nation's interests. The Supreme Court has described this statute as one that "exudes deference" to the President and "vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [INA]." *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018).

Relying on this authority, Executive Order No. 14163 stated that the President "determine[d] that entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States" and directed that "entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended" pending further findings. The order thus operated as a complete moratorium on the admission of refugees under USRAP. This suspension of admissions is one of the two core governmental actions that the plaintiffs challenged.

## Executive Order No. 14169: Suspension of USRAP Funding

Four days later, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State suspended all funding for USRAP. The State Department purported to act pursuant to a separate executive order—"Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid," Executive Order No. 14169—rather than under Executive Order No. 14163 itself. The funding suspension was broad: it encompassed not only overseas processing of refugee applications but also domestic resettlement services for refugees already admitted to the United States. These domestic services—often called "reception and placement" services—include employment training and placement, English language training, cash assistance, medical assistance, and specialized support for unaccompanied refugee minors. Congress has authorized these services through 8 U.S.C. § 1522, a provision of the Refugee Act of 1980, and has historically funded them through cooperative agreements between the State Department and private nonprofit resettlement organizations.

This distinction between the two executive orders and the two categories of governmental action—suspension of admissions versus suspension of funding—is critical for understanding the Ninth Circuit's eventual ruling. While the court would find that the President likely possessed authority under § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions, it reached a different conclusion regarding the termination of funding for resettlement services, which it analyzed under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) and 8 U.S.C. § 1522.

## The Plaintiffs and Their Claims

The plaintiffs in this case fall into two categories. The first group consists of individual refugees—some already admitted to the United States, others approved for resettlement but still outside the country. The second group comprises three nonprofit organizations that had cooperative agreements with the State Department to provide overseas processing for refugee applicants and domestic resettlement services for admitted refugees. These cooperative agreements are the primary mechanism through which the federal government has historically delivered reception and placement services to refugees, as Congress authorized in 8 U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii).

In February 2025, the plaintiffs filed suit in the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Washington. They advanced two principal legal claims. First, they argued that Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions violated the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102, which is codified in various provisions of the INA at 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq. The plaintiffs characterized the Refugee Act as establishing "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons," quoting *Negusie v. Holder*, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009). The essence of this claim was that the Refugee Act, as a more specific statute, constrained the broad authority the President claimed under § 1182(f).

Second, the plaintiffs argued that the defunding of USRAP violated various provisions of the APA, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. APA claims typically challenge agency actions as arbitrary and capricious, contrary to law, or in excess of statutory authority. Here, the plaintiffs contended that the termination of cooperative agreements and the suspension of resettlement funding violated the statutory mandates Congress imposed in 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which the plaintiffs read as requiring the government to provide certain reception and placement services to admitted refugees. This APA-based challenge to the funding suspension would prove to be the more successful of the two claims before the Ninth Circuit.

## The District Court's Preliminary Injunctions

The district court moved quickly to provide relief. On February 25, 2025, it orally issued a preliminary injunction prohibiting the enforcement or implementation of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. A preliminary injunction is a court order that maintains the status quo pending the resolution of the case on the merits. It does not represent a final decision on the legal claims; rather, it provides interim relief to prevent irreparable harm while the litigation proceeds. To obtain a preliminary injunction, a plaintiff typically must demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable harm absent the injunction, that the balance of equities favors the plaintiff, and that the injunction serves the public interest.

The government's response to the oral injunction was swift. On February 26—the day after the oral injunction—the government terminated every cooperative agreement to provide reception and placement services for refugees in the United States and all but one of the cooperative agreements to provide USRAP processing support abroad. This action effectively cut off the funding and infrastructure through which resettlement services were delivered, prompting the plaintiffs to return to the district court.

On February 28, the district court issued a written order to define the scope of its February 25 oral injunction. The court specified that the injunction also prohibited the suspension of USRAP funding, thereby bringing the funding terminations within the protective scope of the preliminary injunction. This February 28 written order would become one of the two injunctions at issue in the appeal.

On March 24, 2025, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction. This order directed the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements that had been terminated after the February 25 ruling. The purpose of this second injunction was to ensure that the resettlement infrastructure—comprising the nonprofit organizations that actually delivered services to refugees—remained functional while the case proceeded. The March 24 injunction would become the second of the two injunctions at issue in the appeal.

The government filed notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025, challenging both injunctions. These appeals brought the case to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit.

## The March 25 Stay and the Limited Carveout

Once the appeals were filed, the government sought a stay pending appeal of the February 28 district court order. A stay pending appeal is an order by the appellate court that suspends the effect of the lower court's order while the appeal is resolved. The Supreme Court has identified four factors that courts consider when deciding whether to grant a stay: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing that it is likely to succeed on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure the other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). The first two factors—the likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable injury—are considered "the most critical." *Id.*

A motions panel of the Ninth Circuit granted the stay in part on March 25, 2025. The panel quoted *Trump v. Hawaii* for the proposition that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and vests him with broad authority to impose entry restrictions. The panel concluded that the government was likely to succeed on the merits of the challenge to Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions, and it stayed the district court's injunction insofar as it prohibited that suspension.

However, the panel denied the stay "to the extent the district court's preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025." This created what the panel called a "limited carveout from the stay": the stay went into effect for the broad injunction, but the injunction remained in force for one specific category of refugees—those who had received conditional approval before the executive order was issued. The rationale for this carveout, as the panel saw it, was that these individuals had progressed far enough in the refugee admission process that their equitable interests warranted continued protection pending appeal.

The motions panel directed full briefing and ordered the Clerk of Court to place the appeal on the next available calendar, setting the case on a path toward oral argument.

## Clarifying Orders, the Enforcement Framework, and the Administrative Stay

The "limited carveout" proved difficult to administer in practice, leading to a series of further proceedings. The Ninth Circuit issued two clarifying orders—on April 21 and May 9, 2025—to define the scope of the carveout more precisely. These orders illustrate a common feature of complex injunctions: when courts create exceptions or carveouts, the parties often need judicial clarification to determine exactly who is covered and what obligations the government retains.

Meanwhile, the district court remained involved in enforcing its own orders. On July 14, 2025, the district court issued an order establishing a framework for enforcing the limited carveout and appointing a magistrate judge to oversee the framework. This order reflected the district court's effort to ensure that refugees within the carveout actually received the benefits the injunction preserved for them. The appointment of a magistrate judge to oversee compliance is a procedural tool sometimes used when ongoing supervision of a complex injunction is needed.

The government responded to the July 14 enforcement order by filing an emergency motion with the Ninth Circuit on July 17, 2025. The motion asked the court to enforce, clarify, or amend its stay order—essentially arguing that the district court's enforcement framework exceeded the scope of what the Ninth Circuit had permitted under the carveout. The plaintiffs opposed the motion.

Before the Ninth Circuit could resolve the emergency motion, the court issued an administrative stay on July 18, 2025. An administrative stay is a temporary measure that preserves the status quo while the court considers the pending motion. It is typically issued without full briefing or argument and is intended to provide short-term stability until the court can rule. The July 18 administrative stay temporarily halted the district court's July 14 enforcement order while the panel considered the government's emergency motion.

## Expedited Oral Argument and the Procedural Context for the Interim Order

The Ninth Circuit scheduled oral argument on an expedited basis, which the panel conducted on September 3, 2025. Expedited treatment is commonly used in cases involving time-sensitive government policies or where ongoing harm to the parties requires prompt resolution. At oral argument, the panel heard from both sides on the merits of the stay questions, including the government's likelihood of success on the plaintiffs' claims under both the Refugee Act and the APA.

A notable moment came during oral argument when the government revealed that it was "unaware whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency or agencies that could provide reception and placement services." This admission proved significant. The government had previously represented to the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency." The gap between that representation and the uncertainty expressed at oral argument would factor into the panel's decision to require reinstatement of cooperative agreements for services to already-admitted refugees.

Following oral argument, the case was "submitted for decision," meaning that the panel had received all briefing and argument necessary to decide the case. However, rather than issuing a full opinion immediately, the panel issued the September 12, 2025 order that is the subject of this casebook chapter. The order was explicitly described as an interim measure: "An opinion on the merits will issue in due course." The panel vacated both its prior administrative stay of July 18 and its prior stay of March 25 (as amended on April 21 and May 9), and issued a new stay pending final resolution of the appeal. This procedural posture—an interim order that resolves the stay questions while a full merits opinion is pending—is important because it means the September 12 order does not represent a final decision on the merits. It resolves only the question of what relief should be in place while the appeal proceeds, though the panel's assessment of the parties' likelihood of success offers a strong signal of how the court is likely to rule when it issues its full opinion.

# The Legal Framework: Presidential Authority Over Refugee Admissions Under Section 1182(f)

## 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f): The Statutory Text Authorizing the President to Suspend Entry of Any Class of Aliens Found Detrimental to U.S. Interests

The central statutory provision at issue in *Pacito v. Trump* is 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which vests the President with sweeping authority to suspend the entry of noncitizens into the United States. The Ninth Circuit panel described the statute as providing "in relevant part that '[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate.'" The breadth of this language is striking. It empowers the President to act not only with respect to specific named individuals but also to entire "class[es] of aliens," and it permits both complete suspension of entry and the imposition of lesser "restrictions" the President "may deem appropriate." The statute places no explicit time limit on such suspensions, leaving the duration to the President's own judgment—"[for] such period as he shall deem necessary."

In *Pacito*, President Trump invoked § 1182(f) in Executive Order No. 14163, entitled "Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program." The Executive Order stated that the President "determined that 'entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States' and directed that 'entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended' pending further findings." The panel treated this invocation as falling squarely within the textual scope of § 1182(f): the President identified a class of aliens—refugees admitted under USRAP—made the requisite finding of detriment, and ordered suspension of their entry. The statutory text, by its terms, asks nothing more.

The statute's capacious language is the foundation for the panel's conclusion that the government was likely to succeed on the merits of the challenge to Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions. Understanding this provision is essential to understanding why the court stayed the district court's preliminary injunction insofar as it prohibited enforcement of the suspension. The statute does not simply authorize the President to act in emergencies or under narrow circumstances; it creates a broad delegation of authority whose boundaries, as interpreted by the Supreme Court, are remarkably permissive.

## Trump v. Hawaii (2018): The "Exudes Deference" Standard and the Scope of Presidential Authority Under § 1182(f)

The Ninth Circuit panel did not interpret § 1182(f) in a vacuum. It relied heavily on the Supreme Court's decision in *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018), which remains the authoritative interpretation of the scope of presidential authority under § 1182(f). The panel quoted *Hawaii* for the proposition that § 1182(f) "'exudes deference' to the President and 'vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].'" This formulation—drawn directly from the Supreme Court—signals that § 1182(f) is not a narrow exception to the broader immigration scheme but rather an independent and expansive source of presidential authority that supplements the INA's other provisions.

*Hawaii* arose from a different executive order—the so-called "travel ban"—but its interpretive framework governs the *Pacito* panel's analysis. The Supreme Court in *Hawaii* rejected the argument that § 1182(f) was constrained by other provisions of the INA that govern the admission of specific classes of aliens. Instead, the Court held that the statute gives the President authority to impose entry restrictions "in addition to those elsewhere enumerated." This reasoning is directly relevant to *Pacito*, where the plaintiffs argued that the Refugee Act of 1980 cabined the President's § 1182(f) authority with respect to refugees specifically. The panel's application of *Hawaii* suggests that § 1182(f) operates independently of the Refugee Act's framework, at least with respect to the question of whether the President may suspend the entry of a class of aliens.

The "exudes deference" standard is more than rhetorical flourish. It reflects a judicial posture in which the court acknowledges that the political branches—particularly the President—possess primary constitutional authority over immigration and foreign affairs. Under this standard, courts reviewing exercises of § 1182(f) authority do not subject the President's policy choices to ordinary administrative law scrutiny. They do not ask whether the President's decision was well-reasoned, supported by substantial evidence, or consistent with the best reading of the overall immigration scheme. Instead, as the panel's opinion makes clear, they ask only whether the President has fulfilled the minimal statutory prerequisites the text of § 1182(f) requires.

## The Sole Statutory Prerequisite: The President's "Finding" That Entry Would Be Detrimental to the Interests of the United States

Perhaps the most important doctrinal point the *Pacito* panel makes regarding § 1182(f) is its identification of the sole statutory prerequisite for the President's exercise of authority under the provision. Quoting *Hawaii*, the panel stated: "'The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President "find[]" that the entry of the covered aliens "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.'" This is a remarkable statement about the minimalism of judicial review under § 1182(f). The statute requires only a "finding," and the panel concluded that the President's declaration in Executive Order No. 14163—that entry of refugees under USRAP "would be detrimental to the interests of the United States"—satisfied that requirement.

The word "finding" in § 1182(f) deserves careful attention. In administrative law, a "finding" often suggests a factual determination supported by evidence in the record. But the *Pacito* panel, following *Hawaii*, did not require that the President's finding be supported by a particular evidentiary showing or that it be backed by a formal record. The President's own declaration in the Executive Order was sufficient. The finding need not be detailed; it need not identify specific threats or articulate a granular rationale. The President must simply state that entry of the class of aliens would be detrimental to U.S. interests. The panel's treatment of this requirement is consistent with *Hawaii*'s holding that the statute's language is "clear" and that the executive order at issue there "d[id] not exceed any textual limit on the President's authority."

This minimal prerequisite stands in sharp contrast to the standards courts apply under the Administrative Procedure Act, which typically require agency action to be supported by a reasoned explanation and a factual record. The *Pacito* panel's distinction between the § 1182(f) analysis and the APA analysis is central to understanding the opinion's structure. While the panel concluded that the government was likely to prevail on the challenge to the suspension of refugee admissions under § 1182(f), it reached the opposite conclusion on at least one of the plaintiffs' APA challenges regarding the defunding of resettlement services—a topic addressed in a separate chapter.

## The Prohibition on "Searching Inquiry" Into the Persuasiveness of the President's Justifications

Closely related to the minimal statutory prerequisite is the corollary principle that courts may not conduct a "searching inquiry" into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications for exercising § 1182(f) authority. The *Pacito* panel quoted *Hawaii* directly: "we cannot engage in 'a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications.'" This principle has profound implications for judicial review. It means that even if a court might disagree with the President's policy judgment, or find the stated rationale unconvincing, the court cannot override the President's exercise of § 1182(f) authority on that basis. The President's finding of detriment is, for practical purposes, non-reviewable on the merits.

This prohibition on searching inquiry is not unique to § 1182(f); it reflects a broader pattern of judicial deference to the political branches in matters of immigration and national security. But § 1182(f) is an especially clear example because the Supreme Court in *Hawaii* articulated the standard in unambiguous terms. The *Pacito* panel applied that standard straightforwardly: the President made the required finding, the statutory text imposes no additional requirements, and the court would not probe the adequacy of the President's reasoning. The panel therefore concluded that the government had made a "strong showing" of likely success on the merits with respect to the suspension of refugee admissions.

The practical consequence of this prohibition is significant for the plaintiffs in *Pacito*. They could not prevail on their challenge to the suspension of refugee admissions by arguing that the President's stated reasons were pretextual, inadequately supported, or inconsistent with the purposes of the Refugee Act. The court's inquiry under § 1182(f) is essentially binary: Did the President make the required finding? If yes, the statutory prerequisite is satisfied, and the court lacks authority to second-guess the substantive judgment.

## The Refugee Act of 1980 as "a General Rule for the Ongoing Treatment of All Refugees" (*Negusie v. Holder*)

The *Pacito* panel acknowledged the plaintiffs' argument that Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions violated the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (codified in various provisions of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.). In doing so, it cited *Negusie v. Holder*, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009), which described the Refugee Act as "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons." This characterization is important because it frames the Refugee Act as a comprehensive statutory scheme—one that establishes norms and procedures for how the United States admits and resettles refugees on an ongoing basis, rather than merely providing ad hoc authority.

The plaintiffs' argument, in essence, was that the Refugee Act created a framework for refugee admissions that the President could not unilaterally suspend under § 1182(f). If the Refugee Act establishes "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees," then perhaps the President's authority to suspend the entry of refugees under § 1182(f) is limited by the more specific provisions of the Refugee Act, which provide structured processes for refugee admissions and resettlement. This is a classic argument from statutory specificity: when a general statute and a specific statute address the same subject, the specific statute controls. Under this reasoning, the Refugee Act's detailed framework for refugee admissions would constrain the President's broad authority under § 1182(f).

The *Pacito* panel did not directly resolve this statutory conflict on the merits—the order is a stay pending appeal, not a final decision. But the panel's reliance on *Hawaii* strongly suggests that it viewed the Refugee Act as not displacing the President's § 1182(f) authority. *Hawaii* held that § 1182(f) vests the President with authority to impose entry restrictions "in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [INA]," which would include the Refugee Act's provisions. The panel quoted this language and concluded that the government was likely to succeed on the merits of the challenge to the suspension. While the panel's analysis is necessarily preliminary given the procedural posture, it signals that the broad delegation in § 1182(f) is likely to prevail over the more specific provisions of the Refugee Act, at least with respect to the question of entry suspensions.

## The Relationship Between § 1182(f)'s Broad Delegation and the Refugee Act's Framework for Refugee Admissions

The tension between § 1182(f) and the Refugee Act of 1980 is a central doctrinal problem the *Pacito* opinion navigates. Section 1182(f) grants the President broad, largely discretionary authority to suspend the entry of any class of aliens. The Refugee Act, by contrast, creates a detailed framework for refugee admissions and resettlement, including provisions governing the designation of refugee processing priorities, the allocation of refugee admissions among regions, and the provision of domestic resettlement services. These two statutory schemes operate on different axes: § 1182(f) concerns the President's authority at the border—whether aliens may enter—while the Refugee Act concerns both admissions and the treatment of refugees after admission.

The *Pacito* panel's approach to this tension is revealing. With respect to the suspension of refugee admissions, the panel treated § 1182(f) as dispositive. The President made the required finding; the statute "exudes deference"; the court cannot conduct a "searching inquiry"; therefore, the government is likely to succeed. The Refugee Act's framework for refugee admissions does not appear to constrain the President's § 1182(f) authority in the panel's analysis. This is consistent with *Hawaii*'s holding that § 1182(f) authority operates "in addition to" other INA provisions.

But the panel drew a critical distinction between the suspension of refugee admissions and the defunding of resettlement services. While § 1182(f) governs entry, other statutes—specifically 8 U.S.C. § 1522—govern the services the government must provide to refugees who have already been admitted. The panel concluded that the government was not likely to prevail on at least one APA challenge to the defunding of these services, because § 1522 independently requires the government to provide certain reception and placement services to admitted refugees. This bifurcated approach—deference to the President on entry under § 1182(f), but more rigorous APA review of agency action regarding post-admission services—reflects the panel's view that the statutory landscape is not uniform. Different provisions delegate different levels of discretion, and courts must attend to the specific statutory language at issue in each claim.

This distinction is essential for understanding the overall structure of the *Pacito* opinion. The panel stayed the district court's injunction in its entirety with respect to the suspension of refugee admissions, but it directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide statutorily mandated reception and placement services to refugees already admitted. The broad delegation of § 1182(f) does not extend to post-admission obligations created by other statutes. The President's authority over entry is near-plenary under § 1182(f); the government's obligations to admitted refugees under the Refugee Act are governed by different statutory provisions with different standards of judicial review. Judge Lee's partial dissent challenges this distinction, arguing that § 1522 authorizes but does not mandate the provision of services—a dispute addressed in a separate chapter but one that turns on the interpretation of "authorization" versus "mandate" in the Refugee Act's text.

# The Standard for a Stay Pending Appeal and Its Application to the Refugee Admission Suspension

## The Nken v. Holder Four-Factor Test for Stays Pending Appeal

When a party seeks to pause the effect of a lower court's order while an appeal is pending, the party is seeking what is known as a "stay pending appeal." A stay is an interim form of relief: it does not decide the merits of the appeal but instead preserves the status quo (or a particular version of it) while the appellate court prepares its decision. In *Pacito v. Trump*, the government asked the Ninth Circuit to stay the district court's preliminary injunctions, which had blocked enforcement of Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions and required reinstatement of cooperative agreements for refugee resettlement services.

The governing standard for evaluating a stay motion comes from the Supreme Court's decision in *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009). Under *Nken*, a court evaluates four factors: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing that it is likely to succeed on the merits of the appeal; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure other parties interested in the proceeding; and (4) where the public interest lies. These factors are not rigid mathematical formulas but rather interrelated considerations that the court weighs together in the exercise of its discretion. The court's task is to assess the balance of equities and the likelihood that the applicant's legal position will ultimately prevail.

The *Pacito* panel applied this framework to determine whether to continue or modify the stays it had previously issued. The procedural history is important here. A prior motions panel of the Ninth Circuit had granted a partial stay of the district court's February 28 preliminary injunction on March 25, 2025, but had carved out protection for individuals conditionally approved for refugee status before January 20, 2025. The panel had issued clarifying orders on April 21 and May 9, 2025. Then, after the district court issued a July 14 enforcement order, the Ninth Circuit issued an administrative stay of that order on July 18, 2025. The September 12 Order at issue here vacates both the administrative stay and the prior March 25 stay (as amended), and replaces them with a new stay pending final resolution of the appeal.

## The Primacy of the First Two Factors: Likelihood of Success on the Merits and Irreparable Injury

Although *Nken* lists four factors, the Supreme Court has emphasized that the first two—"the showing of success on the merits and irreparable injury"—are "the most critical." This means that a court evaluating a stay motion will focus most of its analytical energy on whether the applicant is likely to win on appeal and whether the applicant faces irreparable harm if the stay is denied. The remaining two factors—harm to other parties and the public interest—serve as important but secondary considerations that can tip the balance in closer cases.

This hierarchy of factors has practical significance. An applicant that cannot make a strong showing of likely success on the merits will generally not obtain a stay, regardless of how compelling the other factors might be. Conversely, a strong showing on the merits can sometimes compensate for a less compelling showing of irreparable injury. The *Pacito* panel's analysis reflects this structure: the bulk of its reasoning addresses the government's likelihood of success on the merits, with the remaining factors treated more summarily.

## Application of the Likelihood-of-Success Factor to the Challenge to EO 14163's Suspension of Refugee Admissions

The central legal question on the merits of the admission suspension challenge is whether Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) is lawful. The plaintiffs argued that the suspension violated the Refugee Act of 1980, which was enacted to establish "a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons," as the Supreme Court described it in *Negusie v. Holder*, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009). The plaintiffs' theory was that Congress, through the Refugee Act, had set up a system for refugee admissions that the President could not simply override by executive order.

The panel majority concluded that the government was likely to prevail on this challenge. Its reasoning rested on the breadth of the President's statutory authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), as interpreted by the Supreme Court in *Trump v. Hawaii*, 585 U.S. 667 (2018). The panel explained that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and "vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act]." This quotation from *Hawaii* signals that the panel viewed the President's authority under § 1182(f) as sweeping and largely insulated from searching judicial review.

The practical effect of this analysis is significant. If the President's power under § 1182(f) is as broad as *Hawaii* suggests, then the Refugee Act's framework for refugee admissions does not constrain the President's ability to suspend the entry of refugees as a class of aliens. The plaintiffs' argument that the Refugee Act created an entitlement to refugee admission that the President could not override was thus likely to fail. The panel's conclusion on this point was sufficiently firm that it stated, without qualification, that "the government is likely to prevail on plaintiffs' challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions."

## Why § 1182(f)'s Text Is "Clear" and the Executive Order "Does Not Exceed Any Textual Limit on the President's Authority"

Section 1182(f) provides, in relevant part, that "[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate." The panel's analysis of this provision focused on its text and structure.

The panel, drawing directly on *Hawaii*, described the language of § 1182(f) as "clear." This characterization is legally significant. When a court says that a statute's text is "clear," it means that the statute unambiguously confers the authority in question, and there is no need to consult legislative history, statutory purpose, or other interpretive tools to resolve ambiguity. The clarity of the text forecloses arguments that the statute should be read more narrowly.

The panel then applied *Hawaii*'s holding that the executive order at issue in that case (and, by extension, Executive Order No. 14163 here) "does not exceed any textual limit on the President's authority." This phrasing is crucial. It acknowledges that the President's power under § 1182(f) is not unlimited—it must operate within the "textual limits" of the statute—but holds that the statute's text imposes only a minimal constraint. The statute authorizes the President to suspend the entry of "any class of aliens," and refugees admitted under USRAP constitute such a class. The statute permits the President to act "for such period as he shall deem necessary," meaning the duration of the suspension is within the President's discretion. And the statute allows the President to impose "any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate," giving the President broad latitude in shaping the scope of the suspension.

The plaintiffs' counterargument—that the Refugee Act of 1980 imposed additional, more specific constraints on the President's authority over refugee admissions—was effectively subordinated to the broader authority conferred by § 1182(f). The panel's reasoning suggests that § 1182(f) operates as a general grant of authority that is not displaced by more specific provisions relating to refugee admissions, at least insofar as the President is exercising the power to suspend entry.

## Why the President's Finding of Detrimentality Satisfies the Sole Statutory Prerequisite

One of the most striking aspects of the panel's analysis is its treatment of the statutory prerequisite for exercising authority under § 1182(f). The panel, again quoting *Hawaii*, stated that "[t]he sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President 'find[]' that the entry of the covered aliens 'would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.'" The word "sole" is doing significant analytical work here. It means that the statute requires only one thing: a presidential finding of detrimentality. No other procedural or substantive precondition must be met.

The panel then concluded that "[t]he President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here." Executive Order No. 14163, § 3(a) contains the President's determination that "entry into the United States of refugees under the [USRAP] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States." This express finding in the text of the executive order satisfied the statutory prerequisite.

Equally important is what the panel said about the nature of judicial review of this finding. The panel stated that courts "cannot engage in 'a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications.'" This quotation from *Hawaii* establishes a highly deferential standard of review. The court will not second-guess the President's assessment of whether refugee admissions are detrimental to the national interest. The court will not evaluate the quality of the evidence supporting the finding. The court will not weigh the finding against contrary evidence or alternative assessments. The court's role is limited to verifying that the President made the finding; the substance of the finding is beyond judicial scrutiny.

This deferential posture has profound implications for the plaintiffs' case. Any argument that the President's finding of detrimentality was poorly reasoned, unsupported by evidence, or motivated by improper purposes would face a nearly insurmountable barrier under the panel's reading of *Hawaii*. The court's inquiry is essentially formal: Did the President make the requisite finding? If yes, the statutory prerequisite is satisfied.

## How the Remaining Factors Favor the Government

Having concluded that the government made a strong showing of likely success on the merits of the admission suspension issue, the panel turned to the remaining stay factors. Its treatment of these factors was brief but instructive.

With respect to irreparable injury, the panel did not engage in an extended analysis of the specific harms the government might suffer absent a stay. Instead, it referenced the "broad discretion" conferred on the President in matters touching upon "both domestic and foreign interests of the United States," as determined by the President himself under the authority of § 1182(f). This framing effectively treats the government's inability to implement its chosen policy as a form of irreparable injury: when the President has determined that a particular immigration policy is necessary to protect the national interest, being prevented from implementing that policy presumptively causes irreparable harm to the government.

The third and fourth factors—harm to other parties and the public interest—were similarly analyzed through the lens of presidential discretion. The panel stated that the remaining factors "favor the United States as well," citing the broad discretion conferred on the President over domestic and foreign affairs as articulated in *Hawaii*. This approach reflects a broader principle in stay analysis involving the federal government: when the government is acting pursuant to statutory authority that confers broad discretion on the executive branch, the balance of equities and the public interest tend to favor allowing the government to implement its chosen policy, at least where the government has shown a strong likelihood of success on the merits.

Notably, the panel did not separately analyze the harms to the plaintiffs—the refugees whose admissions were suspended and the organizations whose cooperative agreements were terminated—in the context of the stay factors as applied to the admission suspension. This reflects the panel's view that the President's authority under § 1182(f) is so broad that the balance of equities necessarily tips in the government's favor when the government is exercising that authority.

## The Panel's Decision to Stay the District Court's Injunctions on the Admission Suspension in Their Entirety

The cumulative effect of the panel's analysis of the four *Nken* factors was a decision to stay the district court's preliminary injunctions in their entirety with respect to the admission suspension issue. This means that Executive Order No. 14163's suspension of refugee admissions could go into effect while the appeal was pending. The district court's February 28 order, which had prohibited enforcement of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of the executive order, was stayed. The district court's March 24 order, which had required reinstatement of terminated cooperative agreements, was also stayed.

This stay replaced the prior partial stay issued on March 25, which had included a "limited carveout" protecting individuals conditionally approved for refugee status before January 20, 2025. By vacating the March 25 stay (as amended on April 21 and May 9) and the July 18 administrative stay, and by issuing a new stay that did not include the carveout, the panel effectively allowed the government to suspend refugee admissions for all categories of refugees, including those who had been conditionally approved before the executive order was issued.

It is important to understand what the panel did and did not decide. The panel did not decide the merits of the plaintiffs' challenge to the executive order. It did not hold that the executive order is lawful. It did not hold that the Refugee Act imposes no constraints on the President's authority. It held only that the government was likely to succeed on the merits—an assessment sufficient for stay purposes but not a final determination. The panel expressly stated that "[a]n opinion on the merits will issue in due course." The stay is thus an interim measure, designed to maintain a particular status quo while the court prepares its substantive ruling.

The one exception to the complete stay concerned the APA challenge to the defunding of resettlement services. The panel concluded that the government was not likely to prevail on at least one of the plaintiffs' APA claims, based on the mandatory service provisions of 8 U.S.C. § 1522. Accordingly, the panel directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who had already been admitted to the United States. This partial ruling reflects the panel's separation of two distinct legal issues: the President's authority to suspend refugee admissions (where the government likely prevails) and the statutory obligation to provide resettlement services to already-admitted refugees (where the plaintiffs likely prevail on at least one claim).

Judge Lee's partial dissent underscores the contested nature of this second issue. Judge Lee read § 1522 as permissive rather than mandatory, emphasizing the word "authorization" in the section's title and the repeated use of "authorized" in the funding provisions. This interpretive disagreement highlights the complexity of the statutory framework and foreshadows the detailed analysis that will appear in the court's merits opinion.

# The APA Challenge and the Statutory Mandate for Refugee Resettlement Services Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522

## The Statutory Framework of Section 1522(a)(1)(A): Mandatory Language and Appropriations

The central legal question in this portion of the *Pacito v. Trump* order is whether 8 U.S.C. § 1522 creates a mandatory obligation on the federal government to provide refugee resettlement services, or whether it merely authorizes the government to do so at its discretion. The panel majority concluded that the government was not likely to prevail on at least one of the plaintiffs' challenges under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), and the foundation for that conclusion lies in the text of § 1522.

Section 1522(a)(1)(A) states that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement—an office within the Department of Health and Human Services—"to the extent of available appropriations" shall carry out several enumerated functions. The use of the word "shall" is significant. In statutory interpretation, "shall" is generally understood as mandatory, imposing a duty on the actor to whom it is directed, as distinguished from "may," which is permissive. The panel majority's reasoning implicitly treats the "shall" in § 1522(a)(1)(A) as imposing binding obligations on the Director, subject only to the qualifier "to the extent of available appropriations."

That qualifier— "to the extent of available appropriations"—is a standard congressional formulation that limits the scope of a statutory mandate to the funds Congress has actually appropriated for the purpose. It does not, in the panel's view, convert the provision into a discretionary grant of authority. Rather, it cabins the mandate: the government must provide the specified services, but only up to the amount Congress has made available. If appropriations are insufficient to cover all eligible recipients, the government may allocate resources accordingly, but it cannot simply decline to provide the services at all so long as appropriated funds exist.

This interpretive dispute—that is, whether § 1522 is mandatory or permissive—is the fault line between the panel majority and Judge Lee's partial dissent. Judge Lee reads the title of § 1522, "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees," as signaling that the provision is permissive. He argues that "authorization" connotes permission rather than command, and that the specific funding provisions in subsections (b) through (e) use the phrase "the Director is authorized," which he treats as a signal of executive discretion. The panel majority, by contrast, focuses on the "shall" language in subsection (a) and the specific obligations enumerated there, treating the authorization language in the subsections as describing the mechanism for fulfilling the mandatory duties imposed by subsection (a).

## Specific Statutory Obligations Under Section 1522(a)(1)(A)

Section 1522(a)(1)(A) enumerates four specific obligations the Director must fulfill, to the extent of available appropriations. First, the Director must "make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible." This provision reflects Congress's policy goal that refugees should transition to economic independence, and it places on the Director the affirmative responsibility to ensure that adequate employment-related resources are available.

Second, the Director must "provide refugees with the opportunity to acquire sufficient English language training to enable them to become effectively resettled as quickly as possible." This obligation targets linguistic integration, again with the objective of rapid and effective resettlement. Third, the Director must "insure that cash assistance is made available to refugees in such a manner as not to discourage their economic self-sufficiency." This provision addresses direct financial support, but it includes a conditioning principle: cash assistance must be structured so as not to undermine the goal of self-sufficiency. Fourth, the Director must "insure that women have the same opportunities as men to participate in training and instruction," an anti-discrimination provision ensuring gender equity in access to the programs.

The panel majority treated these as concrete, mandatory duties. The opinion lists them in full and then proceeds to analyze the government's conduct against them. The significance of enumerating these specific obligations is that they define the content of the "reception and placement" services that the government is required to provide. The panel's later directive—that the government reinstate cooperative agreements "necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522"—draws its substantive content from these enumerated functions and the related provisions discussed below.

## The Director's Duty to Unaccompanied Minor Refugees Under Section 1522(d)(2)(B)

Section 1522(d)(2)(B) addresses the treatment of unaccompanied minor refugees—children who arrive in the United States without a parent or guardian. The provision states that "[d]uring any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child's immediate care."

The panel majority cited this provision as further evidence of mandatory statutory obligations. The use of "shall" signals a duty, and the provision specifies both legal and financial responsibility for the child. The phrase "if necessary" introduces a degree of conditional discretion—it is only when such responsibility is necessary that the duty attaches—but the majority treated the provision as part of the broader statutory scheme mandating services.

Judge Lee, in dissent, argued that this provision "drips with deference to the executive branch and invokes no firm mandate." He emphasized the word "necessary" and noted that the section also uses the word "authorized," which he reads as permissive. He also observed that it was unclear from the record whether any unaccompanied refugee children were actually at issue in the case. This last point is procedurally significant: if no unaccompanied minor refugees are affected by the government's actions, the provision may not have operative relevance to the plaintiffs' claims, even if it is part of the broader statutory context the majority relies upon.

## The Secretary's Duty Under Section 1522(e)(7)(A) to Develop Alternative Projects for Interim Support

Section 1522(e)(7)(A) imposes a duty on the Secretary of Health and Human Services (rather than the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement) to "develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed." The panel majority cited this provision as another example of mandatory language in § 1522, noting that the Secretary "shall develop and implement" the described projects.

The phrase "as needed" functions similarly to the "if necessary" language in § 1522(d)(2)(B): it introduces a degree of situational discretion into the obligation. The government must develop and implement alternative projects, but the scope and intensity of the services provided—interim support, medical services, support services, and case management—depends on the needs of the refugees. Judge Lee read the "as needed" language as conferring executive discretion over the "metes and bounds" of the programs, arguing that this discretion is inconsistent with a firm congressional mandate. The panel majority, however, appears to have treated "as needed" as qualifying the level of services required, not as eliminating the duty to provide services altogether.

## Section 1523: Congressional Accountability Through Annual Reporting

The panel also referenced 8 U.S.C. § 1523, which requires the Secretary to submit "a detailed report on activities under this subchapter to the Committees on the Judiciary of the House of Representatives and of the Senate not later than January 31 following the end of each fiscal year." This reporting requirement is part of the statutory scheme that the panel invoked to support its characterization of § 1522 as creating binding obligations. The logic is that a reporting requirement presupposes activities to report: if the executive branch were free to simply decline to operate the programs authorized under § 1522, the reporting requirement would have little purpose. While the opinion does not dwell on § 1523 at length, its inclusion reinforces the majority's reading of the overall statutory framework as one in which Congress expected and required the executive branch to carry out refugee resettlement functions, subject to the availability of appropriations.

## Congressional Appropriations for FY 2025

A critical element of the panel's analysis is the existence of appropriated funds. The opinion notes that for FY 2025, Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance. This appropriation was reduced by $800 million under the Rescissions Act of 2025, leaving approximately $2.378 billion. The panel's citation of these figures directly addresses the "to the extent of available appropriations" qualifier in § 1522(a)(1)(A). Because Congress had in fact appropriated substantial funds for refugee assistance, the qualifier does not excuse the government from its statutory obligations on the ground that no funds were available. The appropriated funds exist; the question is whether the government may decline to use them for the purposes Congress designated.

This is a crucial analytical move. If no funds had been appropriated, the government's obligation under § 1522 might be purely theoretical—an unfunded mandate with no practical consequence. But the existence of a substantial appropriation makes the statutory duty operative. The government cannot invoke the appropriations qualifier as a reason to suspend services when Congress has already provided the funding. The panel's recitation of the appropriation figures thus closes a potential gap in the plaintiffs' claim and supports the conclusion that the government is not likely to prevail on this APA challenge.

## Cooperative Agreements as the Mechanism for Providing Reception and Placement Services

Section 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii) provides that the resettlement services required under § 1522 may be provided "through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies." The panel noted that the United States has historically entered into cooperative agreements with various resettlement organizations to provide these services. The organizational plaintiffs in this case are three such organizations, each of which had a cooperative agreement with the Department of State.

The concept of a cooperative agreement is a specific federal funding instrument. Unlike a procurement contract, which is primarily used when the government is acquiring goods or services for its own use, a cooperative agreement is used when the government is providing financial assistance to a recipient to carry out a public purpose authorized by law. The cooperative agreement mechanism reflects the statutory design: Congress authorized the government to fund nonprofit organizations to provide resettlement services directly to refugees. The government's ability to fulfill its statutory obligations under § 1522 thus depends, in practice, on the existence of functioning cooperative agreements with organizations capable of delivering those services.

This practical dependency is central to the panel's analysis. The statutory mandate under § 1522 is not self-executing; the government cannot provide employment training, English language classes, cash assistance, and case management directly to every refugee. It relies on cooperative agreements with specialized nonprofit organizations to do so. When the government terminated these agreements, it effectively dismantled the only mechanism through which it could fulfill its statutory obligations.

## The Government's Termination of Cooperative Agreements and Its March 10 Representation

The factual sequence is important to the panel's reasoning. On February 26, 2025—one day after the district court orally issued its preliminary injunction—the government terminated every cooperative agreement to provide reception and placement services for refugees in the United States, as well as all but one of the cooperative agreements to provide USRAP processing support abroad. The timing suggests that the terminations were part of the government's implementation of Executive Order 14163, though the opinion does not explicitly characterize the motive.

On March 10, 2025, the government advised the district court that it was "actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services." This representation is significant because it constitutes a acknowledgment by the government that the need for reception and placement services persists and that some mechanism for providing them must exist. In other words, the government did not argue that it had no obligation to provide such services; instead, it represented that it was working to establish a new mechanism for doing so. This representation is consistent with the panel majority's reading of § 1522 as imposing a mandatory obligation—had the government believed the services were purely discretionary, there would have been no need to prepare a request for proposals.

## The Government's Uncertainty at Oral Argument

The panel's oral argument on September 3, 2025, appears to have been a turning point. Despite the government's March 10 representation that it was actively preparing a request for proposals, at oral argument the government could not confirm whether any progress had been made toward securing a new agency or agencies to provide reception and placement services. The opinion states that the government "advised the panel at oral argument that it was unaware whether any progress had been made."

This uncertainty is critical to the panel's decision to direct the reinstatement of cooperative agreements. The government had represented to the district court that it was working toward a solution, but it could not demonstrate to the court of appeals that any concrete steps had been taken. In the context of a stay pending appeal, the panel needed to assess whether the government was likely to comply with its statutory obligations during the pendency of the appeal. The government's uncertainty about its ability to provide the statutorily mandated services meant that refugees already admitted to the United States might be left without the support that Congress required the government to provide. This practical gap—between the statutory mandate and the government's actual capacity to fulfill it—provided the basis for the panel's directive.

## The Panel's Directive to Reinstate Cooperative Agreements

The culmination of the panel's analysis of the APA challenge is its directive to the government. The panel ordered the government to "reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States." The panel further specified that such services "shall include the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements."

Several features of this directive are noteworthy. First, it is limited to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States. The panel did not require the government to provide resettlement services to refugees who have not yet been admitted, because the President's authority to suspend admissions under § 1182(f) was upheld. Second, the directive references "the usual and customary services" provided under prior cooperative agreements, grounding the scope of the required services in the established practice that existed before the government's terminations. Third, the directive uses the cooperative agreement mechanism—that is, the same statutory framework Congress established under § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii)—as the means of compliance. The panel did not order the government to create a new program or invent a new delivery mechanism; it ordered the reinstatement of the existing one.

The legal significance of the directive is that it treats the APA challenge to the defunding of resettlement services differently from the challenge to the suspension of refugee admissions. On the admissions question, the panel found the government likely to succeed and stayed the district court's injunction. On the resettlement services question, the panel found the government not likely to succeed and declined to stay the district court's injunction insofar as it required the provision of services to already-admitted refugees. The stay is partial: the district court's injunctions are stayed "in their entirety, except as noted," and the reinstatement directive constitutes the exception. This bifurcation reflects the panel's view that the President's § 1182(f) authority over admissions is broad and deferentially reviewed, but that the statutory mandate for services to already-admitted refugees under § 1522 is a separate legal question governed by ordinary principles of statutory interpretation and administrative law.

Judge Lee's dissent takes the opposite view on the services question. He would stay the district court's injunctions in their entirety, reading § 1522 as permissive rather than mandatory and arguing that "the law does not demand the executive branch provide these services." His analysis highlights the interpretive ambiguity in § 1522's text—the tension between the "shall" language in subsection (a) and the "authorized" language in the funding subsections—and illustrates the competing readings that the panel's forthcoming merits opinion will need to resolve.

# Judge Lee's Partial Concurrence and Partial Dissent: Section 1522 as Authorization, Not Mandate

## Agreement with the Majority on Refugee Admissions and the Scope of the Stay

Judge Lee's separate opinion begins with a point of agreement: the President possesses vast discretion in immigration matters, and the district court erred in enjoining the executive branch from implementing the President's decision to suspend refugee admissions. On this issue, Judge Lee would stay the district court's injunctions in their entirety, reasoning that the President's authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend the entry of classes of aliens is broad and that courts cannot conduct "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications," as the Supreme Court held in *Trump v. Hawaii*. This alignment with the majority is significant because it means the central disagreement between Judge Lee and his colleagues is not about the scope of presidential power over immigration but about the separate question of what Congress required of the executive branch once refugees have already been admitted into the United States.

The divergence arises on a narrow but consequential issue: whether the federal government has a legal duty to provide reception and placement services to refugees who were admitted before the suspension took effect. The majority concluded that the government is likely to lose on at least one of the plaintiffs' claims under the APA and directed the reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide statutorily mandated services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. Judge Lee disagrees. In his view, § 1522 is most naturally read as an authorization—a grant of permissive power—rather than a mandate that compels the executive branch to act. This interpretive disagreement is the heart of his partial dissent and raises a fundamental question about the proper role of courts in enforcing statutory provisions that may or may not require specific government action.

## The Title of Section 1522 as Textual Evidence of Permissive Intent

Judge Lee begins his statutory analysis at the beginning: with the title of the provision. Section 1522 is captioned "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." The word "authorization," Judge Lee emphasizes, is permissive, not mandatory. He invokes the definition from *Black's Law Dictionary*, which defines "authorization" as "permission to do something" or the "official document granting such permission." The significance of this starting point is that when Congress intends to require government action, it typically uses language of obligation—words like "shall" or "must"—in both the title and the operative provisions. Here, the title signals that Congress is granting permission for programs to exist, not commanding that they be created or maintained.

This textualist approach to statutory titles is a recognized method of statutory interpretation. While titles are not always dispositive of meaning, courts frequently look to them as one indicator of legislative intent. Judge Lee uses the title here as an interpretive anchor: if Congress intended to mandate that the executive branch provide resettlement services, one would expect the provision to be titled something closer to "Requirement for programs" or "Duty to provide domestic resettlement." The word "Authorization" sets the stage for Judge Lee's broader argument that the entire structure of § 1522 is permissive in nature.

## The "Authorized" Language in Subsections (b) Through (e) as a Signal of Executive Discretion

Building on the title, Judge Lee points to the operative language of the statute. The four specific provisions that provide funding for different refugee services—covering topics such as the "Program of initial resettlement" and "Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees"—all state that the "Director is authorized" to provide assistance. 8 U.S.C. §§ 1522(b)–(e). Judge Lee reads this repeated use of "authorized" as a statutory signal that the executive branch retains discretion over whether to fund these programs.

The contrast between permissive and mandatory statutory language is a well-established principle of statutory interpretation. When Congress says an official "is authorized" to do something, it grants that official the power to act but does not compel action. When Congress says an official "shall" do something, it imposes an obligation. The repeated use of "authorized" across the four substantive subsections of § 1522 thus forms a pattern that, in Judge Lee's view, confirms the permissive reading suggested by the statute's title. The executive branch has the authority to create and fund these programs, but the statute does not require that the programs operate.

## The Qualifying Phrase in Section 1522(a) and the Limits of "Shall"

The majority's contrary reading relies on § 1522(a), which is titled "Conditions and consideration" and uses the word "shall" along with the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations." The majority treats this language as evidence that the government must provide the services described. Judge Lee counters by emphasizing a critical qualifying phrase that immediately precedes the mandatory language: "In providing assistance under this section." This phrase, Judge Lee argues, fundamentally changes the character of the obligations that follow.

In Judge Lee's reading, § 1522(a) does not independently mandate that the government provide refugee assistance. Rather, it imposes mandatory conditions and considerations that apply only if the government chooses to fund the refugee programs authorized under §§ 1522(b)–(e). The word "shall" in § 1522(a) thus operates as a conditional mandate: if the government decides to provide assistance under the discretionary funding provisions, then it must do so in a manner that satisfies certain conditions—such as making available sufficient resources for employment training and placement to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible. The mandatory language operates within the framework of the discretionary authorization; it does not transform the authorization itself into a requirement.

This interpretive move is significant because it resolves the tension between the permissive language of the title and the operative subsections and the mandatory language of § 1522(a). Judge Lee's reading harmonizes these provisions: Congress authorized the executive branch to create refugee resettlement programs and, should it choose to do so, imposed conditions on how those programs must operate. The majority's reading, by contrast, would read the "shall" language in § 1522(a) as overriding the permissive signals in the rest of the statute, creating an obligation to fund programs that Congress described in authorization language.

## Mandatory Conditions on Discretionary Funding: Judge Lee's Structural Reading

Judge Lee's interpretation of § 1522(a) reflects a structural approach to statutory interpretation, one that reads individual provisions in the context of the statute as a whole. He identifies a coherent architecture: §§ 1522(b)–(e) authorize the executive branch to fund various refugee services, and § 1522(a) specifies the conditions that govern the exercise of that authorization. Under this reading, the statute creates a two-step framework. First, the executive branch decides whether to provide assistance under the discretionary funding provisions. Second, if it does provide assistance, it must comply with the mandatory conditions set out in § 1522(a).

For example, § 1522(a)(1)(A) requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, "to the extent of available appropriations," to make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement, provide English language training, ensure cash assistance does not discourage economic self-sufficiency, and ensure women have equal opportunities to participate in training. Judge Lee reads each of these requirements as a condition attached to the discretionary funding authorized under § 1522(c), which covers "Project grants and contracts for services for refugees." The mandatory language tells the Director how to administer the funding if it is provided, not that the funding must be provided in the first instance.

This structural reading has implications for the separation of powers. If § 1522 is understood as an authorization, the decision whether to fund resettlement programs rests with the executive branch, subject to Congress's power of the purse and its ability to enact more specific legislation. If § 1522 is understood as a mandate, courts must order the executive branch to fund and operate programs regardless of the executive's policy judgments—a result that, in Judge Lee's view, exceeds the proper judicial role.

## Subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A): Why "Necessary" and "As Needed" Inject Executive Discretion

Judge Lee also addresses two specific subsections that the majority cites as evidence of mandatory obligations: §§ 1522(d)(2)(B) and 1522(e)(7)(A). These provisions are notable because they use the word "shall," which the majority reads as signaling a mandatory duty. Judge Lee argues that the surrounding language in each provision introduces significant executive discretion, undermining any firm mandate.

Subsection (d)(2)(B) addresses unaccompanied minor refugees. It provides that during any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit, "the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child's immediate care." Judge Lee acknowledges the word "shall" but emphasizes the qualifier "if necessary," which appears twice in close proximity. The Director's obligation to assume legal and financial responsibility arises only when such responsibility is "necessary," and the Director is "authorized" to make "necessary" decisions about the child's immediate care. Judge Lee reads these qualifiers as deferring to the executive branch's judgment about what is necessary in a given situation. The subsection, he writes, "drips with deference to the executive branch and invokes no firm mandate." He also notes that the record is unclear whether any unaccompanied refugee children are actually at issue in this case, which further weakens the provision's relevance to the disposition.

Subsection (e)(7)(A) addresses cash and medical assistance for recently resettled refugees. It provides that the Secretary of Health and Human Services "shall develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed." Judge Lee again acknowledges the word "shall" but focuses on the phrase "as needed." In his reading, this phrase leaves the scope and content of the projects to executive discretion. The Secretary must develop and implement projects, but the "metes and bounds of the programs are up to executive discretion—hardly a congressional mandate." The phrase "as needed" functions as a built-in limit on the obligation, making the extent of the required services dependent on the executive branch's assessment of need.

Judge Lee's analysis of these two subsections illustrates a broader interpretive principle: the presence of the word "shall" does not automatically convert a provision into an unfettered mandate. Courts must read mandatory language in context, paying attention to qualifying terms that introduce discretion. When Congress includes qualifiers like "if necessary" and "as needed," it signals that the executive branch retains judgment over whether and how to act. Judge Lee's reading thus treats these subsections as consistent with the overall permissive structure of § 1522, rather than as exceptions that transform the statute into a mandate.

## The Judicial Role: Courts Cannot Mandate What Congress Has Not Required

Judge Lee's conclusion flows directly from his statutory interpretation. Because § 1522 authorizes the executive branch to craft refugee resettlement programs under its discretion so long as appropriations flow, but does not require that those programs operate, the court is "in no position to mandate them." This statement captures a fundamental principle of judicial restraint: courts can enforce statutory obligations, but they cannot create obligations that Congress has not imposed. If Congress wanted to require the executive branch to provide reception and placement services, it could have used mandatory language throughout § 1522, not just in the conditional provisions of § 1522(a) and the qualified language of §§ 1522(d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A).

This principle has particular force in the context of immigration and foreign affairs, areas in which courts have historically deferred to the political branches. Judge Lee's dissent thus reflects two layers of deference: deference to the President's authority over immigration admissions under § 1182(f), and deference to the executive branch's discretion over the implementation of authorized but not mandated programs under § 1522. The majority's approach, in Judge Lee's view, oversteps the judicial role on the second layer by ordering the reinstatement of cooperative agreements that Congress has not clearly required.

## Broader Implications for Separation of Powers in Refugee Policy

The disagreement between the majority and Judge Lee reflects a deeper tension in the separation of powers over refugee policy. Congress enacted the Refugee Act of 1980 to establish a framework for the admission and resettlement of refugees, but the language it chose in § 1522 leaves substantial room for executive discretion. The majority reads this discretion narrowly, finding a mandatory obligation to provide services once refugees have been admitted. Judge Lee reads the same language as preserving executive branch policymaking authority over whether and how to operate resettlement programs.

The stakes of this interpretive debate extend beyond the immediate parties. If § 1522 is a mandate, then the executive branch's ability to reshape refugee policy through executive orders and funding decisions is constrained by judicially enforceable obligations to provide services. If § 1522 is an authorization, the executive branch retains greater latitude to adjust or suspend programs in response to changing policy priorities, subject only to Congress's power to legislate more specific requirements. Judge Lee's dissent thus advances a vision of refugee policy in which the elected branches—not the courts—bear primary responsibility for deciding whether resettlement services must be provided, so long as Congress has spoken in terms of authorization rather than mandate.
