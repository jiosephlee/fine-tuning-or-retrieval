Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### Why does the court distinguish between refugee admissions and services for already-admitted refugees?
Question:
The court stays the injunction on the refugee admission suspension (favoring the government) but refuses to stay the injunction requiring reinstatement of cooperative agreements for reception and placement services to refugees already admitted (favoring the plaintiffs). I understand the practical distinction—entry vs. post-entry services—but I'm confused about the legal basis for treating them differently. If § 1182(f) gives the President broad discretion over immigration, why doesn't that same deference extend to decisions about funding services for refugees who are already here? Is there a statutory or constitutional principle that limits executive discretion once a refugee has been admitted?
Answer:
The short answer is that **different statutes govern different stages of the refugee process**, and the broad deference the President enjoys under § 1182(f) does not automatically carry over to a separate statutory scheme that Congress enacted specifically to govern post-admission services.

**Entry is governed by § 1182(f).** That statute gives the President authority to suspend the *entry* of any class of aliens he finds detrimental to the United States. As the Ninth Circuit notes, quoting *Trump v. Hawaii*, this provision "exudes deference" to the executive and sets only one prerequisite: that the President make the requisite finding. 585 U.S. at 684–85. The court applies that standard straightforwardly to the admission moratorium, concluding the government is likely to succeed on the merits.

**Post-entry services are governed by 8 U.S.C. § 1522**, part of the Refugee Act of 1980. This statute addresses what the government owes refugees *after* admission—employment training, English language instruction, cash and medical assistance, and care for unaccompanied minors. The court reads several provisions of § 1522 as mandatory: subsection (a)(1)(A) states the Director "shall" make available specified resources; (d)(2)(B) provides the Director "shall assume legal responsibility" for unaccompanied minors "if necessary"; and (e)(7)(A) states the Secretary "shall develop and implement" alternative support projects. The court also notes that § 1522 expressly envisions services being delivered through cooperative agreements with nonprofit agencies—exactly the agreements the government terminated.

The legal principle at work is straightforward statutory interpretation: **when Congress enacts a specific statute imposing obligations on the executive, the executive cannot avoid those obligations by invoking a different, broader statute that grants discretionary authority over a different subject matter.** Section 1182(f) speaks to *entry*; § 1522 speaks to *treatment after entry*. The deference owed under the former does not dissolve the duties imposed by the latter.

This is also why the court finds the government's practical position problematic. At oral argument, the government could not confirm whether any progress had been made toward securing replacement service providers. Given that § 1522 appears to mandate these services and Congress has appropriated funds for them, the court declines to stay the injunction requiring reinstatement of cooperative agreements for already-admitted refugees.

Judge Lee's partial dissent is worth noting because it highlights the interpretive dispute at the heart of the case. He argues § 1522 is permissive, not mandatory, pointing to the statute's title ("Authorization for programs…"), the "is authorized" language in subsections (b)–(e), and the qualifying phrase in subsection (a) that conditions its "shall" language on the government having first chosen to fund programs under those subsections. On his reading, the mandatory-sounding language in (a), (d)(2)(B), and (e)(7)(A) only triggers *if* the government opts to provide services—imposing conditions on voluntary action rather than compelling the action itself.

So the split boils down to this: the majority reads § 1522 as creating enforceable statutory obligations for already-admitted refugees, distinct from the President's § 1182(f) admission authority. Judge Lee reads § 1522 as a grant of discretionary authority no different in kind from § 1182(f), and therefore concludes the court has no basis to mandate services the statute does not require.
