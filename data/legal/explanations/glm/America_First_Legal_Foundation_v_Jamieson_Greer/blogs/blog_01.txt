Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Procedural Rights in Vacuo: AFL v. Greer and the Limits of Procedural Standing

When does a statutory right to have the government investigate your complaint become a judicially cognizable injury? The D.C. Circuit's recent decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* offers a sharp reminder that the answer is not always—and perhaps not often—when the statute creates only a procedural entitlement to agency action. The case, decided October 3, 2025, by Judge Katsas, vacates a district court judgment on Article III standing grounds and, in doing so, tightens the doctrinal screws on plaintiffs who would leverage statutory oversight mechanisms into federal lawsuits.

## Why This Case Matters

The case matters because it tests the boundary between statutory entitlements and Article III injury in the context of agency oversight—a boundary that has absorbed considerable judicial attention in recent years. AFL sought to compel the Office of Special Counsel to investigate an alleged DOJ policy of arbitrarily withholding FOIA records. The underlying dispute was real enough: AFL had requested DOJ emails as part of an investigation into judicial recusals, and DOJ's Civil Division reportedly refused to search employee emails without the employees' consent. But AFL's chosen path to redress—suing OSC for failing to investigate that policy under § 1216(a)(3) of the Civil Service Reform Act—required the court to confront a threshold question that neither party had initially raised: Does a plaintiff who demands an agency investigation have standing to compel it?

The D.C. Circuit's answer, grounded in the "procedural right in vacuo" doctrine from *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*, 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009), is that a statutory procedural right, standing alone, cannot supply Article III standing. Something more is needed—a concrete interest that the procedural right is designed to protect. This principle, though well established, has proven difficult to apply at the margins, and *AFL v. Greer* illustrates its operational force in a setting that might have seemed promising for the plaintiff.

## The Statutory Framework and AFL's Theory

The statutory architecture here is layered. Section 1216(a)(3) directs OSC to "conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552"—that is, under FOIA. Section 1216(c) adds that OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." Separately, FOIA's own enforcement provision, § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), requires OSC to initiate disciplinary proceedings when a court has ordered production of records, awarded fees, and specifically questioned whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously.

OSC declined to investigate, taking the position that the three predicates under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) had not been satisfied. AFL countered that § 1216 independently required an investigation regardless of whether the FOIA-specific triggers were met. The district court agreed with AFL on the legal question—holding that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not cabin OSC's § 1216 authority—but nonetheless declined to order an investigation, reading § 1216(c) as committing the investigative decision to OSC's discretion. After a remand on which OSC again declined to act, the district court entered final judgment against AFL.

## The Standing Analysis

The D.C. Circuit never reached the merits. Judge Katsas, writing for the panel, framed the standing question around the well-established principle that "the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient to confer Article III standing." *Summers*, 555 U.S. at 496. This is the doctrinal hinge on which the case turns.

AFL advanced two theories of concrete informational injury, each attempting to tether its § 1216 procedural right to a concrete interest. The court found both wanting, but for instructively different reasons.

The first theory posited that OSC's failure to investigate caused AFL to be denied the FOIA records it had requested from DOJ. The court rejected this on causation and redressability grounds. Any informational injury from DOJ's withholding was "plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC." The causal chain AFL proposed—OSC investigates, OSC pursues corrective or disciplinary action, the Merit Systems Protection Board sanctions offending DOJ employees, and then DOJ produces the records—required speculation about the choices of two entities not before the court, plus speculation about how OSC would exercise its enforcement discretion. Citing *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, 602 U.S. 367, 383 (2024), the court observed that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." And citing *Haaland v. Brackeen*, 599 U.S. 255, 293–94 (2023), the court reminded that redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might influence non-parties.

The second theory was more creative. AFL argued that if OSC conducted the § 1216 investigation, AFL might receive the notices and information that § 1214 requires when OSC investigates a "prohibited personnel practice." Because § 1216(c) incorporates § 1214 procedures, AFL reasoned, an OSC investigation would carry procedural entitlements to information. The court dispatched this theory by noting that AFL itself did not contend that OSC *must* apply § 1214 notice requirements to § 1216 investigations. AFL acknowledged—and the Sixth Circuit had held in *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*, 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011)—that OSC's decision to follow § 1214 procedures in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary. Without a statutory entitlement to receive information, there is no concrete informational injury. And if OSC need not provide § 1214 notices, ordering an investigation would not be likely to redress the asserted information deficit.

## The Procedural Standing Framework in Context

The decision sits comfortably within the D.C. Circuit's evolving procedural standing jurisprudence. The governing framework, drawn from *Center for Law & Education v. Department of Education*, 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005), relaxes—though does not eliminate—the imminence and redressability requirements when a plaintiff alleges the violation of a procedural right designed to protect a threatened concrete interest. But the relaxation is conditional: the plaintiff must still demonstrate that the procedural violation caused injury to a "concrete, particularized interest." The court in *Citizens for Constitutional Integrity v. Census Bureau*, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024), applied this same framework to reject standing where plaintiffs sought to compel the Census Bureau to perform a statutory quality-review procedure, finding no concrete interest affected by the procedural deprivation.

*AFL v. Greer* extends this logic in a significant way. The plaintiff here was not seeking information directly from the defendant agency, as in *Citizens for Constitutional Integrity*. Rather, AFL sought to compel an oversight agency to investigate a *different* agency, with the downstream hope that the oversight process would eventually yield either the original records or procedural information about the investigation itself. The attenuated, multi-link causal chain—OSC to MSPB to DOJ, or OSC investigation to discretionary § 1214 notices—proved too long and too speculative to satisfy even the relaxed requirements.

One notable feature of the opinion is the court's explicit choice not to decide whether AFL's alleged injuries were procedural or substantive. Because AFL could not satisfy even the relaxed standing requirements for procedural injuries, the court saw no need to classify the injuries. This is doctrinally tidy—it avoids an unnecessary constitutional question—but it leaves some ambiguity about how courts should characterize statutory rights to agency oversight. If § 1216 creates a mandatory duty to investigate, is the denial of that duty a procedural injury (because it involves a process right) or a substantive injury (because the duty is mandatory and non-discretionary)? The court's avoidance of this question means that a future plaintiff with a stronger concrete interest might still press the classification issue, and the D.C. Circuit has left the door open.

## Downstream Implications

The practical implications for plaintiffs seeking to compel agency oversight are considerable. The decision erects a significant barrier for any litigant who hopes to use statutory oversight provisions as a vehicle for judicial intervention in agency behavior. The key problem is not that such provisions lack legal force—it may well be, as the district court concluded, that § 1216 creates a mandatory duty independent of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—but that the *judicial remedy* for violating that duty is constrained by Article III in ways the statute does not address.

This creates something of an enforcement gap. If OSC has a statutory duty to investigate FOIA violations under § 1216, but no plaintiff has standing to compel that investigation, the duty is, as a practical matter, unenforceable in federal court. Congress may need to build standing into such provisions more deliberately—either by creating explicit information-disclosure entitlements for complainants or by designating specific persons or entities with recognized concrete interests. The D.C. Circuit's approach suggests that without such legislative care, oversight duties will remain largely self-executing, subject only to whatever internal or political accountability mechanisms exist.

The case also illustrates the continuing force of *Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. 821 (1985), in the oversight context. Even where a statute uses the word "shall," the prospect of compelling an agency's exercise of enforcement discretion through judicial process remains doctrinally fraught. Judge Katsas's citation to *Heckler* signals that the speculative nature of agency enforcement decisions remains a core redressability problem, even when the plaintiff's claim is framed not as a direct request to enforce but as a request to investigate.

Finally, the opinion underscores a structural point about Article III that the Supreme Court has emphasized with increasing clarity: federal courts are not oversight bodies. They resolve concrete disputes between parties with concrete, particularized, and redressable injuries. A statute that creates an oversight duty does not, without more, create a justiciable controversy when that duty goes unfulfilled. Whether that is a feature or a defect of the constitutional design depends on one's perspective, but *AFL v. Greer* confirms that the D.C. Circuit will enforce that design with care.
