Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Why does redressability fail when ordering the defendant to do what the statute says it 'shall' do?
Question:
The court says a favorable judgment wouldn't likely redress AFL's injuries because redressability "turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might persuade actors who are not before the court" (citing Haaland v. Brackeen). But if the court orders OSC to conduct the investigation that §1216 says it "shall" conduct, isn't OSC bound by that order? Even if MSPB and DOJ aren't parties, the OSC investigation could lead to corrective action against DOJ employees. Why is the connection between an OSC investigation and eventual FOIA disclosure too remote for redressability? Is the court saying that even mandatory agency actions can't redress injuries if the ultimate relief depends on subsequent decisions by non-party actors?
Answer:
## The investigation is only the first link in a speculative causal chain

Your instinct that OSC would be bound by a court order is correct — but binding OSC to investigate is not the same as redressing AFL's injury. The court's reasoning turns on the difference between what a judgment directly compels a party to do and what *might* flow from that compelled action through a series of discretionary decisions by non-parties.

### What redressability requires

Redressability asks whether a favorable decision is **likely** to remedy the plaintiff's injury — not merely whether it might set in motion a process that could eventually lead to relief. The court invokes *Haaland v. Brackeen* for the principle that redressability "turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might persuade actors who are not before the court." This is the analytical frame for the entire standing analysis.

### Why ordering the investigation doesn't redress the FOIA injury

AFL's injury is the denial of records it requested from DOJ. Even if the court ordered OSC to conduct the §1216 investigation, producing those records would require a multi-step chain of events, each step of which depends on discretionary decisions by actors not before the court:

1. **OSC's enforcement discretion.** Even after investigating, OSC must decide whether to seek corrective action under §1214 or disciplinary action under §1215. Section 1216(c) says OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action" — it does not require OSC to pursue enforcement. The court cites *Heckler v. Chaney* for the principle that agency enforcement decisions are generally committed to agency discretion and not subject to judicial compulsion.

2. **MSPB's adjudicatory decision.** Even if OSC brings a case, the Merit Systems Protection Board — not a party to this lawsuit — must decide whether to impose sanctions. That decision is not bound by any judgment against OSC.

3. **DOJ's compliance decision.** Even if MSPB sanctions individual DOJ employees, DOJ itself — also not a party — must decide whether to produce the requested records. Nothing in a judgment against OSC would compel DOJ to do anything.

The court draws on *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* for the rule that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." The same logic applies to redressability: a judgment that binds only OSC cannot guarantee relief that depends on subsequent choices by MSPB and DOJ.

### The key distinction

The court is **not** saying that mandatory agency actions can never redress injuries. It is saying that when the plaintiff's injury can only be remedied through a chain of speculative decisions by non-parties, a judgment binding only one party in that chain does not redress the injury. Compare a scenario where ordering OSC to investigate would itself produce the information AFL wants — that would be redressable because the judgment directly produces the relief. Here, the investigation is just the first step, and the ultimate relief (FOIA disclosure by DOJ) depends on two subsequent decisions by non-parties plus an exercise of OSC's own enforcement discretion.

### The second theory fails for a similar reason

AFL's alternative theory — that it has an informational interest in the investigation itself — also fails because §1216 creates no entitlement to receive information from OSC. AFL acknowledges that OSC's decision whether to follow §1214's notice requirements in a §1216 investigation is discretionary (*Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*). So even a court order compelling the investigation would not be likely to produce the notices and information AFL claims it wants, because OSC retains discretion over whether to provide them.

### Bottom line

Redressability fails not because the court order would lack binding force, but because the injury AFL asserts (denial of FOIA records) is several discretionary steps removed from the only action a court could order (an OSC investigation). The judgment would bind OSC, but the relief AFL seeks depends on decisions by MSPB and DOJ — actors who are not before the court and whose choices cannot be compelled or predicted with the likelihood Article III requires.
