Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### How does Heckler v. Chaney's presumption against judicial review of agency enforcement apply when a statute uses mandatory 'shall' language?
Question:
The court cites Heckler v. Chaney for the proposition that courts generally may not compel agencies to exercise enforcement discretion, and uses this to support its causation analysis. But §1216 says OSC "shall conduct an investigation" of alleged FOIA violations. Doesn't mandatory statutory language override the Heckler presumption? Under the APA, agency action "committed to agency discretion by law" is unreviewable, but doesn't mandatory "shall" language create a clear standard for review that takes the matter out of the Heckler presumption? The court seems to treat OSC's decision about whether to pursue corrective or disciplinary action after investigating as discretionary, but what about the initial investigation itself—is that also discretionary despite the "shall" language?
Answer:
## The Opinion Doesn't Resolve the "Shall" Question — It Dismisses on Standing

The short answer is that the D.C. Circuit does not decide whether § 1216's mandatory "shall" language overrides the *Heckler* presumption, because it never reaches the merits. The court dismisses for lack of Article III standing and uses *Heckler* not in an APA reviewability analysis under 5 U.S.C. § 701(a)(2), but rather within its causation and redressability analysis.

## How *Heckler* Functions in the Opinion

The court invokes *Heckler v. Chaney* for a narrow proposition: that "it requires speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel." The citation appears in the standing analysis, where the court is evaluating whether a favorable judgment would redress AFL's informational injury — the first standing theory, based on AFL's interest in receiving the FOIA records from DOJ.

The court's causal chain reasoning is the key. Even if OSC were ordered to investigate, redressability would require speculation that: (1) OSC *might* pursue corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215; (2) the MSPB *might* sanction DOJ employees; and (3) DOJ *might* then produce the records. *Heckler* supports the point that step (1) — OSC's decision whether to pursue enforcement after investigating — is discretionary, making the chain too attenuated under *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* and *Haaland v. Brackeen*.

## The Investigation vs. Post-Investigation Distinction

The opinion does implicitly acknowledge the distinction you identify between the initial investigation and subsequent enforcement decisions, but it does so without resolving it.

The district court below had held that § 1216 *does* mandate the investigation and that OSC's refusal was based on legal error — but that § 1216(c) committed the *post-investigation decision* to OSC's discretion. The D.C. Circuit does not endorse or reject this reasoning. It says only that "even if section 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it creates no entitlement to receive information." The court thus assumes arguendo that the investigation could be mandatory and still finds standing lacking.

On the post-investigation side, the court cites *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel* (6th Cir. 2011) for the proposition that OSC's "decision whether to follow section 1214's procedural requirements" in a § 1216 investigation is "discretionary." AFL itself acknowledged this. So the opinion treats the procedures and enforcement decisions *following* an investigation as discretionary, but it does not hold that the initial investigation is discretionary.

## Why the APA § 701(a)(2) Question Remains Open

Your question about whether mandatory "shall" language creates a clear standard that takes the matter out of the § 701(a)(2) exception ("committed to agency discretion by law") is a substantive one — but it is not addressed here. The court's standing disposition means the APA reviewability question is never reached. The *Heckler* presumption and the § 701(a)(2) exception are related but distinct doctrines: *Heckler* establishes a presumption against judicial review of agency *non-enforcement* decisions, while § 701(a)(2) precludes review of action "committed to agency discretion by law." The "shall" language argument — that mandatory statutory language provides an intelligible standard for review and thus overcomes the presumption — would need to be addressed on the merits, which this opinion does not do.

## Bottom Line

The opinion uses *Heckler* to support a causal-chain argument in the standing context, not to hold that the § 1216 investigation itself is unreviewable. The court expressly leaves open whether the "shall" language makes the initial investigation mandatory and reviewable, deciding only that even if it does, AFL lacks standing because the downstream consequences depend on discretionary choices by OSC, the MSPB, and DOJ — actors and choices too speculative to support redressability.
