Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### What makes information 'entitled' under a statute for purposes of informational injury standing?
Question:
The court rejects AFL's second standing theory because, even if §1216 requires OSC to investigate, it "creates no entitlement to receive information." AFL argued that if OSC investigated under §1216, it might provide the notices and information required under §1214. But the court notes that §1214's notice requirements are discretionary when applied to §1216 investigations (citing Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel). I'm confused about what distinguishes a statutory "entitlement" to information from mere potential access. In FEC v. Akins and Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, plaintiffs had statutory rights to receive information. What specific statutory language or structure creates an "entitlement"? Is it purely about mandatory vs. discretionary disclosure, or is there more to it?
Answer:
## What Creates a Statutory "Entitlement" to Information

The short answer is: **the statute must require that the information be disclosed to the plaintiff.** A mandatory duty to take some other action—even investigation—does not, by itself, create an entitlement to information produced by that action.

### The Standard

The opinion frames the requirement clearly: a plaintiff suffers a concrete informational injury only if denied information that "a statute entitled him to receive." The entitlement must come from the statute itself—not from what an agency *might* choose to disclose in its discretion. This is the thread connecting the cases AFL cites. In *FEC v. Akins*, the Federal Election Campaign Act required the FEC to make certain campaign finance reports *available to the public*. In *Public Citizen v. DOJ*, the Federal Advisory Committee Act required that committee meetings be *open to the public* and records *available for public inspection*. In both cases, the statutes contained mandatory disclosure provisions directed at providing information to persons like the plaintiffs.

### Why AFL Had No Entitlement

This case illustrates the distinction the question is getting at. AFL pointed to two statutory provisions:

- **§ 1216(a)(3)** requires OSC to "conduct an investigation" of alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings. This is a mandatory duty to *act*—but it says nothing about providing information to the complainant.
- **§ 1214** contains notice requirements that would obligate OSC to keep a complainant informed during an investigation—but those requirements apply by their terms to investigations of "prohibited personnel practices."

The critical link between the two is **§ 1216(c)**, which provides that for FOIA violations, OSC *"may"* investigate and seek corrective action under § 1214 "in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." The court treats this as discretionary: OSC *may* borrow § 1214's procedures, including its notice requirements, but is not *required* to. AFL itself acknowledged as much, citing *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*, which held that OSC's decision whether to follow § 1214's procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is "discretionary."

So the chain breaks: even if OSC must investigate under § 1216, it has discretion whether to provide § 1214 notices and information. Because disclosure to AFL is discretionary rather than mandatory, AFL has no statutory entitlement to receive anything. And without an entitlement, there is no concrete informational injury.

### Mandatory Investigation vs. Mandatory Disclosure

The distinction you're sensing is real, and it runs deeper than just "mandatory vs. discretionary disclosure" in a narrow sense. The court is drawing a line between:

1. **A statutory mandate to provide information to the plaintiff** (which creates an entitlement and thus an informational injury if denied), and
2. **A statutory mandate to take some action that *might* result in information being disclosed** (which does not).

In *Akins* and *Public Citizen*, the statutes fell into category (1)—they directly required that information be made available to the plaintiff. Here, § 1216 falls into category (2)—it requires investigation, but any disclosure of information from that investigation depends on OSC's discretionary choice to apply § 1214 procedures.

This is also why the court's redressability analysis reinforces the standing conclusion. Even if the court ordered OSC to conduct the § 1216 investigation, OSC could choose not to apply § 1214's notice requirements. A favorable judgment would not be "reasonably likely" to produce the information AFL seeks, because disclosure remains within OSC's discretion.

### Summary

The "entitlement" is not purely about whether disclosure is mandatory vs. discretionary—though that is the most direct way to think about it. More precisely, it is about whether **the statute itself obligates the government to provide the information to the plaintiff**, as opposed to merely requiring some government action from which information *might* flow if the agency chooses to disclose it. Here, § 1216 mandates investigation but not disclosure, and § 1214's disclosure requirements are discretionary when applied to § 1216 investigations. That combination means AFL has no statutory entitlement to information, and thus no concrete informational injury.
