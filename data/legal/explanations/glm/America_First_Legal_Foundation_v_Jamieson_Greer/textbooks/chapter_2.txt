Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 2: # Factual Background and Procedural History

## AFL's FOIA Request to DOJ and the Alleged Email Policy

This case arises from a dispute over access to federal agency records, but it reaches the court of appeals through an unusual procedural path—one that does not involve a direct challenge to the withholding of records, but rather a challenge to an agency's refusal to investigate that withholding. Understanding the posture requires tracing the parallel tracks of litigation that the plaintiff, America First Legal Foundation ("AFL"), pursued simultaneously.

As part of its effort to investigate judicial recusals, AFL submitted a Freedom of Information Act ("FOIA") request to the Department of Justice ("DOJ"). AFL sought certain emails between an outside attorney and DOJ's Civil Division. FOIA, codified at 5 U.S.C. § 552, generally requires federal agencies to disclose agency records upon request, subject to enumerated exemptions. The request at issue here was apparently straightforward in scope, but DOJ's response was not.

According to AFL, DOJ's Civil Division denied the request on a dubious ground: it does not search employee emails for responsive records without the employee's consent. This alleged policy—declining to search employee emails absent consent—became the focal point of AFL's subsequent legal challenges. AFL characterized this practice as an arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under FOIA, a characterization that would later carry legal significance under the Civil Service Reform Act.

## AFL's Parallel FOIA Lawsuit Against DOJ

AFL pursued two separate but related legal strategies in response to DOJ's alleged policy. First, AFL sued DOJ directly under FOIA, seeking to compel production of the requested emails. This was a conventional FOIA enforcement action, the type of suit expressly authorized by 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B), which permits district courts to order the production of agency records improperly withheld.

In that FOIA lawsuit against DOJ, DOJ produced—and was continuing to produce—records responsive to AFL's request. The opinion notes that the sufficiency of this production is not at issue in the present case. This detail is significant because it means the case before the D.C. Circuit is not a FOIA production dispute at all. The direct FOIA action against DOJ was proceeding on its own track, with documents being produced. The present case instead concerns AFL's second, parallel strategy: seeking to compel a different federal agency—the Office of Special Counsel—to investigate DOJ's alleged FOIA policy.

## AFL's Request to OSC Under Section 1216(a)(3)

AFL's second strategy involved the Office of Special Counsel ("OSC"), an investigative and prosecutorial agency established to seek "a fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service." The Civil Service Reform Act empowers OSC to investigate "prohibited personnel practices" and, when warranted, to bring enforcement proceedings before the Merit Systems Protection Board ("MSPB"). Under 5 U.S.C. § 1214, OSC may seek corrective action for prohibited personnel practices, and under 5 U.S.C. § 1215, it may seek disciplinary action. Section 1214 also includes provisions for notifying the complaining party about the progress of an investigation.

Section 1216 extends OSC's authority to investigate and seek corrective action for certain other forms of misconduct by government employees. Critically for this case, section 1216(a)(3) provides that OSC "shall … conduct an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under section 552"—that is, withholdings prohibited by FOIA. For such alleged FOIA violations, section 1216(c) provides that OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved."

Relying on this statutory scheme, AFL asked OSC to investigate DOJ's alleged FOIA policy of not searching employee emails without consent. AFL invoked section 1216(a)(3), emphasizing its mandatory language: OSC "shall" conduct an investigation of alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings. In AFL's view, this language required OSC to investigate DOJ's policy, regardless of any other statutory prerequisites.

## OSC's Refusal to Investigate and the Statutory Dispute

OSC refused to investigate AFL's allegation. It disclaimed authority to do so because the three predicates for triggering an investigation under a different statutory provision—5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—were not satisfied. That provision, which is part of FOIA itself rather than the Civil Service Reform Act, states that if a court (1) orders production of agency records improperly withheld under FOIA, (2) awards attorneys' fees against the agency, and (3) specifically questions whether the agency "acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding," then OSC "shall promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted against the officer or employee who was primarily responsible for the withholding."

The dispute between AFL and OSC thus turned on the relationship between two statutory provisions. Section 1216(a)(3) states that OSC "shall" investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings, using mandatory language with no apparent preconditions. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i), by contrast, requires OSC to initiate a proceeding only after a court has ordered production of records, awarded attorneys' fees, and specifically questioned whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously. OSC took the position that the three predicates in section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were prerequisites for any OSC investigation of FOIA withholdings, including under section 1216. AFL disagreed, arguing that section 1216 mandated an investigation even if the section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates were not satisfied.

## AFL's Lawsuit Against OSC and the District Court's Mixed Ruling

AFL sued OSC in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia. The district court issued a mixed ruling. On the key statutory question, the court sided with AFL: it concluded that section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC's power to investigate alleged FOIA violations under section 1216. The court declared that OSC's decision not to investigate was therefore based on a legal error, because OSC had refused to investigate solely on the ground that the section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) predicates were not met.

However, the district court did not simply order OSC to investigate. Instead, it concluded that section 1216(c) committed the decision whether to investigate to OSC's discretion. Recall that section 1216(c) provides that OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." The district court read the word "may" in this provision as conferring discretion on OSC rather than imposing a mandatory duty. Having so concluded, the court declined to order OSC to investigate any FOIA policy. Instead, it remanded the case to OSC for further consideration of whether to exercise its discretion to investigate.

## The Remand, OSC's Second Refusal, and the District Court's Dismissal

On remand, OSC again declined to investigate. The opinion does not detail OSC's reasoning on remand, but the result was clear: OSC persisted in its refusal to investigate AFL's allegation. AFL then moved for entry of final judgment, and the district court dismissed its remaining claims. AFL appealed.

## The Appeal and the Standard of Review

The case arrived at the D.C. Circuit as a dismissal for failure to state a claim. The D.C. Circuit reviews such dismissals de novo, as the court notes, citing *North American Butterfly Association v. Wolf*, 977 F.3d 1244, 1249 (D.C. Cir. 2020). De novo review means the appellate court considers the legal questions anew, without deferring to the district court's conclusions.

However, before reaching the merits of the dismissal, the D.C. Circuit confronted a threshold jurisdictional question that neither party had initially addressed: whether AFL had Article III standing to pursue its claims. The court ordered supplemental briefing on this question. This is a critical feature of the procedural posture. Under *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998), federal courts must satisfy themselves of their subject-matter jurisdiction before reaching the merits of a case. Standing is a core component of Article III jurisdiction, and the obligation to verify standing exists regardless of whether the parties raise the issue.

The standing question would dominate the D.C. Circuit's analysis. As the court explains, Article III of the Constitution restricts the federal "judicial Power" to resolving certain "Cases" and "Controversies." Standing doctrine follows from this case-or-controversy requirement. To have standing, a plaintiff must have suffered an injury caused by the defendant and likely to be redressed by a favorable decision. At the motion-to-dismiss stage, a plaintiff must allege facts plausibly supporting each element of standing. The court's analysis of whether AFL could satisfy these requirements—particularly given that AFL was seeking to compel an investigation by one agency based on the conduct of another—would prove decisive.
