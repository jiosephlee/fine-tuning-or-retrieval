Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Does §1216 independently require OSC to investigate FOIA violations, or does §552(a)(4)(F)(i) limit OSC's investigative authority?
Question:
The district court held that §552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC's power to investigate FOIA violations under §1216, meaning OSC's refusal to investigate was based on a legal error. But the D.C. Circuit never reached this merits question because it dismissed on standing grounds. I'm confused about the relationship between these two provisions. §1216(a)(3) says OSC "shall conduct an investigation" of arbitrary FOIA withholdings, while §552(a)(4)(F)(i) requires OSC to initiate disciplinary proceedings only after a court has (1) ordered production of records, (2) awarded attorneys' fees, and (3) specifically questioned whether the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously. Do these provisions create independent obligations, or does the FOIA provision function as a limitation on the broader §1216 duty? Does it matter that one is in the Civil Service Reform Act and the other in FOIA itself?
Answer:
## Answer

The short answer is that the D.C. Circuit did not decide this question—it dismissed on standing grounds and expressly never reached the merits. But the district court's reasoning, as described in the opinion, and the statutory text itself both support reading the two provisions as creating **independent obligations** rather than treating § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) as a limitation on § 1216.

### What the District Court Held

The district court concluded that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) "does not limit OSC's power to investigate alleged FOIA violations under section 1216," and that OSC's refusal to investigate was therefore "based on a legal error." The D.C. Circuit summarizes this holding but vacates the judgment for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, so the district court's merits analysis is not affirmed (or reversed) by the appellate court.

### Why the Text Supports Independent Obligations

The two provisions operate at different levels of generality and are triggered by different events:

- **§ 1216(a)(3)** is a mandatory duty triggered by **"any allegation"** of arbitrary or capricious withholding under FOIA. It requires OSC to "conduct an investigation." Under § 1216(c), OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved."

- **§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i)** is also mandatory ("shall promptly initiate a proceeding"), but it is triggered only by **three specific judicial predicates**: (1) a court orders production of records, (2) the court awards attorneys' fees, and (3) the court specifically questions whether the agency "acted arbitrarily or capriciously." Its remedy is narrowly directed at "disciplinary action" against the responsible employee.

These provisions differ in three key respects:

1. **Trigger**: § 1216(a)(3) requires investigation of "any allegation"—no prior litigation is necessary. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) requires prior judicial action satisfying three cumulative conditions.

2. **Remedial scope**: § 1216(c) allows both corrective action (§ 1214) and disciplinary action (§ 1215). § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) addresses only disciplinary proceedings.

3. **Placement**: § 1216 is in the Civil Service Reform Act; § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is in FOIA itself. That they appear in different statutes does not resolve the question—Congress can create overlapping obligations across titles. But it does suggest Congress was addressing different concerns: § 1216 gives OSC a general watchdog function over FOIA compliance as a species of civil-service misconduct, while § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) provides a court-triggered enforcement mechanism specific to FOIA litigation outcomes.

### The Surplusage Problem

Reading § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) as a limitation on § 1216 would largely render § 1216(a)(3) redundant. If OSC could investigate FOIA withholdings only after the three judicial predicates in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) are satisfied, then § 1216(a)(3)'s mandatory "shall conduct an investigation" would do no independent work—§ 552(a)(4)(F)(i) already compels OSC to act in exactly those circumstances. The more natural reading is that § 1216(a)(3) creates a freestanding duty to investigate any allegation of arbitrary FOIA withholding, while § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) creates a **separate, additional** obligation that arises once a court has made specific findings in FOIA litigation.

### An Important Caveat: Discretion Under § 1216(c)

Even if § 1216 independently requires OSC to investigate, the opinion notes that this does not necessarily mean the requester gets everything it wants. The district court held that § 1216(c) "committed that decision to OSC's discretion"—at least as to investigating a general FOIA *policy* rather than a specific withholding. The D.C. Circuit also notes (in its standing analysis) that AFL "acknowledges cases holding that OSC's 'decision whether to follow section 1214's procedural requirements' in a section 1216 investigation is 'discretionary,'" citing *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*, 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011).

So the independence of the two provisions matters for **whether OSC must investigate**, but § 1216(c)'s "may investigate and seek corrective action ... in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved" language gives OSC latitude in **how** it conducts that investigation—including whether it affords the requesting party the notice and information rights available under § 1214.

### Summary

Based on the text and structure described in the opinion, the better reading is that § 1216 and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) create **independent obligations**: § 1216(a)(3) imposes a general duty to investigate any allegation of arbitrary FOIA withholding, while § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) imposes a narrower, litigation-triggered duty to initiate disciplinary proceedings. The D.C. Circuit did not reach this question, but the district court's reasoning—and the canon against reading one provision to render another surplusage—points in this direction.

### What is a 'procedural right in vacuo' and how does it interact with relaxed standing for procedural injuries?
Question:
The court says the deprivation of a procedural right "in vacuo" is insufficient for standing (citing Summers v. Earth Island Institute). But it also discusses "relaxed standing requirements" for procedural injuries (citing Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep't of Educ.), under which imminence and redressability are relaxed but injury in fact and causation are not. I'm confused about how these concepts fit together. If AFL has a procedural right to an OSC investigation under §1216, why doesn't the violation of that right itself constitute an injury in fact? What is the "concrete interest" that must be affected by the procedural deprivation, and why wasn't AFL's interest in the FOIA records or in the investigation's fruits sufficient?
Answer:
## How "Procedural Right in Vacuo" and Relaxed Standing Fit Together

The two concepts work in tandem, not in tension. The "procedural right in vacuo" principle establishes the threshold requirement, and the relaxed standing doctrine adjusts the analytical framework once that threshold is met—but only in certain respects.

### The Core Problem: Procedural Rights Alone Don't Confer Standing

The opinion draws from *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* for the principle that "the deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo—is insufficient" for Article III standing. In other words, Congress can create procedural rights, but the bare violation of such a right, divorced from any concrete harm to a plaintiff's protected interest, is not itself an injury in fact. The plaintiff must identify a **concrete, particularized interest** that the procedural right was *designed to protect* and that was *actually harmed* by the procedural deprivation.

### What Relaxed Standing Does—and Doesn't Do

The relaxed standing doctrine from *Ctr. for Law & Educ.* applies *only after* the plaintiff has identified a concrete interest protected by the procedural right. Once that link is established, courts relax the **imminence** and **redressability** prongs—but explicitly **not** injury in fact or causation. The plaintiff still must show that the violation of the procedural right "resulted in injury" to the concrete, particularized interest.

So the framework operates in two steps:

1. **Identify the concrete interest** the procedural right protects, and show the deprivation harmed that interest (injury in fact + causation—*not* relaxed).
2. **Only then** do the relaxed imminence and redressability standards apply.

### Why AFL's Procedural Right to an Investigation Wasn't Enough

AFL had a plausible procedural right to an OSC investigation under §1216. But that right, standing alone, was a procedural right in vacuo unless AFL could tie it to a concrete interest harmed by the deprivation. AFL tried to supply that concrete interest through two theories, and both failed:

**First theory: the FOIA records themselves.** AFL argued that OSC's failure to investigate prevented it from receiving the records it requested from DOJ. This failed on **causation** (which is not relaxed). The informational injury—DOJ's refusal to produce records—was traceable to DOJ, not OSC. The causal chain from OSC's failure to investigate to eventual production of records was too speculative: it required speculation about whether OSC would pursue corrective or disciplinary action, whether the MSPB would sanction employees, and whether DOJ would then produce records. The court emphasized that causation cannot rest on "speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts."

**Second theory: the fruits of the investigation.** AFL argued it had an informational interest in the notices and information that an OSC investigation would produce. This failed because §1216, even if it mandates an investigation, creates **no statutory entitlement to receive information**. AFL acknowledged that §1214's notice requirements are discretionary when applied to §1216 investigations. Without an entitlement to information, there is no concrete informational injury—and thus no concrete interest for the procedural right to protect.

### The Bottom Line

The "concrete interest" requirement is the bridge between a procedural right and Article III standing. A procedural right in vacuo is a procedural right with no concrete interest attached; it confers no standing. Relaxed standing doesn't eliminate the need for that concrete interest—it merely relaxes the downstream imminence and redressability requirements once the concrete interest is properly identified and causally linked to the procedural violation. AFL's problem was not that it lacked a procedural right, but that it could not identify a concrete interest harmed by the deprivation of that right that satisfied the unrelaxed requirements of injury in fact and causation.

### How attenuated is too attenuated for traceability when an injury requires a chain of actions by multiple non-party agencies?
Question:
The court rejects AFL's first standing theory because the causal chain from OSC's refusal to investigate to AFL not receiving FOIA records is too speculative. The chain requires: (1) OSC investigates, (2) OSC decides to pursue corrective or disciplinary action, (3) MSPB decides to sanction DOJ employees, and (4) DOJ decides to produce records. MSPB and DOJ are not parties to the lawsuit. The court cites FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine for the rule that causation can't rely on "unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." But many standing cases involve indirect causation through third parties. Where is the line between acceptable indirect causation and impermissibly speculative chains? Does the number of links matter, or is it about the nature of each link—e.g., whether each step is mandatory vs. discretionary?
Answer:
## The line turns on whether each link requires discretionary choices, especially by non-parties

The court's reasoning in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer* clarifies that the problem is not simply the *number* of links in the causal chain, but the *nature* of each link—specifically, whether each step requires an independent actor to make a discretionary choice. You're on the right track with the mandatory/discretionary distinction.

### Mandatory links can support standing; discretionary links by non-parties cannot

The court identifies two distinct problems with AFL's causal chain, and both stem from discretion:

1. **OSC's enforcement discretion.** Even if the court ordered OSC to investigate, OSC would still have to *decide* whether to pursue corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215. The court cites *Heckler v. Chaney* for the principle that courts generally may not compel agencies to exercise enforcement discretion. So the very next step after the ordered investigation is itself discretionary and committed to the defendant's own judgment.

2. **Independent choices by non-party actors.** Beyond OSC's discretionary choice, the chain requires MSPB to *decide* whether to sanction DOJ employees, and then DOJ to *decide* whether to produce records. Both MSPB and DOJ are not parties to the lawsuit. The court quotes *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* for the rule that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts."

### Why non-party status matters independently of discretion

The court adds a separate redressability point: a "judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might persuade actors who are not before the court" (quoting *Haaland v. Brackeen*). This means even if MSPB and DOJ's choices were somehow predictable, a judgment against OSC would not bind them. The chain depends on the rationale of the court's decision *persuading* non-parties to act, which is not how redressability works.

So the problem is compounded: each link requires (a) a discretionary choice, (b) by an actor not bound by the judgment, (c) with no legal mandate compelling the outcome.

### Contrast with cases involving indirect causation that supports standing

The court doesn't say all indirect causation is impermissible. It acknowledges the relaxed standing framework for procedural injuries, under which "courts relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability." The key is that even relaxed causation and redressability require the plaintiff to show that the procedural violation "resulted in injury" to a "concrete, particularized interest."

Indirect causation can support standing when the intermediate steps are legally mandated rather than discretionary. For example, if a statute required an agency to take action upon receiving a court order, and that action would directly produce the plaintiff's desired information, the causal chain would run through a mandatory link. Here, by contrast, every link after the investigation is discretionary—OSC's decision to pursue enforcement, MSPB's decision to sanction, and DOJ's decision to produce records. No statute compels any of these outcomes.

### The bottom line

The opinion supports this framework: the line between acceptable and impermissible indirect causation turns on whether the causal chain depends on **unfettered discretionary choices by actors not before the court**. A chain with multiple links can support standing if each link is legally mandated. But when the chain requires speculation about how multiple independent actors—especially non-parties—might choose to exercise their discretion, standing fails. It's the combination of discretion and non-party status at *each* link that makes the chain "too speculative and attenuated to support Article III standing."

### What makes information 'entitled' under a statute for purposes of informational injury standing?
Question:
The court rejects AFL's second standing theory because, even if §1216 requires OSC to investigate, it "creates no entitlement to receive information." AFL argued that if OSC investigated under §1216, it might provide the notices and information required under §1214. But the court notes that §1214's notice requirements are discretionary when applied to §1216 investigations (citing Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel). I'm confused about what distinguishes a statutory "entitlement" to information from mere potential access. In FEC v. Akins and Pub. Citizen v. DOJ, plaintiffs had statutory rights to receive information. What specific statutory language or structure creates an "entitlement"? Is it purely about mandatory vs. discretionary disclosure, or is there more to it?
Answer:
## What Creates a Statutory "Entitlement" to Information

The short answer is: **the statute must require that the information be disclosed to the plaintiff.** A mandatory duty to take some other action—even investigation—does not, by itself, create an entitlement to information produced by that action.

### The Standard

The opinion frames the requirement clearly: a plaintiff suffers a concrete informational injury only if denied information that "a statute entitled him to receive." The entitlement must come from the statute itself—not from what an agency *might* choose to disclose in its discretion. This is the thread connecting the cases AFL cites. In *FEC v. Akins*, the Federal Election Campaign Act required the FEC to make certain campaign finance reports *available to the public*. In *Public Citizen v. DOJ*, the Federal Advisory Committee Act required that committee meetings be *open to the public* and records *available for public inspection*. In both cases, the statutes contained mandatory disclosure provisions directed at providing information to persons like the plaintiffs.

### Why AFL Had No Entitlement

This case illustrates the distinction the question is getting at. AFL pointed to two statutory provisions:

- **§ 1216(a)(3)** requires OSC to "conduct an investigation" of alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings. This is a mandatory duty to *act*—but it says nothing about providing information to the complainant.
- **§ 1214** contains notice requirements that would obligate OSC to keep a complainant informed during an investigation—but those requirements apply by their terms to investigations of "prohibited personnel practices."

The critical link between the two is **§ 1216(c)**, which provides that for FOIA violations, OSC *"may"* investigate and seek corrective action under § 1214 "in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." The court treats this as discretionary: OSC *may* borrow § 1214's procedures, including its notice requirements, but is not *required* to. AFL itself acknowledged as much, citing *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*, which held that OSC's decision whether to follow § 1214's procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is "discretionary."

So the chain breaks: even if OSC must investigate under § 1216, it has discretion whether to provide § 1214 notices and information. Because disclosure to AFL is discretionary rather than mandatory, AFL has no statutory entitlement to receive anything. And without an entitlement, there is no concrete informational injury.

### Mandatory Investigation vs. Mandatory Disclosure

The distinction you're sensing is real, and it runs deeper than just "mandatory vs. discretionary disclosure" in a narrow sense. The court is drawing a line between:

1. **A statutory mandate to provide information to the plaintiff** (which creates an entitlement and thus an informational injury if denied), and
2. **A statutory mandate to take some action that *might* result in information being disclosed** (which does not).

In *Akins* and *Public Citizen*, the statutes fell into category (1)—they directly required that information be made available to the plaintiff. Here, § 1216 falls into category (2)—it requires investigation, but any disclosure of information from that investigation depends on OSC's discretionary choice to apply § 1214 procedures.

This is also why the court's redressability analysis reinforces the standing conclusion. Even if the court ordered OSC to conduct the § 1216 investigation, OSC could choose not to apply § 1214's notice requirements. A favorable judgment would not be "reasonably likely" to produce the information AFL seeks, because disclosure remains within OSC's discretion.

### Summary

The "entitlement" is not purely about whether disclosure is mandatory vs. discretionary—though that is the most direct way to think about it. More precisely, it is about whether **the statute itself obligates the government to provide the information to the plaintiff**, as opposed to merely requiring some government action from which information *might* flow if the agency chooses to disclose it. Here, § 1216 mandates investigation but not disclosure, and § 1214's disclosure requirements are discretionary when applied to § 1216 investigations. That combination means AFL has no statutory entitlement to information, and thus no concrete informational injury.

### Why does redressability fail when ordering the defendant to do what the statute says it 'shall' do?
Question:
The court says a favorable judgment wouldn't likely redress AFL's injuries because redressability "turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might persuade actors who are not before the court" (citing Haaland v. Brackeen). But if the court orders OSC to conduct the investigation that §1216 says it "shall" conduct, isn't OSC bound by that order? Even if MSPB and DOJ aren't parties, the OSC investigation could lead to corrective action against DOJ employees. Why is the connection between an OSC investigation and eventual FOIA disclosure too remote for redressability? Is the court saying that even mandatory agency actions can't redress injuries if the ultimate relief depends on subsequent decisions by non-party actors?
Answer:
## The investigation is only the first link in a speculative causal chain

Your instinct that OSC would be bound by a court order is correct — but binding OSC to investigate is not the same as redressing AFL's injury. The court's reasoning turns on the difference between what a judgment directly compels a party to do and what *might* flow from that compelled action through a series of discretionary decisions by non-parties.

### What redressability requires

Redressability asks whether a favorable decision is **likely** to remedy the plaintiff's injury — not merely whether it might set in motion a process that could eventually lead to relief. The court invokes *Haaland v. Brackeen* for the principle that redressability "turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale might persuade actors who are not before the court." This is the analytical frame for the entire standing analysis.

### Why ordering the investigation doesn't redress the FOIA injury

AFL's injury is the denial of records it requested from DOJ. Even if the court ordered OSC to conduct the §1216 investigation, producing those records would require a multi-step chain of events, each step of which depends on discretionary decisions by actors not before the court:

1. **OSC's enforcement discretion.** Even after investigating, OSC must decide whether to seek corrective action under §1214 or disciplinary action under §1215. Section 1216(c) says OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action" — it does not require OSC to pursue enforcement. The court cites *Heckler v. Chaney* for the principle that agency enforcement decisions are generally committed to agency discretion and not subject to judicial compulsion.

2. **MSPB's adjudicatory decision.** Even if OSC brings a case, the Merit Systems Protection Board — not a party to this lawsuit — must decide whether to impose sanctions. That decision is not bound by any judgment against OSC.

3. **DOJ's compliance decision.** Even if MSPB sanctions individual DOJ employees, DOJ itself — also not a party — must decide whether to produce the requested records. Nothing in a judgment against OSC would compel DOJ to do anything.

The court draws on *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* for the rule that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." The same logic applies to redressability: a judgment that binds only OSC cannot guarantee relief that depends on subsequent choices by MSPB and DOJ.

### The key distinction

The court is **not** saying that mandatory agency actions can never redress injuries. It is saying that when the plaintiff's injury can only be remedied through a chain of speculative decisions by non-parties, a judgment binding only one party in that chain does not redress the injury. Compare a scenario where ordering OSC to investigate would itself produce the information AFL wants — that would be redressable because the judgment directly produces the relief. Here, the investigation is just the first step, and the ultimate relief (FOIA disclosure by DOJ) depends on two subsequent decisions by non-parties plus an exercise of OSC's own enforcement discretion.

### The second theory fails for a similar reason

AFL's alternative theory — that it has an informational interest in the investigation itself — also fails because §1216 creates no entitlement to receive information from OSC. AFL acknowledges that OSC's decision whether to follow §1214's notice requirements in a §1216 investigation is discretionary (*Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel*). So even a court order compelling the investigation would not be likely to produce the notices and information AFL claims it wants, because OSC retains discretion over whether to provide them.

### Bottom line

Redressability fails not because the court order would lack binding force, but because the injury AFL asserts (denial of FOIA records) is several discretionary steps removed from the only action a court could order (an OSC investigation). The judgment would bind OSC, but the relief AFL seeks depends on decisions by MSPB and DOJ — actors who are not before the court and whose choices cannot be compelled or predicted with the likelihood Article III requires.

### How does Heckler v. Chaney's presumption against judicial review of agency enforcement apply when a statute uses mandatory 'shall' language?
Question:
The court cites Heckler v. Chaney for the proposition that courts generally may not compel agencies to exercise enforcement discretion, and uses this to support its causation analysis. But §1216 says OSC "shall conduct an investigation" of alleged FOIA violations. Doesn't mandatory statutory language override the Heckler presumption? Under the APA, agency action "committed to agency discretion by law" is unreviewable, but doesn't mandatory "shall" language create a clear standard for review that takes the matter out of the Heckler presumption? The court seems to treat OSC's decision about whether to pursue corrective or disciplinary action after investigating as discretionary, but what about the initial investigation itself—is that also discretionary despite the "shall" language?
Answer:
## The Opinion Doesn't Resolve the "Shall" Question — It Dismisses on Standing

The short answer is that the D.C. Circuit does not decide whether § 1216's mandatory "shall" language overrides the *Heckler* presumption, because it never reaches the merits. The court dismisses for lack of Article III standing and uses *Heckler* not in an APA reviewability analysis under 5 U.S.C. § 701(a)(2), but rather within its causation and redressability analysis.

## How *Heckler* Functions in the Opinion

The court invokes *Heckler v. Chaney* for a narrow proposition: that "it requires speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel." The citation appears in the standing analysis, where the court is evaluating whether a favorable judgment would redress AFL's informational injury — the first standing theory, based on AFL's interest in receiving the FOIA records from DOJ.

The court's causal chain reasoning is the key. Even if OSC were ordered to investigate, redressability would require speculation that: (1) OSC *might* pursue corrective action under § 1214 or disciplinary action under § 1215; (2) the MSPB *might* sanction DOJ employees; and (3) DOJ *might* then produce the records. *Heckler* supports the point that step (1) — OSC's decision whether to pursue enforcement after investigating — is discretionary, making the chain too attenuated under *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* and *Haaland v. Brackeen*.

## The Investigation vs. Post-Investigation Distinction

The opinion does implicitly acknowledge the distinction you identify between the initial investigation and subsequent enforcement decisions, but it does so without resolving it.

The district court below had held that § 1216 *does* mandate the investigation and that OSC's refusal was based on legal error — but that § 1216(c) committed the *post-investigation decision* to OSC's discretion. The D.C. Circuit does not endorse or reject this reasoning. It says only that "even if section 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it creates no entitlement to receive information." The court thus assumes arguendo that the investigation could be mandatory and still finds standing lacking.

On the post-investigation side, the court cites *Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel* (6th Cir. 2011) for the proposition that OSC's "decision whether to follow section 1214's procedural requirements" in a § 1216 investigation is "discretionary." AFL itself acknowledged this. So the opinion treats the procedures and enforcement decisions *following* an investigation as discretionary, but it does not hold that the initial investigation is discretionary.

## Why the APA § 701(a)(2) Question Remains Open

Your question about whether mandatory "shall" language creates a clear standard that takes the matter out of the § 701(a)(2) exception ("committed to agency discretion by law") is a substantive one — but it is not addressed here. The court's standing disposition means the APA reviewability question is never reached. The *Heckler* presumption and the § 701(a)(2) exception are related but distinct doctrines: *Heckler* establishes a presumption against judicial review of agency *non-enforcement* decisions, while § 701(a)(2) precludes review of action "committed to agency discretion by law." The "shall" language argument — that mandatory statutory language provides an intelligible standard for review and thus overcomes the presumption — would need to be addressed on the merits, which this opinion does not do.

## Bottom Line

The opinion uses *Heckler* to support a causal-chain argument in the standing context, not to hold that the § 1216 investigation itself is unreviewable. The court expressly leaves open whether the "shall" language makes the initial investigation mandatory and reviewable, deciding only that even if it does, AFL lacks standing because the downstream consequences depend on discretionary choices by OSC, the MSPB, and DOJ — actors and choices too speculative to support redressability.

### Why did the court vacate the district court's judgment rather than simply reversing it?
Question:
The D.C. Circuit "vacate[s] the district court's judgment and remand[s] with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction." The district court reached the merits and dismissed AFL's claims for failure to state a claim under Rule 12(b)(6). The D.C. Circuit found a jurisdictional defect (lack of Article III standing). Why vacatur rather than just reversal? Is this because the district court never should have reached the merits without first establishing jurisdiction, per Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment? And what's the practical difference for AFL between a dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction under Rule 12(b)(1) and a dismissal for failure to state a claim under Rule 12(b)(6)—can AFL refile either way, or is the jurisdictional dismissal more final?
Answer:
The D.C. Circuit vacated rather than reversed because the district court's judgment was entered without subject-matter jurisdiction, rendering it void. Jurisdiction is the threshold question that must be resolved before a federal court may reach the merits. The opinion invokes this principle directly: "Before reaching the merits, we must consider whether AFL satisfied the requirements for Article III standing, a question bearing on whether the district court had subject-matter jurisdiction," citing *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998).

Your instinct about *Steel Co.* is correct. Under that decision, a court lacks authority to address the merits of a case in which the plaintiff lacks Article III standing. The district court here skipped the jurisdictional inquiry and dismissed AFL's claims under Rule 12(b)(6) for failure to state a claim. Because the court never had jurisdiction, its merits disposition was ultra vires. The proper appellate remedy is therefore to vacate the judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for the actual defect—lack of subject-matter jurisdiction under Rule 12(b)(1).

The choice of "vacate" over "reverse" tracks this logic. To reverse signals disagreement with the district court's merits reasoning and substitution of a different merits outcome. Here, the D.C. Circuit is not disagreeing with the district court on the merits so much as holding that the district court never had power to decide the merits at all. Vacatur wipes the void judgment from the books and leaves it to the district court to enter the only judgment it was ever authorized to enter—one dismissing for lack of jurisdiction.

As to the practical difference for AFL:

A dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction under Rule 12(b)(1) is not an adjudication "on the merits" and does not carry claim-preclusive effect. Rule 41(b) expressly exempts dismissals for lack of jurisdiction from operating as adjudications on the merits. A dismissal for failure to state a claim under Rule 12(b)(6), by contrast, generally operates as an adjudication on the merits unless the court specifies otherwise—and the district court here did not. So in the abstract, the 12(b)(1) dismissal leaves AFL free to refile, whereas the 12(b)(6) dismissal would have precluded refiling under res judicata principles.

"Free to refile," however, is largely theoretical here. The same jurisdictional defect that defeated standing will doom any refiled suit raising the same allegations. The opinion explains at length why AFL cannot establish the injury, causation, and redressability elements: its claimed informational injuries are traceable to DOJ (a non-party) rather than OSC, the causal chain to any relief requires speculation about choices by MSPB and DOJ, and section 1216 creates no entitlement to receive information from OSC. Unless AFL can allege new facts curing these defects, a refiled complaint would simply produce another 12(b)(1) dismissal. So while the jurisdictional dismissal is not formally preclusive, the practical barrier to relitigation is the standing analysis itself, not claim preclusion.
