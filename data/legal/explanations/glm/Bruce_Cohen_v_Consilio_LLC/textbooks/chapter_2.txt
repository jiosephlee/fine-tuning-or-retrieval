Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 2: # The MPWA Claim: Statutory Interpretation and the Commissioner's Exclusive Role in Seeking Penalties

## The Statutory Framework: § 181.171 as the Enforcement Mechanism

Cohen's claim under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) required the Eighth Circuit to engage in a focused exercise in statutory interpretation. The MPWA is a collection of Minnesota statutes governing the payment of wages, and Cohen's claim centered on two specific provisions: § 181.171 and § 181.101. Section 181.171 serves as the enforcement mechanism for certain provisions of the MPWA. It provides that "[a] person may bring a civil action seeking redress for violations" of specified MPWA provisions, including § 181.101. When an employer "is found to have violated" such a provision, it is "liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief." Critically for this case, § 181.171 also states that an employer who is found to have violated one of the enumerated sections "is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated." The question before the court was whether § 181.101, the specific provision Cohen alleged Consilio violated, actually "provides for" civil penalties that an aggrieved employee may recover — or whether the authority to seek penalties belongs exclusively to the Commissioner of the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry.

## Cohen's Argument: Plain Language of § 181.101

Cohen argued that the "plain language and unambiguous meaning" of § 181.101 authorized employees to seek what he called "average daily wage penalties." Under this theory, § 181.171's reference to "civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated" would incorporate whatever penalties § 181.101 makes available, and those penalties would be recoverable by the aggrieved employee who brings the civil action. This was an attractive reading for Cohen because it would mean he, as a private plaintiff, could pursue penalties directly against Consilio — penalties he valued at $172,080. The court, however, concluded that a careful reading of § 181.101 did not support this interpretation.

## The Court's Plain Language Analysis of § 181.101(a)

The court began its analysis with the text of § 181.101(a), applying the well-established Minnesota principle that "[w]hen the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect." The court observed that § 181.101(a) references penalties only in connection with the Commissioner — not in connection with employees. This was the decisive textual feature of the statute.

The court identified several structural elements of § 181.101(a) that tied penalties exclusively to the Commissioner's authority. First, penalties become available only if the Commissioner first "serve[s] a demand for payment [of wages] on behalf of an employee." This is a procedural prerequisite that only the Commissioner can initiate. Second, only after that demand has been served and payment is not made within ten days may "the commissioner" — and the statute uses that specific term — "charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty." The statute thus places the power to assess and collect penalties squarely with the Commissioner. Third, the statute directs that any "[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned." The court emphasized this language: it is the Commissioner who collects, and the collected funds then flow to the employee. The employee does not independently seek or recover penalties under this provision.

## The Employee's Reserved Right: Prosecuting a Claim for Wages, Not Penalties

The court acknowledged that § 181.101(a) does preserve certain rights for employees. The statute provides that "[t]his section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages" and that the section "provides a substantive right for employees to the payment of wages . . . as well as commissions, in addition to the right to be paid at certain times." But the court read these provisions as reserving to employees the right to pursue wages themselves — not penalties. The statute's references to penalties, by contrast, are consistently and exclusively tied to the Commissioner's actions. The employee's role under § 181.101(a) is to benefit from penalties the Commissioner collects, but the employee has no statutory authority to seek those penalties directly.

## The Court's Comparison to Other Minnesota Statutes

To reinforce its reading of § 181.101, the court turned to a comparison with other Minnesota wage statutes — a common and powerful tool in statutory interpretation. The court examined § 181.13(a), which governs discharged employees, and § 181.14, subdivision 2, which governs employees who quit or resign. In both of those statutes, the Minnesota legislature expressly empowered the aggrieved employee to collect penalties. Section 181.13(a), for example, provides that upon a discharged employee's demand, the employee may recover unpaid wages and "may charge and collect a penalty." Section 181.14, subdivision 2, contains similar language for employees who resign. In these provisions, the legislature used explicit language granting the employee the authority to seek penalties directly.

The contrast with § 181.101 was stark. Where §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14 expressly authorize employees to "charge and collect a penalty," § 181.101(a) contains no such grant. Instead, § 181.101(a) consistently assigns the penalty function to the Commissioner. The court drew the inference that this was a deliberate legislative choice.

## The "Knows How" Principle of Legislative Inference

The court's comparison to §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14 rested on a recognized principle of statutory interpretation sometimes called the "knows how" doctrine. As the Minnesota Supreme Court explained in *Lagasse v. Horton*, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022), "the Legislature's action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature 'knows how' to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so." The logic is straightforward: if the legislature has demonstrated in other statutes that it knows how to grant employees the power to seek penalties directly, its failure to do so in a particular statute is presumed to be intentional. The legislature could have written § 181.101(a) to give employees the right to "charge and collect a penalty," just as it did in §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14. It did not. The court therefore concluded that the legislature chose not to empower employees with that authority under § 181.101.

## The Court's Holding and Affirmance of Summary Judgment

Based on this analysis, the court held that only the Commissioner may seek daily average wage penalties under § 181.101. Because Cohen, as a private employee, lacked statutory authority to pursue these penalties, his MPWA claim for penalties could not succeed as a matter of law. The district court's grant of summary judgment to Consilio on this claim was therefore proper. The court's reasoning illustrates a methodical approach to statutory interpretation: it began with the plain language of the statute at issue, examined how that language assigned roles and responsibilities, compared the statute to related provisions where the legislature had acted differently, and drew a reasoned inference about legislative intent. The result was a conclusion that the MPWA's penalty provision under § 181.101 is enforced through the Commissioner, not through private civil actions by aggrieved employees.
