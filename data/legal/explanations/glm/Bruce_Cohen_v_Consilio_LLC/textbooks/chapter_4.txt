Chapter 4: # The MWTA Claim and Injunctive Relief: Procedural Default and the Failure to Properly Raise Claims

## Cohen's MWTA Claim: Statutory Notice Requirements and the Consequence of Inadequate Briefing

Cohen's remaining claims under the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) and for injunctive relief met a common fate: each failed not on the substantive merits of the underlying legal theory, but because of procedural deficiencies in how Cohen presented and preserved those claims. The court's treatment of these claims illustrates fundamental principles of appellate advocacy and procedural default that every law student should understand.

### The MWTA Claim and Statutory Notice

Cohen's MWTA claim was based on an allegation that Consilio failed to provide statutorily required notice of a change in its overtime policy. The relevant statutory provisions, Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.032(f) and (d)(4), outline employer obligations regarding wage statements and notice of changes to compensation policies. Cohen alleged that Consilio's implementation of the new overtime policy in August 2019—without proper notice—violated these provisions.

### The District Court's Limited Discussion

On appeal, Cohen complained that the district court "included little to no discussion" of his MWTA claim. This objection is notable for what it reveals about appellate practice. A party's complaint that a lower court failed to address a claim does not, without more, establish reversible error. The appellate court's review focuses on whether the district court reached the correct result, not whether it wrote extensively about every argument presented to it. Here, the Eighth Circuit did not dwell on the district court's brevity, instead examining whether the record supported dismissal on the basis that was presented.

### The Stipulation's Effect on the MWTA Claim

A critical fact complicated Cohen's MWTA claim. The parties had stipulated that Consilio had compensated Cohen for all overtime wages and liquidated damages owed under his MFLSA claim. This stipulation that Cohen "received all pay Consilio owes him" narrowed the scope of what remained in dispute. With his wage claims satisfied, Cohen sought penalties under the MWTA—but failed to identify any statutory authority authorizing such penalties. This omission proved fatal to his claim.

### The Absence of Statutory Authority for Civil Penalties

The most significant deficiency in Cohen's MWTA claim was his failure to point to any statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under the MWTA provisions he cited. Unlike his MFLSA claim (where statutory penalties were at least theoretically available, though payable to the Commissioner), Cohen identified no provision in the MWTA that would entitle him to the penalties he sought. The court noted this absence without extended discussion, treating it as a straightforward deficiency. This portion of the opinion underscores a foundational principle of statutory interpretation: a claimant must ground their request for relief in the text of the statute they allege was violated. Without identifying a statutory basis for penalties, Cohen's MWTA claim could not survive summary judgment.

### The Waiver Principle: Milligan v. City of Red Oak

The Eighth Circuit's disposition of the MWTA claim relies on a core principle of appellate practice: arguments not adequately briefed are waived. Quoting Milligan v. City of Red Oak, 230 F.3d 355, 340 (8th Cir. 2000), the court reiterated the rule that "[I]nasmuch as [defendant's] brief does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it." The court applied this rule to Cohen's MWTA claim, finding "no meaningful argument in support of reversal."

The waiver principle serves several important functions. First, it preserves judicial economy by ensuring courts do not expend resources addressing arguments that parties have not seen fit to develop. Second, it respects the adversarial system by holding parties accountable for the arguments they choose to make—and for those they choose not to make. Third, it provides fairness to opposing parties, who should not be surprised by theories advanced for the of an appellate decision. A party cannot simply assert a claim and expect the court to construct the legal support for it; the party must affirmatively demonstrate why the law entitles them to relief.

## The Injunctive Relief Claim: New Theories and the Requirement of Amended Pleadings

### Cohen's Shifting Request for Injunctive Relief

Cohen's claim for injunctive relief failed for a different but equally instructive procedural reason. In his complaint, Cohen sought specific forms of injunctive relief. However, by the time of summary judgment briefing, Cohen had changed course and requested different relief: an affirmative injunctive order requiring Consilio to rescind its 2019 overtime policy. This shift created a procedural mismatch between the pleadings and the briefing. The district court—understandably, in the Eighth Circuit's view—did not consider the new form of injunctive relief because it had not been properly raised.

### The Procedural Rule: Amending Pleadings vs. Raising New Claims in Briefing

The Eighth Circuit's resolution of the injunctive relief claim rests on a fundamental distinction between pleadings and briefs. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a) governs the amendment of pleadings and provides specific procedures by which a party may amend its complaint. A party may amend once as a matter of course within a specified period, or may seek the court's leave or consent of the adverse party to amend. The rule embodies the principle that the complaint defines the scope of the litigation and that opponents and the court are entitled to know what claims are being asserted.

Cohen attempted to do through summary judgment briefing what should have been done through an amended complaint. Citing InfoDeli, LLC v. Tech. Connections, Inc., 136 F.4th 797, 801 n.6 (8th Cir. 2025), the court explained that "[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs." This principle ensures that when a party seeks to assert a new claim—or, as here, to request substantially different relief than originally pleaded—the opposing party has proper notice and the procedural protections of Rule 15 apply. Opposition briefs are for arguing existing claims; they are not vehicles for introducing new ones.

### The Issue Is Not Properly Before the Court

Because Cohen had not amended his complaint to request the rescission of the 2019 policy, the court concluded that the issue was "not properly before us." This phrase signals that the court is declining to address the merits of an argument because a procedural prerequisite has not been satisfied. The court's conclusion reinforces a critical lesson for law students: courts are generally constrained by the claims and arguments as framed by the parties. A district court is not obliged to search through briefing to identify and evaluate relief that was not requested in the pleadings. When a party shifts its requested relief mid-litigation without following the proper procedural mechanisms, the new request is not properly preserved for appeal.

## Conclusion

The Eighth Circuit's treatment of Cohen's MWTA and injunctive relief claims demonstrates the critical importance of procedural discipline in litigation. Cohen's MWTA claim failed because he did not provide the legal support necessary to sustain it—specifically, statutory authority for the penalties he sought. His injunctive relief claim failed because he sought different relief in briefing than he had in his complaint without amending his pleadings. Both outcomes illustrate that substantive entitlement to relief is necessary but not sufficient for success in litigation. A litigant must also follow the procedural rules that govern how claims are raised, supported, and preserved. The court's disposition of these claims—affirming dismissal of the MWTA claim and declining to consider the injunctive relief issue—reflects the principle that procedural default can be outcome-determinative, regardless of the underlying merits of a claim.
