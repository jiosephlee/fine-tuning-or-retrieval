Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural Posture

## The Parties and the Employment Relationship

This case arises from an employment dispute between Bruce Cohen and his employer, Consilio, LLC. Consilio is a nationwide legal document review company with offices in Minnesota. Cohen began working for Consilio in 2018 as an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer. The term "hourly" is significant: it means Cohen was not exempt from overtime protections and was entitled to be compensated for each hour worked, including any overtime hours, under applicable wage laws. As a "licensed attorney document reviewer," Cohen performed document review work—a common practice in legal proceedings where attorneys examine large volumes of documents for relevance, privilege, and other issues. The nature of Cohen's work and his hourly compensation structure form the backdrop for the wage law claims at the center of this case.

## Consilio's July 2019 Overtime Policy Change

In July 2019, Consilio sent an email to Cohen and other Minnesota-based hourly licensed attorney document reviewers announcing a change to its overtime compensation practices. Effective August 2019, Consilio instituted what it described as a "new premium overtime policy." Despite the label "premium overtime," the policy actually eliminated overtime premium pay. Instead, Cohen and his fellow employees would be paid "at [their] base pay rate" for all hours worked, meaning they would receive the same hourly rate regardless of whether they worked standard or overtime hours. This policy change is the factual foundation of Cohen's claims. Under Minnesota wage law, the question of whether an employer may prospectively eliminate overtime premium pay for hourly employees—and what notice and remedies are required when doing so—became the central legal dispute.

## Cohen's August 2020 Lawsuit

In August 2020, Cohen sued Consilio on behalf of himself and a "putative class" of similarly situated employees. A putative class action is a lawsuit in which the plaintiff purports to represent a group of similarly affected individuals, but the court has not yet certified the class under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23. Cohen alleged that Consilio's actions violated three Minnesota statutes: the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.101 and 181.171, subd. 1; the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 177.25 and 177.27, subd. 8; and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. Ann. §§ 181.032(f), (d)(4), 181.171, subd. 1, and 177.27. Each of these statutes governs different aspects of wage payment and employer obligations under Minnesota law. The MPWA addresses the timing and payment of wages; the MFLSA is Minnesota's counterpart to the federal Fair Labor Standards Act and governs overtime pay requirements; and the MWTA addresses wage theft, including employer notice obligations regarding changes to compensation.

## Remedies Sought

Cohen sought four categories of relief: overtime pay, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief. Overtime pay represents the additional compensation Cohen alleged he and the putative class were owed for hours worked beyond the standard workweek. Liquidated damages are a fixed monetary award designed to compensate for losses that are difficult to quantify and, in many wage statutes, serve a compensatory and sometimes punitive function. Statutory penalties are specific monetary penalties set by statute for violations, which may be payable to the employee or to a government official depending on the statutory scheme. Injunctive relief is an equitable remedy in which a court orders a party to do or refrain from doing a particular act—in this case, Cohen sought an order compelling Consilio to rescind its unlawful overtime policy. The distinction among these remedies becomes important because Consilio ultimately satisfied some but not all of them, and the remaining disputes centered on penalties and injunctive relief.

## Consilio's Payment and the Parties' Stipulations

After Cohen filed suit, Consilio paid Cohen and other Minnesota licensed attorney document reviewers a total of $256,010.01 to cover the overtime wages and liquidated damages demanded under Cohen's MFLSA claim. Cohen personally received $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages. Following these payments, the parties entered into a stipulation—a formal agreement filed with the court—in which they acknowledged that Cohen had "been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit" and that "Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA]." This stipulation effectively resolved the overtime wage and liquidated damages components of Cohen's MFLSA claim. What remained in dispute was whether Cohen could obtain statutory penalties under the three statutes and injunctive relief.

## The Dispute Over Penalties

With the overtime and liquidated damages claims satisfied, the central dispute narrowed to penalties. Cohen argued he was entitled to $172,080 in "average daily wage penalties" under the MPWA, $42,000 in penalties under the MFLSA, and $42,000 in penalties under the MWTA. Consilio countered that the applicable statutes did not authorize Cohen to obtain penalties. This dispute over penalties is the primary issue before the court on appeal. The question of who may seek penalties under each statute—and whether penalties are available to private plaintiffs at all—requires careful statutory interpretation, which the Eighth Circuit addresses in the substantive sections of the opinion.

## The Parties' Agreement to Resolve Through Summary Judgment

Rather than continue with discovery and litigation on the remaining penalty and injunctive relief disputes, the parties agreed that the most efficient path forward would be for the district court to hear and decide Consilio's summary judgment motion on all three statutory claims before any further proceedings. The parties acknowledged that if the district court granted Consilio's summary judgment motion in full, no claims would remain and the case would be fully resolved. This procedural strategy effectively condensed the remaining issues into a single legal question suitable for resolution by the court, since the parties had stipulated to the key facts regarding payment and the only open questions were legal ones about the availability of penalties under the respective statutes.

## The District Court's Grant of Summary Judgment and Cohen's Appeal

The district court granted Consilio's motion for summary judgment on all three claims—MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA. Summary judgment is a procedural mechanism under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56 by which a court resolves a case when there is no genuine dispute of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Because the parties had stipulated to the relevant facts, the district court's role was to determine the legal questions: whether the statutes at issue permitted Cohen to obtain the penalties he sought. The district court concluded they did not, and dismissed all claims. Cohen then appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit, challenging the district court's rulings on the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims, as well as his claim for injunctive relief.

## Standard of Review on Appeal

On appeal, the Eighth Circuit reviews a district court's grant of summary judgment de novo. De novo review means the appellate court examines the legal question anew, giving no deference to the district court's conclusions. As the court explained, it views "the evidence and the inferences that may be reasonably drawn from the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party"—here, Cohen, since Consilio was the moving party who sought summary judgment. Under this standard, summary judgment is proper if there are no genuine issues of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Because the parties had stipulated to the key facts, the appeal turned entirely on questions of statutory interpretation—legal questions that the Eighth Circuit would review fresh, without deferring to the district court's reasoning.
