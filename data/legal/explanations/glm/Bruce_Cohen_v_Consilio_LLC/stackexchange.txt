Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### What is the 'legislature knows how' canon of statutory interpretation and how is it applied?
Question:
In this opinion, the court concludes that only the Commissioner—not the employee—can seek "average daily wage penalties" under Minnesota's Payment of Wages Act (§ 181.101). Part of the court's reasoning relies on the fact that other Minnesota statutes (§§ 181.13 and 181.14) explicitly authorize employees to seek penalties, so the legislature's failure to include similar language in § 181.101 must be intentional. The court cites this as the "legislature knows how" canon, referencing Lagasse v. Horton. I understand the basic idea that expressio unius est exclusio alterius, but I'm confused about how this canon works in practice. Is this a standalone canon or just an application of negative implication? And how do courts decide when it's appropriate to infer legislative intent from the presence of language in one statute but absence in another, versus assuming the legislature just made a drafting oversight?
Answer:
---

## What the "Legislature Knows How" Canon Is

The "legislature knows how" canon is a specialized form of negative implication that operates *across statutes* rather than within a single statutory text. The basic idea, as the Minnesota Supreme Court explained in *Lagasse v. Horton*, 982 N.W.2d 189, 199 n.2 (Minn. 2022), is that "the Legislature's action in one statute but inaction in another shows that the Legislature 'knows how' to accomplish a particular objective if it wishes to do so." When the legislature has demonstrated that it knows how to draft language achieving a specific result—and then omits that language from a related statute addressing the same subject—courts infer that the omission was deliberate rather than accidental.

In *Cohen v. Consilio*, the Eighth Circuit applied this canon to hold that only the Commissioner of the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry—not the employee—may seek "average daily wage penalties" under Minn. Stat. § 181.101.

## How the Canon Differs from Ordinary *Expressio Unius*

You're right to sense the connection to *expressio unius est exclusio alterius*, but the "legislature knows how" canon has a distinct analytical structure:

- **Expressio unius** typically operates *within* a single statute: if a provision lists A, B, and C, the omission of D implies exclusion of D.
- **"Legislature knows how"** operates *across* statutes: if Statute 1 explicitly grants a particular right or power, and Statute 2—addressing the same general subject—omits that grant, the omission is presumed intentional.

The opinion makes this clear. The court didn't just look at § 181.101 in isolation. It compared § 181.101 to **sister provisions** in the same Payment of Wages Act: §§ 181.13 and 181.14, which govern wages owed to discharged employees and employees who quit or resign, respectively. Both of those statutes explicitly provide that the employee "may charge and collect a penalty." § 181.101, by contrast, references only the Commissioner's authority to "charge and collect" penalties, with collected funds then paid to the employee. The contrast across these related provisions is what makes the inference powerful.

## How the Court Applies It Here

The court's reasoning proceeds in three steps:

1. **The plain language of § 181.101 references only the Commissioner.** The statute provides that "if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty." Any money collected "must be paid to the employee concerned." The employee's role is limited to "prosecuting a claim for wages"—not to seeking penalties directly.

2. **The legislature has demonstrated it knows how to grant employees penalty-seeking authority.** In §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14, subd. 2—provisions dealing with the same general subject of unpaid wages—the legislature explicitly gave employees the power to "charge and collect a penalty." The presence of this specific language in related statutes shows the legislature had a available template it could have used in § 181.101.

3. **The absence of similar language in § 181.101 is therefore presumed intentional.** Because the legislature knew how to empower employees to seek penalties and chose not to do so in § 181.101, the court infers that § 181.101 deliberately reserves penalty-seeking authority to the Commissioner alone.

As the court put it: "the Minnesota legislature, if it wished, knew how to empower an aggrieved employee with a statutory right to obtain penalties."

## When Courts Will Infer Intent vs. Drafting Oversight

The opinion doesn't lay out a formal multi-factor test, but the reasoning suggests the key considerations courts look to:

- **Relatedness of the statutes.** The canon has force when the statutes being compared address the same general subject matter. Here, §§ 181.101, 181.13, and 181.14 are all provisions of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act, dealing with wage collection. This makes the comparison meaningful.

- **Specificity of the omitted language.** The language at issue isn't vague or incidental. The legislature used a specific, recognizable formulation—"may charge and collect a penalty"—in the comparison statutes. Its complete absence from § 181.101 is hard to attribute to carelessness.

- **Plain language of the target statute.** The court begins with the text of § 181.101 itself, confirming that it "only references the Commissioner regarding penalties." The canon then *reinforces* what the plain text already suggests. The court isn't using the canon to create ambiguity where none exists; it's using it to confirm that the statute means what it appears to say.

- **The overall statutory structure.** § 181.171, the general enforcement provision, says employees may recover "civil penalties or damages . . . provided for in the section violated." So the employee's right to penalties depends on whether the *specific section* violated actually provides for them. § 181.101 provides penalties only to the Commissioner, not to the employee. The "legislature knows how" canon confirms this structural reading.

In short, the canon is most persuasive when the statutory texts are closely related, the omitted language is specific and deliberate-looking, the target statute's plain text already points in the same direction, and the overall statutory architecture supports the inference. When these conditions are met—as the court found here—courts treat the omission as intentional rather than as a drafting oversight.

### Can a federal appeals court raise Article III jurisdictional concerns like mootness sua sponte when neither party has briefed the issue?
Question:
In this case, the Eighth Circuit vacated the district court's grant of summary judgment on Cohen's MFLSA claim and remanded for the district court to determine its own jurisdiction, specifically whether Cohen's claim for statutory penalties is moot. The court says it has a "serious concern regarding our jurisdiction" even though Cohen was apparently still actively pursuing this claim and neither party raised the issue. I always thought courts generally decide the issues parties present to them. Under what authority can a federal court raise jurisdictional concerns on its own? Is there a distinction between standing and mootness in terms of when courts must raise them sua sponte versus when they can choose to? And does the fact that penalties under the MFLSA are payable to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen affect the analysis?
Answer:
Yes. Federal courts have an independent obligation to ensure that Article III jurisdiction exists, and this obligation does not depend on the parties raising the issue. The Eighth Circuit's action here is consistent with that principle.

The opinion grounds its sua sponte inquiry in the fundamental relationship between standing and mootness. Quoting *Sisney v. Kaemingk*, the court explains that "[t]he requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)." The court then applies this framework to Cohen's MFLSA penalty claim, noting that while Cohen may have had standing when he filed suit, subsequent events—specifically, Consilio's payment of all owed overtime wages and liquidated damages to Cohen—raised a "serious concern regarding our jurisdiction" as to whether his claim for statutory penalties remained live.

On your question about the distinction between standing and mootness: the opinion treats them as two points on the same continuum of Article III justiciability rather than as categorically different inquiries. Standing is assessed at the outset of litigation; mootness is the continuing obligation to confirm that the personal stake that supported standing at the outset persists throughout the case. Both are jurisdictional requirements that a court must satisfy before exercising its authority, and neither can be waived or conferred by party agreement. The court's quotation of *Laidlaw*—that federal courts are not "license[d] to retain jurisdiction over cases in which one or both of the parties plainly lack a continuing interest"—captures the principle that jurisdiction is not a matter of party consent but of judicial power.

The opinion does not suggest that a court has discretion to *ignore* a jurisdictional defect simply because the parties have not flagged it. To the contrary, the court raises the mootness concern on its own initiative, explains why the record is insufficient to resolve it, and remands for the district court to make the jurisdictional determination in the first instance. The remand reflects the court's view that it cannot adjudicate the MFLSA penalty claim without first confirming that an Article III controversy still exists.

As for the fact that MFLSA statutory penalties are payable to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen, the opinion addresses this directly through *Laidlaw*. The court acknowledges that "civil penalties, even if they are paid to the government, may 'afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct'" because such penalties "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones." Thus, the fact that penalties flow to the Commissioner does not automatically defeat standing or mootness. However, it does shape the analysis: because Cohen has already received all personal monetary compensation (wages and liquidated damages), the viability of his penalty claim depends on whether Consilio's allegedly unlawful overtime policy remains in place. If the policy continues, the deterrent function of civil penalties may keep the claim alive. If the policy has been abandoned and it is "absolutely clear" the wrongful conduct will not recur, the claim may be moot.

Judge Kobes's concurrence adds a wr worth noting. While agreeing that the penalty claim "might be moot," Judge Kobes expresses skepticism about whether Cohen has standing to seek statutory penalties at all under *Laidlaw*, noting "differences between the penalties in that case and those available under the MFLSA." The concurrence does not resolve this standing question, suggesting only that "the district court may decide the mootness question first." This illustrates the practical point that standing and mootness can blur when the plaintiff's personal stake in the remedy is indirect—as it is here, where penalties go to the Commissioner rather than to the plaintiff. The majority resolves this ambiguity by focusing on mootness and remanding for factual development, while Judge Kobes leaves the door open for the district court to also examine standing.

In short: the court's sua sponte raise of the mootness issue is not a departure from the adversarial norm but an exercise of the court's independent obligation to confirm its own jurisdiction. The opinion does not distinguish between standing and mootness in terms of when courts *must* versus *may* raise them—both are treated as non-negotiable Article III prerequisites that the court is responsible for ensuring. The Commissioner-penalty structure does not automatically resolve the jurisdictional question, but it does make the analysis turn on whether an ongoing violation provides Cohen with the continuing personal interest that Article III requires.

### Does a plaintiff have Article III standing to seek civil penalties that are payable to the government rather than to the plaintiff?
Question:
I'm struggling with the standing analysis in this case. Cohen is seeking statutory penalties under the MFLSA, but the parties agree these penalties would be payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen personally. The court cites Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw for the proposition that civil penalties paid to the government can still provide redress to citizen plaintiffs because they deter future violations. But Judge Kobes's concurrence expresses skepticism about whether Laidlaw really applies given differences between the penalties at issue there and those under the MFLSA. How does the redressability prong of standing work when the plaintiff doesn't personally receive any money? Is the deterrence of future violations really sufficient injury to satisfy Article III, or does this depend on the specific statutory context?
Answer:
## Article III Redressability When Penalties Flow to the Government

The short answer, as the Eighth Circuit frames it here, is that **yes, civil penalties payable to the government can satisfy the redressability prong of standing—but only when the plaintiff is seeking redress for an ongoing or threatened injury, and the penalties serve to deter that ongoing conduct.** The court derives this principle from *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw Environmental Services*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000).

### The Redressability Logic from *Laidlaw*

The standard redressability inquiry asks whether a favorable decision is likely to redress the plaintiff's injury. When a plaintiff personally receives money, the redressability chain is straightforward: the defendant pays the plaintiff, and the plaintiff's monetary injury is addressed. When penalties flow to the government, the chain is less direct but, per *Laidlaw*, still functional: civil penalties imposed on the defendant **deter ongoing and future violations**, and it is the cessation of those violations that redresses the plaintiff's injury.

The Eighth Circuit quotes *Laidlaw* directly on this point:

> Civil penalties, even if they are paid to the government, may "afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct" because such penalties may "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones."

The key is that the plaintiff's injury is not the loss of the penalty money—it is the ongoing unlawful conduct itself. The penalties redress that injury by incentivizing the defendant to stop.

### Why the Court Remanded Rather Than Resolving Standing

The court did not definitively hold that Cohen has standing for the MFLSA penalties. Instead, it identified a "serious concern regarding our jurisdiction" and remanded for the district court to examine the issue in the first instance. The reason is that the same logic supporting standing under *Laidlaw* also creates a **mootness problem** once the underlying conduct ceases.

The court explains that "the requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness)." Here, Cohen has already been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages he is owed. What remains in dispute is **whether Consilio's violative overtime policy is still in place.** This factual question is dispositive:

- **If the policy remains**, then Cohen is still threatened with injury from ongoing unlawful conduct, and the deterrence rationale from *Laidlaw* supports standing—the penalties would incentivize Consilio to discontinue the policy.
- **If the policy is no longer in place** and it is "absolutely clear" the wrongful conduct will not recur, then there is nothing left to deter, the claim "may in fact be moot," and the court lacks jurisdiction.

The court thus treats the standing/mootness question as turning on the same factual predicate: the existence of ongoing or threatened unlawful conduct that the penalties would deter.

### Judge Kobes's Concurrence: A Statutory-Context Caveat

Judge Kobes concurs specially to flag that the transferability of *Laidlaw* to this context is not straightforward. He notes "there are differences between the penalties in that case and those available under the MFLSA" and that "[n]either party briefed the issue."

This is a meaningful caveat. *Laidlaw* involved civil penalties under the Clean Water Act's citizen-suit provision, a statute specifically designed to allow citizens to enforce environmental laws. The MFLSA, by contrast, may not share the same enforcement architecture, and the differences could affect whether the deterrence rationale applies with equal force. However, because the parties did not brief the standing question, and because the claim might be moot in any event, Judge Kobes agrees that remand is the appropriate course.

### Bottom Line

The opinion stands for the proposition that **penalties payable to the government can satisfy Article III redressability when the plaintiff is injured by ongoing unlawful conduct and the penalties would deter that conduct.** But the opinion does not definitively resolve whether that standard is met here. Instead, it ties the standing inquiry to the factual question of whether the defendant's violative conduct is ongoing—making the standing analysis and the mootness analysis two sides of the same coin. If there is no continuing violation to deter, there is no ongoing injury to redress, and the court lacks jurisdiction regardless of whether *Laidlaw* would otherwise apply.

### How does the voluntary cessation doctrine apply when an employer pays owed wages but the challenged policy may still be in effect?
Question:
The court discusses whether Consilio's payment of Cohen's overtime wages and liquidated damages moots his claim for civil penalties. The court notes that voluntary cessation of a challenged practice doesn't automatically deprive a federal court of jurisdiction, citing City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle. But the court also says the claim "might become moot" if it's "absolutely clear" the wrongful conduct won't recur. The opinion then remands because there's a dispute about whether Consilio's violative overtime policy is still in place. I'm confused about how the burden works here. Does the defendant bear the burden of showing it's "absolutely clear" the conduct won't recur? And what would Consilio need to show to meet that standard—just that they paid Cohen, or that they actually changed their company-wide policy? Also, how does this interact with the fact that the penalty claim is about deterrence rather than compensation?
Answer:
## Answer

The voluntary cessation doctrine places the burden squarely on the defendant to demonstrate that the challenged conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur. The Eighth Circuit's opinion in *Cohen v. Consilio* applies this framework in a straightforward way, and the deterrence-oriented nature of the penalty claim is precisely why the policy question matters.

### The Burden Is on the Defendant

Under the voluntary cessation doctrine, a defendant who stops the challenged practice does not automatically moot the case. The court cites *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle* for this principle: "a defendant's voluntary cessation of a challenged practice does not deprive a federal court of its power to determine the legality of the practice." The corollary, drawn from *Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw* and *United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Export Ass'n*, is that a claim "might become moot" only if it is "absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur."

That "absolutely clear" language is the defendant's burden. Consilio, as the party asserting mootness, would need to show that its wrongful conduct—specifically, the violative overtime policy—has been discontinued and will not return. Mere payment of owed wages to Cohen does not satisfy this standard. The opinion makes this explicit by noting that "whether Consilio's violative overtime policy is still in place remains in dispute." The court frames the inquiry around the existence of the policy itself, not around whether Cohen has been compensated.

### What Consilio Would Need to Show

The opinion draws a clear distinction between compensating the individual plaintiff and ceasing the challenged practice. Consilio paid Cohen his overtime wages and liquidated damages, and the parties stipulated that Cohen "has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed." But the court does not treat this payment as resolving the mootness question. Instead, it says:

> If Consilio's policy remains, Cohen's claim for civil penalties may still be viable. However, if Consilio's policy is no longer in place and it is "absolutely clear" that its wrongful conduct will not recur, Cohen's MFLSA claim may in fact be moot.

So to meet the "absolutely clear" standard, Consilio would need to show that the violative overtime policy itself has been eliminated or modified to comply with the law—not simply that Cohen personally received a check. The burden is about the company-wide practice that gave rise to the violation, not the individual plaintiff's injury.

### Why Deterrence Keeps the Claim Alive

The reason the policy question is dismissible rather than the payment is the nature of the relief at issue. The statutory penalties under the MFLSA are payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen. The court nonetheless recognizes that such penalties can provide redress to a private plaintiff, citing *Laidlaw*:

> Civil penalties, even if they are paid to the government, may "afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct" because such penalties may "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones."

This is the key to the mootness analysis. A compensatory claim becomes moot when the plaintiff has been fully compensated because there is nothing left to redress. But a penalty claim aimed at deterrence is not about backward-looking compensation for Cohen's injury—it is about forward-looking deterrence of ongoing or future unlawful conduct. If the violative policy remains in place, the deterrent function of the penalty is still serving a live purpose, and Cohen's claim remains viable. If the policy is gone and cannot reasonably be expected to return, the deterrent rationale may no longer support a live case or controversy, and the claim may be moot.

### The Remand

The court remanded because the record contained a genuine dispute of material fact about whether the violative overtime policy was still in effect. This is a factual question that the district court must resolve in the first instance to determine whether it retains jurisdiction. If the policy persists, the claim is not moot because the deterrent purpose of the penalty remains relevant to ongoing conduct. If the policy has been eliminated and its return is "absolutely clear" to be impossible, the claim may be moot because the deterrent function no longer addresses a live controversy.

In short: the burden is on Consilio; payment to Cohen alone is insufficient; the company must show the violative policy is gone for good; and the deterrence rationale is exactly why the policy's status—not Cohen's compensation—controls the mootness question.

### What is the consequence of failing to provide legal authority or argument for a claim on appeal?
Question:
The court quickly dismisses Cohen's MWTA claim by stating he "points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute" and provides "no meaningful argument in support of reversal," citing Milligan v. City of Red Oak for the proposition that unsupported assertions are waived. But Cohen did raise this claim below and the district court addressed it. I'm confused about the standard for waiver on appeal. Is this issue preservation, appellate waiver, or something else? How much argument and authority does a party need to provide to avoid waiver? Does it matter that the district court ruled on the claim? And is there any difference between failing to cite legal authority and failing to make any argument at all?
Answer:
## What's happening here is appellate waiver, not issue preservation

The court is applying **appellate waiver** (sometimes called "waiver on appeal" or " forfeiture of appellate argument"), which is distinct from issue preservation. Issue preservation concerns whether a party raised an issue below so that the appellate court will consider it at all. Here, there's no indication Cohen failed to preserve the MWTA claim—it was raised below and the district court ruled on it. The problem is what Cohen did (or didn't do) **in his appellate brief**.

## The standard from *Milligan*

The Eighth Circuit's rule, as articulated in *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000), is that when a party's appellate brief "does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it." The court in *Cohen* applies this rule directly: Cohen "points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute" and provides "no meaningful argument in support of reversal," so the court "will not disturb the district court's order dismissing this claim."

The key point is that the court isn't saying the claim was never preserved or that it lacks jurisdiction—it's saying that even assuming the claim was properly before the court, Cohen gave the court no basis on which to reverse. The appellate court's role is to review the district court's decision for error, and a party who wants reversal must explain, with reasoning and authority, why the district court got it wrong. When the appellant fails to do that, the court treats the issue as waived and declines to disturb the ruling below.

## The district court's prior ruling doesn't save the claim

The fact that the district court addressed the MWTA claim (even if briefly) does not relieve Cohen of his obligation to brief the issue on appeal. Cohen himself complained that the district court "included little to no discussion of this claim," but the court of appeals essentially responded that this doesn't matter—the burden was on Cohen as the appellant to provide "meaningful argument in support of reversal." The court's posture is: we review for error, and you haven't given us any argument or authority showing error. Without that, there's nothing for the court to work with, and it won't construct the argument for the appellant.

## Authority vs. argument: the *Milligan* formulation treats them together

The *Milligan* standard uses the disjunctive: a brief that does not support an assertion with "**any argument or legal authority**" has waived the issue. This suggests that the absence of either one can trigger waiver. In *Cohen*, the court identifies both deficiencies—Cohen "points to no statutory authority" **and** provides "no meaningful argument." The opinion doesn't separately analyze whether one deficiency alone would have been enough, but the *Milligan* formulation, by using "or," indicates that failing to provide either adequate argument or supporting authority is sufficient for waiver.

In practice, the two are related. Here, Cohen's claim depended on the existence of a statutory right to civil penalties under the MWTA. Without citing statutory authority for that proposition, there's no legal foundation for the argument. And without developing the argument, the court has nothing to evaluate. The court treats these as compounded deficiencies leading to the same result: waiver.

## Bottom line

The standard is straightforward: on appeal, a party must support each claim with both argument and legal authority. The fact that the issue was raised and decided below doesn't excuse the appellant from this obligation. If the appellate brief provides neither meaningful argument nor supporting authority for a claim, the issue is waived under *Milligan*, and the court will not disturb the district court's ruling—regardless of how thoroughly the district court addressed it.

### Can a plaintiff seek different injunctive relief in a summary judgment brief than what was requested in the complaint?
Question:
Cohen originally sought certain specific forms of injunctive relief in his complaint, but then requested different relief—withdrawing the 2019 policy—in his summary judgment briefing. The court says this issue is not properly before it, citing InfoDeli, LLC for the proposition that "new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs" and referencing Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a). I'm confused about the line between modifying a claim and raising an entirely new one. Why is requesting different injunctive relief treated as a "new claim" rather than just a refinement of the existing claim? Would this have been permissible if Cohen had sought leave to amend under Rule 15(a)? And what should a plaintiff do when they realize during litigation that the relief they originally requested isn't quite what they need?
Answer:
## Why Different Injunctive Relief Is Treated as a New Claim

The short answer is that the complaint defines the scope of the claims and the relief sought. When a plaintiff requests substantively different injunctive relief in a summary judgment brief than what was pleaded in the complaint, the defendant has not had fair notice of—nor an opportunity to respond to—that new request in the pleadings themselves. The court in *Cohen v. Consilio* treated Cohen's request to withdraw the 2019 policy as a new claim rather than a refinement because it was a materially different form of injunctive relief from the "certain specific forms of injunctive relief" he had originally pleaded. The court characterized Cohen as having "change[d] course and request[ed] different relief," which is qualitatively different from narrowing or clarifying relief already within the complaint's four corners.

The distinction matters because of the notice function of pleadings. A defendant is entitled to know, from the complaint, what relief the plaintiff is seeking. Litigation briefs are not pleadings—they are argumentative documents. As the Eighth Circuit put it in *InfoDeli, LLC*, cited by the court: "New claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs." The problem is not that Cohen's new request was related to the same underlying dispute; it is that he sought to expand or alter the relief through a vehicle that does not put the opposing party on formal notice of the change.

## Why Rule 15(a) Is the Proper Mechanism

The court's citation to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a) signals the correct path. Rule 15(a)(2) permits a party to amend its pleading "only with the opposing party's written consent or the court's leave," and courts "should freely give leave when justice so requires." If Cohen had sought leave to amend his complaint to request withdrawal of the 2019 policy, the district court could have evaluated whether to permit the amendment—considering factors such as undue delay, prejudice to Consilio, and futility. That process would have given Consilio notice of the new relief and an opportunity to respond before the issue was decided. By bypassing Rule 15(a) and requesting the new relief for the first time in his summary judgment briefing, Cohen deprived the district court of the procedural mechanism for evaluating whether the new claim should be added at all.

## What a Plaintiff Should Do Instead

When a plaintiff realizes during litigation that the relief originally requested does not capture what they actually need, the proper step is to **seek leave to amend the complaint under Rule 15(a)**. This ensures that:

1. **The new relief is formally pleaded**, placing the defendant on notice.
2. **The defendant has an opportunity to respond**, including by opposing the amendment if it is prejudiced.
3. **The court can evaluate the amendment** under the standard Rule 15(a) factors before the new claim is litigated on the merits.

The key takeaway from *Cohen* is that the complaint—not the briefing—defines the universe of claims and relief before the court. A summary judgment motion tests the claims as pleaded; it is not a vehicle for asserting new ones. If your understanding of the appropriate remedy evolves during discovery, amend the complaint rather than springing the new request on the court and opposing counsel in a brief.
