Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# The Waiver Trap: Procedural Missteps in Employment Class Action Appeals

When an employer pays everything it owes before judgment, what's left? In *Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit confronted that question and largely answered "not much"—but the opinion's most instructive lessons for practitioners lie not in its statutory interpretation of Minnesota wage law, but in the procedural pitfalls that swallowed two of Cohen's remaining claims. The decision serves as a cautionary tale about the consequences of underdeveloped arguments and shifting legal theories, particularly in class actions where the named plaintiff's choices bind putative class members.

## The MWTA Claim: Silence as Concession

Cohen alleged that Consilio violated the Minnesota Wage Theft Act by failing to provide statutorily required notice of its overtime policy change. The district court dismissed the claim with little discussion, and Cohen protested that the court had shortchanged his argument. But the Eighth Circuit was unmoved. The panel noted that Cohen had stipulated to receiving all pay Consilio owed him, and—critically—that he "points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute." Citing *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, the court treated the absence of supporting legal authority and meaningful argument as a waiver, declining to disturb the district court's dismissal.

This is a familiar but easily forgotten principle: appellate courts are not research assistants. The *Milligan* rule—that unsupported assertions are waived—is not a technicality; it is a structural feature of adversarial litigation. The burden falls on the appellant to identify both the factual basis and the legal authority supporting each claim. In the class action context, this burden takes on added weight. A named plaintiff who fails to develop a statutory theory does not merely forfeit his own recovery; he may foreclose it for the entire class, assuming certification was or would be granted. Cohen's MWTA claim required him to identify a specific penalty provision in the statute that created a private right to recover. His failure to do so—whether by oversight or strategic miscalculation—proved fatal.

## The Injunctive Relief Claim: The Right Remedy Through the Wrong Vehicle

Cohen's injunctive relief claim illustrates a different but equally common procedural trap: changing course mid-litigation without amending the pleadings. In his complaint, Cohen sought specific forms of injunctive relief. By the time he filed his summary judgment opposition, however, he had pivoted to requesting a different remedy—an affirmative order that Consilio rescind its 2019 overtime policy. The district court, understandably, did not address this newly formulated request, and the Eighth Circuit declined to consider it, citing *InfoDeli, LLC* for the proposition that "[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs" and invoking Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a).

The court's reasoning here is straightforward and doctrinally sound. Summary judgment briefing is not a vehicle for reformulating claims. Rule 15(a) provides a clear mechanism for amending pleadings—before trial, leave to amend should be "freely given when justice so requires"—and parties who bypass that mechanism cannot expect appellate courts to rescue them. The district court was entitled to evaluate Cohen's request for injunctive relief based on what he pleaded, not what he argued at the eleventh hour.

From a strategic standpoint, the shift suggests that Cohen's litigation theory evolved as the case progressed—perhaps as discovery revealed that his originally pleaded injunctive relief was unworkable or that the policy he challenged had been modified. That kind of evolution is normal in complex litigation, but the procedural mechanism matters. An amended complaint would have given Consilio fair notice and an opportunity to respond. Raising the issue in summary judgment briefing instead denied the district court a clean record and deprived the appellate court of a properly developed question.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The Eighth Circuit's treatment of these two claims reinforces several principles that practitioners should heed, particularly in wage-and-hour class actions where the stakes extend beyond the named plaintiff.

First, the *Milligan* waiver rule applies with full force on appeal. Appellate briefs must identify the statutory basis for each form of relief sought, including penalties. General invocations of a statute are insufficient. Counsel must trace the specific subsection that creates the right of action and the specific penalty provision that authorizes recovery—failure to do so is treated as a concession that no such authority exists.

Second, *InfoDeli* and Rule 15(a) together establish that summary judgment briefing is not a substitute for the pleadings. When litigation strategy shifts, the complaint must follow. This is especially important in the class action context, where class certification motions and merits briefing often proceed on parallel tracks. A named plaintiff who changes his injunctive relief theory without amending the complaint risks losing the claim entirely—and may face arguments that the class representative is inadequate under Rule 23(a)(4) for failing to prosecute the class's claims consistently.

Third, the opinion's remand of the MFLSA claim on mootness grounds—while not the focus of this post—casts a shadow over the remaining claims. The court's concern about whether Consilio's voluntary cessation of its overtime policy rendered Cohen's penalty claim moot underscores the fragility of penalty-only claims once the underlying conduct has ceased. Practitioners pursuing such claims should develop a robust record on whether the challenged policy remains in effect, as the burden of showing that wrongful conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur falls on the defendant.

## Conclusion

*Cohen v. Consilio* is, at its core, a case about what remains when the substantive claims have been satisfied. The Eighth Circuit's answer is: procedurally proper claims, adequately briefed and properly pleaded. Cohen's MWTA and injunctive relief claims failed not because they lacked merit, but because they were not presented in the form the adversarial system requires. For employment class action practitioners, the lesson is clear. Develop every statutory theory from the outset, identify the specific penalty provisions authorizing recovery, and when litigation strategy shifts, amend the complaint. The waiver trap is easy to fall into and nearly impossible to escape on appeal.
