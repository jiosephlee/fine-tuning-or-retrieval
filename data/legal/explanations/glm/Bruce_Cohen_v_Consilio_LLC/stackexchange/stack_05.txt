Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### What is the consequence of failing to provide legal authority or argument for a claim on appeal?
Question:
The court quickly dismisses Cohen's MWTA claim by stating he "points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute" and provides "no meaningful argument in support of reversal," citing Milligan v. City of Red Oak for the proposition that unsupported assertions are waived. But Cohen did raise this claim below and the district court addressed it. I'm confused about the standard for waiver on appeal. Is this issue preservation, appellate waiver, or something else? How much argument and authority does a party need to provide to avoid waiver? Does it matter that the district court ruled on the claim? And is there any difference between failing to cite legal authority and failing to make any argument at all?
Answer:
## What's happening here is appellate waiver, not issue preservation

The court is applying **appellate waiver** (sometimes called "waiver on appeal" or " forfeiture of appellate argument"), which is distinct from issue preservation. Issue preservation concerns whether a party raised an issue below so that the appellate court will consider it at all. Here, there's no indication Cohen failed to preserve the MWTA claim—it was raised below and the district court ruled on it. The problem is what Cohen did (or didn't do) **in his appellate brief**.

## The standard from *Milligan*

The Eighth Circuit's rule, as articulated in *Milligan v. City of Red Oak*, 230 F.3d 355, 360 (8th Cir. 2000), is that when a party's appellate brief "does not support his assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue and we do not address it." The court in *Cohen* applies this rule directly: Cohen "points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute" and provides "no meaningful argument in support of reversal," so the court "will not disturb the district court's order dismissing this claim."

The key point is that the court isn't saying the claim was never preserved or that it lacks jurisdiction—it's saying that even assuming the claim was properly before the court, Cohen gave the court no basis on which to reverse. The appellate court's role is to review the district court's decision for error, and a party who wants reversal must explain, with reasoning and authority, why the district court got it wrong. When the appellant fails to do that, the court treats the issue as waived and declines to disturb the ruling below.

## The district court's prior ruling doesn't save the claim

The fact that the district court addressed the MWTA claim (even if briefly) does not relieve Cohen of his obligation to brief the issue on appeal. Cohen himself complained that the district court "included little to no discussion of this claim," but the court of appeals essentially responded that this doesn't matter—the burden was on Cohen as the appellant to provide "meaningful argument in support of reversal." The court's posture is: we review for error, and you haven't given us any argument or authority showing error. Without that, there's nothing for the court to work with, and it won't construct the argument for the appellant.

## Authority vs. argument: the *Milligan* formulation treats them together

The *Milligan* standard uses the disjunctive: a brief that does not support an assertion with "**any argument or legal authority**" has waived the issue. This suggests that the absence of either one can trigger waiver. In *Cohen*, the court identifies both deficiencies—Cohen "points to no statutory authority" **and** provides "no meaningful argument." The opinion doesn't separately analyze whether one deficiency alone would have been enough, but the *Milligan* formulation, by using "or," indicates that failing to provide either adequate argument or supporting authority is sufficient for waiver.

In practice, the two are related. Here, Cohen's claim depended on the existence of a statutory right to civil penalties under the MWTA. Without citing statutory authority for that proposition, there's no legal foundation for the argument. And without developing the argument, the court has nothing to evaluate. The court treats these as compounded deficiencies leading to the same result: waiver.

## Bottom line

The standard is straightforward: on appeal, a party must support each claim with both argument and legal authority. The fact that the issue was raised and decided below doesn't excuse the appellant from this obligation. If the appellate brief provides neither meaningful argument nor supporting authority for a claim, the issue is waived under *Milligan*, and the court will not disturb the district court's ruling—regardless of how thoroughly the district court addressed it.
