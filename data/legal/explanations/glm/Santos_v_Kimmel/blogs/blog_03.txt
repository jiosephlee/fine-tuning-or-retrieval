Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Platform Terms of Service and the Limits of Enforcement by Non-Parties

When a comedian dupes a disgraced former congressman into recording absurd personalized videos, the result is predictable late-night television. When that congressman sues over it, the result is a useful lesson in the structural limits of platform governance. The Second Circuit's decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* resolves the copyright questions in Kimmel's favor on fair use grounds, but the opinion's most quietly significant holdings concern Santos's state law claims—particularly his attempt to enforce Cameo's Terms of Service against Jimmy Kimmel despite never having agreed to them. The court's analysis exposes a recurring tension in platform ecosystems: participants may depend on terms of service to structure their interactions yet lack any practical mechanism to enforce those terms when violations harm them.

## The Third-Party Beneficiary Problem

Santos's direct breach of contract claim depended on a straightforward but ultimately fatal premise. Kimmel, using pseudonyms, had agreed to Cameo's Terms of Service when he submitted his video requests. Those terms, Santos argued, restricted how purchasers could use the videos they received. When Kimmel aired the videos on his show, he breached those terms—and Santos, as the creator whose work was misused, sought to enforce them.

The problem is that Santos was not a party to that contract. The Terms of Service governed the relationship between Cameo and its users; Santos, as a talent provider on the platform, was outside that agreement. Under Illinois law, which governed the Terms of Service, courts apply a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." To overcome that presumption, Santos would have needed to show that the implication of third-party benefit was "so strong as to be practically an express declaration."

What made Santos's failure particularly telling was a structural detail the court emphasized. Other provisions of the very same Terms of Service did contain express third-party beneficiary language. The presence of such language in some provisions but not the ones Santos sought to enforce undermined his argument that the parties intended to extend enforceable rights to him by implication. When draftsmen know how to confer third-party rights and do so in some sections but not others, courts reasonably infer that the omission was deliberate.

This reasoning reflects a sensible application of contract doctrine, but it also reveals something about platform architecture. Platforms like Cameo depend on creators to supply content and users to purchase it, but the contractual relationships are typically bilateral—platform-to-user and platform-to-creator—rather than triangular. The Terms of Service that govern user behavior are designed to protect the platform's interests, not necessarily to create enforceable rights in the creators whose work flows through the system. Santos's situation illustrates the gap: he relied on Cameo's rules to structure how buyers could use his videos, but the contractual architecture gave him no standing to vindicate that reliance.

## Implied Contract and the Pleading of Essential Terms

Santos's alternative theory—an implied-in-fact contract between him and Kimmel—fared no better. Under New York law, an implied contract requires a "meeting of the minds" on essential terms. Santos's complaint, the court held, failed to allege those terms with the specificity New York requires. He did not identify the specific provisions under which liability was predicated, nor did he plausibly allege that Kimmel had agreed to any restrictions on his use of the videos.

This holding is less doctrinely interesting than the third-party beneficiary ruling, but it carries practical implications. Santos apparently could not articulate what the implied contract's terms actually were—perhaps because the entire premise of the arrangement was that Kimmel was concealing his identity and purpose. It is difficult to establish a meeting of the minds when one party is actively misrepresenting himself. The court's observation that Santos failed to allege "an indication of a meeting of minds" underscores a basic difficulty: a contract formed through deception may be voidable, but it is hard to argue in the alternative that a contract was both fraudulently induced and genuinely agreed upon in its essential terms.

## Fraudulent Inducement and the Out-of-Pocket Rule

Santos's fraudulent inducement claim foundered on a distinct New York requirement: the need to allege actual out-of-pocket loss. Santos had been paid for the videos. He did not allege that he had been financially harmed by Kimmel's deception; indeed, the commercial transaction was completed. Under New York's out-of-pocket rule, a fraud plaintiff must demonstrate actual pecuniary loss directly resulting from the misrepresentation. The court found no such loss in the complaint.

This requirement functions as an independent barrier for creators in Santos's position. If a buyer tricks a creator into producing content that is later used in ways the creator did not intend, the creator may feel deceived and may have a strong moral claim, but if the creator was compensated for the work, New York law asks whether there was a tangible financial loss. The answer here was no. The fraudulent inducement claim thus collapsed not because Santos was not deceived—he plainly was—but because New York's damages rule demands a specific kind of economic injury that deception alone does not produce.

## The Structural Gap

Taken together, the state law holdings in *Santos* reveal a pattern that extends beyond this case. Platform participants who produce content are often the parties most affected when users violate platform terms, but the contractual architecture of platforms typically does not grant them enforcement rights. They cannot sue as third-party beneficiaries because the terms are written to protect the platform. They may struggle to establish implied contracts with users because the platform mediates the interaction. And fraudulent inducement claims require economic losses that may not exist when the transaction was completed and paid for.

None of this is surprising as a matter of doctrine. The court's analysis is careful, conventional, and well-supported by precedent. But the cumulative effect is that creators like Santos are left without state law remedies for deceptive solicitation of their content, even when the deception is undisputed. The fair use ruling on the copyright side means that the First Amendment interests in commentary and criticism prevail over the creator's rights in the work. That is almost certainly the correct result. But it leaves a zone where a buyer can intentionally deceive a creator into producing content, broadcast that content to millions, and face no contractual or tort liability under state law—so long as the use qualifies as transformative commentary and the creator was paid for the initial transaction.

Whether that zone should exist is a policy question. Whether it does exist is now clearer after *Santos*: it does, at least in the Second Circuit, at least under New York and Illinois law, and at least when the platform's own terms do not expressly provide otherwise.
