Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Strategic Avoidance: The Second Circuit Sidesteps Copyright Preemption in Santos v. Kimmel

When a former congressman sues a late-night comedian over personalized Cameo videos aired for laughs, the temptation is to treat the case as farce. But *Santos v. Kimmel*, decided last week by the Second Circuit, raises a serious and recurring doctrinal question that the court chose not to answer: where does copyright preemption end and state law contract liability begin when creative works are at issue? The court's decision to resolve Santos's state law claims on narrow state-law grounds—while explicitly declining to address whether those claims were preempted by the Copyright Act—reflects a pragmatic judicial instinct. But it also leaves a doctrinal gap that litigants and lower courts will continue to struggle with.

## The Case and the Preemption Question

The facts are by now familiar. Jimmy Kimmel, using pseudonyms, commissioned personalized greeting videos from then-Congressman George Santos through the Cameo platform. Santos fulfilled the requests. Kimmel aired the videos on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* in a recurring segment called "Will Santos Say It?" Santos sued for copyright infringement and, relevant here, brought state law claims for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement.

The District Court (Cote, J.) dismissed the copyright claims on fair use grounds and concluded that Santos's contract claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act under 17 U.S.C. § 301(a) or independently failed to state a claim. On appeal, Santos argued that his contract claims were "qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim" and thus survived preemption under the Second Circuit's framework in *Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc.* (2d Cir. 2022).

The Second Circuit's response was striking in its brevity. After quoting the relevant standard from *Melendez*, Judge Lohier wrote: "We need not resolve this issue, however, because Santos's state law contract claims fail to state a claim for relief under state law." The court then devoted several paragraphs to explaining why Santos lacked standing to enforce Cameo's Terms of Service under Illinois law, failed to plead essential terms of an implied contract under New York law, and failed to allege out-of-pocket loss for his fraudulent inducement claim.

This is a perfectly defensible approach. Courts should generally decide cases on the narrowest available grounds, and the state law deficiencies in Santos's complaint were clear enough. But the avoidance has costs that deserve attention.

## The Doctrinal Vacuum

Copyright preemption under § 301(a) withdraws state law protection from claims that are "equivalent to any of the exclusive rights within the general scope of copyright" and that concern works "within the subject matter of copyright." The Second Circuit applies what it calls the "extra element" test: a state law claim is not preempted if it requires an extra element that makes it "qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim." Contract claims can survive preemption when they turn on a breach of an express or implied agreement that is independent of the rights protected by copyright—typically by involving obligations that go beyond mere reproduction, distribution, or public performance.

The difficulty, of course, is determining which contractual obligations qualify as "extra elements" and which are merely reformulated copyright claims. Must the contract impose obligations that are categorically different from the copyright bundle of rights, or is it enough that the claim sounds in contract rather than property? How specific must the contractual terms be to save a claim from preemption? These questions arise repeatedly in cases involving creative works—software licenses, musical performances, photograph usage rights, and now, apparently, personalized video recordings made through online platforms.

By declining to reach the preemption question, the Second Circuit missed an opportunity to clarify these boundaries. The *Santos* facts presented a relatively clean vehicle. Santos's contract claims were predicated on the creation and use of copyrighted works. The Cameo Terms of Service governed the relationship between the user (Kimmel) and the platform, not directly between Santos and Kimmel. The implied contract claim appeared to rest on little more than the assertion that Kimmel should have known the videos would not be used for commercial broadcast—a theory that sits uncomfortably close to the exclusive rights granted by copyright itself. A preemption analysis could have helped delineate when platform-mediated transactions involving creative content give rise to enforceable contractual expectations distinct from copyright protections.

## When Courts Should Confront Preemption

To be fair, the Second Circuit's restraint is not unusual. The court has previously declined to resolve preemption questions when state law grounds suffice for disposal. And there is a legitimate argument that courts should avoid constitutional and federal statutory questions when alternative grounds are available—a principle that applies with some force to preemption analysis, which requires interpreting the scope of federal copyright law.

But preemption doctrine under § 301(a) is not merely an academic exercise. It serves a structural function in the federal intellectual property system, ensuring that state law does not fill gaps that Congress intended the Copyright Act to occupy—or does not occupy. When courts sidestep preemption, they leave litigants without guidance on whether state law remedies remain available for harms involving creative works. This uncertainty has real consequences. Plaintiffs may continue to file state law claims that are doctrinally preempted, adding years of litigation and expense before courts dispose of them on other grounds. Defendants cannot assess their exposure without knowing whether state law claims survive preemption. And the doctrine itself develops in an ad hoc, piecemeal fashion.

There is also a subtle but important point about how avoidance can skew doctrinal development. When courts resolve preemption only in cases where state law claims are strong enough to survive dismissal, they develop preemption jurisprudence primarily in the context of meritorious state law claims. Cases like *Santos*, where the state law claims are weak, might have provided a useful counterpoint—a context in which the court could clarify that preemption bars certain reformulated copyright claims even before reaching the merits of the underlying state law theory.

## Implications for Platform-Mediated Creative Transactions

The *Santos* avoidance also has implications beyond formal preemption doctrine. As creative production increasingly occurs through digital platforms—Cameo, Substack, YouTube, Patreon, and others—the relationship between platform terms of service, creator expectations, and copyright law becomes more complex. Creators like Santos produce copyrighted works pursuant to platform agreements they may not fully control. Users commission or access those works under terms set by the platform. When disputes arise, the question of who can enforce what obligations—and under what legal theory—becomes pressing.

The Second Circuit's resolution of Santos's direct breach of contract claim illustrates this complexity. Santos could not enforce the Cameo Terms of Service because he was not a party to that agreement under Illinois law. The court noted that other provisions of the Terms did contain third-party beneficiary language, suggesting the platform could have drafted provisions to allow creators to enforce certain obligations. This observation hints at a regulatory architecture that platforms could adopt but currently leave unaddressed: clear contractual frameworks that define the rights and obligations of all parties to platform-mediated creative transactions, reducing the need for litigation over implied contracts and preemption boundaries.

## Conclusion

The Second Circuit's decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* is doctrinally unremarkable in its outcome—Santos's claims failed for straightforward reasons. But the court's strategic avoidance of the preemption question reflects a broader pattern in copyright jurisprudence that deserves scrutiny. When courts resolve cases on alternative grounds, they conserve judicial resources and avoid unnecessary federal questions. But they also leave the preemption boundary undefined, and litigants continue to litigate claims that might be more efficiently disposed of with clearer doctrinal guidance. The Second Circuit has the doctrinal tools to provide that guidance. Whether it will choose to use them in the next case remains to be seen.
