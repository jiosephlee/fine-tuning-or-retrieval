Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### How can fair use be decided on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion without a factual record?
Question:
In Santos v. Kimmel, the Second Circuit affirms dismissal of the copyright infringement claim at the pleading stage, stating that fair use may be "so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal." But fair use involves a four-factor test that seems fact-intensive—transformative use, nature of the work, amount used, and market harm all seem to require evidence. How can a court evaluate these factors when all it has is the complaint? Is there something special about fair use that makes it different from other affirmative defenses at the motion-to-dismiss stage? When is it appropriate to dismiss on fair use grounds versus letting it go to discovery?
Answer:
## Fair Use at the Pleading Stage: The Complaint Itself Can Supply the Necessary Facts

There is nothing unique to fair use that allows it to be resolved on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion. The same principle that governs other affirmative defenses applies: **when the complaint's own allegations establish the defense, dismissal is appropriate.** What makes fair use amenable to early resolution in some cases is that the comparison between the original work and the secondary use—central to the transformative use and market harm analyses—can often be conducted by looking at the works themselves, which may be attached to or incorporated by reference in the complaint.

### The Governing Standard

The Second Circuit has held that fair use "may be so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim" at the pleading stage. In conducting this analysis, the court considers "the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference in the complaint," and may take judicial notice of copyright registrations. The critical point is that the court need only consider "the original version of [the works] and the [secondary works] at issue" to decide the question of fair use. If those materials are before the court at the motion-to-dismiss stage, the four-factor analysis can proceed.

### How the Factors Were Resolved Here

The complaint itself supplied the facts necessary to evaluate the first and fourth factors, which the court found dispositive.

**Factor One (Purpose and Character of the Use):** Santos's own allegations described his videos as conveying "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." The JKL segments, by contrast, used the videos "to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money." Because the transformative use inquiry turns on what a reasonable observer would perceive—not the subjective intent of either party—the court could compare these two purposes directly from the face of the complaint and conclude the use was transformative.

Santos's argument that Kimmel's bad faith (using false names to solicit the videos) nullified fair use was rejected. The court noted that the Supreme Court has "distanced itself" from the *Harper & Row* good-faith requirement, and that the complaint itself portrayed the defendants as motivated by "sarcastic criticism and commentary," not by an intent to supplant Santos's commercial market.

**Factor Four (Market Harm):** This factor was also resolvable on the face of the complaint because the relevant question is not whether criticism devalues the original, but whether the secondary use "usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute." The court observed that "the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market." Nothing in the complaint suggested Kimmel's segments served as a substitute for Santos's Cameo videos.

### When Dismissal Is Appropriate Versus Discovery

The lesson is procedural, not substantive: fair use can be decided at the pleading stage **when the complaint and accompanying materials establish the facts needed to evaluate the four factors without genuine dispute.** Here, the plaintiff's own description of his works' purpose, combined with the obviously commentary-driven nature of the JKL segments, meant no factual development was necessary. Where the transformative nature of the use, the amount taken, or market effects would depend on evidence not apparent from the complaint, the defense would not support dismissal and the case should proceed to discovery.
