Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### Why does the "reasonable observer" standard control transformative use instead of the creator's intent?
Question:
Santos argues that Kimmel "instigated the creation" of the Cameo videos and is therefore the "designer" of their original purpose, making Kimmel's use non-transformative. The court rejects this, citing Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith for the principle that "the subjective intent of the user... [does not] determine the purpose of the use." Instead, the court applies a "reasonable observer" standard. I'm confused: Santos says the videos were meant to convey "hope, strength, perseverance," while Kimmel used them to mock Santos's willingness to "say absurd things for money." Why does the reasonable observer's perception of the secondary use matter more than either party's intent? What is the theoretical justification for this objective standard in transformative use analysis?
Answer:
## Why the Reasonable Observer Standard Controls Transformative Use

The short answer is that transformative use is fundamentally about **how the secondary work functions in context**, not about what either party privately intended. The court adopts an objective standard because the transformative use inquiry asks whether the secondary work "adds something new, with a further purpose or different character" compared to the original—and that question is answered by examining the works themselves, not by probing the mental states of the people who made or used them.

### The Problem with Subjective Intent

Santos's argument illustrates exactly why an objective standard is necessary. He claims that Kimmel "instigated the creation" of the videos and thus designed their original purpose, and that his own purpose in making them was the same as Kimmel's purpose in airing them (commentary on his willingness to say absurd things for money). If subjective intent controlled, Santos could always defeat a transformative use defense by claiming his intent matched the secondary user's. Conversely, a secondary user could always claim transformative intent regardless of what they actually did with the work. The inquiry would collapse into competing assertions about hidden mental states, which courts are poorly equipped to resolve and which would produce unpredictable, unadministerable results.

The Supreme Court flagged this problem directly in *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023), which the Second Circuit quotes here: "the subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use." The point is that purpose, for fair use purposes, is something discernible from the work and its context—not something locked inside someone's head.

### How the Standard Operates Here

The court's application makes the mechanics clear. Santos alleged in his own complaint that the videos conveyed "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." The District Court found that Kimmel's purpose was "to comment on the willingness of Santos... to say absurd things for money." Santos does not dispute that finding—his argument is that this was *also* his original purpose.

But the court says that is irrelevant. What matters is what a reasonable observer would perceive looking at the original videos and then at the JKL segments. A reasonable observer would see the original Cameo videos as conveying hope and encouragement (as Santos himself alleged). That same observer would see the JKL segments as mocking Santos's willingness to say absurd things for money. Because the secondary use presents a **different purpose and character** from what the original work communicates to an observer, the use is transformative. Santos's private claim that he secretly intended the same thing Kimmel did cannot override what the works actually convey.

### The Theoretical Justification

The theoretical foundation is that copyright protects **expression**, not intention. The fair use doctrine exists to serve the constitutional purpose of promoting the progress of knowledge and culture. The transformative use inquiry asks whether a secondary use contributes something new to public discourse—whether it serves a different function in the marketplace of ideas than the original. That contribution is measured by how the work operates when presented to an audience, because that is how works actually function in culture and commerce. A use that is transformative in effect promotes the goals of copyright regardless of whether the secondary user subjectively intended commentary, and a use that merely supersedes the original does not become transformative just because the user says it was meant to.

By focusing on the reasonable observer's perception, the standard keeps the inquiry grounded in something courts can actually evaluate: the works themselves and their observable context. That is why the court looks to what the videos communicate, not to what Santos or Kimmel may have intended them to communicate.
