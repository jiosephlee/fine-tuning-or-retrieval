Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 1: # Case Background, Facts, and Procedural Posture

## The Parties

This case involves a dispute between former Congressman George Santos and several defendants connected to the late-night television program *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* ("JKL"). The primary defendant is Jimmy Kimmel, the host of the show. Also named as defendants are American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. ("ABC"), the network that airs JKL, and The Walt Disney Company, ABC's parent company. Together, these parties form the basis of a dispute that spans copyright law, contract law, and fraud—though, as we will see, the legal questions resolve largely at the pleading stage.

## The Cameo Platform and Kimmel's Use of Fictitious Names

The facts giving rise to this dispute center on the Cameo platform. Cameo is a service through which users can pay celebrities or public figures to record short, personalized video messages. In this case, Kimmel, using various fictitious names, submitted requests to Santos for personalized videos through the Cameo platform. Santos fulfilled each of these requests, apparently without knowing that the requests originated with Kimmel or that the videos would be used on a late-night comedy show. This factual backdrop—Kimmel's use of false identities to solicit the videos—is important because Santos later argued that Kimmel's misrepresentations should undermine the fair use defense and support state law claims for fraudulent inducement. As the opinion explains, however, these arguments ultimately did not succeed.

## The "Will Santos Say It?" Segments

After Santos recorded and delivered the personalized videos, Kimmel aired them on JKL as part of a series of segments titled "Will Santos Say It?" These segments were mocking in nature, presenting the videos as part of a comedic commentary on Santos. The District Court characterized the purpose of the allegedly infringing use as "to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money." Santos himself did not dispute this characterization of the segments' purpose, though he argued that this purpose was also his original purpose in making the videos—an argument the Second Circuit rejected. The comedic and critical character of the segments is central to the court's fair use analysis, which we will examine in detail in the next chapter.

## Santos's Copyright Registrations

Santos registered the videos with the Copyright Office. The copyright registrations establish that the videos were published on the same day they were created—a fact relevant to the second fair use factor, which considers the nature of the copyrighted work. The Second Circuit took judicial notice of these registrations, noting that courts may take judicial notice of "copyright registrations as published in the Copyright Office's registry." This procedural point is important: even at the pleading stage, when a court evaluates a motion to dismiss, it may look to publicly available copyright registrations to inform its analysis without converting the motion into one for summary judgment.

## Santos's Claims

Santos brought four claims. First, he asserted a claim for copyright infringement, arguing that Kimmel and the other defendants' unauthorized airing of the videos on JKL infringed his exclusive rights under the Copyright Act. Second, he brought a claim for breach of contract, alleging that Kimmel's use of the videos violated the Cameo Terms of Service to which users must agree when using the platform. Third, he asserted a claim for breach of implied contract, arguing that an implied-in-fact contract existed between himself and the defendants that was breached when the videos were aired on JKL. Fourth, he brought a claim for fraudulent inducement, arguing that Kimmel's use of fictitious names to request the videos constituted fraud that induced Santos into creating and delivering the videos.

These claims arise under different bodies of law: the copyright infringement claim is a federal claim under the Copyright Act, while the remaining three claims are state law claims. The distinction between federal and state law claims matters because the Copyright Act can, in certain circumstances, preempt state law claims that are "equivalent" to claims for copyright infringement—a question the Second Circuit noted but did not need to resolve, as it found the state law claims failed for independent reasons.

## The District Court's Dismissal Under Rule 12(b)(6)

The United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.) dismissed Santos's complaint in its entirety under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. Rule 12(b)(6) allows a defendant to move to dismiss a complaint for "failure to state a claim upon which relief can be granted." At this stage, the court does not weigh evidence or make factual findings. Instead, it accepts the factual allegations in the complaint as true and asks whether those allegations, if proven, would entitle the plaintiff to legal relief.

The District Court concluded that Santos's copyright claims were barred by the fair use doctrine, which serves as an affirmative defense to copyright infringement. The court also concluded that Santos's state law claims either were preempted by the Copyright Act or independently failed to state a claim under state law. The Second Circuit affirmed on slightly different reasoning: it agreed that the copyright claims were barred by fair use and that each of the state law claims failed to state a claim, but it did not need to reach the preemption question because the state law claims failed for other reasons.

## Standard of Review on Appeal

The Second Circuit reviews a district court's dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) de novo, meaning the court of appeals independently assesses the complaint without deferring to the district court's reasoning. In conducting this review, the court considers "the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference in the complaint." This means the appellate court's analysis is confined to the four corners of the complaint and certain closely related materials.

In addition to the complaint itself, the court may take judicial notice of certain publicly available facts. Here, the court took judicial notice of Santos's copyright registrations as published in the Copyright Office's registry. This is significant because the registrations contained information—specifically, the publication dates of the videos—that was relevant to the fair use analysis. The ability to consider such materials at the pleading stage allows courts to resolve certain issues, including fair use, even before any factual discovery has occurred. Indeed, the Second Circuit noted that fair use "may be so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim" at the pleading stage, a point that proved central to the outcome of this case.
