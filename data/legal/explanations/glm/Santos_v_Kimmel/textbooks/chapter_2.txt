Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

Chapter 2: # Copyright Infringement and the Fair Use Defense

## Fair Use as a Flexible Affirmative Defense Under the Copyright Act

Former Congressman George Santos's appeal required the Second Circuit to address a fundamental question in copyright law: when does the unauthorized use of a copyrighted work constitute infringement, and when is it excused as "fair use"? The fair use doctrine operates as an affirmative defense to copyright infringement. As the court explained, it "excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior, allowing courts to avoid rigid application of the Copyright Act when it would stifle the very creativity the Act is meant to promote." This formulation captures the tension at the heart of copyright law. The Copyright Act grants creators exclusive rights in their works, but those rights are not absolute. Without some mechanism for flexibility, the enforcement of copyright could paradoxically suppress the very expressive and creative activity the law is designed to foster.

The court emphasized that fair use is "a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context." This flexibility is what allowed the court to evaluate Kimmel's use of Santos's videos—a use that involved deception in its procurement but served a commentary function in its execution—under a doctrine designed to accommodate a wide range of secondary uses. The court's discussion makes clear that fair use is not a narrow technical exemption but a broad, context-sensitive standard that courts apply by weighing multiple factors.

## The Four Fair Use Factors Under 17 U.S.C. § 107

The Copyright Act sets out four non-exclusive factors that courts consider in determining whether fair use has been established. Under 17 U.S.C. § 107, these are: (1) the purpose and character of the use; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. The court noted that these factors are "non-exclusive," meaning courts may also consider other relevant circumstances, though the four statutory factors provide the core analytical framework.

The court's opinion in this case focused primarily on the first and fourth factors, consistent with the general observation that these two factors often carry the greatest weight in fair use analysis. The second factor received briefer treatment, and the third factor was not separately addressed in the court's reasoning. This selective treatment is itself instructive: the factors are not a mechanical checklist in which each must receive equal attention, but rather a set of considerations that courts weigh together, with some factors taking on greater significance depending on the facts of the case.

## First Factor: Purpose and Character of the Use and the Transformative Use Test

The first fair use factor—the "purpose and character of the use"—asks whether the secondary user has done something with the copyrighted work that adds new meaning, message, or purpose. The court explained that in assessing this factor, it "focus[es] chiefly on the degree to which the use is transformative, i.e., whether the new work merely supersedes the objects of the original creation, or instead adds something new, with a further purpose or different character." The key distinction is between a use that merely replaces the original work and one that gives it a new function or character.

In this case, the District Court had found that the purpose of Kimmel's use was "to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money." Santos did not dispute this characterization of the secondary use's purpose. The transformative nature of the use was therefore apparent: Santos created the videos to convey personalized messages of encouragement, while Kimmel aired them as part of a comedic segment that mocked Santos's willingness to say outrageous things for payment. The purpose of the secondary use was fundamentally different from, and did not supersede, the purpose of the original creation.

## Santos's Argument That Kimmel "Instigated" the Creation of the Videos and Was Their "Designer"

Santos advanced a creative argument to resist the conclusion that Kimmel's use was transformative. He maintained that Kimmel "instigated the creation of the original Works" by submitting the video requests through Cameo using fictitious names, and that Kimmel was therefore the "designer" of the videos' original purpose. Under this theory, because Kimmel conceived of the videos' purpose from the outset, the use could not be transformative—Kimmel was simply using the videos for the very purpose he had designed them to serve.

This argument reflects an attempt to collapse the distinction between the original work and the secondary use by tracing the origin of the original work back to the secondary user. If Kimmel was the architect of the videos' purpose, Santos argued, then Kimmel's later use of the videos on his show was not a transformation of that purpose but a continuation of it. The argument has a certain logical appeal: when the secondary user procures the creation of the original work, the usual assumption that the original creator's purpose and the secondary user's purpose are different may seem unsettled.

## The Objective Standard for Transformative Use: The Perspective of a Reasonable Observer vs. Subjective Intent

The court rejected Santos's instigation argument, relying on a critical principle of fair use analysis: the transformative character of a use is assessed from the perspective of a reasonable observer, not from the subjective intent of either the copyright holder or the secondary user. Citing the Supreme Court's decision in *Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508 (2023), the court explained that "the subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use."

This objective standard is significant for several reasons. First, it means that a copyright holder cannot defeat a fair use defense simply by claiming that the secondary user's purpose was identical to the original purpose. Second, it means that a secondary user cannot defeat the defense by claiming subjective transformative intent if an objective observer would not perceive the use as transformative. The inquiry is about what the use communicates and accomplishes, not about what was in the minds of the parties.

Applying this standard, the court found that Santos's own complaint undermined his argument. Santos had alleged that the videos conveyed "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." A reasonable observer, the court concluded, would understand the videos as carrying this message—not as communicating a willingness to say absurd things for money. Because Kimmel's use on JKL conveyed a different message—commentary and mockery of that willingness—the use was transformative regardless of who instigated the creation of the videos.

## Santos's Bad Faith Argument Based on Kimmel's False Representations and Harper & Row

Santos raised a separate challenge under the first fair use factor, arguing that Kimmel's false representations—his use of fictitious names to submit the Cameo requests—demonstrated bad faith and should nullify the fair use defense. In support, Santos cited *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises*, 471 U.S. 539 (1985), in which the Supreme Court stated that "[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing."

The argument had surface plausibility. Kimmel had, after all, deceived Santos by using false identities to procure the videos. If fair use requires good faith, Santos's argument went, then Kimmel's deception should disqualify him from invoking the defense. This argument attempted to import a moral dimension into the fair use analysis—using the doctrine's flexibility to account for the equities of how the copyrighted work was obtained.

## The Court's Rejection of the Bad Faith Argument and the Lack of Market Usurpation

The court rejected Santos's bad faith argument for two reasons. First, the court observed that the Supreme Court has "distanced itself" from the *Harper & Row* statement that fair use presupposes good faith. This is an important point for students to note: Supreme Court language about good faith in fair use, once articulated, has not been reaffirmed and has effectively been narrowed or abandoned in subsequent jurisprudence. The court did not elaborate extensively on this point, but the citation signals that Santos's argument relied on precedent that no longer carries the weight it once may have had.

Second, and more substantively, the court looked at what Santos's complaint actually alleged about the purpose of Kimmel's use. The complaint, the court observed, painted "a portrait of defendants motivated by (sarcastic) criticism and commentary." Citing *Swatch Group Management Services Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P.*, 756 F.3d 73 (2d Cir. 2014), the court explained that the relevant concern is whether the secondary user acted to "supplant" the copyright holder's "commercially valuable right" in the work. Santos's complaint did not allege that Kimmel intended to supplant Santos's commercial rights in the videos; it alleged that Kimmel intended to mock and criticize Santos. Citing *Authors Guild v. Google, Inc.*, 804 F.3d 202 (2d Cir. 2015), the court recognized that uses motivated by criticism and commentary are consistent with fair use, even when the procurement of the work involved deception.

The court therefore agreed with the District Court that the first factor "strongly supports a finding of fair use." The combination of a transformative purpose and the absence of any intent to supplant the original work's commercial value—despite the deception involved in obtaining the videos—made the first factor decisively favorable to Kimmel.

## Second Factor: Nature of the Copyrighted Work and the Publication Timing of the Videos

The second fair use factor—the "nature of the copyrighted work"—considers whether the work is factual or creative, and whether it is published or unpublished, among other characteristics. The court's treatment of this factor was brief but turned on a specific factual point established by the copyright registrations. The court took judicial notice of the copyright registrations, as published in the Copyright Office's registry, which established that the videos "were published on the same day they were created."

This timing matters because the fair use analysis draws a distinction between published and unpublished works. Unpublished works generally receive greater protection under the second factor, since the copyright holder has a strong interest in controlling the first public appearance of their work. Here, however, the videos were published simultaneously with their creation, meaning they were already available to the public when Kimmel used them. This factual finding, established by the copyright registrations themselves, undercut any argument that the second factor weighed against fair use on the ground that the works were unpublished.

## Fourth Factor: Market Harm—Usurpation of a Market vs. Criticism or Parody Under Campbell and Hachette

The fourth fair use factor examines "the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work." The court clarified the proper framing of this inquiry, explaining that the question is not whether the secondary work damages the market for the original—through, for example, parody or criticism that devalues it—but whether the secondary work "usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute." This distinction is critical. A use that criticizes or parodies a copyrighted work may well reduce its market value, but that is not the kind of harm the fourth factor is designed to capture. The factor targets uses that substitute for the original work in its existing or potential markets.

The court cited *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.*, 510 U.S. 569 (1994), for the proposition that "the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market." In other words, if the copyright holder would never have licensed the kind of use at issue—because creators do not typically authorize parodies or critical lampoons of their own work—then the secondary use has not usurped a potential market. Santos could not claim that Kimmel's mocking use had deprived him of a licensing market for parody or criticism of his own videos, because such a market is not realistically available to a copyright holder.

The court concluded that Santos had not suffered market harm within the meaning of the fourth factor. Kimmel's segments on JKL did not offer a competing substitute for Santos's Cameo videos. Someone who wanted a personalized video message from Santos would not substitute a JKL clip of Santos being mocked. The uses served different functions entirely.

## Fair Use as a Basis for Dismissal at the Pleading Stage Under TCA Television Corp.

A notable feature of this case is that the fair use defense was evaluated and upheld at the pleading stage—on a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6)—rather than after a developed factual record at summary judgment or trial. The court explained that fair use "may be so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim" at the pleading stage, citing *TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum*, 839 F.3d 168 (2d Cir. 2016).

This is an important procedural point. Fair use is often thought of as a fact-intensive inquiry that resists early resolution. But the court's analysis demonstrates that when the facts alleged in the complaint itself establish the elements of fair use, a court may resolve the defense as a matter of law at the motion-to-dismiss stage. The court noted that it needed only to consider "the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue" to decide the question of fair use, citing *Cariou v. Prince*, 714 F.3d 694 (2d Cir. 2013). Both the original works and the allegedly infringing uses were described in the complaint and were available for comparison. The transformative purpose was evident from Santos's own allegations about the videos' message and the District Court's characterization of Kimmel's purpose, which Santos did not dispute. The market harm analysis turned on the nature of the use as commentary rather than substitution, a conclusion that followed from the face of the complaint.

## Affirmance of the District Court's Dismissal of the Copyright Claims

Having addressed Santos's challenges to the District Court's analysis of the fair use factors, the court affirmed the dismissal of Santos's copyright claims. The first factor strongly supported fair use because Kimmel's use was transformative—adding a new purpose of sarcastic commentary—and Santos's bad faith argument failed both because the Supreme Court had distanced itself from the *Harper & Row* good faith language and because the complaint did not allege that Kimmel intended to supplant Santos's commercial rights. The second factor did not weigh against fair use because the videos were published on the same day they were created. The fourth factor favored fair use because Kimmel's use did not usurp Santos's market but instead offered criticism and parody, the kind of use that falls outside the notion of a potential licensing market under *Campbell*.

The court's analysis illustrates how the fair use doctrine, with its flexible, multi-factor approach, can resolve copyright disputes even at the pleading stage when the relevant facts are clear from the complaint itself. The court's opinion teaches that the doctrine's flexibility is not a license for unconstrained judicial discretion but a structured framework that, applied carefully, can distinguish between uses that supplant the original work and uses that transform it into something new.
