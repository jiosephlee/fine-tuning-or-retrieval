Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

### How can fair use be decided on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion without a factual record?
Question:
In Santos v. Kimmel, the Second Circuit affirms dismissal of the copyright infringement claim at the pleading stage, stating that fair use may be "so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal." But fair use involves a four-factor test that seems fact-intensive—transformative use, nature of the work, amount used, and market harm all seem to require evidence. How can a court evaluate these factors when all it has is the complaint? Is there something special about fair use that makes it different from other affirmative defenses at the motion-to-dismiss stage? When is it appropriate to dismiss on fair use grounds versus letting it go to discovery?
Answer:
## Fair Use at the Pleading Stage: The Complaint Itself Can Supply the Necessary Facts

There is nothing unique to fair use that allows it to be resolved on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion. The same principle that governs other affirmative defenses applies: **when the complaint's own allegations establish the defense, dismissal is appropriate.** What makes fair use amenable to early resolution in some cases is that the comparison between the original work and the secondary use—central to the transformative use and market harm analyses—can often be conducted by looking at the works themselves, which may be attached to or incorporated by reference in the complaint.

### The Governing Standard

The Second Circuit has held that fair use "may be so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim" at the pleading stage. In conducting this analysis, the court considers "the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference in the complaint," and may take judicial notice of copyright registrations. The critical point is that the court need only consider "the original version of [the works] and the [secondary works] at issue" to decide the question of fair use. If those materials are before the court at the motion-to-dismiss stage, the four-factor analysis can proceed.

### How the Factors Were Resolved Here

The complaint itself supplied the facts necessary to evaluate the first and fourth factors, which the court found dispositive.

**Factor One (Purpose and Character of the Use):** Santos's own allegations described his videos as conveying "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." The JKL segments, by contrast, used the videos "to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money." Because the transformative use inquiry turns on what a reasonable observer would perceive—not the subjective intent of either party—the court could compare these two purposes directly from the face of the complaint and conclude the use was transformative.

Santos's argument that Kimmel's bad faith (using false names to solicit the videos) nullified fair use was rejected. The court noted that the Supreme Court has "distanced itself" from the *Harper & Row* good-faith requirement, and that the complaint itself portrayed the defendants as motivated by "sarcastic criticism and commentary," not by an intent to supplant Santos's commercial market.

**Factor Four (Market Harm):** This factor was also resolvable on the face of the complaint because the relevant question is not whether criticism devalues the original, but whether the secondary use "usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute." The court observed that "the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market." Nothing in the complaint suggested Kimmel's segments served as a substitute for Santos's Cameo videos.

### When Dismissal Is Appropriate Versus Discovery

The lesson is procedural, not substantive: fair use can be decided at the pleading stage **when the complaint and accompanying materials establish the facts needed to evaluate the four factors without genuine dispute.** Here, the plaintiff's own description of his works' purpose, combined with the obviously commentary-driven nature of the JKL segments, meant no factual development was necessary. Where the transformative nature of the use, the amount taken, or market effects would depend on evidence not apparent from the complaint, the defense would not support dismissal and the case should proceed to discovery.

### Why does the "reasonable observer" standard control transformative use instead of the creator's intent?
Question:
Santos argues that Kimmel "instigated the creation" of the Cameo videos and is therefore the "designer" of their original purpose, making Kimmel's use non-transformative. The court rejects this, citing Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith for the principle that "the subjective intent of the user... [does not] determine the purpose of the use." Instead, the court applies a "reasonable observer" standard. I'm confused: Santos says the videos were meant to convey "hope, strength, perseverance," while Kimmel used them to mock Santos's willingness to "say absurd things for money." Why does the reasonable observer's perception of the secondary use matter more than either party's intent? What is the theoretical justification for this objective standard in transformative use analysis?
Answer:
## Why the Reasonable Observer Standard Controls Transformative Use

The short answer is that transformative use is fundamentally about **how the secondary work functions in context**, not about what either party privately intended. The court adopts an objective standard because the transformative use inquiry asks whether the secondary work "adds something new, with a further purpose or different character" compared to the original—and that question is answered by examining the works themselves, not by probing the mental states of the people who made or used them.

### The Problem with Subjective Intent

Santos's argument illustrates exactly why an objective standard is necessary. He claims that Kimmel "instigated the creation" of the videos and thus designed their original purpose, and that his own purpose in making them was the same as Kimmel's purpose in airing them (commentary on his willingness to say absurd things for money). If subjective intent controlled, Santos could always defeat a transformative use defense by claiming his intent matched the secondary user's. Conversely, a secondary user could always claim transformative intent regardless of what they actually did with the work. The inquiry would collapse into competing assertions about hidden mental states, which courts are poorly equipped to resolve and which would produce unpredictable, unadministerable results.

The Supreme Court flagged this problem directly in *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023), which the Second Circuit quotes here: "the subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use." The point is that purpose, for fair use purposes, is something discernible from the work and its context—not something locked inside someone's head.

### How the Standard Operates Here

The court's application makes the mechanics clear. Santos alleged in his own complaint that the videos conveyed "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." The District Court found that Kimmel's purpose was "to comment on the willingness of Santos... to say absurd things for money." Santos does not dispute that finding—his argument is that this was *also* his original purpose.

But the court says that is irrelevant. What matters is what a reasonable observer would perceive looking at the original videos and then at the JKL segments. A reasonable observer would see the original Cameo videos as conveying hope and encouragement (as Santos himself alleged). That same observer would see the JKL segments as mocking Santos's willingness to say absurd things for money. Because the secondary use presents a **different purpose and character** from what the original work communicates to an observer, the use is transformative. Santos's private claim that he secretly intended the same thing Kimmel did cannot override what the works actually convey.

### The Theoretical Justification

The theoretical foundation is that copyright protects **expression**, not intention. The fair use doctrine exists to serve the constitutional purpose of promoting the progress of knowledge and culture. The transformative use inquiry asks whether a secondary use contributes something new to public discourse—whether it serves a different function in the marketplace of ideas than the original. That contribution is measured by how the work operates when presented to an audience, because that is how works actually function in culture and commerce. A use that is transformative in effect promotes the goals of copyright regardless of whether the secondary user subjectively intended commentary, and a use that merely supersedes the original does not become transformative just because the user says it was meant to.

By focusing on the reasonable observer's perception, the standard keeps the inquiry grounded in something courts can actually evaluate: the works themselves and their observable context. That is why the court looks to what the videos communicate, not to what Santos or Kimmel may have intended them to communicate.

### What happened to the good faith requirement for fair use after Harper & Row?
Question:
Santos argues that Kimmel's use of fictitious names to request the Cameo videos constitutes bad faith, which should negate the fair use defense, citing Harper & Row's statement that "fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing." The Second Circuit says the Supreme Court has "since distanced itself from this statement" but doesn't cite specific cases showing how. What cases retreated from the good faith requirement? Is bad faith now completely irrelevant to fair use, or does it still matter in some circumstances? The court seems to reframe the inquiry as whether the use "supplants" the copyright holder's rights—is that now the operative test instead of good faith?
Answer:
## The opinion doesn't identify specific cases, but shows how the Second Circuit now handles the issue

The Second Circuit's assertion that the Supreme Court has "distanced itself" from *Harper & Row*'s good faith statement is made without citation to specific cases. The opinion doesn't identify which cases did the distancing. What it does instead is show how the court now treats the concern that animated the good faith requirement—preventing users from taking unfair advantage of the copyright holder—by folding it into the existing fair use factors.

## The "supplant" inquiry is the operative approach, not a standalone good faith test

After noting the Supreme Court's distancing, the court says "In any event" and pivots to whether the use "supplants" Santos's "commercially valuable right" in the videos, citing *Swatch Group Management Services Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P.* (2d Cir. 2014). This is the operative inquiry: whether the secondary use "merely supersedes the objects of the original creation, or instead adds something new, with a further purpose or different character" (quoting *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*, 2d Cir. 2021). The concern about bad faith is absorbed into the question of whether the use is transformative and whether it usurps the market for the original work.

The opinion supports this in two key ways:

1. **Subjective intent doesn't determine purpose.** The court quotes the Supreme Court's decision in *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023), for the proposition that "the subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use." This directly undercuts the premise that Kimmel's deceptive conduct in obtaining the videos should bear on the fair use analysis. The purpose is assessed from the perspective of a reasonable observer, not the user's subjective motivations.

2. **Market harm asks about substitution, not devaluation.** The court frames the fourth factor as asking "whether [the secondary use] usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute," not whether it damages the market through criticism or parody (citing *Hachette Book Group* and *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.*, 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994)). The court quotes *Campbell* for the principle that "the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market." This means the kind of "harm" that bad faith might cause—devaluing the original through mockery—is not the harm the fourth factor targets.

## Bad faith isn't completely irrelevant, but it's not a standalone bar

The opinion doesn't say bad faith can *never* matter. It says Santos's complaint "contradicts any claim of a purpose on the Defendants' part to 'supplant' Santos's 'commercially valuable right.'" The court then notes that the complaint instead "paints a portrait of defendants motivated by (sarcastic) criticism and commentary" (citing *Authors Guild v. Google, Inc.*, 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015)). This suggests that bad faith could theoretically still be relevant if it reflected a genuine attempt to supplant the copyright holder's market—i.e., if the deception were in service of producing a competing substitute rather than commentary. But standalone deception in obtaining the work, where the use is transformative and doesn't usurp the market, doesn't negate fair use.

In short: the Second Circuit treats the "supplant" inquiry as the operative test. The good faith requirement from *Harper & Row* has not been expressly overruled, but the Second Circuit treats it as subsumed within the existing fair use factors—particularly the first (transformative purpose, assessed objectively) and the fourth (market substitution, not mere devaluation).

### Why did the Second Circuit avoid the copyright preemption issue and decide contract claims on state law grounds instead?
Question:
The district court dismissed Santos's breach of contract and breach of implied contract claims as preempted by the Copyright Act. On appeal, Santos argues these claims are "qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim" and thus not preempted. The Second Circuit says it "need not resolve this issue" because the claims fail under state law anyway. What principle allows the court to affirm on alternative grounds without reaching the preemption question? Is this avoidance discretionary? Are there situations where a court must address preemption before reaching the merits of the state law claim?
Answer:
## Answer

The principle at work here is straightforward: **an appellate court may affirm on any basis supported by the record, and when a claim can be resolved on narrower grounds, the court should do so rather than reaching broader, unnecessary legal questions.** This is a routine application of judicial economy and the broader avoidance canon, not unique to copyright preemption.

The Second Circuit explicitly says it "**need not resolve**" the preemption question "because Santos's state law contract claims fail to state a claim for relief under state law." The logic is simple: if a claim fails on its own merits under state law, there is no live state-law cause of action left for federal copyright law to preempt. Resolving preemption would be an advisory opinion on a hypothetical—exactly what federal courts avoid.

### Is this avoidance discretionary?

Yes. The court *could* have reached the preemption question, but it chose the narrower path. This reflects a strong, well-established preference in federal appellate practice for deciding cases on the least sweeping basis available. The Supreme Court has repeatedly instructed that courts should not "formulate a rule of constitutional law broader than is required by the precise facts to which it is to be applied," and the same logic extends to federal preemption questions, which often have significant implications for the balance of federal and state regulatory authority.

Here, the court had before it two independent, sufficient reasons to affirm dismissal of the contract claims: (1) potential Copyright Act preemption and (2) failure to state a claim under Illinois and New York law. Having concluded that Santos's claims fail under state contract law—for lack of third-party beneficiary status and failure to plead essential terms—the court had no occasion to decide whether the Copyright Act would also have barred them. The state-law deficiency is logically antecedent: a non-claim cannot be preempted.

### When must a court address preemption before the merits?

A court generally needs to address preemption first (or at least cannot skip it) in a few situations:

1. **When the state-law claim is potentially meritorious.** If Santos had plausibly pleaded all elements of a valid contract under state law, the court could not simply affirm dismissal without addressing preemption—preemption would be the only remaining basis for dismissal. The court avoids preemption here precisely *because* the state-law deficiency is dispositive on its own.

2. **When the preemption question is jurisdictional.** Some forms of preemption operate as a limit on subject-matter jurisdiction rather than as an affirmative defense. Copyright preemption under 17 U.S.C. § 301, however, is generally treated as an affirmative defense, not jurisdictional. Because it is not jurisdictional, the court is not obligated to resolve it before reaching the merits.

3. **When the state-law analysis depends on the scope of federal rights.** In some contexts, the elements of the state-law claim are intertwined with the scope of federal protection—such that you cannot evaluate the state claim without first determining what federal law covers. The Second Circuit's preemption test (whether the claim is "qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim," *Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio*, 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022)) can sometimes require this kind of preliminary inquiry. But here, the contract claims failed for ordinary pleading deficiencies—failure to identify essential terms, failure to establish third-party beneficiary status—that had nothing to do with the reach of copyright protection. The state-law analysis was self-contained.

### Bottom line

The court applied the unremarkable principle that **federal courts should decide the narrowest question necessary to resolve the dispute.** Because Santos's contract claims failed under state law for independent reasons, the preemption question was moot and the court properly declined to reach it. This is discretionary in the sense that the court *could* have decided the preemption issue, but the preference for avoidance is strong and, where the state-law ground is independently sufficient and logically prior, declining to reach preemption is the standard and prudent course.

### How does the third-party beneficiary analysis work when some contract provisions have express third-party language but others don't?
Question:
Santos tries to enforce the Cameo Terms of Service as a third-party beneficiary, but the court applies Illinois law's "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." The court notes that other provisions of the Terms of Service "do contain third-party beneficiary language," suggesting that when the parties wanted third parties to enforce provisions, they said so expressly—and the absence of such language in the provisions Santos relies on means he can't enforce them. Is this a recognized canon of contract interpretation? How does this negative inference interact with the requirement that the implication must be "so strong as to be practically an express declaration"?
Answer:
## The Negative Inference Is a Recognized Canon, and It Reinforces—Rather Than Competes With—the "Practically an Express Declaration" Standard

Yes. What the court is doing is a well-established application of **_expressio unius est exclusio alterius_**—the expression of one thing implies the exclusion of others—in the third-party beneficiary context. The principle is straightforward: when parties include specific language in some provisions of a contract but omit it from others, courts infer that the omission was deliberate. This is sometimes called a "negative inference" or "differential treatment" canon, and it is widely recognized in contract interpretation. The idea is that parties who demonstrated they knew how to draft express third-party beneficiary language when they wanted it are unlikely to have intended the same result silently in other provisions.

## How the Canon Works in This Case

The court's reasoning proceeds in two steps. First, it states the governing standard: under Illinois law, there is a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties," and "the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration" (quoting *Barry v. St. Mary's Hosp. Decatur*). Second, the court notes that "other provisions of the Terms of Service do contain third-party beneficiary language, indicating that when the parties to that agreement intended to allow provisions of the contract to be enforced by third parties, they said so expressly." The presence of express third-party language elsewhere in the same agreement makes it implausible that the parties intended to confer third-party enforcement rights in the provisions Santos relies on—because if they had, they knew how to say so and presumably would have.

## How the Negative Inference Interacts with the "Practically an Express Declaration" Standard

The two principles work together rather than in tension. The "practically an express declaration" standard already sets a very high bar: the implication of intent to benefit a third party must be so strong that it is, for all practical purposes, the equivalent of an express statement. The negative inference from express third-party language in other provisions serves as **evidence within that test**, making it even harder to clear the threshold.

The logic is that if the implication were truly "so strong as to be practically an express declaration," one would expect the parties—who demonstrably knew how to include express third-party beneficiary language when they wanted it—to have included such language in the provisions at issue. Their failure to do so, against the backdrop of having included it elsewhere, is strong evidence that no such intent existed. The negative inference thus does not replace the "practically an express declaration" standard; it supplies a powerful reason why the standard cannot be satisfied on the facts before the court.

To put it slightly differently: the "practically an express declaration" test asks whether implied intent is strong enough to substitute for express intent. The negative inference answers that question by pointing to the parties' own drafting conduct—showing that where they wanted third-party enforcement, they made it express. This makes it very difficult to argue that the same parties intended the same result by implication in provisions where they chose not to include the language.

## Why This Is Not a Tension but a Convergence

One might initially think there is friction: the standard contemplates that sufficiently strong *implied* intent can substitute for express language, while the negative inference seems to say "if they meant it, they would have said so expressly." But the negative inference does not categorically foreclose implied third-party beneficiary status. It simply makes it exceedingly difficult to satisfy the already-exacting standard when the parties' own contract demonstrates a pattern of expressing such intent expressly. The canon and the standard point in the same direction: both demand near-express clarity, and the parties' own drafting choices are the best evidence of whether that clarity exists.

In short, the court's analysis is a straightforward and well-established application of a recognized canon. The presence of express third-party beneficiary language in some provisions and its absence in the ones Santos relies on is evidence that the parties did not intend third-party enforcement of those provisions—evidence that, combined with Illinois's strong presumption against third-party beneficiaries, makes it impossible to conclude that the implication was "so strong as to be practically an express declaration."

### Why doesn't performing services in reliance on fraud constitute "out-of-pocket loss" under New York law?
Question:
Santos's fraudulent inducement claim was dismissed because he failed to allege "out-of-pocket loss" as required under New York law. But Santos created and delivered personalized videos based on Kimmel's false representations about who he was and the intended use. He gave something of value—his performance and likeness—based on the fraud. Why doesn't providing the videos constitute an out-of-pocket loss? Is it because he was paid through Cameo? Would the difference between what he was paid and what he would have charged had he known the true purpose qualify as out-of-pocket loss?
Answer:
## The Out-of-Pocket Rule and Why Paid Services Don't Qualify

Under New York law, fraudulent inducement requires a plaintiff to allege **actual out-of-pocket loss**—the difference between the value of what the plaintiff gave and the value of what the plaintiff received in return. The opinion cites *Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.*, 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996), for this requirement, and *Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc.*, 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017), for the principle that the plaintiff must have suffered "actual out-of-pocket loss as the victim of the alleged fraud."

This is the key: New York follows the **out-of-pocket** rule for fraud damages, not the benefit-of-the-bargain rule. The question isn't whether Santos got what he *thought* he was bargaining for. The question is whether he lost money—whether the value of what he parted with exceeded the value of what he got back.

Here, Santos was compensated through the Cameo platform for each video he produced. He provided services, but he also received payment for those services. The mere act of performing services in reliance on fraud does not, by itself, constitute an out-of-pocket loss when the plaintiff was paid for the work. If the compensation received equals or exceeds the value of the services rendered, there is no net pecuniary loss. Santos's complaint apparently contained no allegations establishing that the value of what he gave (the videos) exceeded the value of what he received (his Cameo payment), so he failed to plead the required element.

## The "What I Would Have Charged" Theory

Your instinct about the difference between what Santos was paid and what he would have charged had he known the true purpose is the right analytical move—and it's the closest thing to a viable out-of-pocket theory here. If Santos had alleged that the fair market value of his services, given the true purpose of the use (being mocked on national television), exceeded what he was actually paid, that gap could in principle constitute an out-of-pocket loss: he gave something worth *X* and received something worth less than *X* in return because of the fraud.

But the opinion says Santos "failed to allege that he suffered any actual out-of-pocket loss." This tells us Santos's complaint didn't contain the factual allegations needed to make that theory work. On a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, the court evaluates only what the plaintiff has actually pleaded. If Santos didn't allege facts showing that the compensation he received was less than the fair value of his services under the true circumstances, the claim fails—regardless of whether such a theory might have been available to him.

## The Bottom Line

Providing the videos isn't an out-of-pocket loss by itself because Santos was paid for them. Under New York's out-of-pocket rule, fraud damages require a showing of net pecuniary harm—the value of the consideration given minus the value of the consideration received. Santos didn't allege facts showing that gap, so the claim was properly dismissed.

### How does the fourth fair use factor distinguish between "damaging" a market and "usurping" it?
Question:
The court quotes Hachette Book Group for the principle that the fourth fair use factor asks "not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute." I'm struggling with this distinction. If Kimmel's segments made Santos's original videos less valuable by mocking them, why isn't that market harm? The court also cites Campbell v. Acuff-Rose for the idea that creators won't license "critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions"—does this mean parody and criticism can essentially never cause cognizable market harm under the fourth factor?
Answer:
The distinction turns on whether the secondary use serves as a **substitute** for the original in the marketplace, not merely whether it makes the original less attractive or valuable.

The fourth factor targets what economists would call market substitution. The court, quoting *Hachette Book Group*, frames the inquiry as whether the second work "usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute." The concern is that a potential consumer of the original work would instead consume the secondary work, thereby depriving the copyright holder of a sale or license they would otherwise have obtained. That is market usurpation.

Market "damage," by contrast, covers a broader category of effects that the fourth factor does **not** recognize as cognizable harm. A parody, critical review, or lampoon may well reduce the public's appetite for the original—people may find the original less appealing, less credible, or simply less interesting after seeing it mocked. But that kind of devaluation flows from the critical or transformative character of the secondary use itself. It is not the same thing as the secondary work stepping into the commercial role the original occupies. As the court notes, Kimmel's segments on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* served the purpose of commentary and criticism on Santos's willingness to "say absurd things for money." Someone who watches those segments is not thereby satisfying a demand that Santos's original Cameo videos were meant to fulfill. The segments do not compete with the videos; they comment on them.

Your second question—whether *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose* means parody and criticism can *never* cause cognizable market harm—gets at a common point of confusion. The answer is no, but the doctrine does narrow what counts. *Campbell* stands for the proposition that when a secondary use is critical or lampooning in character, the copyright holder would not ordinarily license that kind of use, and so the "potential licensing market" for such uses is not one the fourth factor will protect. The court quotes *Campbell* directly: "the unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market." In other words, Santos cannot claim that Kimmel's segments deprived him of a licensing opportunity because Santos would never have licensed someone to mock him in the first place. That is not a market the law recognizes as his to lose.

This does not create an absolute immunity for parody or criticism under the fourth factor. The principle is narrower: a copyright holder cannot bootstrap criticism-induced devaluation into cognizable market harm by pointing to lost sales or lost licensing fees that were never realistically available. If, however, a work that purports to be parody or criticism actually functions in the marketplace as a substitute for the original—such that consumers treat it as a replacement rather than as commentary—that would still weigh against fair use. The fourth factor asks about the actual market relationship between the two works, not the label attached to the second one.

In *Santos*, the court's analysis is straightforward on this point: Kimmel's segments did not compete with Santos's Cameo videos. They did not offer personalized video messages to consumers. They offered televised comedy and commentary. Whatever reputational damage Santos may have suffered, the court concludes it was not market harm of the kind the fourth factor recognizes.
