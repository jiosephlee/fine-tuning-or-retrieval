Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# The "Instigation" Problem in Fair Use: Does Commissioning a Work Preclude Transformative Use?

When a late-night comedian tricks a disgraced former congressman into recording personalized videos—and then airs them for laughs—who owns the joke? The Second Circuit's decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* answers that question with a clean affirmative: fair use protects the parody, and the copyright holder cannot sue his way out of being the butt of it. But the opinion does more than dispatch a somewhat amusing lawsuit. It confronts a genuinely novel question in fair use doctrine: whether a secondary user who *instigates* the creation of a copyrighted work can still claim that a later use of that work is transformative. The court's answer—yes, and the inquiry is objective, not subjective—has implications that extend well beyond the world of Cameo videos and late-night television.

## The Factual Setup and the "Instigation" Argument

The facts are by now familiar. Jimmy Kimmel, using fictitious names, submitted requests through the Cameo platform to then-Congressman George Santos for personalized videos. Santos fulfilled the requests, apparently under the impression that the videos would convey "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity" to the fictitious recipients. Kimmel then aired the videos on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* as part of a series titled "Will Santos Say It?"—a comedic segment designed to mock Santos's apparent willingness to say absurd things for money. Santos sued for copyright infringement, among other claims, and the district court dismissed the case on fair use grounds at the pleading stage.

Santos's central argument on appeal was creative. He conceded that the purpose of Kimmel's use was to comment on Santos's willingness to say absurd things for money. But he argued that this was *also* his original purpose in making the videos—because Kimmel had "instigated" their creation through the fictitious Cameo requests. In other words, Santos contended that Kimmel was the "designer" of the videos' original purpose, and therefore the secondary use could not be transformative. The use, on this theory, merely superseded the object of the original creation rather than adding something new.

This is not a frivolous argument. If a secondary user commissions a work specifically so they can later use it in a transformative way, one might ask whether the "transformation" is real or merely theatrical. The secondary user, after all, controlled both sides of the transaction. There is something circular about commissioning a work for one purpose, then claiming your later use of it serves a *different* purpose. Santos was essentially arguing that Kimmel engineered the original purpose and therefore could not claim to have transformed it.

## The Objective Standard and the Rejection of "Instigation"

The Second Circuit rejected this argument, and its reasoning is doctrinally significant. The court held that "whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user," citing the Supreme Court's 2023 decision in *Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*. This is the key move. By focusing on the perspective of a reasonable observer, the court made Santos's instigation theory irrelevant as a matter of doctrine—regardless of its factual force.

The court noted that Santos's own complaint described the videos as conveying "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." A reasonable observer seeing the *JKL* segments, by contrast, would understand them as commentary on Santos's willingness to say absurd things for money. The two purposes are different. That the secondary user may have orchestrated the original creation does not change what a reasonable observer would perceive. The transformative use inquiry, in other words, is not about the genealogy of the work but about the contrast between the original and secondary uses as they appear to the audience.

This is a clean application of *Warhol*, which held that the purpose of a use must be assessed objectively rather than by reference to the subjective intent of either party. But *Santos* pushes the principle into new territory. In *Warhol*, the question was whether Lynn Goldsmith's photograph of Prince, commissioned for a magazine profile, was transformed when Andy Warhol created a series of silkscreen prints based on it. The Court emphasized that the purpose of Goldsmith's photograph—depicting Prince as he really was—differed from the purpose of Warhol's prints, which commented on celebrity and consumption. *Santos* presents a more unusual scenario: the secondary user *caused* the original work to exist in the first place, and did so under false pretenses. The court's answer—that this factual backdrop does not alter the objective transformative use analysis—clarifies that *Warhol*'s objective standard is robust and resistant to contextual complications.

One might wonder whether this is the right place to draw the line. There are contexts in which the relationship between the parties and the circumstances of creation seem relevant to whether a use is "fair." A photographer who commissions a portrait and then uses it in a way the subject would never have agreed to is in a different position from a comedian who tricks a public figure into recording a video. But the court's approach has the virtue of administrative simplicity. If the transformative use inquiry turned on the subjective intentions of the parties or the circumstances of the work's commission, fair use would become an even more fact-intensive inquiry than it already is—and one less amenable to resolution at the pleading stage.

## Bad Faith, Fictitious Names, and the Retreat from *Harper & Row*

Santos also argued that Kimmel's use of fictitious names to submit the Cameo requests demonstrated bad faith, which should nullify the fair use defense. This argument relied on the Supreme Court's statement in *Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises* that "fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing." The court's treatment of this argument is notable for what it reveals about the evolving status of the good faith requirement.

The Second Circuit observed that "the Court has since distanced itself from this statement" from *Harper & Row*. This is an understatement. The *Harper & Row* good faith language has been effectively moribund for years, and the Second Circuit has previously cabined its application to cases where the secondary user's conduct suggests an intent to "supplant" the copyright holder's "commercially valuable right" in the work. Here, the court found that Santos's own complaint portrayed the defendants as motivated by "sarcastic criticism and commentary"—not by a desire to usurp Santos's commercial market for the videos.

The court's framing is important. By framing the bad faith inquiry in terms of market supplantation rather than generic dishonesty, the court effectively reduces the good faith factor to a variant of the fourth fair use factor—market harm. Kimmel's deception in using fictitious names, however unseemly, did not suggest that he was trying to compete with Santos in the market for Cameo videos. He was trying to get material for a comedy segment. That the material was obtained through misrepresentation was, for fair use purposes, beside the point.

This is a sensible approach, but it leaves a gap. What if a secondary user obtains a copyrighted work through truly egregious deception—say, hacking into a server or bribing an employee—and then uses it in a way that is technically transformative? The court's reasoning suggests that even such conduct would not defeat fair use so long as the use does not supplant the copyright holder's market. Whether that is the right result is a question the Second Circuit did not have to answer, but it may come back.

## Fair Use at the Pleading Stage

Perhaps the most practically significant aspect of the decision is the court's willingness to affirm dismissal of the copyright claims on fair use grounds at the Rule 12(b)(6) stage. The court noted that fair use "may be so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim" at the pleading stage, citing its earlier decision in *TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum*. This is not a new proposition in the Second Circuit, but *Santos* reinforces it in a context where the facts are colorful enough that one might have expected the court to allow discovery.

The court's approach makes sense here. The complaint itself established the purpose of both the original works and the secondary use, the works were published on the same day they were created, and Santos could not allege market harm because the secondary use was not a competing substitute. When the complaint itself supplies the facts necessary to resolve the fair use inquiry, there is no reason to force the defendant to litigate through discovery.

But the pleading-stage fair use doctrine cuts both ways. It benefits defendants like Kimmel who can point to complaint allegations that themselves establish the transformative nature of the use. But it also risks locking plaintiffs out of fair use determinations that might benefit from factual development. In cases where the transformative nature of the use is less obvious from the face of the complaint, courts should be cautious about resolving fair use too early. *Santos* is an easy case on this dimension—the complaint virtually conceded the relevant facts—but not all cases will be so cooperative.

## The State Law Claims: A Brief Postscript

The court also dispatched Santos's state law claims with efficient brevity. The breach of contract claim failed because Santos was not a party to the Cameo Terms of Service and could not invoke them as a third-party beneficiary under Illinois law. The implied contract claim failed for failure to plead essential terms. And the fraudulent inducement claim failed because Santos did not allege any out-of-pocket loss, as New York law requires. None of these rulings breaks new doctrinal ground, but they collectively illustrate the difficulty of using state law claims to circumvent copyright doctrine—a point the court implicitly reinforced by declining to reach the preemption question.

## Implications and Downstream Effects

*Santos v. Kimmel* is, on one level, a case about a comedian and a congressman. But its doctrinal contributions are real. First, it confirms that *Warhol*'s objective standard for transformative use applies even in the unusual context where the secondary user commissioned the original work. The "instigation" theory, however clever, does not survive an objective inquiry. Second, it further marginalizes the *Harper & Row* good faith requirement, reorienting the inquiry toward market supplantation rather than moral character. Third, it reinforces the Second Circuit's willingness to resolve fair use at the pleading stage when the complaint supplies the necessary facts.

The instigation question is the most interesting of these, and it may arise again in less comedic contexts. Consider a documentary filmmaker who commissions a subject to record a series of interviews, then uses the footage in a film that is highly critical of the subject. Or a journalist who obtains access to a copyrighted work under false pretenses and then publishes a transformative analysis of it. In each case, the secondary user's role in bringing the work into existence might tempt a court to treat the use as non-transformative. *Santos* suggests that temptation should be resisted. The transformative use inquiry asks what a reasonable observer would see, not what the parties intended or how the work came to be. That is a principled position, and one that should provide useful guidance in future cases—whether or not they involve late-night television.



# Strategic Avoidance: The Second Circuit Sidesteps Copyright Preemption in Santos v. Kimmel

When a former congressman sues a late-night comedian over personalized Cameo videos aired for laughs, the temptation is to treat the case as farce. But *Santos v. Kimmel*, decided last week by the Second Circuit, raises a serious and recurring doctrinal question that the court chose not to answer: where does copyright preemption end and state law contract liability begin when creative works are at issue? The court's decision to resolve Santos's state law claims on narrow state-law grounds—while explicitly declining to address whether those claims were preempted by the Copyright Act—reflects a pragmatic judicial instinct. But it also leaves a doctrinal gap that litigants and lower courts will continue to struggle with.

## The Case and the Preemption Question

The facts are by now familiar. Jimmy Kimmel, using pseudonyms, commissioned personalized greeting videos from then-Congressman George Santos through the Cameo platform. Santos fulfilled the requests. Kimmel aired the videos on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* in a recurring segment called "Will Santos Say It?" Santos sued for copyright infringement and, relevant here, brought state law claims for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement.

The District Court (Cote, J.) dismissed the copyright claims on fair use grounds and concluded that Santos's contract claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act under 17 U.S.C. § 301(a) or independently failed to state a claim. On appeal, Santos argued that his contract claims were "qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim" and thus survived preemption under the Second Circuit's framework in *Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc.* (2d Cir. 2022).

The Second Circuit's response was striking in its brevity. After quoting the relevant standard from *Melendez*, Judge Lohier wrote: "We need not resolve this issue, however, because Santos's state law contract claims fail to state a claim for relief under state law." The court then devoted several paragraphs to explaining why Santos lacked standing to enforce Cameo's Terms of Service under Illinois law, failed to plead essential terms of an implied contract under New York law, and failed to allege out-of-pocket loss for his fraudulent inducement claim.

This is a perfectly defensible approach. Courts should generally decide cases on the narrowest available grounds, and the state law deficiencies in Santos's complaint were clear enough. But the avoidance has costs that deserve attention.

## The Doctrinal Vacuum

Copyright preemption under § 301(a) withdraws state law protection from claims that are "equivalent to any of the exclusive rights within the general scope of copyright" and that concern works "within the subject matter of copyright." The Second Circuit applies what it calls the "extra element" test: a state law claim is not preempted if it requires an extra element that makes it "qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim." Contract claims can survive preemption when they turn on a breach of an express or implied agreement that is independent of the rights protected by copyright—typically by involving obligations that go beyond mere reproduction, distribution, or public performance.

The difficulty, of course, is determining which contractual obligations qualify as "extra elements" and which are merely reformulated copyright claims. Must the contract impose obligations that are categorically different from the copyright bundle of rights, or is it enough that the claim sounds in contract rather than property? How specific must the contractual terms be to save a claim from preemption? These questions arise repeatedly in cases involving creative works—software licenses, musical performances, photograph usage rights, and now, apparently, personalized video recordings made through online platforms.

By declining to reach the preemption question, the Second Circuit missed an opportunity to clarify these boundaries. The *Santos* facts presented a relatively clean vehicle. Santos's contract claims were predicated on the creation and use of copyrighted works. The Cameo Terms of Service governed the relationship between the user (Kimmel) and the platform, not directly between Santos and Kimmel. The implied contract claim appeared to rest on little more than the assertion that Kimmel should have known the videos would not be used for commercial broadcast—a theory that sits uncomfortably close to the exclusive rights granted by copyright itself. A preemption analysis could have helped delineate when platform-mediated transactions involving creative content give rise to enforceable contractual expectations distinct from copyright protections.

## When Courts Should Confront Preemption

To be fair, the Second Circuit's restraint is not unusual. The court has previously declined to resolve preemption questions when state law grounds suffice for disposal. And there is a legitimate argument that courts should avoid constitutional and federal statutory questions when alternative grounds are available—a principle that applies with some force to preemption analysis, which requires interpreting the scope of federal copyright law.

But preemption doctrine under § 301(a) is not merely an academic exercise. It serves a structural function in the federal intellectual property system, ensuring that state law does not fill gaps that Congress intended the Copyright Act to occupy—or does not occupy. When courts sidestep preemption, they leave litigants without guidance on whether state law remedies remain available for harms involving creative works. This uncertainty has real consequences. Plaintiffs may continue to file state law claims that are doctrinally preempted, adding years of litigation and expense before courts dispose of them on other grounds. Defendants cannot assess their exposure without knowing whether state law claims survive preemption. And the doctrine itself develops in an ad hoc, piecemeal fashion.

There is also a subtle but important point about how avoidance can skew doctrinal development. When courts resolve preemption only in cases where state law claims are strong enough to survive dismissal, they develop preemption jurisprudence primarily in the context of meritorious state law claims. Cases like *Santos*, where the state law claims are weak, might have provided a useful counterpoint—a context in which the court could clarify that preemption bars certain reformulated copyright claims even before reaching the merits of the underlying state law theory.

## Implications for Platform-Mediated Creative Transactions

The *Santos* avoidance also has implications beyond formal preemption doctrine. As creative production increasingly occurs through digital platforms—Cameo, Substack, YouTube, Patreon, and others—the relationship between platform terms of service, creator expectations, and copyright law becomes more complex. Creators like Santos produce copyrighted works pursuant to platform agreements they may not fully control. Users commission or access those works under terms set by the platform. When disputes arise, the question of who can enforce what obligations—and under what legal theory—becomes pressing.

The Second Circuit's resolution of Santos's direct breach of contract claim illustrates this complexity. Santos could not enforce the Cameo Terms of Service because he was not a party to that agreement under Illinois law. The court noted that other provisions of the Terms did contain third-party beneficiary language, suggesting the platform could have drafted provisions to allow creators to enforce certain obligations. This observation hints at a regulatory architecture that platforms could adopt but currently leave unaddressed: clear contractual frameworks that define the rights and obligations of all parties to platform-mediated creative transactions, reducing the need for litigation over implied contracts and preemption boundaries.

## Conclusion

The Second Circuit's decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* is doctrinally unremarkable in its outcome—Santos's claims failed for straightforward reasons. But the court's strategic avoidance of the preemption question reflects a broader pattern in copyright jurisprudence that deserves scrutiny. When courts resolve cases on alternative grounds, they conserve judicial resources and avoid unnecessary federal questions. But they also leave the preemption boundary undefined, and litigants continue to litigate claims that might be more efficiently disposed of with clearer doctrinal guidance. The Second Circuit has the doctrinal tools to provide that guidance. Whether it will choose to use them in the next case remains to be seen.



# Platform Terms of Service and the Limits of Enforcement by Non-Parties

When a comedian dupes a disgraced former congressman into recording absurd personalized videos, the result is predictable late-night television. When that congressman sues over it, the result is a useful lesson in the structural limits of platform governance. The Second Circuit's decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* resolves the copyright questions in Kimmel's favor on fair use grounds, but the opinion's most quietly significant holdings concern Santos's state law claims—particularly his attempt to enforce Cameo's Terms of Service against Jimmy Kimmel despite never having agreed to them. The court's analysis exposes a recurring tension in platform ecosystems: participants may depend on terms of service to structure their interactions yet lack any practical mechanism to enforce those terms when violations harm them.

## The Third-Party Beneficiary Problem

Santos's direct breach of contract claim depended on a straightforward but ultimately fatal premise. Kimmel, using pseudonyms, had agreed to Cameo's Terms of Service when he submitted his video requests. Those terms, Santos argued, restricted how purchasers could use the videos they received. When Kimmel aired the videos on his show, he breached those terms—and Santos, as the creator whose work was misused, sought to enforce them.

The problem is that Santos was not a party to that contract. The Terms of Service governed the relationship between Cameo and its users; Santos, as a talent provider on the platform, was outside that agreement. Under Illinois law, which governed the Terms of Service, courts apply a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." To overcome that presumption, Santos would have needed to show that the implication of third-party benefit was "so strong as to be practically an express declaration."

What made Santos's failure particularly telling was a structural detail the court emphasized. Other provisions of the very same Terms of Service did contain express third-party beneficiary language. The presence of such language in some provisions but not the ones Santos sought to enforce undermined his argument that the parties intended to extend enforceable rights to him by implication. When draftsmen know how to confer third-party rights and do so in some sections but not others, courts reasonably infer that the omission was deliberate.

This reasoning reflects a sensible application of contract doctrine, but it also reveals something about platform architecture. Platforms like Cameo depend on creators to supply content and users to purchase it, but the contractual relationships are typically bilateral—platform-to-user and platform-to-creator—rather than triangular. The Terms of Service that govern user behavior are designed to protect the platform's interests, not necessarily to create enforceable rights in the creators whose work flows through the system. Santos's situation illustrates the gap: he relied on Cameo's rules to structure how buyers could use his videos, but the contractual architecture gave him no standing to vindicate that reliance.

## Implied Contract and the Pleading of Essential Terms

Santos's alternative theory—an implied-in-fact contract between him and Kimmel—fared no better. Under New York law, an implied contract requires a "meeting of the minds" on essential terms. Santos's complaint, the court held, failed to allege those terms with the specificity New York requires. He did not identify the specific provisions under which liability was predicated, nor did he plausibly allege that Kimmel had agreed to any restrictions on his use of the videos.

This holding is less doctrinely interesting than the third-party beneficiary ruling, but it carries practical implications. Santos apparently could not articulate what the implied contract's terms actually were—perhaps because the entire premise of the arrangement was that Kimmel was concealing his identity and purpose. It is difficult to establish a meeting of the minds when one party is actively misrepresenting himself. The court's observation that Santos failed to allege "an indication of a meeting of minds" underscores a basic difficulty: a contract formed through deception may be voidable, but it is hard to argue in the alternative that a contract was both fraudulently induced and genuinely agreed upon in its essential terms.

## Fraudulent Inducement and the Out-of-Pocket Rule

Santos's fraudulent inducement claim foundered on a distinct New York requirement: the need to allege actual out-of-pocket loss. Santos had been paid for the videos. He did not allege that he had been financially harmed by Kimmel's deception; indeed, the commercial transaction was completed. Under New York's out-of-pocket rule, a fraud plaintiff must demonstrate actual pecuniary loss directly resulting from the misrepresentation. The court found no such loss in the complaint.

This requirement functions as an independent barrier for creators in Santos's position. If a buyer tricks a creator into producing content that is later used in ways the creator did not intend, the creator may feel deceived and may have a strong moral claim, but if the creator was compensated for the work, New York law asks whether there was a tangible financial loss. The answer here was no. The fraudulent inducement claim thus collapsed not because Santos was not deceived—he plainly was—but because New York's damages rule demands a specific kind of economic injury that deception alone does not produce.

## The Structural Gap

Taken together, the state law holdings in *Santos* reveal a pattern that extends beyond this case. Platform participants who produce content are often the parties most affected when users violate platform terms, but the contractual architecture of platforms typically does not grant them enforcement rights. They cannot sue as third-party beneficiaries because the terms are written to protect the platform. They may struggle to establish implied contracts with users because the platform mediates the interaction. And fraudulent inducement claims require economic losses that may not exist when the transaction was completed and paid for.

None of this is surprising as a matter of doctrine. The court's analysis is careful, conventional, and well-supported by precedent. But the cumulative effect is that creators like Santos are left without state law remedies for deceptive solicitation of their content, even when the deception is undisputed. The fair use ruling on the copyright side means that the First Amendment interests in commentary and criticism prevail over the creator's rights in the work. That is almost certainly the correct result. But it leaves a zone where a buyer can intentionally deceive a creator into producing content, broadcast that content to millions, and face no contractual or tort liability under state law—so long as the use qualifies as transformative commentary and the creator was paid for the initial transaction.

Whether that zone should exist is a policy question. Whether it does exist is now clearer after *Santos*: it does, at least in the Second Circuit, at least under New York and Illinois law, and at least when the platform's own terms do not expressly provide otherwise.
