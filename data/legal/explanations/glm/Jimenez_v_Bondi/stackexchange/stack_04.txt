Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### What counts as sufficient reporting to police for the "unable or unwilling" government protection analysis?
Question:
The court in Jimenez v. Bondi emphasizes that reporting past persecution to the government is "a key fact" in the unable-or-unwilling analysis. The petitioners spoke to two individual police officers who told them to leave, but the court said this was insufficient because it doesn't show the "Colombian government's position"—just individual officer apathy. The court also notes the petitioners didn't report through Jimenez's law enforcement contacts or outside their neighborhood. So my question is: how much reporting is enough? Do you need to file formal police reports, escalate to higher authorities, or try multiple precincts? At what point does individual officer failure become evidence of systemic government inability or unwillingness? The court says a "one-off conversation" isn't enough, but how many conversations would be?
Answer:
## What Counts as Sufficient Reporting

The short answer is that there is no magic number of conversations. The court's concern is not primarily quantitative but qualitative: the applicant must provide evidence that the *government as an institution*—not just individual officers—was on notice of the persecution and failed to respond, or that seeking such institutional recourse would have been futile or dangerous.

### The Legal Standard

The Tenth Circuit frames the unable-or-unwilling analysis as an inquiry into "a government's response to an applicant's past persecution," citing *Singh v. Bondi*. Two categories of evidence carry particular weight: (1) whether the government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors, and (2) whether the government offered protection to the applicant. Critically, the court emphasizes that intertwined in this analysis is "an inquiry into whether the government had notice of the alleged persecution and an opportunity to respond."

Reporting matters because without it, "there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped." A failure to report "can undercut a claim" but is "not necessarily fatal"—the applicant can instead demonstrate that filing a report would have been futile or dangerous.

### Why the Petitioners' Reporting Was Insufficient

The court found the petitioners' reporting deficient for two distinct reasons:

**First, the reporting did not reflect the government's institutional position.** The petitioners spoke to two individual officers who told them to leave and said nothing could be done. But the court, citing *Osorio-Morales v. Garland* (7th Cir.), held that "a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is 'unable or unwilling' to protect a victim." Individual officer apathy must be distinguished from systemic government failure. The court requires "evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures."

**Second, the petitioners had meaningful avenues of recourse they did not use.** Jimenez was a provisional lawyer and former intern at the National Prosecutor's office. His father "still works for the criminal justice system." Yet neither Jimenez nor his father reported the wrongdoing or corruption through these channels. The court also noted that the petitioners never "sought help outside their immediate neighborhood." The implication is that a single conversation with a beat cop in one's own neighborhood does not constitute a good-faith effort to engage the government's protective apparatus, particularly when the applicant has access to law enforcement contacts who could escalate the matter.

### What Would Have Been Sufficient

The opinion does not establish a numerical threshold, and asking "how many conversations" frames the question too narrowly. The real inquiry is whether the applicant gave the government a meaningful opportunity to respond and whether the evidence shows an institutional—not merely individual—failure. Several factors from the opinion illustrate what would strengthen a claim:

- **Formal reporting through established channels.** Filing a formal police report, rather than having a casual conversation with a patrol officer, would create a record and trigger an institutional response—or at least create evidence of what that response was.

- **Escalation.** If a local officer is unhelpful, attempting to report through higher authorities, other precincts, or—where available—professional contacts in law enforcement would show that the applicant tested the government's capacity to respond at multiple levels.

- **Evidence of systemic failure.** If multiple authorities at multiple levels are unresponsive, that pattern would support an inference of institutional inability or unwillingness, moving beyond individual officer apathy.

- **Demonstrated futility or danger.** If the applicant can show that reporting would have been futile or dangerous—for example, with specific evidence that the persecutors control the relevant institutions—they may be excused from the reporting requirement. Here, the petitioners argued futility based on the persecutors' alleged alignment with the government, but the court found this "unsupported by the record." Notably, the government had *itself* attempted searches and seizures at Don Rafa's property, which the court treated as evidence *against* the claim that the government was aligned with the persecutors and unwilling to act.

### The Bottom Line

The court's reasoning suggests the analysis is contextual rather than formulaic. The question is not "how many officers did you talk to" but "did you give the government a meaningful opportunity to protect you, and does the evidence show that the government—at an institutional level—failed to do so?" A single formal report that is met with documented institutional inaction could suffice. Conversely, many informal conversations with individual officers, without escalation or formal reporting, may still fall short if they only show individual apathy rather than systemic failure.

In this case, the petitioners had unusually strong access to law enforcement channels and failed to use them, which made their unable-or-unwilling argument particularly weak. The court's observation that Jimenez and his father had government connections but reported nothing is doing significant work in the analysis—it underscores that the petitioners could have tested the government's response through channels likely to reach decision-makers and chose not to.
