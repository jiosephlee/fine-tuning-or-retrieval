Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### How does the substantial evidence standard work when reviewing BIA findings of fact in asylum cases?
Question:
In Jimenez v. Bondi, the court says it reviews the BIA's findings of fact under a "substantial-evidence standard" and that findings are "conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary." I'm confused about how deferential this really is in practice. The court seems to go through the evidence and agree with the BIA at every step, but how is this different from de novo review? At what point would a court actually find that the evidence "compels" a contrary conclusion? Is there a meaningful difference between substantial evidence review and just deferring to whatever the agency decided?
Answer:
## How Substantial Evidence Review Actually Works

Your confusion is common. The court's opinion reads like it's independently evaluating the evidence, but the standard of review is doing something quite different. Here's the key distinction.

### The Standard: Highly Deferential, Not De Novo

Under substantial evidence review, the BIA's findings are **"conclusive"** unless the record compels a contrary conclusion. That word matters. The court is not asking: "What do *we* think the right answer is?" It's asking: "Could a reasonable adjudicator have reached this conclusion based on the record?" As the Tenth Circuit puts it elsewhere in the opinion, quoting *Yuk v. Ashcroft*: **"it is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ's [or the BIA's] decision."**

Under de novo review, the court would substitute its own judgment for the agency's. Under substantial evidence review, the court can acknowledge that it might have reached a different conclusion—and still affirm—so long as the agency's conclusion is supported by enough evidence that a reasonable adjudicator could reach it.

### What "Compels a Contrary Conclusion" Means

The petitioner bears the burden of showing that **any reasonable adjudicator** would be **compelled** to conclude differently. This is a very high bar:

- The evidence must be so one-sided that only one conclusion is reasonably possible.
- If the record could support *either* the agency's finding *or* a different finding, the court must affirm the agency.
- The court does not overturn merely because it finds the agency's reasoning unpersuasive or because it would have weighed the evidence differently.

### How the Opinion Applies the Standard

Watch the court's language carefully throughout. It does not say "we agree with the BIA." It says, repeatedly, that **"there is substantial evidence in the record supporting"** the BIA's findings.

On **past persecution**, the court identifies specific evidence supporting the BIA: Petitioners conceded no physical harm, threats alone are insufficient as a matter of law, and the emotional harm cited does not exceed what prior cases have rejected. The court then notes that its own case law has denied asylum in "more extreme fact patterns." That's not de novo reweighing—it's confirming that a reasonable adjudicator could reach the BIA's conclusion.

On **nexus**, the court points to evidence that the escalation in threats coincided with Jimenez's property dispute with Don Rafa, not with his political activity. The men who threatened Petitioners referenced Jimenez's work for the prosecutor's office and blamed him for searches at Don Rafa's property. The court concludes the BIA's finding—that political opinion was "tangential and subordinate"—is supported by substantial evidence. The court isn't saying no reasonable adjudicator could find a political nexus; it's saying a reasonable adjudicator *could* find there wasn't one, which is all the standard requires.

On **government protection**, the court notes that Petitioners made minimal efforts to contact authorities, that the two conversations with individual officers don't establish a systemic government failure, and that the government had actually attempted searches at Don Rafa's property. Again, the court is identifying evidence that supports the BIA's finding—not independently concluding the government would protect Petitioners.

### When Would the Evidence Actually "Compel" a Contrary Result?

A court would find the evidence compels a contrary conclusion when the record is so one-sided that no reasonable adjudicator could reach the agency's finding. Hypothetically, that might look like: uncontroverted testimony that the government explicitly refused to protect the applicant, documentary evidence directly contradicting the agency's factual finding, or the agency's own reasoning resting on a factual premise the record plainly contradicts.

In *Jimenez*, the court never reaches that point because at each step, the court can point to evidence supporting the BIA. The threats were serious but fell short of persecution as defined in prior case law. The nexus evidence pointed at least as strongly to personal motives as to political ones. The government protection evidence was thin and could support a finding either way—which means the court must affirm.

### The Practical Takeaway

Substantial evidence review is meaningfully different from de novo review, but the difference is in the **question the court asks**, not necessarily in the **process**. The court still walks through the evidence, but it does so to identify whether a reasonable adjudicator *could* have reached the agency's conclusion—not to determine whether the court *would* reach the same one. In a close case, that distinction is dispositive: under de novo, the court resolves the close call itself; under substantial evidence, the agency does.
