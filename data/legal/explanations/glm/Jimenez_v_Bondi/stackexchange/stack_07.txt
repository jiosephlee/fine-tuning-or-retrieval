Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### How does internal relocation factor into asylum analysis when the applicant hasn't established past persecution?
Question:
In Jimenez v. Bondi, the court addresses safe internal relocation even though it already concluded the petitioners failed to establish past persecution or a well-founded fear of future persecution. The court says relocation is relevant because the government can rebut a "presumption of a well-founded fear" by showing the applicant could relocate. But if there's no presumption because there's no past persecution, why is the court analyzing relocation at all? Is the court just being thorough, or is internal relocation an independent requirement that applicants must disprove? Also, the court notes the persecutors told petitioners to leave the neighborhood, not the country—how much weight does the scope of the persecutor's threat carry in the relocation analysis?
Answer:
## Answer

The short answer is that the court is providing an alternative basis for denial — but it's doing so in a way that's more than mere thoroughness, because relocation is relevant to the well-founded fear inquiry even without a presumption.

### Why the court addresses relocation without a presumption

The standard asylum framework works in two stages. First, an applicant who establishes past persecution receives a *rebuttable presumption* of a well-founded fear of future persecution. Second, the government can rebut that presumption by showing either a fundamental change in circumstances or that the applicant could avoid persecution by relocating internally. The court in *Jimenez* frames relocation in this standard way: "The government may rebut an applicant's presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution . . . if it shows by a preponderance of the evidence that . . . the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant's country."

But here's the key: the court already found no past persecution, so no presumption ever arose. The court explicitly acknowledges this: "Petitioners have not demonstrated a fear of future persecution, but even if they had, the record supports the finding that they could reasonably relocate to another part of Colombia." So the court is analyzing relocation in the alternative — as a belt-and-suspenders ground that would independently defeat the asylum claim even if the court were wrong about the well-founded fear analysis.

This is not just judicial overcaution, though. The court signals it is responding to the petitioners' own arguments on appeal: the petitioners briefed that relocation was unworkable due to economic burdens, judicial infrastructure problems, and the persecutors' ties to the Colombian president. The court addresses and rejects each of those arguments. So the relocation analysis serves two purposes: it provides an alternative holding and it responds to the petitioners' specific contentions on appeal.

That said, the opinion does not treat internal relocation as an independent requirement that applicants must independently disprove in every case. The court frames it as a rebuttal mechanism tied to a presumption — and then applies that mechanism hypothetically. There is a regulatory framework (8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(3)(ii)) under which applicants who have *not* established past persecution bear the burden of showing relocation is unreasonable, but the court does not explicitly invoke that regulation. The court instead keeps its analysis at the level of "even if they had established a fear, relocation would defeat the claim."

### The significance of the threat's geographic scope

The scope of the persecutor's threat carries real evidentiary weight in the relocation analysis, though it is not the only factor. The court notes: "the men who threatened Petitioners told them to move out of the neighborhood; they did not threaten the family to move out of the country." The court uses this fact to support its conclusion that "Petitioners can mitigate any potential for future persecution by safely and reasonably relocating within Colombia."

The logic is straightforward: if the persecutors' objective was to remove the petitioners from a specific neighborhood — presumably to protect Don Rafa's criminal enterprise at that property — then the threat is geographically bounded. Moving elsewhere in Colombia would satisfy the persecutors' demand and eliminate the danger. A threat to leave the *country* would suggest the persecutors' reach is nationwide and that internal relocation would be futile. The neighborhood-level threat cuts against that inference.

But the court does not treat this fact alone as dispositive. It bundles it with other evidence: the petitioners' family members continue to live in Colombia unharmed, Jimenez's father still works for the criminal justice system, and the petitioners themselves safely lived with Jimenez's mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before departing for the United States. The geographic scope of the threat is one corroborating factor among several, all pointing toward the conclusion that relocation within Colombia is reasonable.

### Bottom line

The court analyzes relocation because (1) the petitioners raised it as an issue on appeal, and (2) it provides an alternative ground for denial that would survive even if the court's well-founded fear finding were reversed. The court does not treat relocation as a standalone independent requirement that applies irrespective of any showing of fear. Rather, it says: even assuming a well-founded fear existed, the government could rebut it by showing reasonable internal relocation — and the record supports that showing here.
