Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Why does failing the asylum standard automatically mean failing withholding of removal?
Question:
At the end of Jimenez v. Bondi, the court says that because petitioners fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, "they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal." I know withholding of removal has a higher burden of proof, but I'm confused about how the standards actually differ. Is it just the percentage of likelihood (more likely than not vs. reasonable possibility)? Are the elements (persecution, nexus, government protection) the same for both? If the elements are identical but just the burden is higher, why does the court even need to separately analyze withholding—couldn't it just say "higher standard, so automatically fails"? What's the practical difference between these two forms of relief?
Answer:
## The elements are the same; only the burden of proof differs

You've essentially identified the answer in your question. The reason failing asylum automatically means failing withholding of removal is straightforward: **both forms of relief require the same elements, but withholding demands a higher burden of proof.** If you cannot clear the lower bar, you necessarily cannot clear the higher one.

### The shared elements

The opinion lays out the three elements an asylum applicant must prove: "(1) an incident, or incidents, that rise to the level of persecution; (2) that is on account of one of the statutorily protected grounds; and (3) is committed by the government or forces the government is either unable or unwilling to control." These same elements—persecution, nexus, and government inability or unwillingness to protect—apply to withholding of removal as well. The opinion doesn't restate them for withholding because they are identical.

### The different burdens

The asylum standard requires a **"well-founded fear of persecution,"** which the opinion frames as requiring a showing that the applicant's harm satisfies the three elements above. The withholding standard is **"more likely than not"** that the applicant would be persecuted on account of a protected ground—a meaningfully higher threshold. The opinion references this when it says Petitioners "necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal," citing *Carias-Mejia* and *Uanreroro*.

So yes, it is largely about the degree of likelihood. "Well-founded fear" (a reasonable possibility) is a lower bar than "more likely than not" (a clear probability).

### Why the court doesn't separately analyze withholding

You asked: *couldn't the court just say "higher standard, so automatically fails"?* **That is effectively what the court does.** The opinion analyzes the asylum claim element by element—past persecution, nexus, government protection, and safe relocation—and then, in a single sentence at the end, disposes of withholding by noting that the higher standard is necessarily unmet. The court does **not** conduct a separate element-by-element analysis for withholding. It doesn't need to, because the elements are the same and the burden is higher. If the Petitioners cannot show a well-founded fear under asylum, they cannot logically show persecution is more likely than not under withholding.

### The practical differences between the two forms of relief

Although the substantive elements overlap, the two forms of relief differ significantly in practice:

- **Discretion vs. mandatory relief:** Asylum is a discretionary form of relief—the Attorney General *may* grant it to eligible applicants. Withholding of removal, by contrast, is mandatory: if the applicant meets the higher burden, the government *must* withhold removal to that specific country.
- **Scope of protection:** Asylum, if granted, can eventually lead to lawful permanent resident status. Withholding only prevents removal to the particular country of persecution; the applicant can still be removed to any other country that will accept them.
- **Procedural thresholds:** Asylum is subject to a one-year filing deadline (with exceptions). Withholding has no such deadline.

These practical differences explain why the two claims are pleaded in the alternative even though, on the facts of a given case, they often rise or fall together. An applicant pursues asylum as the preferred outcome but requests withholding as a fallback—if they can't meet the lower asylum standard, the withholding claim collapses as a matter of logic, as the court notes here. But if they *can* meet the asylum standard but the Attorney General exercises discretion to deny, the mandatory nature of withholding might still provide protection if the higher burden is independently met.

In *Jimenez*, however, the Petitioners couldn't even establish the baseline elements under the lower standard, so the withholding claim fell with the asylum claim.
