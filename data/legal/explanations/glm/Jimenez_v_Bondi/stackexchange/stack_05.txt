Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### How does the futility exception work when an applicant claims reporting to police would be dangerous?
Question:
In Jimenez v. Bondi, the court says an applicant can satisfy the government protection requirement by showing that filing a police report would have been "futile or dangerous." The petitioners argued that contacting the government would be futile because their persecutors are aligned with the president of Colombia. But the court rejected this because the government had actually attempted searches and seizures at Don Rafa's property. I'm confused about what evidence is needed to prove futility. If the persecutor is allegedly connected to the government, does the applicant need to prove that every possible avenue of protection would fail? Does evidence of some government enforcement action against the persecutor automatically defeat a futility claim?
Answer:
## How the Futility Exception Works Under *Jimenez v. Bondi*

The futility exception is a narrow one, and the opinion illustrates both what an applicant must show and what defeats such a claim.

### The doctrinal framework

Under Tenth Circuit law, the "unable or unwilling" analysis asks whether the government had **notice** of the persecution and an **opportunity to respond**. A "key fact" is whether the applicant reported the persecution to the government. Failure to report "can undercut" the claim, but is "not necessarily fatal" if the applicant can show that reporting would have been "futile or dangerous."

Two categories of evidence "weigh heavily" in this analysis: (1) whether the government "stopped or tried to stop the persecutors," and (2) whether the government "offered protection to the applicant." The court also cited *Osorio-Morales* for the proposition that the court "often require[s] evidence of **systemic, rather than individual**, failures to prove that a government is unable or unwilling to protect its people."

### Answering your first question: Must the applicant prove every avenue would fail?

Not exactly "every avenue," but the applicant must do more than show that one or two individual officers were unhelpful. The petitioners in *Jimenez* spoke to two individual police officers who told them not to "seek trouble" and to leave. The court held this was insufficient because "there is nothing to show that this reflects the Colombian government's position." The court also emphasized that the petitioners never sought help "outside their immediate neighborhood or through Jimenez's law enforcement contacts"—notably, Jimenez worked as an intern for the National Prosecutor's office and his father still works in the criminal justice system. The court treated these unexhausted avenues as undercutting the futility claim.

So the standard is not that the applicant must prove every conceivable avenue would fail. Rather, the applicant must produce evidence of **systemic** government failure, and the court will look at whether the applicant meaningfully engaged available reporting channels (or can explain why doing so would have been dangerous). A bare assertion that persecutors are "aligned with" the government, without more, does not substitute for that evidentiary showing.

### Answering your second question: Does evidence of enforcement action automatically defeat futility?

Not "automatically," but it is powerful counter-evidence. The petitioners argued their persecutor, Don Rafa, was politically aligned with Colombia's president, making protection futile. The court rejected this because "the government was aware of the property at issue and had attempted several searches and seizures to address the ongoing criminal conduct there." The court framed this as evidence that "counters Petitioners' argument that their persecutors are closely aligned with the government and would be unwilling or unable to stop the persecutors."

The key word is "counters"—the court treated the enforcement actions as directly rebutting the factual premise of the futility claim (that the government was in league with the persecutor). Under the substantial-evidence standard, the court asked only whether "any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary." The government's own attempts to raid Don Rafa's property meant the record did not compel a finding of futility, even if those attempts were "seemingly unsuccessful."

### The bottom line

To invoke the futility exception successfully, an applicant needs **concrete evidence of systemic government inability or unwillingness to protect**, not just an allegation that the persecutor has political connections. Evidence that the government has taken enforcement action against the persecutor—even unsuccessful action—strongly undercuts the claim, because it demonstrates the government is neither aligned with the persecutor nor indifferent to their conduct. The opinion also signals that applicants with personal or professional ties to law enforcement will be expected to explain why they did not use those contacts before claiming futility.
