Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### How does the substantial evidence standard work when reviewing BIA findings of fact in asylum cases?
Question:
In Jimenez v. Bondi, the court says it reviews the BIA's findings of fact under a "substantial-evidence standard" and that findings are "conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary." I'm confused about how deferential this really is in practice. The court seems to go through the evidence and agree with the BIA at every step, but how is this different from de novo review? At what point would a court actually find that the evidence "compels" a contrary conclusion? Is there a meaningful difference between substantial evidence review and just deferring to whatever the agency decided?
Answer:
## How Substantial Evidence Review Actually Works

Your confusion is common. The court's opinion reads like it's independently evaluating the evidence, but the standard of review is doing something quite different. Here's the key distinction.

### The Standard: Highly Deferential, Not De Novo

Under substantial evidence review, the BIA's findings are **"conclusive"** unless the record compels a contrary conclusion. That word matters. The court is not asking: "What do *we* think the right answer is?" It's asking: "Could a reasonable adjudicator have reached this conclusion based on the record?" As the Tenth Circuit puts it elsewhere in the opinion, quoting *Yuk v. Ashcroft*: **"it is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ's [or the BIA's] decision."**

Under de novo review, the court would substitute its own judgment for the agency's. Under substantial evidence review, the court can acknowledge that it might have reached a different conclusion—and still affirm—so long as the agency's conclusion is supported by enough evidence that a reasonable adjudicator could reach it.

### What "Compels a Contrary Conclusion" Means

The petitioner bears the burden of showing that **any reasonable adjudicator** would be **compelled** to conclude differently. This is a very high bar:

- The evidence must be so one-sided that only one conclusion is reasonably possible.
- If the record could support *either* the agency's finding *or* a different finding, the court must affirm the agency.
- The court does not overturn merely because it finds the agency's reasoning unpersuasive or because it would have weighed the evidence differently.

### How the Opinion Applies the Standard

Watch the court's language carefully throughout. It does not say "we agree with the BIA." It says, repeatedly, that **"there is substantial evidence in the record supporting"** the BIA's findings.

On **past persecution**, the court identifies specific evidence supporting the BIA: Petitioners conceded no physical harm, threats alone are insufficient as a matter of law, and the emotional harm cited does not exceed what prior cases have rejected. The court then notes that its own case law has denied asylum in "more extreme fact patterns." That's not de novo reweighing—it's confirming that a reasonable adjudicator could reach the BIA's conclusion.

On **nexus**, the court points to evidence that the escalation in threats coincided with Jimenez's property dispute with Don Rafa, not with his political activity. The men who threatened Petitioners referenced Jimenez's work for the prosecutor's office and blamed him for searches at Don Rafa's property. The court concludes the BIA's finding—that political opinion was "tangential and subordinate"—is supported by substantial evidence. The court isn't saying no reasonable adjudicator could find a political nexus; it's saying a reasonable adjudicator *could* find there wasn't one, which is all the standard requires.

On **government protection**, the court notes that Petitioners made minimal efforts to contact authorities, that the two conversations with individual officers don't establish a systemic government failure, and that the government had actually attempted searches at Don Rafa's property. Again, the court is identifying evidence that supports the BIA's finding—not independently concluding the government would protect Petitioners.

### When Would the Evidence Actually "Compel" a Contrary Result?

A court would find the evidence compels a contrary conclusion when the record is so one-sided that no reasonable adjudicator could reach the agency's finding. Hypothetically, that might look like: uncontroverted testimony that the government explicitly refused to protect the applicant, documentary evidence directly contradicting the agency's factual finding, or the agency's own reasoning resting on a factual premise the record plainly contradicts.

In *Jimenez*, the court never reaches that point because at each step, the court can point to evidence supporting the BIA. The threats were serious but fell short of persecution as defined in prior case law. The nexus evidence pointed at least as strongly to personal motives as to political ones. The government protection evidence was thin and could support a finding either way—which means the court must affirm.

### The Practical Takeaway

Substantial evidence review is meaningfully different from de novo review, but the difference is in the **question the court asks**, not necessarily in the **process**. The court still walks through the evidence, but it does so to identify whether a reasonable adjudicator *could* have reached the agency's conclusion—not to determine whether the court *would* reach the same one. In a close case, that distinction is dispositive: under de novo, the court resolves the close call itself; under substantial evidence, the agency does.

### Can threats and psychological harm ever constitute persecution, or is physical harm always required?
Question:
The court in Jimenez v. Bondi says that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution" and that it has "rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone." But the court also says physical harm is "not necessary" to satisfy the persecution prong and that incidents should be considered "collectively" for their cumulative effect. The petitioners here received death threats, were held at gunpoint, had an employee assaulted, and had a man grab their child at school. If all of that combined doesn't rise to persecution, what would? Is there any Tenth Circuit case where non-physical harm alone was found to constitute persecution? I'm having trouble understanding where the line actually is.
Answer:
## The short answer

The Tenth Circuit does not require physical harm, but in practice it sets the bar for non-physical persecution so high that it is nearly impossible to clear without at least some physical component. The opinion in *Jimenez* is less contradictory than it first appears once you understand the sliding-scale framework the court applies.

## The doctrinal framework

The Tenth Circuit defines persecution as "the infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ (in race, religion, or political opinion) in a way regarded as offensive" and requiring "more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty." The opinion draws from *Wiransane v. Ashcroft* for this definition.

Within that framework, the court applies two related but distinct principles:

1. **Physical harm is not strictly required.** The court expressly states that "physical harm is not necessary to satisfy the persecution prong," citing *Karki v. Holder*, 715 F.3d at 804. This means the door is theoretically open for non-physical harm to suffice.

2. **But threats alone are never enough.** The court separately holds that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution." And it goes further: "we have rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone."

These two principles coexist because the court treats persecution as a threshold concept requiring harm that is qualitatively *offensive*—not merely frightening. Psychological distress, sleeplessness, and panic attacks, while real, are treated as consequences *of* threats rather than independent acts of persecution. The court notes that such symptoms "stemming from their received threats are insufficient to rise to the level of persecution."

## The cumulative-effects doctrine and why Jimenez failed

The court does say incidents should be considered "collectively" because "the cumulative effects of multiple incidents may constitute persecution." But the cumulative-effects doctrine is additive, not transformative—it stacks incidents to reach a higher total severity, but it does not change the *nature* of what is being stacked. If the individual incidents are threats and psychological harm, their cumulative weight is still threats and psychological harm.

In *Jimenez*, the petitioners had:

- Repeated threatening phone calls
- A threatening brochure
- Two men holding Jimenez at gunpoint
- An employee assaulted at the factory (a third party)
- A man grabbing the petitioners' child at school

The court found this insufficient for two key reasons stated in the opinion. First, the petitioners "conceded at their merits hearing they were not physically harmed" and "experienced no lasting physical harm." Second, the primary harm was threats, and the psychological symptoms (sleeplessness, panic attacks) derived from those threats rather than constituting independent persecution. The employee assault and the child incident, while alarming, did not amount to physical harm to the *petitioners* themselves.

## Where the line actually is

The opinion offers no Tenth Circuit example of non-physical harm alone constituting persecution. The court's own language—"rarely, if ever"—is telling. The "if ever" signals that the court may not be able to identify a case where it has actually done this, even while maintaining the theoretical possibility.

The cases the court cites as comparative benchmarks illustrate how high the bar is:

- ***Brandy v. Holder***: A 10-hour arrest and detention plus threats was "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling" but still not persecution.
- ***Chhetri v. Rosen***: Four telephone threats and one vague in-person threat did not constitute persecution.
- ***Carias-Mejia v. Garland***: Threats with machetes and being robbed six times was insufficient because the threats were "contingent on compliance with a demand."

Note that *Carias-Mejia* involved a machete—a weapon displayed in a threatening manner—and six robberies, yet the court still found no persecution. These cases suggest that even the threat of physical violence, when it does not result in actual physical harm to the applicant, falls below the Tenth Circuit's threshold.

## Practical takeaway

The line in the Tenth Circuit is not cleanly between "physical" and "non-physical" harm. It is between harm that is *offensive*—meaning it involves actual infliction of suffering—and harm that is *threatening*—meaning it creates fear of future suffering. The court treats threats, even credible and repeated ones, as categorically insufficient standing alone. Psychological harm flowing from those threats is treated the same way.

What would clear the bar without physical harm? The opinion does not say, and it does not cite a Tenth Circuit case that answers that question. The most you can take from *Jimenez* is that the theoretical possibility exists under *Karki*, but the court's "rarely, if ever" language, combined with its rejection of cumulative threats far more severe than most applicants will ever face, means that as a practical matter in the Tenth Circuit, non-physical harm alone is an extraordinarily difficult path to establishing persecution.

### How does the "at least one central reason" nexus test work in mixed-motive persecution cases?
Question:
In Jimenez v. Bondi, the court discusses mixed motives—the persecutor can have multiple reasons for targeting someone, but the protected ground must be "at least one central reason." Here, the petitioners were politically active AND Jimenez interfered with Don Rafa's property/criminal operations. The court found the political opinion was "tangential and subordinate" to the property dispute. But what if the persecutor was motivated 40% by political opinion and 60% by the personal dispute—is that enough for nexus? How do courts actually weigh or rank motives to determine if a protected ground is "central" versus "incidental"? Is there a threshold percentage or is it purely qualitative?
Answer:
## There Is No Percentage Threshold—It's a Qualitative, Totality-of-the-Circumstances Inquiry

The short answer to your question is that courts do not use a percentage-based or quantitative test. The "at least one central reason" standard is qualitative: the factfinder examines the totality of the circumstances to determine whether the protected ground is a *central* reason for the persecution or merely an incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate one.

### The Legal Standard

The Tenth Circuit (and other circuits) derive the "at least one central reason" test from the statute itself: 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i). As the court explains in *Jimenez*, the applicant's political opinion "cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm" and cannot play "a minor role" in the persecution. (quoting *Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d 641, 646 (10th Cir. 2012)).

Critically, the court acknowledges that "a persecutor can have multiple motives for targeting someone." In mixed-motive cases, the applicant does not need to prove that the protected ground was the *only* reason or even the *primary* reason—just that it was "at least one central reason." But there is a line: if the protected ground is subordinate to another motive, nexus fails.

### How Courts Actually Weigh Motives

Your hypothetical—40% political, 60% personal—can't be answered with arithmetic because courts don't assign weights that way. Instead, they look at circumstantial evidence to assess the *nature and centrality* of each motive. In *Jimenez*, the court examined several factors that are typical of this analysis:

1. **Timing and escalation.** The petitioners received some political threats before the property dispute, but the threats *escalated* only after Jimenez confronted Don Rafa about the property. The court treated this sequence as evidence that the property dispute—not politics—was the real catalyst.

2. **Content of the threats.** When the armed men confronted Jimenez, they called him a "snitch" for the prosecutor's office and held him "responsible for the searches and seizures at the property." When they returned to threaten the employees, they said the order came from Don Rafa. The court found the *substance* of the threats pointed to the property dispute and Jimenez's interference with Don Rafa's criminal operations, not to his political activity.

3. **Absence of evidence linking the persecutor to political motives.** The court noted that the petitioners "do not point to any evidence in the record that suggests Don Rafa was behind those initial politically affiliated threats." Without evidence connecting Don Rafa to the earlier political threats, the court could not conclude that politics centrally motivated *this* persecutor.

4. **The persecutor's own statements.** The men explicitly referenced Jimenez's work for the prosecutor's office and the searches at the property. They told the family to leave the *neighborhood*, not the country or the political party. This further undercut any claim that political opinion was central.

### So Where Is the Line?

The line between "central" and "incidental" is necessarily imprecise—that's the nature of a qualitative standard. But *Jimenez* illustrates the kind of evidence that pushes a motive from one side to the other. When the protected ground surfaces only in the background (the men mentioned that the petitioners were "in politics" during one of the threats, which the court noted but did not find dispositive), while the dominant evidence points to a personal or economic dispute, the protected ground is "tangential and subordinate."

If, by contrast, the evidence showed that Don Rafa specifically targeted Jimenez *because* of his Liberal Party activism—that, say, Don Rafa was a political rival who escalated the property dispute as a pretext to go after a political opponent—the analysis could come out differently. The question is always whether, looking at everything in the record, a reasonable factfinder could conclude that the protected ground was more than a minor factor.

### One More Thing: Standard of Review Matters

It's worth noting that the court was applying the **substantial evidence** standard, under which the BIA's findings are "conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary." This means the court was not reweighing the evidence from scratch—it was asking whether the agency's characterization of the motives was supported by enough evidence that a reasonable adjudicator could reach that conclusion. This deferential posture means that close calls on motive tend to resolve in the government's favor, since the petitioner bears the burden of proof and the court will not reweigh the evidence.

### What counts as sufficient reporting to police for the "unable or unwilling" government protection analysis?
Question:
The court in Jimenez v. Bondi emphasizes that reporting past persecution to the government is "a key fact" in the unable-or-unwilling analysis. The petitioners spoke to two individual police officers who told them to leave, but the court said this was insufficient because it doesn't show the "Colombian government's position"—just individual officer apathy. The court also notes the petitioners didn't report through Jimenez's law enforcement contacts or outside their neighborhood. So my question is: how much reporting is enough? Do you need to file formal police reports, escalate to higher authorities, or try multiple precincts? At what point does individual officer failure become evidence of systemic government inability or unwillingness? The court says a "one-off conversation" isn't enough, but how many conversations would be?
Answer:
## What Counts as Sufficient Reporting

The short answer is that there is no magic number of conversations. The court's concern is not primarily quantitative but qualitative: the applicant must provide evidence that the *government as an institution*—not just individual officers—was on notice of the persecution and failed to respond, or that seeking such institutional recourse would have been futile or dangerous.

### The Legal Standard

The Tenth Circuit frames the unable-or-unwilling analysis as an inquiry into "a government's response to an applicant's past persecution," citing *Singh v. Bondi*. Two categories of evidence carry particular weight: (1) whether the government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors, and (2) whether the government offered protection to the applicant. Critically, the court emphasizes that intertwined in this analysis is "an inquiry into whether the government had notice of the alleged persecution and an opportunity to respond."

Reporting matters because without it, "there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped." A failure to report "can undercut a claim" but is "not necessarily fatal"—the applicant can instead demonstrate that filing a report would have been futile or dangerous.

### Why the Petitioners' Reporting Was Insufficient

The court found the petitioners' reporting deficient for two distinct reasons:

**First, the reporting did not reflect the government's institutional position.** The petitioners spoke to two individual officers who told them to leave and said nothing could be done. But the court, citing *Osorio-Morales v. Garland* (7th Cir.), held that "a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is 'unable or unwilling' to protect a victim." Individual officer apathy must be distinguished from systemic government failure. The court requires "evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures."

**Second, the petitioners had meaningful avenues of recourse they did not use.** Jimenez was a provisional lawyer and former intern at the National Prosecutor's office. His father "still works for the criminal justice system." Yet neither Jimenez nor his father reported the wrongdoing or corruption through these channels. The court also noted that the petitioners never "sought help outside their immediate neighborhood." The implication is that a single conversation with a beat cop in one's own neighborhood does not constitute a good-faith effort to engage the government's protective apparatus, particularly when the applicant has access to law enforcement contacts who could escalate the matter.

### What Would Have Been Sufficient

The opinion does not establish a numerical threshold, and asking "how many conversations" frames the question too narrowly. The real inquiry is whether the applicant gave the government a meaningful opportunity to respond and whether the evidence shows an institutional—not merely individual—failure. Several factors from the opinion illustrate what would strengthen a claim:

- **Formal reporting through established channels.** Filing a formal police report, rather than having a casual conversation with a patrol officer, would create a record and trigger an institutional response—or at least create evidence of what that response was.

- **Escalation.** If a local officer is unhelpful, attempting to report through higher authorities, other precincts, or—where available—professional contacts in law enforcement would show that the applicant tested the government's capacity to respond at multiple levels.

- **Evidence of systemic failure.** If multiple authorities at multiple levels are unresponsive, that pattern would support an inference of institutional inability or unwillingness, moving beyond individual officer apathy.

- **Demonstrated futility or danger.** If the applicant can show that reporting would have been futile or dangerous—for example, with specific evidence that the persecutors control the relevant institutions—they may be excused from the reporting requirement. Here, the petitioners argued futility based on the persecutors' alleged alignment with the government, but the court found this "unsupported by the record." Notably, the government had *itself* attempted searches and seizures at Don Rafa's property, which the court treated as evidence *against* the claim that the government was aligned with the persecutors and unwilling to act.

### The Bottom Line

The court's reasoning suggests the analysis is contextual rather than formulaic. The question is not "how many officers did you talk to" but "did you give the government a meaningful opportunity to protect you, and does the evidence show that the government—at an institutional level—failed to do so?" A single formal report that is met with documented institutional inaction could suffice. Conversely, many informal conversations with individual officers, without escalation or formal reporting, may still fall short if they only show individual apathy rather than systemic failure.

In this case, the petitioners had unusually strong access to law enforcement channels and failed to use them, which made their unable-or-unwilling argument particularly weak. The court's observation that Jimenez and his father had government connections but reported nothing is doing significant work in the analysis—it underscores that the petitioners could have tested the government's response through channels likely to reach decision-makers and chose not to.

### How does the futility exception work when an applicant claims reporting to police would be dangerous?
Question:
In Jimenez v. Bondi, the court says an applicant can satisfy the government protection requirement by showing that filing a police report would have been "futile or dangerous." The petitioners argued that contacting the government would be futile because their persecutors are aligned with the president of Colombia. But the court rejected this because the government had actually attempted searches and seizures at Don Rafa's property. I'm confused about what evidence is needed to prove futility. If the persecutor is allegedly connected to the government, does the applicant need to prove that every possible avenue of protection would fail? Does evidence of some government enforcement action against the persecutor automatically defeat a futility claim?
Answer:
## How the Futility Exception Works Under *Jimenez v. Bondi*

The futility exception is a narrow one, and the opinion illustrates both what an applicant must show and what defeats such a claim.

### The doctrinal framework

Under Tenth Circuit law, the "unable or unwilling" analysis asks whether the government had **notice** of the persecution and an **opportunity to respond**. A "key fact" is whether the applicant reported the persecution to the government. Failure to report "can undercut" the claim, but is "not necessarily fatal" if the applicant can show that reporting would have been "futile or dangerous."

Two categories of evidence "weigh heavily" in this analysis: (1) whether the government "stopped or tried to stop the persecutors," and (2) whether the government "offered protection to the applicant." The court also cited *Osorio-Morales* for the proposition that the court "often require[s] evidence of **systemic, rather than individual**, failures to prove that a government is unable or unwilling to protect its people."

### Answering your first question: Must the applicant prove every avenue would fail?

Not exactly "every avenue," but the applicant must do more than show that one or two individual officers were unhelpful. The petitioners in *Jimenez* spoke to two individual police officers who told them not to "seek trouble" and to leave. The court held this was insufficient because "there is nothing to show that this reflects the Colombian government's position." The court also emphasized that the petitioners never sought help "outside their immediate neighborhood or through Jimenez's law enforcement contacts"—notably, Jimenez worked as an intern for the National Prosecutor's office and his father still works in the criminal justice system. The court treated these unexhausted avenues as undercutting the futility claim.

So the standard is not that the applicant must prove every conceivable avenue would fail. Rather, the applicant must produce evidence of **systemic** government failure, and the court will look at whether the applicant meaningfully engaged available reporting channels (or can explain why doing so would have been dangerous). A bare assertion that persecutors are "aligned with" the government, without more, does not substitute for that evidentiary showing.

### Answering your second question: Does evidence of enforcement action automatically defeat futility?

Not "automatically," but it is powerful counter-evidence. The petitioners argued their persecutor, Don Rafa, was politically aligned with Colombia's president, making protection futile. The court rejected this because "the government was aware of the property at issue and had attempted several searches and seizures to address the ongoing criminal conduct there." The court framed this as evidence that "counters Petitioners' argument that their persecutors are closely aligned with the government and would be unwilling or unable to stop the persecutors."

The key word is "counters"—the court treated the enforcement actions as directly rebutting the factual premise of the futility claim (that the government was in league with the persecutor). Under the substantial-evidence standard, the court asked only whether "any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary." The government's own attempts to raid Don Rafa's property meant the record did not compel a finding of futility, even if those attempts were "seemingly unsuccessful."

### The bottom line

To invoke the futility exception successfully, an applicant needs **concrete evidence of systemic government inability or unwillingness to protect**, not just an allegation that the persecutor has political connections. Evidence that the government has taken enforcement action against the persecutor—even unsuccessful action—strongly undercuts the claim, because it demonstrates the government is neither aligned with the persecutor nor indifferent to their conduct. The opinion also signals that applicants with personal or professional ties to law enforcement will be expected to explain why they did not use those contacts before claiming futility.

### Why does failing the asylum standard automatically mean failing withholding of removal?
Question:
At the end of Jimenez v. Bondi, the court says that because petitioners fail to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, "they necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal." I know withholding of removal has a higher burden of proof, but I'm confused about how the standards actually differ. Is it just the percentage of likelihood (more likely than not vs. reasonable possibility)? Are the elements (persecution, nexus, government protection) the same for both? If the elements are identical but just the burden is higher, why does the court even need to separately analyze withholding—couldn't it just say "higher standard, so automatically fails"? What's the practical difference between these two forms of relief?
Answer:
## The elements are the same; only the burden of proof differs

You've essentially identified the answer in your question. The reason failing asylum automatically means failing withholding of removal is straightforward: **both forms of relief require the same elements, but withholding demands a higher burden of proof.** If you cannot clear the lower bar, you necessarily cannot clear the higher one.

### The shared elements

The opinion lays out the three elements an asylum applicant must prove: "(1) an incident, or incidents, that rise to the level of persecution; (2) that is on account of one of the statutorily protected grounds; and (3) is committed by the government or forces the government is either unable or unwilling to control." These same elements—persecution, nexus, and government inability or unwillingness to protect—apply to withholding of removal as well. The opinion doesn't restate them for withholding because they are identical.

### The different burdens

The asylum standard requires a **"well-founded fear of persecution,"** which the opinion frames as requiring a showing that the applicant's harm satisfies the three elements above. The withholding standard is **"more likely than not"** that the applicant would be persecuted on account of a protected ground—a meaningfully higher threshold. The opinion references this when it says Petitioners "necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal," citing *Carias-Mejia* and *Uanreroro*.

So yes, it is largely about the degree of likelihood. "Well-founded fear" (a reasonable possibility) is a lower bar than "more likely than not" (a clear probability).

### Why the court doesn't separately analyze withholding

You asked: *couldn't the court just say "higher standard, so automatically fails"?* **That is effectively what the court does.** The opinion analyzes the asylum claim element by element—past persecution, nexus, government protection, and safe relocation—and then, in a single sentence at the end, disposes of withholding by noting that the higher standard is necessarily unmet. The court does **not** conduct a separate element-by-element analysis for withholding. It doesn't need to, because the elements are the same and the burden is higher. If the Petitioners cannot show a well-founded fear under asylum, they cannot logically show persecution is more likely than not under withholding.

### The practical differences between the two forms of relief

Although the substantive elements overlap, the two forms of relief differ significantly in practice:

- **Discretion vs. mandatory relief:** Asylum is a discretionary form of relief—the Attorney General *may* grant it to eligible applicants. Withholding of removal, by contrast, is mandatory: if the applicant meets the higher burden, the government *must* withhold removal to that specific country.
- **Scope of protection:** Asylum, if granted, can eventually lead to lawful permanent resident status. Withholding only prevents removal to the particular country of persecution; the applicant can still be removed to any other country that will accept them.
- **Procedural thresholds:** Asylum is subject to a one-year filing deadline (with exceptions). Withholding has no such deadline.

These practical differences explain why the two claims are pleaded in the alternative even though, on the facts of a given case, they often rise or fall together. An applicant pursues asylum as the preferred outcome but requests withholding as a fallback—if they can't meet the lower asylum standard, the withholding claim collapses as a matter of logic, as the court notes here. But if they *can* meet the asylum standard but the Attorney General exercises discretion to deny, the mandatory nature of withholding might still provide protection if the higher burden is independently met.

In *Jimenez*, however, the Petitioners couldn't even establish the baseline elements under the lower standard, so the withholding claim fell with the asylum claim.

### How does internal relocation factor into asylum analysis when the applicant hasn't established past persecution?
Question:
In Jimenez v. Bondi, the court addresses safe internal relocation even though it already concluded the petitioners failed to establish past persecution or a well-founded fear of future persecution. The court says relocation is relevant because the government can rebut a "presumption of a well-founded fear" by showing the applicant could relocate. But if there's no presumption because there's no past persecution, why is the court analyzing relocation at all? Is the court just being thorough, or is internal relocation an independent requirement that applicants must disprove? Also, the court notes the persecutors told petitioners to leave the neighborhood, not the country—how much weight does the scope of the persecutor's threat carry in the relocation analysis?
Answer:
## Answer

The short answer is that the court is providing an alternative basis for denial — but it's doing so in a way that's more than mere thoroughness, because relocation is relevant to the well-founded fear inquiry even without a presumption.

### Why the court addresses relocation without a presumption

The standard asylum framework works in two stages. First, an applicant who establishes past persecution receives a *rebuttable presumption* of a well-founded fear of future persecution. Second, the government can rebut that presumption by showing either a fundamental change in circumstances or that the applicant could avoid persecution by relocating internally. The court in *Jimenez* frames relocation in this standard way: "The government may rebut an applicant's presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution . . . if it shows by a preponderance of the evidence that . . . the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant's country."

But here's the key: the court already found no past persecution, so no presumption ever arose. The court explicitly acknowledges this: "Petitioners have not demonstrated a fear of future persecution, but even if they had, the record supports the finding that they could reasonably relocate to another part of Colombia." So the court is analyzing relocation in the alternative — as a belt-and-suspenders ground that would independently defeat the asylum claim even if the court were wrong about the well-founded fear analysis.

This is not just judicial overcaution, though. The court signals it is responding to the petitioners' own arguments on appeal: the petitioners briefed that relocation was unworkable due to economic burdens, judicial infrastructure problems, and the persecutors' ties to the Colombian president. The court addresses and rejects each of those arguments. So the relocation analysis serves two purposes: it provides an alternative holding and it responds to the petitioners' specific contentions on appeal.

That said, the opinion does not treat internal relocation as an independent requirement that applicants must independently disprove in every case. The court frames it as a rebuttal mechanism tied to a presumption — and then applies that mechanism hypothetically. There is a regulatory framework (8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(3)(ii)) under which applicants who have *not* established past persecution bear the burden of showing relocation is unreasonable, but the court does not explicitly invoke that regulation. The court instead keeps its analysis at the level of "even if they had established a fear, relocation would defeat the claim."

### The significance of the threat's geographic scope

The scope of the persecutor's threat carries real evidentiary weight in the relocation analysis, though it is not the only factor. The court notes: "the men who threatened Petitioners told them to move out of the neighborhood; they did not threaten the family to move out of the country." The court uses this fact to support its conclusion that "Petitioners can mitigate any potential for future persecution by safely and reasonably relocating within Colombia."

The logic is straightforward: if the persecutors' objective was to remove the petitioners from a specific neighborhood — presumably to protect Don Rafa's criminal enterprise at that property — then the threat is geographically bounded. Moving elsewhere in Colombia would satisfy the persecutors' demand and eliminate the danger. A threat to leave the *country* would suggest the persecutors' reach is nationwide and that internal relocation would be futile. The neighborhood-level threat cuts against that inference.

But the court does not treat this fact alone as dispositive. It bundles it with other evidence: the petitioners' family members continue to live in Colombia unharmed, Jimenez's father still works for the criminal justice system, and the petitioners themselves safely lived with Jimenez's mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before departing for the United States. The geographic scope of the threat is one corroborating factor among several, all pointing toward the conclusion that relocation within Colombia is reasonable.

### Bottom line

The court analyzes relocation because (1) the petitioners raised it as an issue on appeal, and (2) it provides an alternative ground for denial that would survive even if the court's well-founded fear finding were reversed. The court does not treat relocation as a standalone independent requirement that applies irrespective of any showing of fear. Rather, it says: even assuming a well-founded fear existed, the government could rebut it by showing reasonable internal relocation — and the record supports that showing here.

### When a single BIA member affirms the IJ's decision, what exactly does the reviewing court look at?
Question:
In Jimenez v. Bondi, the court notes that "a single member of the BIA affirmed the IJ's decision" and therefore the court reviews "the BIA's decision" but is "not precluded from consulting the IJ's more complete explanation of those same grounds." I'm confused about the procedural posture here. When a single BIA member issues a brief affirmance without opinion, does the reviewing court treat the IJ's reasoning as the BIA's reasoning? What if the BIA member affirmed on different grounds than the IJ? How do you know which decision is actually being reviewed—the IJ's, the BIA's, or both? Does it matter whether the BIA adopted the IJ's reasoning wholesale or just affirmed the result?
Answer:
## Short Answer

The reviewing court reviews the **BIA's decision** as the final agency action. But when a single BIA member affirms the IJ without issuing a full opinion, the court may consult the IJ's reasoning as a more complete explanation of the same grounds the BIA affirmed. The IJ's opinion does not stand as an independent decision subject to separate review—it serves as a window into the BIA's reasoning, but only to the extent the BIA adopted or relied on the same grounds.

## The Governing Standard in the Tenth Circuit

The opinion cites *Uanreroro v. Gonzales*, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006), for the proposition that when a single BIA member affirms the IJ, the court "review[s] the BIA's decision" but is "not precluded from consulting the IJ's more complete explanation of those same grounds."

This reflects a straightforward principle: the BIA's affirmance is the final order, so that is what the court reviews. But because a single-member affirmance typically provides little or no independent reasoning, the court needs somewhere to look to understand the basis for the decision. The IJ's opinion supplies that explanation, but only for grounds the BIA actually affirmed. The court is not free to treat the IJ's reasoning as the BIA's reasoning on issues the BIA did not adopt.

## How This Plays Out in *Jimenez*

Throughout the opinion, the court references both the BIA's and the IJ's findings, consistent with this framework. For example:

- On past persecution: "substantial evidence to support the **BIA's** finding that Petitioners' harm, even in the aggregate, does not amount to past persecution."
- On nexus: "substantial evidence supporting the **BIA and IJ's** findings that Petitioners were primarily targeted because Jimenez threatened Don Rafa's revenue and security."
- On government protection: "substantial evidence in the record supporting the **BIA's and IJ's** findings."

The court treats the BIA's decision as the operative decision but routinely draws on the IJ's reasoning to explain why substantial evidence supports the result. This is exactly what *Uanreroro* permits.

## Answering Your Specific Questions

**Does the court treat the IJ's reasoning as the BIA's reasoning?** Only for the same grounds the BIA affirmed. The IJ's opinion fills in the reasoning the BIA adopted but did not independently articulate.

**What if the BIA affirmed on different grounds than the IJ?** The court would review the BIA's grounds, not the IJ's. The phrase "those same grounds" in *Uanreroro* limits the court to consulting the IJ's explanation only where the BIA affirmed on the same basis. If the BIA reached the same result but for different reasons, the IJ's reasoning on the rejected grounds is irrelevant to the petition for review.

**Which decision is being reviewed?** The BIA's. The IJ's decision is not independently reviewable when the BIA has affirmed it. But the IJ's opinion functions as an explanatory supplement to the BIA's decision, which is why the court freely cites both.

**Does it matter whether the BIA adopted the IJ's reasoning wholesale or just affirmed the result?** Yes. If the BIA adopted the IJ's reasoning wholesale, the court can consult the IJ's full analysis. If the BIA affirmed only some grounds or provided its own reasoning on certain issues, the court is limited to the IJ's explanation on the grounds the BIA actually affirmed, and must rely on the BIA's own reasoning for any grounds it addressed independently.

## The Practical Takeaway

In practice, the distinction matters less than you might think when the BIA affirms without opinion—because in that scenario, the BIA has implicitly adopted the IJ's reasoning, and the court will look to the IJ's opinion as the operative explanation. The distinction matters more when the BIA issues even a brief opinion that might affirm on some grounds while providing its own analysis on others. In that case, you would need to identify which grounds the BIA adopted from the IJ and which it addressed independently.
