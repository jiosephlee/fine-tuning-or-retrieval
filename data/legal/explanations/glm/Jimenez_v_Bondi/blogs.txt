Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Threats, Psychological Harm, and the Elusive Boundary of 'Persecution' in the Tenth Circuit

When does fear become persecution? That question sits at the heart of every asylum case, yet the answer remains stubbornly elusive. The Tenth Circuit's recent decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* illustrates just how high the bar has risen—at least in that circuit—for applicants who face serious, repeated, and escalating threats but escape without physical injury. The opinion also reveals a growing tension in Tenth Circuit doctrine: the court nominally instructs adjudicators to assess harm cumulatively rather than incident by incident, but in practice, the absence of physical violence appears to function as a near-categorical bar to establishing persecution.

## The Facts: A Pattern of Escalating Intimidation

The petitioners, Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and Angelica Parada Duarte, were a Colombian family with deep civic ties. Jimenez held a provisional law license, interned at the National Prosecutor's office, and actively campaigned for the Colombian Liberal Party. The threats began in mid-2022 with phone calls and a threatening brochure labeling them "snitches from Liberal Party." The situation escalated dramatically after Jimenez, acting in his capacity as a provisional lawyer, became entangled in a property dispute involving Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros—"Don Rafa"—a leader of a narco-trafficking guerrilla group aligned with Colombia's president.

The consequences were serious. Two men held Jimenez at gunpoint at his furniture factory. Weeks later, the same men returned, assaulted an employee at gunpoint, and demanded to know the family's whereabouts. A stranger physically grabbed Jimenez's young son outside his school, covering the boy's mouth and telling him to "run to your dad." The family fled to the countryside, then to the United States. A local police officer, rather than offering help, told Jimenez nothing could be done and that he should leave.

The immigration judge found the petitioners credible. Yet both the IJ and the BIA denied relief, and the Tenth Circuit affirmed.

## The Persecution Analysis: Cumulative Harm Meets a Hard Floor

The most analytically interesting portion of the opinion concerns the past-persecution determination. The Tenth Circuit's framework appears, at first blush, to favor the petitioners. The court reiterated the cumulative-harm doctrine from *Ritonga v. Holder*, under which adjudicators must "consider [incidents] collectively, because the cumulative effects of multiple incidents may constitute persecution." The court also acknowledged that physical harm is not strictly necessary to satisfy the persecution prong, citing *Karki v. Holder*.

But having gestured toward flexibility, the court closed the door. It emphasized that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution"—again from *Karki*—and noted that petitioners had conceded they suffered no physical harm. The court then addressed the family's psychological injuries: Duarte's inability to sleep, the son's panic attacks. These were "unfortunate circumstances," the court acknowledged, but "insufficient to rise to the level of persecution." The court's formulation is striking: "we have rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone."

That parenthetical—"if ever"—does a great deal of work. It effectively concedes that the court cannot identify a case in the Tenth Circuit where threats and psychological harm, standing alone, satisfied the persecution standard. The court then reinforced this point by citing *Chhetri v. Rosen*, which rejected a persecution claim based on four telephone threats and one in-person threat, and *Carias-Mejia v. Garland*, which found insufficient persecution even where the applicant was threatened with machetes and robbed six times. If machete threats and six robberies do not cross the threshold, one is left to wonder what combination of non-physical harms possibly could.

This is where the tension becomes doctrinally significant. The cumulative-harm doctrine is supposed to allow courts to recognize that a campaign of intimidation—one that falls short of outright physical violence—can aggregate into persecution. That is precisely the scenario the doctrine was designed to address: the dissident who receives anonymous calls, finds graffiti on his door, sees colleagues disappear, and watches his child grabbed outside a school. Each incident, in isolation, might be characterized as a threat. Collectively, they describe a targeted campaign of terror designed to drive a family from their home. The Tenth Circuit's own framework says to assess these events together. But the opinion's reasoning effectively collapses back into the very incident-by-incident analysis the cumulative-harm doctrine was meant to avoid. The court acknowledged the full constellation of threats, the gunpoint confrontation, the factory raid, and the attempted grabbing of a child, but concluded that none of it mattered because no one was physically injured and the harm was "merely" psychological and threatening in nature.

## The Nexus Problem: A Separately Troubling Holding

The court's nexus analysis deserves brief mention, if only because it illustrates how facts can defeat a claim on multiple grounds simultaneously. The petitioners argued they were targeted for their political opinion—specifically, their support for a rival of President Petro. The court acknowledged the mixed-motive framework, under which a protected ground need only be "at least one central reason" for the persecution. But the court found that the predominant motive was Don Rafa's desire to protect his criminal enterprise from Jimenez's legal investigation, not the family's political views. The initial political threats, the court noted, did not escalate until after the property dispute began. This is a defensible reading of the record, but it also reflects how a strong nexus claim can be undermined when a persecutor's motives are entangled with professional or personal disputes that happen to involve a politically active applicant.

## The Government Protection and Relocation Analysis: Systemic Versus Individual Failure

The court's treatment of the government-protection requirement raises a distinct concern. The Tenth Circuit has held that an applicant need not report persecution to the government if doing so would have been futile or dangerous. Here, Jimenez did speak to a local police officer, who told him nothing could be done and that he should leave. The court dismissed this interaction as a "one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer" that does not necessarily reflect the government's position, citing the Seventh Circuit's decision in *Osorio-Morales v. Garland*.

That reasoning is not indefensible—the court is right that a single officer's apathy does not establish systemic government failure. But it sits uneasily with the reality on the ground. Jimenez's persecutor was a narco-trafficking guerrilla leader aligned with the sitting president. The family had been told the "order came from Don Rafa." The property at issue had been the subject of multiple law enforcement raids that had failed. The officer's admonition to "not seek problems" was not mere laziness; it was an implicit acknowledgment that the criminal actors operated with impunity. Requiring an applicant in these circumstances to demonstrate systemic government failure through additional reporting—when the persecutors are politically connected and the local police have already signaled unwillingness to act—may set the evidentiary bar unrealistically high for applicants fleeing regions where corruption and intimidation are precisely the mechanisms that render governments unable or unwilling to protect.

The relocation analysis compounds the difficulty. The court found that relocation was feasible because the petitioners' family members still lived in Colombia unharmed, Jimenez's father still worked in the criminal justice system, and the family safely spent three weeks with Jimenez's mother in the Bogota countryside. The court emphasized that the men told the petitioners to leave the neighborhood, not the country. But three weeks of safety in a nearby town, while actively planning departure, is thin evidence of long-term internal safety—particularly when the persecutors are politically connected narco-traffickers with a demonstrated willingness to track down and threaten the family's associates.

## The Doctrinal Question: Has the Persecution Standard Become Functionally Physical?

The deeper question the opinion raises is whether the Tenth Circuit's persecution threshold has become functionally impossible to meet without physical injury. The court insists that physical harm is not strictly required. But its consistent pattern of holdings tells a different story. *Chhetri* rejected telephone and in-person threats. *Carias-Mejia* rejected machete threats and six robberies. *Brandy* rejected a ten-hour detention and threats. And now *Jimenez* rejects gunpoint confrontations, an assault on an employee, the physical grabbing of a child, and a documented campaign of targeted intimidation—all experienced by a credible family that the IJ believed.

The cumulative-harm doctrine is meant to ensure that courts see the forest, not just the trees. But if no number of trees can constitute a forest unless at least one of them has fallen on someone, the doctrine has lost its operative force. For asylum applicants who face campaigns of intimidation that stop just short of physical violence—exactly the kind of persecution that sophisticated bad actors often prefer, precisely because it is harder to document and harder to prove—the Tenth Circuit's standard may now offer no realistic path to relief.

This is not to say the court's decision in *Jimenez* was wrong as a matter of applying existing Tenth Circuit precedent. The court was candid about what that precedent requires. The concern is what that precedent, taken together, has produced: a body of law in which the word "persecution" covers an increasingly narrow band of human experience, and in which the cumulative-harm doctrine functions more as a rhetorical nod than as an operative analytical tool. Whether other circuits or, eventually, the Supreme Court will address this gap remains to be seen. But for now, in the Tenth Circuit, the message to applicants is clear: unless you have been physically injured, the doctrine that asks adjudicators to consider harm collectively will not save you.



# Mixed Motives and the 'Central Reason' Test: When Political Activity Isn't Enough for Nexus

When an asylum applicant has genuine political convictions and faces genuine threats, how should courts determine *why* the persecutor came? The Tenth Circuit's recent decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* offers a clear illustration of how the "at least one central reason" standard operates in practice—and why political activity, even when it provokes threats, may not satisfy the nexus requirement when a more personal dispute lurks behind the persecution.

The case presents a fact pattern that asylum practitioners will recognize as frustratingly common: an applicant with multiple plausible attributes that could attract a persecutor's attention, and a record that doesn't cleanly separate the protected from the unprotected. The court's analysis demonstrates both the doctrinal difficulty and the practical stakes of the mixed-motive framework in asylum law.

## The Facts: Political Activism and a Property Dispute

Wilson Jimenez and his family were active members of Colombia's Liberal Party. They campaigned door-to-door for Federico Gutierrez in the 2022 presidential election, and Jimenez received threatening phone calls about his political involvement. A colleague was kidnapped and murdered. The family pressed on.

Separately—and this is where the case turns—Jimenez, then a law student with a provisional license, took on a client in a property dispute. The tenant refusing to vacate turned out to be Rafael "Don Rafa" Montanez Lancheros, a leader of a narco-trafficking guerrilla group politically aligned with then-President Gustavo Petro. Jimenez investigated the property, discovered it was being used for narco-trafficking and prostitution, and learned that law enforcement had attempted but failed to execute searches there.

The threats escalated dramatically after Jimenez's involvement with the property. Two men held him at gunpoint at his furniture factory, called him a "snitch" for the prosecutor's office, and held him responsible for the searches at Don Rafa's property. They returned later, assaulted an employee, demanded to know where the family was, and said Don Rafa had ordered them killed. A man grabbed Jimenez's young son at school. The family fled.

## The Nexus Question

The legal question is straightforward to state but difficult to resolve: was the family targeted "on account of" their political opinion? The INA requires that a protected ground be "at least one central reason" for the persecution—a standard codified at 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i) and elaborated in Tenth Circuit case law requiring that the political opinion not be "incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm."

The Tenth Circuit acknowledged that persecutors can have mixed motives. Citing its recent decision in *O.C.V. v. Bondi*, the court recognized that a persecutor need not be motivated solely by a protected ground. In mixed-motive cases, the applicant need only show that at least one protected ground was a central reason. But the court concluded that the petitioners' political opinion was merely tangential—the dominant motive was Jimenez's personal and professional interference with Don Rafa's criminal enterprise.

The court's reasoning rests heavily on the temporal sequence. Before the property dispute, the petitioners received threatening calls but continued campaigning undeterred. The escalated threats—gunpoint confrontations, the assault on an employee, the approach to a child—came only after Jimenez began investigating Don Rafa's property. During those confrontations, the men referenced Jimenez's work with the prosecutor's office and blamed him for the searches, not his Liberal Party activism. Don Rafa, the court noted, was the one who sent the men, and his grievance was personal.

## The Doctrinal Challenge of Disentangling Motives

The court's analysis is sound on this record, but the case illustrates a recurring structural problem in asylum law. The "central reason" standard asks adjudicators to reconstruct a private actor's subjective motivations from circumstantial evidence—a task that is often more art than science. Courts have developed several proxy tools: temporal sequencing (when did threats escalate?), the content of threats (what did the persecutor say?), and the persecutor's known characteristics (what incentives did they have?). The Tenth Circuit used all three here, and the evidence pointed convincingly toward the property dispute as the operative cause.

But consider what the petitioners argued and why it wasn't frivolous. Don Rafa was politically aligned with President Petro. The petitioners campaigned for Petro's rival. The threatening brochure Jimenez received—“death to snitches from Liberal Party”—fused political and criminal elements. In Colombia, the line between narco-trafficking organizations and political violence is notoriously porous. One could plausibly argue that Don Rafa's willingness to threaten Jimenez was shaped by Jimenez's political opposition, even if the immediate trigger was the property investigation. The court's framework, however, treats this as a binary question: Was political opinion a central reason, or was it subordinate to the personal dispute? The answer, the court found, was the latter.

This is where the "at least one central reason" standard's practical operation diverges from its doctrinal elegance. In theory, the standard accommodates mixed motives—a persecutor can target someone for both protected and unprotected reasons, so long as one protected ground is central. In practice, courts tend to engage in a form of motive ranking, identifying the "predominant" or "primary" reason and then assessing whether the protected ground survives that ranking. The Tenth Circuit did exactly that here, concluding that the "predominant motive" was the personal dispute. Once a court identifies a predominant non-protected motive, the protected motive is often described as "tangential" or "subordinate"—language that effectively collapses the "central reason" inquiry into a "but-for" or "predominant reason" test.

## The Broader Pattern

*Jimenez* fits within a recognizable pattern in Tenth Circuit asylum jurisprudence. The court has consistently required applicants to produce evidence that specifically links the persecutor's actions to the protected ground, not merely evidence that the applicant held the protected opinion and was persecuted. In *Carias-Mejia v. Garland*, threats with machetes and six robberies were insufficient because the threats were "contingent on compliance with a demand." In *Chhetri v. Rosen*, telephone threats and an in-person threat to the applicant's father did not constitute persecution. The court's approach reflects a consistent skepticism toward nexus claims where the record supports an alternative, non-protected explanation for the harm.

This skepticism is defensible, but it places a significant evidentiary burden on applicants in environments where political and criminal violence are intertwined. In countries like Colombia, Mexico, and parts of Central America, persecutors often have political affiliations that overlap with criminal enterprises. An applicant who angers a politically connected cartel boss by investigating criminal activity may be targeted for both reasons simultaneously. The "central reason" standard, as applied, effectively forces applicants to prove that the political dimension of the persecutor's motive was not just present but operatively central—a showing that is exceedingly difficult when the persecutor's statements and actions can be attributed to a personal grievance.

## What the Case Tells Us

The court's treatment of the government-protection and internal-relocation analyses reinforces the practical message. Jimenez spoke to a single police officer who told him to leave; the court found this insufficient to establish government unwillingness, noting that individual officer apathy does not demonstrate systemic failure. The court observed that the Colombian government had itself attempted searches at Don Rafa's property—evidence that, in the court's view, cut against the argument that the government was complicit. And the family's three-week stay with Jimenez's mother in the Bogota countryside, unharmed, suggested that internal relocation was feasible.

Taken together, the opinion sends a clear signal: applicants who become entangled in personal disputes with politically connected criminals face an uphill climb in establishing asylum eligibility, even when they have genuine political credentials. The nexus requirement, as the Tenth Circuit applies it, demands evidence that the persecutor's motive was anchored in the applicant's protected characteristic—not merely that the applicant happened to hold that characteristic when the persecution occurred.

For practitioners, the lesson is evidentiary: develop the record on motive at the earliest possible stage. The petitioners here had a plausible political-opinion claim, but the record was developed primarily around the property dispute and its consequences. The threatening calls before the Don Rafa incident, the colleague's murder, the Liberal Party brochure—these details were in the record, but they were not tied to Don Rafa or his associates. Without that connection, the political-opinion claim could not survive the "central reason" test. The case is a reminder that in mixed-motive asylum claims, chronology and specificity are everything. Generalized evidence of political activity, standing alone, rarely clears the nexus bar.



## Individual Apathy vs. Systemic Failure: The Reporting Requirement in "Unable or Unwilling" Analysis

The Tenth Circuit's recent decision in *Jimenez v. Bondi* offers a clarifying—if demanding—gloss on one of the more elusive requirements in asylum law: proving that a foreign government is unable or unwilling to protect an applicant from persecution. The case illustrates the fine line courts draw between isolated encounters with unhelpful police officers and evidence of systemic governmental failure, and it highlights the practical burden the reporting requirement places on applicants fleeing countries where corruption and intimidation are endemic.

## The Facts and the Framing

The petitioners, a Colombian family led by Wilson Jimenez and his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, fled Bogota after a series of escalating threats connected to Jimenez's investigation of a property occupied by a narco-trafficking guerrilla leader known as "Don Rafa." The threats began after Jimenez, a provisional lawyer and Liberal Party activist, looked into criminal activity at the property. Two armed men confronted Jimenez at his furniture factory, associates of Don Rafa later assaulted an employee while demanding to know the family's whereabouts, and a man grabbed Jimenez's young son outside his school. Before leaving Colombia, Jimenez reported the situation to a single police patrol officer, who told him to avoid trouble and that officers were themselves "contributing to the problem." The family then left for the United States.

The Tenth Circuit denied the petition for review on multiple grounds, but its analysis of the government-protection prong is doctrinally the most interesting. The court affirmed the BIA's finding that the petitioners had not established that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them—though the reasoning it deployed to get there raises questions about how much an asylum applicant must do before a court will credit claims of governmental complicity or impotence.

## The Reporting Requirement and Its Exception

The Tenth Circuit's framework for the unable-or-unwilling inquiry, drawn from its recent decision in *Singh v. Bondi*, centers on two evidentiary pillars: whether the government tried to stop the persecutors and whether it offered the applicant protection. Critical to both inquiries is whether the applicant gave the government notice of the persecution and an opportunity to respond. Without reporting, the court reasons, there is simply no way to evaluate how the government would have reacted.

The court acknowledges that a failure to report is not necessarily fatal. An applicant can satisfy the government-protection requirement by demonstrating that filing a police report would have been futile or dangerous. But the court describes this exception in passing and does not engage with what evidentiary showing would suffice to invoke it. In *Jimenez*, the petitioners argued that contacting the government would have been pointless because their persecutors were closely aligned with the administration of President Gustavo Petro. The court found this argument "unsupported by the record," but it is worth pausing on what the record would need to contain to support such a claim.

The petitioners did report—to a patrol officer who told them to leave. The court characterized this as a "one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer" that does not necessarily establish governmental inability or unwillingness, citing the Seventh Circuit's decision in *Osorio-Morales v. Garland* for the proposition that courts "often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures." This distinction between individual apathy and systemic breakdown is sensible in principle. But it raises a practical question: how many unhelpful officers must an applicant encounter before a court will infer a pattern? The opinion does not say, and its insistence that petitioners should have sought help "outside their immediate neighborhood or through Jimenez's law enforcement contacts" suggests the court expects applicants to exhaust multiple avenues of reporting before claiming futility—a requirement that sits uneasily with the acknowledged danger that reporting can itself provoke retaliation.

## Evidence of Government Action as Evidence of Government Willingness

The court's most analytically interesting move on the government-protection prong involves its treatment of the prior law enforcement searches at Don Rafa's property. The petitioners argued that Don Rafa's political alignment with the president made government protection futile. The court turned this argument on its head, pointing to evidence that "the government was aware of the property at issue and had attempted several searches and seizures to address the ongoing criminal conduct there." In the court's view, this evidence affirmatively counters the claim that the government was aligned with the persecutor or unable to control him.

This reasoning has a certain surface logic: if the government was actively trying to raid the property, it can hardly be said to be protecting the criminal operating from it. But the inference is more tenuous than the court acknowledges. Failed or unsuccessful law enforcement operations against a powerful criminal figure can support multiple interpretations. They might reflect genuine governmental effort stymied by institutional constraints, corruption at the operational level, or even a performative response designed to create the appearance of enforcement while allowing the underlying criminal enterprise to continue. The court treats the mere existence of searches as evidence of willingness to protect, without examining whether those searches were effective, whether they were followed by prosecutions, or whether they were undermined by corruption within the ranks. The opinion notes only that the searches were "seemingly unsuccessful," then uses their existence to undercut the petitioners' futility argument.

This framing effectively shifts the burden onto the applicant to prove not just that reporting would be futile, but that prior government actions against the persecutor were also futile or insincere. That is a demanding standard, particularly given the substantial-evidence review under which the court operates—where the BIA's findings stand unless "any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary." Under that deferential standard, evidence that the government attempted some action, even if that action failed, will almost always suffice to defeat an unable-or-unwilling claim.

## The Broader Doctrinal Tension

The tension at the heart of *Jimenez* is not unique to the Tenth Circuit. Courts across the circuits have struggled to define what an asylum applicant must show to prove that reporting to the police would have been futile. The general rule—that applicants must report and give the government a chance to respond—makes sense in countries with functioning law enforcement. But in contexts involving powerful criminal organizations, corrupt officers, or persecutors with political connections, the rule can create a catch-22: the applicant must report to trigger the government-protection inquiry, but the act of reporting may itself endanger the applicant and is unlikely to produce results if the government is genuinely unable or unwilling to help.

The Tenth Circuit's case law nominally recognizes this problem, but *Jimenez* suggests the exception is narrow in practice. The court's insistence that the petitioners should have reported through Jimenez's law enforcement contacts—his father still works in the criminal justice system—assumes a level of institutional trust and personal security that may be unrealistic for someone who has just been told by a patrol officer that police are "part of the problem." The opinion does not grapple with the possibility that an applicant in Jimenez's position might reasonably conclude that escalating a complaint through official channels would only expose him further to retaliation from Don Rafa's associates within the government.

## What Standard of Proof for Futility?

The opinion's most significant gap is its failure to articulate what an applicant must show to establish that reporting would have been futile or dangerous. The court says only that the petitioners' argument was "unsupported by the record." But what kind of evidence would have supported it? Country-condition reports documenting systemic corruption? Expert testimony about the futility of reporting to Colombian authorities in cases involving politically connected criminals? Evidence of retaliation against other complainants? The opinion offers no guidance, leaving applicants and practitioners without a clear roadmap for satisfying the futility exception.

This silence is consequential because the reporting requirement functions as a practical gatekeeper. If courts require applicants to demonstrate systemic governmental failure through external evidence but simultaneously treat evidence of any governmental action—however ineffectual—as evidence of willingness to protect, the futility exception risks becoming a dead letter. Applicants from countries with nominally functioning but deeply compromised institutions may find themselves unable to satisfy either prong: they cannot prove the government would have helped because they did not report, and they cannot prove reporting would have been futile because the government has taken some action, however inadequate, against the persecutor.

## Looking Ahead

*Jimenez* reinforces a trend in the Tenth Circuit toward demanding robust evidentiary records on the government-protection prong, particularly in cases involving private actors with alleged political connections. The decision underscores that asylum applicants cannot rely on isolated encounters with unhelpful officers or general assertions of governmental corruption. They must either report through multiple channels and document the government's inadequate response or come armed with compelling evidence—country conditions reports, expert testimony, documented retaliation—that reporting would have been affirmatively dangerous.

Whether this standard is appropriate depends on one's view of the institutional competence of immigration courts to evaluate foreign governmental capacity. The Tenth Circuit clearly believes that applicants should bear the burden of producing specific evidence rather than asking adjudicators to draw inferences from general conditions. But in doing so, the court sets a bar that may be prohibitive for the very applicants most in need of protection—those caught between powerful criminals and governments that are neither wholly willing nor wholly unable to help, but whose response falls into the murky territory in between.
