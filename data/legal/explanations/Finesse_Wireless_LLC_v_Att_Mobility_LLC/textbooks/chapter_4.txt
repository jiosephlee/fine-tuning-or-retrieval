Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC

Chapter 4: # The ’775 Patent—Failure to Prove Seven Required Multiplications from Three Signals

This chapter explains why the Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL as to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent: the trial record did not support the jury’s finding that the accused radios perform the seven specific third‑order multiplications the claim requires. It walks through the claim language at issue, the court’s construction, the evidence Finesse presented at trial, the substantive gap in that evidence, and why appellate attempts to fill the gap could not stand.

## Claim 1 structure (context for the disputed limitation)

Claim 1 of the ’775 patent claims a method performed “in a receiver, with a transmitter and the receiver being co‑located,” that generates intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) to cancel passive IMPs. The claim requires that the ICSs be generated “continuously and near real time” and be “using copies of transmitter signals of the transmitter.” The claim also locates where the passive IMPs are generated (in passive transmitter/receiver components downstream of the HPA and transmitter filter). These contextual elements matter because they tie the claimed technique to cancellation of passive IMPs using transmitter signal copies, but the appeal turned on a much narrower, algebraic limitation about how the cancellation signals are produced.

## The critical limitation — limitation 1[d]: seven enumerated third‑order products

Limitation 1[d] requires that “generating the ICSs is based on a power series description of a non‑linear process for generating the IMPs, and includes generating an n‑th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering” seven explicitly listed third‑order products:
- S1×S1×S2
- S1×S2×S2
- S1×S2×S3
- S1×S1×S3
- S2×S2×S3
- S1×S3×S3
- S2×S3×S3

The dispute focuses narrowly on whether the accused radios actually produce these seven enumerated multiplications (or otherwise satisfy that limitation) as the jury was instructed to find.

## Claim construction background: “three signals”—separately identifiable, not necessarily three unique signals

At summary judgment the district court construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to require that the three signals be “separately identifiable,” but the court declined to read the claim as requiring three distinct, unique input signals. The court relied on intrinsic evidence and inventor testimony and concluded that the amendment during prosecution to recite “three signals” did not amount to a clear and unambiguous disavowal of systems that in practice use two unique signals (for example, where one of those signals has a copy). Thus, for infringement the patent did not demand three different physical transmitter signals; it required three separately identifiable signal streams for use in the claimed multiplications.

That construction matters because Appellants argued the accused radios only use two unique signals (x1 and x2), while Finesse argued three signal terms are present in the accused system. But claim construction alone did not resolve whether the accused system actually performs the enumerated seven multiplications.

## What Finesse actually offered at trial

Finesse’s infringement opinion at trial rested principally on a Nokia technical document and Dr. Wells’ testimony interpreting it. The Nokia document, and Dr. Wells’ explanation of it, identified three distinct multiplication‑like operations appearing in the accused radios’ processing chain: examples cited include x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2' (x2 complex conjugate), and x1·|x2|·|x2| (where |·| is a modulus operation and x' denotes complex conjugation). The document also shows other algebraic terms and lists coefficients a0 through a9 in a formula, which Finesse later pointed to on appeal in arguing more multiplications exist in the code/documentation.

Dr. Wells, however, did not present testimony at trial mapping each of the seven claim‑listed products to specific operations in the Nokia document, nor did he demonstrate that the modulus and complex‑conjugate operations shown were equivalent to multiplying the three signal variables in the exact combinations recited in the claim. The district court relied on the trial testimony and the document to deny JMOL, but the Federal Circuit examined whether that proof was legally sufficient under the substantial‑evidence standard.

## The evidentiary gap: three distinct multiplications but no proof of the seven claimed products

The Federal Circuit identified the key shortfall: the Nokia material and the testimony identify at most three distinct multiplication‑type expressions, and there was no evidence that those expressions are repeated or otherwise instantiated so as to yield the seven enumerated products the claim requires. The opinion emphasized three points:

1. The claim lists seven explicit third‑order products of S1, S2, and S3. Proving infringement of limitation 1[d] required evidence that those seven specific combinations are generated (or an acceptable proof that equivalent operations produce the claimed results).

2. The Nokia document, as presented at trial, shows operations involving modulus and complex conjugation rather than raw multiplications of S1, S2, and S3 in the seven recited combinations. Converting between those listed operations and the claim’s multiplications is not self‑evident and was not shown at trial. For example, showing x1·|x2|·|x2| does not establish that S2×S2×S3 exists unless there is a record linking |x2| and x2' to the claimed S2 or S3 roles in a way that produces the required algebraic products.

3. Dr. Wells did not testify how the three distinct multiplications he identified correspond to the seven enumerated products. There was no trial‑record mapping that connected the three expressions to all seven claim terms, and there was no evidence that the accused system repeats or rearranges those three expressions to yield the full set of seven products required by the claim language.

Because the jury’s verdict depended on the expert testimony and the Nokia document, and that proof at most showed three distinct multiplications and did not tie them to the seven required combinations, the Federal Circuit concluded the evidence was insufficient as a matter of law for a reasonable jury to find limitation 1[d] met.

## Appellate attempts to supply missing mappings and why they could not help

On appeal, Finesse argued (and pointed to portions of the Nokia materials) that the accused system actually performs more algebraic operations—Finesse suggested there are ten multiplications listed in the documentation and that some subset could be the seven claimed ones. The Federal Circuit rejected this as post‑trial supplementation for two related reasons:

- The jury never heard the detailed mapping between the ten‑term list (or any particular subset) and the seven specific claim products; Finesse’s elaboration on appeal amounted to a new theory of infringement that was not supported by the trial record. An appellate court cannot affirm infringement based on arguments and mappings not presented to the jury.

- Even accepting that the document lists more algebraic terms, Finesse still failed to identify on the record which of those terms correspond to each of the seven claimed multiplications, and the document entries that were shown involved complex conjugates and moduli rather than plain multiplications of the S1/S2/S3 variables as claimed. Thus there remained no substantial evidence showing the accused radios actually generated the seven enumerated third‑order products.

The court therefore treated the appellate argument as insufficient to cure the trial‑record deficiency.

## Legal standard applied and the result

The Federal Circuit applied the regional‑circuit standard for JMOL denial review (Fifth Circuit): the court examines whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict, nonetheless points so strongly and overwhelmingly that reasonable jurors could not have reached the verdict. For factual findings the court asks whether there is substantial evidence to support the jury’s verdict. Applying these standards, the court held that no reasonable juror could find the seven specific multiplications required by limitation 1[d] were performed based on the evidence presented at trial.

Because limitation 1[d] was not supported by substantial evidence of infringement, the Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL as to all asserted claims of the ’775 patent. The court’s reversal of infringement also led it to vacate the damages award, since damages depended on a sustained infringement verdict.

## Takeaway for reading opinions about claim‑element proof

When a claim enumerates specific algebraic combinations or other discrete operations, a patentee must present evidence at trial that ties the accused device’s operations to those specific items. Expert testimony that points to similar but non‑identical operations (for example, expressions involving modulus or conjugation rather than the exact multiplications listed) or that offers new mappings only on appeal will not suffice. If the patent claims explicit, enumerated products, the record must show either that those exact products are produced or that the evidence admitted at trial proves equivalence in the manner actually argued at trial; absent that, JMOL is appropriate because the substantial‑evidence requirement is not met.
