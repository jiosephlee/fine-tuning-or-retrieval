Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC

Chapter 3: # The ’134 Patent—Sampling Limitation and Expert Testimony Breakdown

This chapter explains why the Federal Circuit held that Finesse’s proof on the ’134 patent failed as a matter of law. The core problem was proof of limitation 1[a], which requires that the receiver “over-sampl[e], at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals.” The court concluded that Finesse (the patentee) did not present substantial evidence that the accused radios’ receiver sampled a passband containing both types of signals, because the only clear mapping its expert offered was temporally and architecturally impossible and his trial testimony was internally inconsistent.

## Key limitation 1[a]: receiver must sample a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” to create a bit stream

Limitation 1[a] is a fact-bound, element-level requirement for infringement: the accused device must perform an act (over-sampling a passband to create a bit stream) on a passband that includes two kinds of signals simultaneously—“signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Because Finesse was the plaintiff, it bore the burden at trial of proving that the accused radios met this limitation. On appeal the Federal Circuit reviewed the denial of JMOL under Fifth Circuit law (de novo), measuring whether the jury’s verdict was supported by substantial evidence—i.e., whether a reasonable jury could have found for the patentee in light of the evidence presented.

## Finesse’s proof theory: Nokia diagram; RF ADC as the sampler; mapping “signals of interest” to DL(TX) reference and “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path

Finesse relied almost entirely on one theory: its infringement expert, Dr. Wells, pointed to a Nokia technical diagram that depicted an RF analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC) and various signal paths. Dr. Wells testified that the RF ADC was the claimed sampler that creates the bit stream and that the diagram’s legend—“Red – DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path”—showed that the RF ADC sampled both the “signals of interest” (which he equated with the DL (TX) reference) and the “interference generating signals” (which he equated with the modeled PIM path). His expert report made the same mapping (DL (TX) reference = signals of interest; modeled PIM path = interference generating signals). The jury accepted the infringement verdict based on that testimony.

## Temporal/architectural mismatch: modeled PIM path is generated downstream (e.g., in FPGA) after the RF ADC; cannot be sampled before it exists

Appellants countered—and the cross-examination highlighted—that the “modeled PIM path” shown in Nokia’s documents is generated downstream in the radio’s processing chain (in the GROOT FPGA) after the RF ADC has already sampled the incoming analog passband. In other words, the modeled PIM path is not an input present in the analog passband at the time of sampling; it is computed later from sampled data. That temporal and architectural point is fatal to Finesse’s theory if the modeled PIM path is what the patentee claims is the “interference generating signals”: a device cannot sample a signal that does not yet exist. If the modeled PIM path is the mapped interference source, limitation 1[a] cannot be satisfied because the receiver did not sample that signal as part of the passband.

## Cross-examination pivot: reference to x1 and x2 without clearly mapping them to the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals”

Faced with the temporal problem on cross-examination, Dr. Wells attempted to pivot by referring to “x1” and “x2,” labels that also appear on the Nokia diagram as the two inputs on the red path. He suggested there were “two signals on that red path” and at one point said “x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” But crucially he never gave the jury a clear, consistent mapping that x1 = (signals of interest) and x2 = (interference generating signals), nor did he explain how the modeled PIM path label related to x1/x2 in a way that cured the temporal problem. Across testimony spanning many pages, he repeatedly identified the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path as the claimed signals and did not plainly correct or reconcile that identification to show x1/x2 were the claimed signals sampled by the RF ADC.

## District court’s JMOL denial: inferred that x1/x2 were the claimed signals despite expert’s repeated identification of DL(TX) and modeled PIM path

The district court denied JMOL and sustained the jury verdict by treating Dr. Wells’ “two signals” references as an affirmative identification of x1 and x2 as the sampled “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” The court emphasized testimony it read as saying that two signals enter the RF ADC and that those are the claimed signals, and thus concluded there was sufficient evidence for the jury. The Federal Circuit, however, found that reading to be unwarranted: the expert’s testimony remained confusing and internally inconsistent, and the district court’s inference required piecing together fragments of testimony spread far apart in the record rather than relying on any clear, contemporaneous mapping offered to the jury.

## Federal Circuit’s reasoning: testimony confusing and self-contradictory; no clear mapping; reliance on Johns Hopkins v. Datascope regarding insufficiency when an expert’s contradictory testimony underpins the verdict

The Federal Circuit reversed. Two points guided the court’s analysis. First, substantial-evidence review requires that a reasonable jury had a reliable evidentiary basis for its finding on each claim limitation; ambiguous or self-contradictory expert testimony that does not clearly establish an element cannot supply that basis. Second, the court relied on its prior decision in Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp., which recognized that an expert’s contradictory testimony cannot be the linchpin of an infringement finding. There, as here, when the patentee’s case rests on an expert who offers inconsistent statements about a dispositive factual point (in Johns Hopkins, whether a device contacted tissue in three dimensions; here, whether the accused receiver sampled both required signal types), the evidence is legally insufficient to support the verdict. The Federal Circuit applied that principle: Dr. Wells repeatedly equated the modeled PIM path with the interference generating signals (which are generated post-sampling), and his later ambiguous references to x1/x2 did not amount to a clear, admissible mapping that established the receiver sampled both required signals.

## Result: no substantial evidence supports limitation 1[a]; reversal of JMOL denial as to all asserted ’134 claims

Because Finesse failed to present substantial evidence that the RF ADC sampled a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” the Federal Circuit concluded the jury’s infringement verdict could not stand. The court reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’134 patent. The decision underscores two practical lessons for litigators: (1) when a claim element turns on temporal or architectural facts (e.g., whether a particular signal exists at a particular point in the processing chain), the patentee must present affirmative, non-contradictory evidence mapping the claim language to the accused system; and (2) an expert’s confusion or internal inconsistency on a dispositive element can be fatal to the patentee’s case because a jury cannot be left to infer a critical mapping from scattered or equivocal testimony.
