Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC

Chapter 13: # Notes, Questions, and Problems

This chapter gives exercises and hypotheticals to rehearse the doctrinal lessons from Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC and to practice translating those lessons into litigation strategy when the claims at issue are mathematical or signal-processing methods. The Federal Circuit’s decision highlights three recurring themes in patent trials over algorithmic and signal-processing patents: (1) the need for a clear, consistent element‑by‑element mapping from claim language to accused device operations; (2) the limits of what can be counted as a “signal” or a “copy” of a signal for claim purposes; and (3) the evidentiary consequences when an expert’s testimony is internally inconsistent. The problems below ask you to apply those themes, suggest trial tactics, and provide concrete drafting and cross‑examination tools that would reduce the risk of reversal on JMOL.

## Problem: Can |x2| or x2′ count as S3 where the claim recites "three signals"? Explain your mapping and limits.

The ’775 claim language requires “three signals S1, S2 and S3” and further recites generating third‑order ICS terms such as S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, and S1×S2×S3, etc. The district court construed “three signals” to mean “separately identifiable” signals, not necessarily “unique.” The accused system, however, exposes a practical question: can mathematical transforms of a base signal—for example, the modulus |x2| or the complex conjugate x2′—be treated as distinct signals S3 for purposes of satisfying the claim?

Analytic approach. Begin with claim construction and intrinsic evidence. The starting point is the claim text: does the claim require “copies of transmitter signals” or simply “signals” generically? Here the ’775 patent repeatedly discusses generating ICSs “using copies of transmitter signals,” which suggests the claimed inputs should be signals that correspond in a straightforward way to transmitter outputs. That phrase will be important: a “copy” normally means a representation that preserves the essential information content of the original signal, not necessarily an arbitrary mathematical transform. Next examine the specification and prosecution history for any limiting statements addressing whether transforms like modulus or conjugation are contemplated.

Arguments supporting counting |x2| or x2′ as S3. One can argue that |x2| and x2′ are “signals” in any ordinary sense: they are time‑series functions (complex or real) derived deterministically from x2 and carried through the system as data. If the district court accepts the “separately identifiable” construction, then a modulus or conjugate is a separately identifiable stream (it has its own storage or register, and the system operates on that stream independently). From a functional perspective, the claim language identifying S1, S2, S3 does not explicitly preclude derived versions of the same underlying physical waveform. If the specification or prosecution history used language like “S1, S2, S3 represent signals generally” or an inventor testified transforms are contemplated, that supports counting |x2| or x2′ as separate signals.

Arguments against counting |x2| or x2′ as S3. On the other hand, if the claim or intrinsic record emphasizes “using copies of transmitter signals” then a transform that discards phase or otherwise alters the physical relation to the original may not be a “copy” in the sense meant by the patentee. The modulus |x2| loses the phase information of x2; the conjugate x2′ changes the phase sign. If the claimed invention is premised on multiplicative combinations of the original transmitter waveforms, equating those combinations with combinations that involve modulus or conjugates may change the physics of the cancellation mechanism. Further, if the prosecution history shows that the applicant added “three signals” to distinguish over prior art that used two real baseband signals, an argument can be made that the patentee intended three distinct physical or informational inputs, not repeated or transformed versions of only two underlying signals.

How to present the mapping in court. If you are the patentee, make the mapping explicit in the expert report and in testimony. Show (1) where in the accused system the stream |x2| or x2′ is produced and stored, (2) how that stream is functionally used as an input to the multiplicative operations, and (3) how the system treats |x2| or x2′ as distinct (e.g., separate buffer, different timing, different application). Tie this to the claim: explain why an operation that uses x1, x1, and |x2| corresponds to S1×S1×S3. If you are the accused infringer, emphasize that “separately identifiable” does not mean “separately originating” and show that every candidate S3 is merely an algebraic manipulation of x2 and not a distinct transmitter copy contemplated by the patent.

Limits and risk. Even where the court’s construction permits “separately identifiable” signals, mapping transforms to separate S‑labels carries risk. The mapping must be documented and explained repeatedly and consistently; a late or improvised mapping (for example, only developed at trial or on appeal) will be unpersuasive and can be characterized as litigation‑driven. Moreover, contrived mappings that require a single physical signal to play simultaneously the role of S2, S3, and S2’s conjugate invite the very kind of skepticism the Federal Circuit expressed when experts flip between inconsistent descriptions.

Practical conclusion. Whether |x2| or x2′ can count as S3 depends on claim construction and the quality of the evidentiary record. If the patent and prosecution history do not clearly exclude transforms, and the patentee provides corroborating system documentation and a consistent expert mapping showing the derived stream is treated as a distinct input in the accused device, a court may accept the mapping. Absent that, and especially where the claim or intrinsic record emphasizes “copies of transmitter signals,” the safer litigation posture for a patentee is to identify true, independently generated transmitter‑derived streams (or show the transform preserves the “copy” character) rather than rely on strained algebraic identifications.

## Problem: An accused radio computes ten third‑order terms. How would you prove which seven satisfy the claim?

This problem arises directly from the ’775 analysis, where Nokia documentation listed multiple multiplicative terms (ten, perhaps), and the jury was left without a clear explanation of which subset of those terms corresponded to the seven third‑order multiplications claimed. Proving the required seven is an exercise in precise, documentary, and experimental evidence.

Step 1 — identify the claimed algebraic pattern. Begin by writing out the claim’s required third‑order products explicitly: S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, S2×S3×S3. This is the baseline. Make a canonical representation so that you can map the accused device’s terms to these canonical products.

Step 2 — map candidate device terms. Next, collect every source that documents the accused radio’s third‑order computations: design documents, firmware or DSP source code, internal logs, DSP flow diagrams, comment blocks, mathematical expressions in white papers, and testbench outputs. For each documented term (for example, x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2′), prepare a table (for internal use) that shows the term, its algebraic form, and whether it can be rewritten to match a claimed product under reasonable algebraic identities or under the court’s claim construction (e.g., is |x1| equivalent to a separately labeled S1?).

Step 3 — demand operational evidence, not just labels. A label in a document is not enough. You must show the device actually computes the term in an operational sense and that the term is used to generate ICSs that are applied in the cancellation path. Good evidence here includes: code execution traces that show the operation performed at run time; synthesized hardware netlists or FPGA bitstreams indicating the multiplier trees and how many distinct multiplications are instantiated; runtime counters that show how many product outputs are produced per frame; and captured intermediate numeric vectors showing the products computed. When the accused system uses transforms like modulus or conjugation, the expert needs to explain how these map to the claimed products and whether substituting a conjugate for an S‑term preserves the intended cancellation effect.

Step 4 — show multiplicity and repetition. The claim requires seven multiplications. If the device’s documentation lists ten potential terms, you must (a) select which seven correspond to the claim’s set and (b) show that those seven are indeed produced and used. Demonstrate either that exactly those seven are computed (by tracing code or FPGA resources), or if more are computed, show that at least the claimed seven are among them and are selected/used for ICS formation. Where multiplications are produced by reusing a computed result rather than recomputing separate multipliers, demonstrate that the resulting outputs are identical to the claimed products and are used at the relevant points in the cancellation algorithm.

Step 5 — provide incontestable examples. Produce a worked numeric example where the accused radio’s inputs are known and the outputs of the multiplication stage are recorded. Show a concrete three‑sample period: list x1[n], x2[n], and x3[n] (or their derived forms), compute the accused device’s internal products as the device would, and show that the numerics equal the claimed products. A test where the device is forced to run with known inputs (test mode) and where the internal vectors can be logged is the most persuasive.

Step 6 — anticipate and neutralize alternative interpretations. Opposing counsel will likely point to alternative decompositions of the device’s ten terms that exclude some claimed multiplications or show that certain listed terms are mathematical intermediates never used in cancellation. Preempt by: (1) producing code comments or commit history showing the author intended those terms for cancellation; (2) showing signal paths where those outputs are fed forward; and (3) explaining why transforms (modulus/conjugate) do or do not defeat literal matching.

Trial presentation. Present the mapping in the expert report in a clear, consistent manner: numbered claim element, mapped device component, documentary exhibit citation, and demonstration artifact (log or trace). Use demonstratives to show the numeric example side‑by‑side with the claim product. Avoid equivocation or multiple inconsistent mappings; the Federal Circuit reversed on the basis that the expert’s testimony was confusing and internally inconsistent.

Standard of proof and litigation timing. Remember to meet the patentee’s burden to prove infringement by a preponderance of the evidence. Establish the mapping at summary judgment or at the Rule 50(a) stage if possible; otherwise, the record at trial must be coherent enough to survive JMOL review. If you are the accused infringer, focus your Rule 50(a) motion on pointing out gaps: absence of runtime evidence, lack of mapping between the device’s terms and the claim’s seven listed products, or logical impossibility (e.g., device only generates three distinct products).

## Problem: The ADC samples x1 and x2; a PIM model is computed later. Can plaintiff still meet ’134 claim 1(a)?

Claim 1(a) of the ’134 patent requires “over‑sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals.” The litigation posture in Finesse shows that where the accused radio’s modeled PIM path is generated after the ADC, the plaintiff’s expert mapped the “interference generating signals” to that modeled path, which created an obvious temporal problem: a claim that requires the receiver to sample signals that include the interference‑generating signals cannot be met if those interference‑generating signals do not exist until after sampling.

Legal and factual axes to consider. Two distinct questions must be addressed: (1) what do the claims mean by “interference generating signals” and (2) at what point must those signals exist for the sampling limitation to be met? The words “received signals” and “to create a bit stream” suggest that the receiver must sample actual analog signals present at the RF front end. If the “interference generating signals” are artifacts produced later in a digital model, they arguably are not “received” in the sense the claim requires. The intrinsic record and prosecution history can refine this: if the patent contemplates modeling the interference using copies of transmitted signals or using internal digital estimates derived from sampled signals, then a later‑computed model might nevertheless be sufficiently tied to sampled phenomena to satisfy 1(a). But the typical reading is that 1(a) looks to sampling of signals present on the analog passband.

How a plaintiff can succeed. To meet 1(a) despite the PIM model being computed later, the patentee should do one of the following:

- Show that the ADC actually samples analog evidence of the interference‑generating phenomenon. For external PIM, the IMPs originate from passive defects that cause spurious in‑band energy; if the RF ADC receives those spurious analog components, they will be present in the sampled bit stream. Produce spectrum captures, pre‑ADC analog traces, or bitstream captures that show the in‑band IMP energy is present in the samples.

- Show that one of the “signals of interest” or transmitter references is itself the “interference generating signal.” For example, if the transmitter leakage or downlink reference is sampled (or its effect is), then those sampled streams can count as the claimed “interference generating signals” even if the explicit PIM model is computed later.

- Demonstrate that the model is not an after‑the‑fact fabrication but is instead an algorithmic transformation that the receiver computes deterministically from signals it sampled. If the PIM model is computed by applying an invertible deterministic transform to an already‑sampled signal, then one can argue that the sampling limitation is satisfied because the underlying physical signal was sampled and the derivative model is simply a post‑processing representation.

- Produce device documentation showing that the modeled PIM path is a notional label for pre‑existing signals routed internally before the claimed sampling stage, i.e., show that what appears at trial as a “modeled PIM path” is in fact rooted in signals that exist on the red path before the ADC.

How a defendant should attack. If you are defending, emphasize the temporal sequence: sampling occurs first; the purported “interference generating signals” are output of a model produced later; signals cannot be sampled before they exist. Cross‑examine the plaintiff’s expert to force a clear mapping and to lock down timing. If the expert pivots mid‑testimony (as Dr. Wells did), use that to argue the evidence is insufficient as a matter of law.

Doctrinal takeaway. The patentee can only satisfy a sampling limitation that is temporally bounded at the analog, pre‑ADC stage by proving the claimed types of signals are present and sampled at that stage. Post‑processing models will not normally retroactively satisfy a limitation that requires sampling unless the model is shown to be a representation of signals actually present and sampled. The lesson of Finesse is practical: make the temporal mapping explicit in the record and be wary of relying on after‑the‑fact conceptual “paths” unless you can show the physical sampling included the relevant energy.

## Problem: Draft cross‑examination questions to test whether seven distinct multiplications are performed

The cross‑examination should aim to (1) show lack of proof that the claimed seven distinct products are produced, (2) expose any inconsistency between the expert’s report and trial testimony, and (3) force the expert to concede that core record documents do not show the seven multiplications. Below are sample questions organized to move from foundational facts to impeachment. Tailor each to the specific facts and exhibits.

Foundational identity and source
- Doctor, you agree that your infringement opinion is based principally on Nokia documents A, B, and C; correct?
- Exhibit B lists a set of multiplicative terms the implementation nominally uses; do you see that?
- The terms in Exhibit B are labeled as complex conjugates and moduli for certain variables; correct?

Mapping and algebraic equivalence
- You say that x1·|x1|·|x1| corresponds to the claim term S1×S1×S1; is that right?
- Do you agree that |x2| (the modulus) removes phase information that is present in x2?
- Do you agree that x2′ (the complex conjugate) is not algebraically identical to x2 unless x2 is real-valued?
- Which of the claim’s listed products require phase information from S3 to produce the asserted cancellation effect?

Counting and multiplicity
- Exhibit B lists ten possible product terms; is that your Exhibit 3?
- Of those ten, which seven do you contend correspond to the claim’s seven required multiplications? (Pause for an answer.)
- Can you point me to a single line of code, a single runtime trace, or a single DSP register dump where each of those seven outputs is computed and assigned to a distinct internal signal name?
- Is there any document in the product design that explicitly labels the outputs as S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, and so on—the seven claim products? If so, please point to it.

Usage and relevance
- Even if the device computes those ten intermediate terms, can you identify where in the cancellation path the claimed seven are selected and applied to cancellation? Point to the evidence.
- Is it possible that some of the ten terms are merely intermediate calculations that are subsequently discarded or combined into a fewer number of outputs?
- Do you know whether the hardware instantiates seven distinct multiplier units or whether it reuses multiplier hardware to compute multiple products by time‑multiplexing? Show me the evidence.

Prior testimony and consistency
- At your deposition you testified [cite page] that the modeled PIM path is generated in an FPGA downstream of the ADC; correct?
- Today you testified that the modeled PIM path is one of the “signals” sampled by the ADC; is that a fair read?
- Which of those two statements is correct? (If the witness hedges:) Isn’t it true that both statements cannot be simultaneously correct?
- You have not produced a testbench trace in which the numeric values of the seven claimed products are captured side‑by‑side; is that correct?

Hypothetical forcing questions
- Let us suppose the system uses only x1 and x2 as inputs and computes only three algebraically distinct products. Would that satisfy the literal claim requirement for seven distinct third‑order multiplications? (Goal: get witness to concede “no.”)
- If the system uses modulus and conjugates to generate ten variants, but none of those variants are labeled or used in a way that corresponds to the claim’s specific product set, would you still contend literal infringement? (Goal: make witness commit to necessity of mapping evidence.)

Impeachment with documentary contradiction
- Earlier you cited Document D as showing “x1·x1·x2′” is computed; Document D, however, shows simultaneous storage of only three product buffers. How do you reconcile that?
- You earlier told the court that x1 and x2 are the only signals sampled by the ADC; yet today you say the modeled PIM path is sampled. Which is true? (Use the inconsistency to undermine credibility.)

Effective cross‑examination will be crisp: demand specific document and line citations; request to mark precise code or netlist lines; and force the expert to admit the absence of runtime evidence when it does not exist. An admission that the expert lacks a single runtime trace for each claimed product is powerful JMOL fodder.

## Short answer: What record showing would avoid the "self‑contradictory expert" problem?

Conciseness matters here. To avoid the reversal seen in Finesse, a patentee should build an early, coherent, and corroborated record that ties each claim element to specific, contemporaneous artifacts. The minimal elements of that record are:

- A clear, element‑by‑element mapping in the expert report that cites specific exhibits (design docs, source code, FPGA bitstream, block diagrams) next to each claim limitation.

- Demonstrable runtime evidence: logs, register dumps, DSP vector captures, testbench traces, or oscilloscope captures that show the claimed signals exist and that the claimed computations are performed with numeric examples.

- Code or hardware documentation showing the exact arithmetic operations performed (for example, the multiplier tree or the portion of firmware that computes the products), with line numbers or netlist references.

- Demonstrative examples and small numeric test vectors in which the inputs are known, the intermediate products are recorded, and the final cancellation output is shown to use those products.

- Consistent testimony: the expert’s deposition, report, and live testimony should use the same mapping and should explain any necessary assumptions up front; if the expert must change a theory, the change should be disclosed and explained before or at summary judgment, not first raised at trial.

- Corroboration by company witnesses or authors of the documents, where possible, to show that the named document truly reflects the implementation rather than a high‑level conceptualization.

In short, avoid the prospect of an expert improvising at trial. The stronger and more contemporaneous the documentary and experimental evidence that map claim language to accused operations, the less likely the testimony will be characterized as self‑contradictory and thus insufficient as a matter of law.

## Checklist: Building an element‑by‑element mapping for math‑heavy method claims

Below is a practical checklist for counsel and experts preparing infringement proof for mathematical or signal‑processing method claims. Use it as a template when preparing reports, demonstrations, and deposition testimony.

1. Claim parsing
   - Transcribe each asserted claim and number each limitation.
   - Highlight temporally bounded actions (e.g., “over‑sampling … to create a bit stream”) and quantifiers (e.g., “three signals,” “seven multiplications”).

2. Claim construction liaison
   - Record the court’s claim construction for disputed terms.
   - Note whether constructions require “separately identifiable,” “unique,” “copies of transmitter signals,” or other qualifiers.

3. Identify candidate device artifacts for each limitation
   - For each claim limitation, list the device component(s) that could perform the limitation (hardware block, firmware function, FPGA module, software routine).
   - Cite the specific exhibit(s) (document name, version, page/line numbers, code lines, netlist label).

4. Temporal flow and causation
   - Produce a timeline diagram showing when each signal exists relative to sampling, filtering, and modeling.
   - Explicitly tie “receive,” “sample,” “compute,” and “apply” events to device clock cycles or frame indices.

5. Algebraic mapping
   - Write canonical algebraic forms for the claimed operations and for the accused system’s operations.
   - If transforms are present (conjugate, modulus), include algebraic identities and explain whether and how they permit mapping (and whether the mapping preserves required properties like phase).

6. Runtime corroboration
   - For every claimed computation, acquire runtime traces, logs, or bench captures showing the computation occurring.
   - If hardware reuses multipliers by time‑multiplexing, capture the scheduling and buffer names to show output identity.

7. Numerical example
   - Create one or more concrete numeric examples: pick specific input sample values, run the device (or a faithful model), and capture intermediate and final outputs.
   - Present the example in the expert report as an appendix.

8. Source and code evidence
   - Produce the minimal code or RTL snippet where the multiplication is implemented; annotate line numbers.
   - If code is obfuscated or optimized, obtain a developer declaration or a corroborating design note.

9. Usage evidence
   - Show that the outputs of the claimed computations are actually used to generate ICSs or other claimed outputs (not merely computed and discarded).
   - Trace the signal path from multiplication output to applicator (e.g., adder, cancellation injection point).

10. Address prosecution history and claim scope
    - Identify any amendments or statements arguably narrowing claim scope.
    - Prepare counterarguments or evidence showing no clear and unmistakable disavowal.

11. Consistency and disclosure
    - Ensure the expert report, deposition testimony, and trial testimony use identical mappings. If you must refine a mapping, supplement disclosures and explain why the refinement is based on new evidence (cite the new exhibits).

12. Anticipate attack
    - Prepare responses to likely defense points: lack of runtime evidence, use of transforms instead of raw signals, reuse of multipliers, and difference between “listed” vs. “used” terms.

13. Litigation readiness
    - Be ready to produce demonstrative animations showing data flow step‑by‑step.
    - Prepare for cross‑examination: have precise citations ready and know where your evidence is weakest.

Cross‑check this checklist with the GM of the case: if you cannot produce a runtime trace for a critical mapping by the close of fact discovery, consider whether the claim should be asserted or whether to seek technical stipulations.

---

These problems and solutions should help you practice the application of Finesse’s principles: clear mapping, temporal fidelity for sampling claims, and the necessity of contemporaneous documentary and runtime evidence when mathematical operations and signal transforms are in issue. In preparing for trial or summary judgment, treat mapping as a technical‑and‑litigation project equally: the courthouse will want concrete artifacts, not after‑the‑fact labels.
