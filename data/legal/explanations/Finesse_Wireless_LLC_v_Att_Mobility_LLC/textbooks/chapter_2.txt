Chapter 2: # Technical Primer: Intermodulation Products, PIM, and DSP Concepts

This chapter supplies the radio-frequency (RF) and digital-signal-processing (DSP) vocabulary and conceptual grounding necessary to understand the technical disputes in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC (the Federal Circuit opinion summarized above). The case turns on what signals are present where in a radio, when they are sampled, and what mathematical operations are performed on copies of those signals. Those are questions of engineering that translate directly into claim-element disputes (e.g., whether a receiver “samples a passband that includes” particular interference-generating signals, or whether a receiver “generates . . . n-th order” products from three signals). A lawyer who can translate between the legal claim language and the underlying signal flow and DSP operations will be better prepared to analyze infringement and invalidity theories and to evaluate expert testimony.

The sections below review the key concepts and then explain why those concepts mattered in the dispute before the court. Each section aims to be practically useful to a law student preparing to read expert reports and to craft or evaluate claim-chart evidence.

## Intermodulation products (IMPs) and passive intermodulation (PIM): internal vs. external sources

Intermodulation products (IMPs) arise when two or more signals mix in a nonlinear element and generate new frequencies that are algebraic combinations of the original frequencies (for example, f1 ± f2, 2f1 ± f2, 2f2 ± f1, etc.). In RF systems this can happen deliberately (e.g., mixers) or inadvertently in any non‑ideal component that exhibits nonlinear behavior. IMPs are particularly problematic when the new frequencies fall “in band” — that is, into the passband carrying desired signals.

The subset called passive intermodulation (PIM) refers specifically to IMPs produced by otherwise passive structures and connections: corroded connectors, loose metal contacts, metal fences near antennas, rusted junctions, or mechanical defects in the radio chain. Those elements are “passive” because they do not contain active semiconductor devices or amplifiers that intentionally mix signals, yet they can behave nonlinearly under high power and produce intermodulation.

Two distinctions are important for litigation and claim construction:

- Internal (or internal-to-the-radio) PIM: PIM generated by components that are physically inside the radio or inside the transmitter/receiver enclosure — for example, a bad connector in the transmit chain after the high-power amplifier (HPA) or in the antenna feed. When PIM is internal to the radio, it is often produced in or near the transmit chain itself and can be proximate in time and space to the receiver front end.
- External (or outside-the-radio) PIM: PIM generated by external objects or connectors outside the radio housing — for example, a metal fence near the antenna or a corroded adapter in the antenna line outside the radio. External PIM is still a source of interference, but its physical origin is outside the radio hardware.

The distinction matters for infringement when claims specify where and how the IMPs are generated (the ’775 patent focused on passive IMPs generated after an HPA and transmitter filter). It also matters for the practical feasibility of certain cancellation approaches: if the interference source is external and variable in time or location, a model-based cancellation scheme that depends on fixed channel estimates may be less effective than when PIM is produced by a stationary internal component.

In Finesse, the patents address mitigation of PIMs that are generated in “passive transmitter components of the transmitter and receiver components of the receiver after a high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter of the transmitter.” Whether the accused implementation relied on signals that represent the actual interference-generating physical processes at the time of sampling (versus a modeled representation generated later in the digital chain) became a dispositive factual question.

## Transmit/receive paths, co-located transmitter and receiver architectures

Modern wireless systems commonly colocate transmitter and receiver hardware (for example, in a base-station cabinet). Colocation raises the problem that transmitter power can leak into the receiver path and cause or exacerbate PIM. To reason about this you need a mental model of the common blocks and where signals originate:

- Transmit chain: baseband signal generation → digital-to-analog conversion (DAC) → upconversion to RF → power amplifier (HPA) → transmitter filter → antenna feed → antenna.
- Receive chain: antenna → receive filter(s) → low-noise amplifier (LNA) → downconversion to intermediate frequency or baseband → analog-to-digital converter (ADC) → digital baseband processing.

If the transmitter and receiver are physically close, powered transmit signals can interact with passive elements in either the transmit line, the antenna, or the receive line and generate PIM that then enters the receiver front end. The location of the nonlinear element (after the HPA, in the antenna feeder, at an external connector, etc.) and the point at which signals are sampled or copied determines the information available to any cancellation algorithm.

Two practical consequences for patent disputes are worth stressing:

- Timing and location of sampling: A claim that requires sampling “a passband of received signals that include signals of interest and interference generating signals” is only satisfied if the ADC (or a receiver sampling function) actually samples a waveform that contains both those components at the same time. If the alleged “interference generating signal” is only reconstructed or modeled later in a digital block (after sampling), an accused device does not literally sample that modeled signal at the RF ADC.
- Copies of transmit signals: Many cancellation schemes rely on a copy of the transmit waveform (obtained via couplers, taps, or by taking a copy from the baseband transmit path) so that the device can model how nonlinearities would create IMPs from those transmit signals. The provenance of the copy (analog tap vs. digital copy vs. post‑ADC reconstructed signal) affects whether the copy is an actual sampled input or a derived estimate.

The Federal Circuit opinion turned on precisely this: whether the “modeled PIM path” that an expert identified was a physical interference-generating signal that was sampled by the RF ADC, or whether that “modeled” signal was produced inside an FPGA after sampling and therefore not literally present at the sampling instant.

## Oversampling and decimation; creating and filtering bit streams

Understanding the terms “oversampling,” “decimation,” “passband,” and “bit stream” is essential to parsing claims and diagrams in RF/DSP patents.

- Oversampling: Sampling a continuous-time signal at a rate substantially higher than the Nyquist rate (twice the bandwidth of interest). Oversampling can simplify analog anti-aliasing filter design and enable digital filtering and decimation that more cleanly isolate narrow bands inside a wide RF passband.
- Passband and baseband: A passband is a frequency band containing signals of interest (for example, a carrier band at RF). Baseband refers to the signal spectrum shifted to be centered at zero frequency (I/Q representation). Many receivers downconvert the RF passband to baseband before or after sampling.
- Bit stream / sample stream: The ADC converts an analog waveform to a digital stream of samples (commonly called a bit stream or sample stream). Those samples are then processed by digital circuits (filters, decimators, mixers, multipliers).
- Decimation: Reducing the sampling rate by integer factors, typically performed by first applying an anti-aliasing digital filter (lowpass or bandpass) and then downsampling. Decimating filters are often used to isolate a narrower band (e.g., the “signals of interest”) from a wideband sampled stream.

Crucially for patent proof, whether the ADC samples a waveform that includes particular components depends on the order and placement of these stages. If the ADC samples the full RF passband (oversampling), then later digital decimation can separate different components into separate digital streams. But if a putative “interference generating signal” is only constructed by combining digital samples after the ADC stage (i.e., it is not a physical analog input at the ADC), then the ADC did not sample that particular waveform.

In practice, block diagrams in vendor documents often annotate multiple conceptual “paths” on the same chart. The legal problem is that labels like “modeled PIM path” can describe a software-generated estimate derived in the FPGA; such a modeled path is not the same as an analog waveform that was present on the antenna and sampled by the RF ADC. The case illustrates how an expert’s testimony must tie the claim terms to specific physical or sampled signals, not merely to diagram labels.

From an evidence perspective, a party seeking to prove literal infringement of a claim limitation like “oversampling . . . to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals” should produce: (a) the ADC functional description showing what analog spectrum was at the ADC input; (b) timing/ordering of blocks showing whether the alleged interference signal exists at or prior to the ADC; and (c) signal captures, oscilloscope traces, or other measurements demonstrating the two components are present in the sampled stream. Absent that, a modeled reconstruction generated only after sampling cannot satisfy a literal “sampling” claim element.

## Phase and amplitude adjustment; sub-sample phase shifts

Many cancellation schemes require aligning an estimate of an interference waveform to the observed interference so that subtraction is effective. Two basic alignment dimensions are amplitude (scaling) and phase (timing). Claims that require “phase and amplitude adjustment . . . in a closed loop manner” and that specifically require “sub-sample phase shifts” implicate concrete DSP techniques.

What these phrases mean in engineering terms:

- Amplitude adjustment: Multiplying the modeled interference samples by a scalar (or by a slowly time-varying gain) so the cancellation signal has the same magnitude as the unwanted component.
- Phase adjustment: Rotating the phase of the modeled samples so the cancellation signal lines up in time with the unwanted component. At baseband this is often implemented as complex multiplication by a phasor exp(jθ) or by applying a delay.
- Sub-sample phase shifts: A “sample” is a discrete-time tick. A sub-sample shift means aligning signals with a precision finer than the sample period: fractional delays. One cannot implement a non-integer-sample delay simply by shifting the sample index. Instead, one uses fractional delay techniques:
  - Interpolation / fractional-delay filters: Apply an FIR or IIR filter that approximates a time shift smaller than one sample period. These are standard DSP constructs (e.g., using Lagrange interpolation or Farrow structures).
  - Complex phasor multiplication (frequency-domain): For narrowband signals represented in complex baseband, a fractional phase shift corresponds to multiplying by a complex exponential whose phase varies continuously; this is equivalent to a fine-tuned time delay for the tone(s) of interest.
  - All-pass filters: Phase-response-shaping filters can impart frequency-dependent delays and are sometimes used to fine-tune phase without altering amplitude.
- Closed-loop adjustment: The cancellation system measures residual error (the remaining interference after subtraction) and adjusts amplitude/phase parameters adaptively to minimize that residual. Common adaptive algorithms include LMS (least-mean-square) and RLS (recursive least squares). Closed-loop here means an iterative feedback process that updates parameters in near‑real time.

For litigation, the engineering upshot is twofold. First, “sub-sample phase shifts” is a specific technical limitation that is not satisfied by coarse sample-aligned delays; expert testimony should explain what implementation accomplishes sub-sample shifts and point to code, filter coefficients, or design documents showing fractional‑delay filters or equivalent. Second, the location of the adjustment loop matters: if the estimate is adjusted in an FPGA after sampling, an accused system may still satisfy a claim that requires phase/amplitude adjustment — but that depends on whether the claim requires that the adjustments be performed on estimations that are themselves formed from sampled signals that included the interference-generating sources at the ADC.

In Finesse, claims required performing phase and amplitude adjustment on estimations of the interfering signals in a closed‑loop manner and performing sub‑sample phase shifts. Assessing whether the accused radios met those limitations required identifying the exact point where the estimates were formed and whether fractional-delay mechanisms were present.

## Model-based PIM cancellation (PIM‑C) features in commercial radios

Model-based cancellation is a practical, widely used approach to mitigate PIM in colocated transmit/receive systems. The steps of a typical model-based PIM cancellation (PIM-C) implementation are:

1. Obtain copies of the transmit signals. This can be done:
   - Analog tap: a small proportion of the analog RF transmit waveform is tapped off and fed into the receiver or into a modeling circuit.
   - Digital copy: copies of the baseband transmit samples (before DAC) are available in the digital domain.
   - Post-sampling reconstruction: some designs reconstruct a modeled version of a PIM source using signals that have already been sampled by ADCs.

2. Choose a mathematical model of the nonlinear process. Common choices include:
   - Power-series (polynomial) models: assume the PIM generation is approximated by a finite-order polynomial (e.g., third-order terms dominate).
   - Volterra series or memory-polynomial models: model nonlinearities with memory (so past samples influence the present nonlinearity).
   - Parametric or measured lookup-table models.

3. Generate candidate intermodulation terms (basis functions) from the transmit signal copies. For a third-order model with three input signals S1, S2, S3, the basis functions include triple products such as S1·S1·S2, S1·S2·S2, and so on (more detail in the next section). These basis terms are filtered and scaled to produce an estimated PIM waveform.

4. Align the estimated waveform to the observed interference (amplitude/phase/time) using the techniques discussed earlier (fractional delays, complex phasor adjustment).

5. Subtract the aligned estimate from the received samples (cancelation), and measure the residual error for closed-loop adaptation.

Practical radios implement subsets of this architecture. Vendor documents (as in the Nokia documentation cited in the case) often show flowcharts or formulae indicating which basis functions are used (for example, complex conjugate or modulus operations) and how many multiplications are performed. Translating those vendor documents into a claim chart requires careful mapping: which signals in the vendor diagram correspond to claim terms like S1, S2, S3? Are the required multiplications present? Are they performed on the exact signals recited by the claim or only on transformed versions (conjugate/modulus)? And crucially, were the signals used to produce the modeled PIM themselves sampled by the ADC, or are they derived later?

The Federal Circuit rejected the jury’s infringement finding in part because the plaintiff’s expert relied on a “modeled PIM path” that the record showed was generated inside an FPGA after sampling — so it could not have been sampled by the RF ADC as the ’134 claim required. The broader lesson: model-based blocks in vendor diagrams are not ipso facto “signals of interest” or “interference-generating signals”; the difference between an analog sampled waveform and a digitally constructed model must be carefully teased out.

## Multiplication of signals in DSP: baseband/complex representations, modulus (|x|), complex conjugate (x′)

Many PIM models and cancellation schemes operate in complex baseband. A short primer on complex signal operations clarifies why labels and operations matter when mapping to claim limitations that recite multiplications among named signals.

- Complex baseband representation: A real RF sinusoid can be represented in complex baseband as x(t) = I(t) + jQ(t), where I and Q are the in‑phase and quadrature components. Operations that are simple in the RF domain (e.g., frequency translation) correspond to complex multiplication at baseband.
- Complex conjugate x′ or x*: For x = I + jQ, the complex conjugate x′ = I − jQ. Multiplying x by its conjugate gives x·x′ = I^2 + Q^2 = |x|^2, which is real and equals the instantaneous power (magnitude squared) of the complex sample.
- Modulus |x|: Commonly used to mean magnitude (√(I^2 + Q^2)). In some DSP shorthand, |x| is used when the algorithm uses the magnitude waveform; in others, operations use |x|^2 (magnitude squared) because it is simpler to compute (no square root). It is essential to know which is intended because multiplication by |x| versus by |x|^2 or by x′ yields different algebraic terms.
- Multiplying signals: When claims recite multiplying three signals S1·S2·S3 (third-order products), in a complex baseband implementation the actual operations might be S1·S2·S3, or S1·S2·x2′, or S1·|x2|·|x2|, etc. Those are not algebraically equivalent in general, and the mapping must be shown explicitly.

The combinatorics for the third-order terms in the ’775 claim are concrete. Given three signals S1, S2, S3, the claim lists seven third-order products that form the basis set for third-order cancellation:

- S1·S1·S2
- S1·S2·S2
- S1·S2·S3
- S1·S1·S3
- S2·S2·S3
- S1·S3·S3
- S2·S3·S3

(There are seven distinct combinations if order and permutation symmetries are collapsed appropriately; the patent lists these representative groupings.) To prove infringement of a claim that requires producing those seven distinct multiplications, an accused device must be shown to form functions that are algebraically equivalent to those seven terms or to produce a set of products that can be shown to span the same function space used by the patentee’s model.

Two pitfalls arise in practice:

1. Two-versus-three-signal dispute: If the accused device only has two distinct input signals (call them x1 and x2), but the claim requires three separately identifiable signals S1, S2, S3, the question is whether two signals sufficed under the claim language. Some claim constructions permit “three separately identifiable” signals to include two distinct signals plus a copy or duplicate (for example, S2 = S3 or S1 = S2). Whether that satisfies the claim depends on prosecution history and claim language. If the patentee amended claims to say “three” to distinguish over prior art that used only two inputs, courts will scrutinize whether there was a narrowing disclaimer that excluded two-input implementations. The technical reality is that if one substitutes S3 = S2 in the list above, some distinct algebraic products collapse (e.g., S1·S2·S3 becomes S1·S2·S2), reducing the number of algebraically distinct products. The patent required seven distinct multiplications in the ’775 claim; an accused product that produces only three unique combinations cannot satisfy a claim that requires seven unless the record shows that the three listed products are carried out in such a way or repeated to produce the seven required products.

2. Conjugate/modulus versus plain multiplication: Vendor documentation often expresses basis functions in terms of conjugates or absolute values: x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2′, x1·|x2|·|x2|, etc. These are not the same as S1·S1·S2 in algebraic form unless one shows that the modulus or conjugate operations map to particular signals or that the product set spans the same subspace. For example, x1·x1·x2′ uses a conjugate on x2, which flips the sign of the Q component; x1·|x2|·|x2| uses magnitudes and yields a non-linear but real-valued scaling on x1. An expert must explain how the vendor’s combination of conjugate and modulus terms corresponded to or generated the seven algebraic third‑order terms recited in the claim, and whether any algebraic identities or filterings produce the claimed products.

In Finesse, the court concluded that the Nokia document relied on by the plaintiff listed three distinct multiplications and also used conjugate and modulus operations, and there was no evidence that the accused radios actually performed the seven claimed multiplications or repeated the three distinct operations so as to generate all seven. The expert testimony did not supply the necessary algebraic mapping at trial; on appeal the court found the record insufficient to support that aspect of infringement.

## Applying the technical concepts to legal proof: practical pointers

Translating the engineering ideas above into effective evidence for claim elements (or for defenses) requires attention to three recurring themes:

1. Tie claim terms to physical/sampled signals precisely. Labels in vendor diagrams (e.g., “modeled PIM path” or “DL (TX) reference”) are shorthand; an expert should explain whether the labeled path is (a) an analog waveform present at the ADC input, (b) a digital copy of a transmit baseband sample, or (c) a digitally reconstructed model generated after sampling. For a claim element that requires sampling the interference-generating signal, evidence must show the ADC input included that signal at the sampling time.

2. Show exact operations and algebraic equivalence. If a claim requires specific multiplicative terms among S1, S2, S3, an expert should map each claimed product to an explicit operation performed by the accused device (pointing to source code, fixed-point arithmetic blocks, DSP diagrams, or hardware multipliers). If the vendor’s documentation uses conjugates or magnitudes, the expert must show how those operations correspond to the claimed algebraic terms (or explain why the claim covers those transformed operations).

3. Explain timing and placement of adaptive loops and fractional delays. For claim limitations invoking closed-loop phase and amplitude adjustment or sub-sample phase shifts, a competent expert will identify the algorithm (LMS, RLS, or other), show where in the processing chain the adjustments occur, and document the implementation of fractional-delay filters, interpolators, or phasor adjustments. Demonstrating that an adjustment is “sub-sample” requires pointing to an implemented fractional delay or equivalent; mere assertion that a loop “tightens timing” is insufficient.

For litigators, the case teaches that confusing, internally inconsistent, or ambiguous expert testimony that does not cleanly map claim language to concrete elements in the accused system creates vulnerability to JMOL. When the accused architecture uses modeled signals constructed after sampling, claim elements requiring sampling of particular signals often fail on the record, because sampling occurs only once at the ADC. Similarly, when vendor documents list a set of DSP operations, cursory mapping to claim language without algebraic demonstration of equivalence or repetition will invite appellate reversal.

Conclusion

The Federal Circuit’s reversal in Finesse underscores that seemingly minor technical distinctions — whether a PIM estimate is “modeled” downstream of sampling, whether a multiplication uses a conjugate or an absolute value, whether a three‑signal product actually appears or is realized as fewer products — can be decisive in patent cases. This chapter has provided the core DSP and RF concepts you will encounter in such litigation and the practical ways those concepts intersect with claim construction and infringement proof. A careful lawyer will use this vocabulary to audit expert reports, demand precise mappings from claim terms to device elements, and make the timing and algebraic nature of operations a central focus of fact development and cross‑examination.
