Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC

Chapter 2: # Standards of Review and Claim-Construction Framework

This chapter explains the appellate lenses the Federal Circuit applied in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC and how those lenses framed the court’s evaluation of the jury verdicts and the district court’s JMOL/new‑trial rulings. Two distinct review tracks are important in this opinion: (1) the standard for reviewing the denial of JMOL (and denial of a new trial) which follows regional‑circuit law (the Fifth Circuit here), and (2) the standard for claim construction, which the Federal Circuit reviews de novo with any subsidiary factual findings reviewed for clear error. Understanding how those standards operate and which party carried which burdens on appeal explains why the court reversed JMOL for both patents despite some contested procedural rulings below.

## JMOL standard (regional circuit / Fifth Circuit)

The Federal Circuit applied the Fifth Circuit’s standard in reviewing the denial of JMOL. Under that standard the denial is reviewed de novo but with the factual posture that the appellate court must view the trial evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict winner. Reversal is required only if the evidence “points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Practically, that means an appellate court will overturn a jury verdict for insufficiency of evidence only in rare circumstances where the evidentiary record, taken as a whole (and viewed in the non‑movant’s favor), cannot support a contrary verdict. The Federal Circuit applies that regional rule but also asks whether the jury’s factual findings are supported by substantial evidence.

## Substantial‑evidence review of jury infringement findings

For factual findings underlying infringement, the court asked whether “a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” That is the substantial‑evidence standard the Federal Circuit applied when assessing whether the jury’s finding of infringement could stand. Substantial evidence review is more demanding than mere plausibility: the question is whether the record contains sufficient evidentiary support such that reasonable jurors could have reached the verdict. Where the patent owner’s case rested heavily on expert testimony that was internally inconsistent, the court may determine no reasonable juror could have relied on that testimony to find the claim limitation satisfied.

## Claim construction: de novo with clear‑error review of underlying facts

Claim construction remains a question of law reviewed de novo on appeal, but any underlying factual findings that informed the construction are reviewed for clear error. Thus, the Federal Circuit independently determines the ordinary and customary meaning of claim language, consulting intrinsic evidence first and giving deference only to the district court’s subsidiary factual findings (for example, findings about how a term was used in prosecution or about what a skilled artisan would understand) unless those findings are clearly erroneous.

## Construction specific to the ’775 patent: “three signals S1, S2 and S3”

Applying the claim‑construction rules, the district court construed the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in the ’775 patent to mean the signals must be “separately identifiable” but need not be “unique.” The Federal Circuit treated that construction as grounded in the intrinsic record and the prosecution history, and it accepted the district court’s use of inventor testimony as extrinsic evidence supporting a construction that the three labels can represent signals that are the same or different so long as they are separately identifiable in the processing. This construction matters because the infringement question turns on whether the accused systems supply three separately identifiable inputs (even if one is a copy) and whether the accused multiplications correspond to the seven claimed operations.

## Prosecution history and scope: no clear and unambiguous disclaimer to unique inputs

The opinion reiterates the usual rule for prosecution‑history disclaimer: a patentee must make a “clear and unambiguous” disavowal to lose claim scope. The Federal Circuit examined the prosecution record where the applicant originally overcame prior art (Filipovic) and amended claims to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3.” The court concluded that amendment and the prosecution arguments did not amount to the sort of clear and unmistakable surrender of claim scope that would confine the claims to embodiments having three distinct unique inputs. In short, amending to recite “three signals” did not, on the intrinsic record, amount to an express covenant that excludes systems that in practice use copies or non‑unique inputs, given the prosecution explanations. That conclusion framed the infringement inquiry for the ’775 patent: the question was not solely one of claim scope, but whether the proof at trial actually showed the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications using three separately identifiable signals.

## Preservation and waiver context; how the Federal Circuit approached waiver

Preservation issues affected the district court proceedings. The district court found the defendants had waived the argument that “three signals” must be unique because they did not raise that point in a Rule 50(a) motion at the close of evidence. The Federal Circuit acknowledged that waiver ruling but did not rest its decision on waiver alone. Instead, the panel addressed the merits of the prosecution‑history/scope question (rejecting a clear‑disavowal theory) and then proceeded to evaluate whether the evidence at trial was sufficient to support the jury’s findings. For both patents the court ultimately reversed because it concluded the evidentiary record—particularly the way the patentee’s expert framed and mapped signals and multiplications—was insufficient as a matter of substantial evidence to support the jury verdicts. The important practical lesson is twofold: litigants should preserve Rule 50(a) arguments to avoid waiver; and on appeal the Federal Circuit may reach the merits (or an alternative ground) if it can do so while respecting standards of review.

## How the standards steered the court’s outcome in this case

The interaction of these standards explains the result. Under the Fifth Circuit JMOL framework the Federal Circuit asked whether reasonable jurors could have found infringement given the trial evidence. Under substantial‑evidence review the court examined the expert testimony and the technical documents relied on at trial. Under claim‑construction rules it assessed the meaning of “three signals” and reviewed prosecution history for any clear disclaimer. The court concluded that, even accepting the “separately identifiable” construction and finding no clear prosecution disclaimer, the patentee’s expert testimony was internally inconsistent (for the ’134 patent) and the documentary and expert evidence did not show the seven claimed multiplications (for the ’775 patent). Because those failures meant no reasonable jury could have reached the infringement verdicts, the Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s denials of JMOL and vacated damages. The opinion thus illustrates how the interplay of regional JMOL standards, the substantial‑evidence inquiry, and de novo claim construction (with clear‑error review of underlying facts) determines appellate review of jury infringement findings.
