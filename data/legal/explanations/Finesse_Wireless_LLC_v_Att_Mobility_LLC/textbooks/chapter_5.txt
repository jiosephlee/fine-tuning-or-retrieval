Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC

Chapter 5: # Damages, Disposition, and Doctrinal Takeaways

## Damages award
The jury awarded Finesse a lump-sum damages verdict of $166,303,391 for the remaining life of the ’134 and ’775 patents. After the verdict, AT&T and Nokia moved for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) and, alternatively, for a new trial on damages; the district court denied both motions. On appeal the Federal Circuit reversed the denials of JMOL of noninfringement for both patents and vacated the damages award. Because the court found the jury’s infringement findings for both patents were not supported by substantial evidence, the damages award could not stand.

## Disposition
The Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s refusal to enter JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’134 patent and the ’775 patent. Because those reversals eliminate the predicate for any damages, the court vacated the $166,303,391 award. The opinion awards costs to the defendants–appellants. The court did not reinstate or remand a different damages finding; the damages judgment was vacated in light of the noninfringement holdings.

## No need to reach new‑trial‑on‑damages arguments
Although the appellants had sought a new trial on damages (a standard the Fifth Circuit reviews for abuse of discretion), the Federal Circuit did not decide those arguments on the merits. Once the panel concluded that the jury verdicts of infringement lacked substantial evidentiary support and reversed JMOL of noninfringement, the damages award was rendered moot and was vacated. In practice, if a court reverses infringement on JMOL, downstream issues about the size or form of a damages award need not be reached unless the case is remanded with a surviving infringement determination.

## Key lessons for litigating complex signal‑processing patents
The opinion illustrates several practical and doctrinal points that are especially important in cases in which liability depends on technical expert mappings and on careful alignment of claim language with device operation.

First, experts must map claim elements to accused devices clearly and consistently at trial. The Federal Circuit reversed the ’134 infringement verdict in large part because the patentee’s expert gave internally inconsistent testimony about which signals corresponded to claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Self‑contradictory expert testimony that is essential to the patentee’s case can be treated as insufficient to carry the plaintiff’s burden.

Second, respect the physical signal flow and timing constraints of the accused systems. A recurring factual flaw here was attempting to treat as “sampled” a signal that, according to the defendants’ technical documents and the district court record, is generated downstream of the analog‑to‑digital converter. The court emphasized that sampling cannot logically occur before a signal is generated; if a claim requires sampling a passband that includes a given source signal, the patentee must prove that the receiver actually samples that source signal rather than a later, internally generated model or estimate.

Third, documentary evidence must actually disclose the claimed operations, not merely something that can be stretched into correspondence on appeal. For the ’775 patent, the Nokia materials relied on at trial listed operations involving moduli and complex conjugates and showed only three distinct multiplications, whereas the claim required seven specific third‑order multiplications of three signals. The Federal Circuit held there was no trial evidence showing the seven claimed multiplications were performed (and the patentee’s attempt to supply a mapping on appeal could not cure that gap). Put simply: if a claim recites particular mathematical operations or a particular sequence of steps, the patentee’s trial record must show how the accused implementation performs those same operations.

Fourth, do not expect appellate briefing to substitute for a developed trial record. The court repeatedly noted that mappings or explanations first offered on appeal (or late in briefing) were not presented to the jury and therefore could not sustain the verdict. Appellate courts review the evidence that was actually adduced at trial; novel theories or detailed mappings must be presented at trial and tested by opposing counsel and the finder of fact.

Fifth, be careful in prosecution and claim drafting about the scope you accept or argue for, but recognize that narrowing amendments do not automatically equate to clear disavowal. The panel declined to find a “clear and unambiguous” surrender of two‑signal systems merely because the applicant amended claims to recite “three signals” during prosecution. Nevertheless, prosecution history can matter, so patentees should be mindful that explicit statements to overcome prior art can be construed as limiting.

Taken together, these points underscore fundamental trial‑level obligations in technically complex cases: present an internally coherent expert story, tie every element to documents or demonstrable device behavior, honor the system’s physical causation and timing, and preserve your theories in the trial record rather than relying on appellate reconstruction.
