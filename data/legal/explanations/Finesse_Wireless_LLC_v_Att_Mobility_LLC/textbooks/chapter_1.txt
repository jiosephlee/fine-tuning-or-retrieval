Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC

Chapter 1: # Framing the Doctrine: Proving Patent Infringement and Standards of Review

This chapter sets out the doctrinal and procedural frame that governs jury trials and appellate review in patent cases. It explains the elements a patent plaintiff must prove to establish infringement, the burden of proof and how the jury and the trial judge divide responsibility, and the mechanics and standards of judgment as a matter of law under Rule 50. The recent Federal Circuit opinion in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC, reversing the denial of JMOL and vacating a large damages award, provides the principal vehicle for illustrating how these rules operate at the interface of expert proof, claim language, and appellate review.

## Elements of patent infringement and the all‑elements rule

Patent infringement analysis is claim‑driven. A patent claim defines the metes and bounds of the patentee’s exclusionary right; infringement exists only if the accused product or method contains every limitation of an asserted claim, either literally or under the doctrine of equivalents. This is the “all‑elements” or “all‑limitations” rule: the patentee bears the burden of proving, element by element, that the accused device practices the claim language as construed by the court.

In practice, the all‑elements rule shapes both expert proof and litigation strategy. The plaintiff must identify, and the jury must be able to infer, how each claim limitation is present in the accused device or acts. That mapping is often performed by expert testimony that connects technical disclosure about the accused product to the court’s claim constructions. If the plaintiff fails to identify a required limitation or shows it only conjecturally, the absence of proof on that element is fatal to an infringement verdict.

Finesse illustrates these principles clearly. The asserted claims in the ’134 patent required that the accused receiver “over‑sample[] . . . a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals.” The jury was entitled to consider whether the accused radios satisfied that limitation, but only if the evidence—which in this case centered on expert mapping of signals in a Nokia technical document—supported the requisite showing. The Federal Circuit reversed because the plaintiff’s proof on the claimed “interference generating signals” element depended on an expert’s shifting and internally inconsistent testimony about which signals were present at the analog‑to‑digital converter. The court treated the inconsistency as more than a credibility dispute; because the plaintiff had rested its case on that expert mapping, and that mapping was contradictory and legally insufficient to establish the limitation, the all‑elements rule was not satisfied as a matter of law.

The lesson is concrete: a claim limitation is a discrete evidentiary hurdle. Even if other evidence suggests infringement in a general sense, failure to prove a single claim limitation—because the evidence does not establish that particular factual predicate—dooms the claim. Expert testimony is an important tool for satisfying the rule, but expert opinions must be coherent, traceable to the intrinsic claim language, and anchored in admissible, consistent factual foundations.

## Burden of proof: preponderance of the evidence on infringement

The patent plaintiff carries the burden of proving infringement by a preponderance of the evidence—a standard less than proof beyond a reasonable doubt but still requiring that the asserted proposition be more likely true than not. This burden is assigned to the patentee at trial and remains theirs throughout the litigation unless the defendant admits a limitation or stipulates to facts establishing infringement.

Because the preponderance standard is fact‑based, its application turns on the sufficiency and persuasiveness of the evidence submitted to the jury. The plaintiff cannot rely on jury sympathy or general technical plausibility; the evidence must enable a reasonable juror to conclude more likely than not that every claim limitation is met. This often means presenting demonstrative evidence, technical documentation, and expert testimony that identifies specific structures or steps in the accused devices corresponding to the claim limitations.

The Finesse opinion underscores the allocation of this burden. The court emphasized that Finesse “bore the burden of proving the accused radios sample a passband of signals that includes both the ‘signals of interest’ and ‘interference generating signals.’” Because Finesse’s proof on the relevant limitation depended on an expert who equivocated about which items in a technical schematic corresponded to the required signals, the evidence did not suffice to meet the preponderance standard for that claim element. When a patentee’s case turns on one expert’s testimony and that testimony is internally contradictory on a critical point, the preponderance standard may not be satisfied as a matter of law.

## Jury role versus judge role in patent trials

The separation of roles between judge and jury in patent trials is well settled. Claim construction—determining the meaning and scope of the claim language—is a question of law for the court. The court’s constructions frame the jury’s task. The jury’s role is to find the underlying historical and technical facts—e.g., what the accused device does, how the accused device operates, whether a given component corresponds to a claim limitation—applying the court’s claim constructions. Credibility determinations, weighing of conflicting expert opinions, and resolution of factual disputes are classic jury functions.

This allocation produces two recurring practical points. First, the judge’s claim construction is the linchpin of the infringement inquiry; parties must think early about how claim language will be read by the judge because that reading dictates the content of expert testimony and jury instructions. Second, because the jury decides fact questions, appellate review is limited; courts will not ordinarily substitute their view of contested factual inferences for those of jurors unless the evidentiary record cannot support the jury’s finding.

Finesse highlights the interplay. The district court had previously construed certain claim terms (for example, determining that “S1, S2 and S3” in the ’775 patent need be “separately identifiable” but not “unique”), and that construction set the parameters for the jury’s fact‑finding. The jury concluded the accused radios satisfied the constructions, but the Federal Circuit reversed for lack of substantial evidence because the plaintiff’s proofs—again primarily an expert mapping—were legally deficient under the court’s claim constructions. The opinion thus shows how claim construction (judge) and factual mapping (jury) work together, and how an appellate court polices the sufficiency of the jury’s factual conclusions against that combined legal–factual framework.

## JMOL under Rule 50(a)/(b): timing, preservation, and grounds

Rule 50 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure governs judgment as a matter of law (JMOL). The Rule has two critical stages that litigants must understand to preserve appellate rights.

First, Rule 50(a) permits a party to move for JMOL during trial—specifically, after the opposing party has been fully heard on an issue but before submission to the jury. A Rule 50(a) motion must identify the law and the fact issues on which the moving party seeks JMOL; it serves two functions: it affords the trial judge a chance to grant immediate relief and it preserves the issue for appeal if the motion is later renewed following a jury verdict. If the moving party fails to make a timely Rule 50(a) motion, it typically waives the right to pursue a renewed motion after the verdict.

Second, Rule 50(b) allows a renewed motion for JMOL after the jury returns its verdict. The Rule 50(b) motion can be considered only if the moving party made a proper Rule 50(a) motion. When reviewing a Rule 50(b) motion, the trial court applies the same legal standard as in the Rule 50(a) context—asking whether reasonable jurors could reach a contrary conclusion in light of the evidence—and may enter judgment as a matter of law if it concludes no reasonable jury could have found for the nonmoving party.

Substantively, JMOL is appropriate when there is legally insufficient evidence to support a jury verdict on an essential element of the claim. That insufficiency may take several forms: the plaintiff’s evidence might be purely speculative, internally inconsistent, unsupported by a factual basis, or otherwise incapable of supporting a reasonable juror’s verdict. Importantly, JMOL is not an invitation to weigh evidence or reassess credibility; rather, it tests whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict winner, is such that reasonable minds could not differ.

Finesse demonstrates the mechanics and stakes of Rule 50. Appellants moved for JMOL of noninfringement after trial. Their motions raised the foundational argument that Finesse had failed to prove specific claim limitations. The Federal Circuit considered whether the district court’s denial of those motions could stand in light of the evidentiary record. The court concluded that where the patentee’s proof rests on an expert whose testimony is internally contradictory as to a critical limitation, JMOL must be granted because no reasonable jury could have found the element proved. The opinion also touches on preservation issues: for some arguments (for example, the character of “S1, S2 and S3”), the appellants contended waiver for failing to move at Rule 50(a) stage; the panel analyzed waiver but ultimately resolved the case on the merits for the elements at issue.

Practically, Rule 50 timing is a strategic imperative. Moving parties must identify the precise insufficiency during trial; plaintiffs must anticipate and shore up weak evidentiary links where they intend to rest their case on expert testimony.

## Regional‑circuit standard for reviewing denial of JMOL (Fifth Circuit “overwhelming evidence” formulation)

When a district court denies a Rule 50(b) motion, the appellate standard of review is governed by the law of the regional circuit. The Federal Circuit canvasses the controlling regional standard and applies it when reviewing denials of JMOL. In the Fifth Circuit—the regional circuit applicable to Finesse—the denial of JMOL is reviewed de novo, but with an exacting formulation: reversal is warranted only if, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict winner, the evidence “points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” That language reflects a high threshold: the appellate court must conclude that no reasonable juror could have reached the verdict based on the evidence, even when all reasonable inferences and credibility choices are resolved in favor of the prevailing party.

This regional standard differs in texture from the Federal Circuit’s own “substantial evidence” test applied to review of jury findings (discussed in the next section), but the two are related. The regional “overwhelming evidence” formulation preserves deference to jury factfinding and limits appellate reversal to cases of clear evidentiary insufficiency.

In Finesse, the Federal Circuit applied Fifth Circuit law to review the district court’s denial of JMOL. The panel articulated the regional standard and then applied it to the record, concluding that the plaintiff’s expert testimony was so contradictory and insufficient on essential limitations that reasonable jurors could not have found infringement on the asserted claims. Where the patentee’s proof rested on an expert’s equivocation about which signals were present at the sampling stage, and where the technical documents demonstrated that the particular “modeled” signal was generated after sampling, the court found the evidence tipped overwhelmingly in favor of noninfringement on that element. Under the Fifth Circuit formulation, this kind of internally inconsistent, legally inadequate evidence fails even when viewed most favorably to the verdict winner.

## Federal Circuit standards: substantial‑evidence review of jury findings; de novo review of claim construction

The Federal Circuit applies two principal standards on appeal from patent trials. First, claim construction is a question of law reviewed de novo, with the trial court’s subsidiary factual findings at construction reviewed for clear error. This means the appellate court reexamines the judge’s legal interpretation of claim terms without deference, but it will respect any factual findings that undergird those constructions unless they are clearly erroneous. Because claim meaning controls infringement analysis, de novo review of claim construction is often outcome determinative.

Second, factual findings by the jury are reviewed under the substantial‑evidence standard. A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have reached the conclusion in light of the evidence presented at trial. This standard is deferential: appellate courts do not reweigh evidence or reassess witness credibility. However, substantial evidence is not a rubber stamp. If the record lacks any competent evidence on an essential element, or if the only evidentiary support is plainly inadequate—such as conclusory expert testimony with no factual anchoring, testimony that is self‑contradictory on a key point, or an absence of proof as to an indispensable claim limitation—an appellate court will reverse.

Finesse demonstrates the interplay of these standards. The panel noted the court of appeals reviews claim construction de novo but did not need to reexamine certain constructions because its reversal rested on the insufficiency of evidence to prove the court’s own constructions. For the jury’s factual findings, the court applied the substantial‑evidence inquiry and concluded the record could not sustain the verdict. The court relied on its precedent (for example, Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.) recognizing that where a patentee’s case depends on an expert who gives internally inconsistent testimony about a dispositive fact, the testimony may be insufficient as a matter of law to support the verdict. The Federal Circuit therefore balanced deference to jury determinations with the obligation to ensure that factual findings rest on legally sufficient evidence.

Two procedural corollaries follow from this framework. First, where the patentee’s evidence is narrowly focused—say, a single expert mapping the accused device to claim language—the expert’s testimony must be coherent and adequately supported; otherwise appellate courts can and will overturn a jury verdict. Second, because claim construction is legal, litigants must preserve construction arguments at the district court stage and develop the record on how construction affects the mapping to the accused device; absent a preserved construction dispute, appellate review is truncated.

Conclusion and practice points

The Finesse decision exemplifies how evidentiary sufficiency, expert testimony, and the procedural mechanics of Rule 50 converge in patent litigation. The patentee must prove each claim limitation by a preponderance of the evidence; if the proof rests on expert testimony, that testimony must be internally consistent, rooted in factual record evidence, and expressly tied to the court’s claim constructions. Trial counsel should anticipate Rule 50 timing requirements, present clear element‑by‑element mappings in trial evidence and jury instructions, and prepare to challenge at the Rule 50(a) stage any theory the opponent advances as the linchpin of its case. Appellate counsel should remember that the standard governing reversal of a district court’s denial of JMOL is determined by the regional circuit and that the Federal Circuit will police the sufficiency of the jury’s verdict under a substantial‑evidence standard while reexamining claim scope de novo.

For law students, the key takeaways are these: (1) infringement is a claim‑by‑claim, element‑by‑element undertaking; (2) the preponderance burden rests on the patentee; (3) the judge decides claim meaning, the jury decides whether the accused device meets those meanings; (4) Rule 50 motions are technical but potent—move early and precisely; and (5) appellate review affords deference to juries but will not sustain verdicts grounded in legally insufficient expert testimony or where the evidence cannot, as a matter of law, support an essential claim limitation. Finesse is a cautionary illustration of those principles in action.
