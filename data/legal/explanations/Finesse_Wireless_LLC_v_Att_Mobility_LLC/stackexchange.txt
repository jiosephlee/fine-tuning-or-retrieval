Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC

### How does the Federal Circuit reconcile Fifth Circuit JMOL review with its own "substantial evidence" standard for infringement findings?
Question:
The opinion says denials of JMOL are reviewed de novo under Fifth Circuit law and will be reversed only if the evidence points so overwhelmingly to one side that no reasonable jury could find otherwise. Yet it also says a jury's infringement finding is reviewed for substantial evidence. Practically, which lens controls the outcome here? Are these just two ways of stating the same inquiry on appeal, or is there a distinct two-step analysis (regional-circuit JMOL standard layered over Federal Circuit patent-specific substantial evidence review)?
Answer:
Short answer
They’re not two separate, sequential tests that pull in opposite directions. Finesse applies the Fifth Circuit’s JMOL procedural rule (because denials of JMOL are reviewed under the regional circuit’s law) but decides the ultimate question by asking whether the jury’s infringement finding is supported by substantial evidence. In practice the Federal Circuit treats the regional JMOL framework and the patent “substantial evidence” formulation as the same, outcome-determinative inquiry: could a reasonable jury — viewing the record in the light most favorable to the verdict — have found infringement? If not, reversal follows.

Why and how the opinion says this
- Procedural locus: the court begins by stating the governing procedural rule: “We review a district court’s denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit, here the Fifth Circuit.” (Opinion, Discussion I.) It then quotes the Fifth Circuit formulation: denials of JMOL are reviewed de novo and are reversible “only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” (Id.)
- Substantive framing: immediately after, the opinion states the patent-specific substantive standard it will apply to the jury’s infringement finding: “We review a jury’s finding of infringement for substantial evidence,” and defines substantial evidence as meaning “a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” (Id.)
- How the standards operate together: the opinion applies both formulations in the same breath and then performs the same inquiry — asking whether the record supports a reasonable jury verdict. The court’s reversals of JMOL denials are expressly premised on the conclusion that “the jury’s infringement verdict is not supported by substantial evidence.” (See holdings on the ’134 and ’775 patents.)

Practical conclusion
The regional-circuit JMOL rule governs the procedural posture (we review the denial), but the Federal Circuit resolves the appeal by applying the substantial-evidence question the opinion articulates: could a reasonable jury, viewing the evidence favorably to the verdict, have found infringement? Because the two formulations ask essentially the same thing (whether reasonable jurors could have reached the verdict), the court treats them as a single, collapsed inquiry rather than a distinct two-step test. Finesse demonstrates that approach by invoking the Fifth Circuit’s JMOL language and then reversing because the infringement findings lack substantial evidence.

### When does contradictory expert testimony require JMOL rather than letting a jury weigh credibility?
Question:
For the '134 patent, the panel held Dr. Wells’s testimony was too confusing/contradictory to support the verdict, citing Johns Hopkins v. Datascope. The district court inferred he actually meant x1/x2 were the claimed signals despite repeated references to the modeled PIM path. What's the line between permissible inference-building by the factfinder and an impermissible attempt to salvage a case with self-contradictory testimony? Is there a doctrinal test or set of factors (e.g., frequency of contradiction, lack of reconciliation, centrality to an element) that triggers reversal?
Answer:
Short answer
There is no single bright‑line rule, but Finesse (following Johns Hopkins) shows the court will take the case from the jury and grant JMOL only when the plaintiff’s proof of an element rests on an expert’s testimony that is internally contradictory, irreconcilable, and central to the claim so that no reasonable juror could accept it. Put differently: when the expert is the only source of proof for a claim element, the expert’s testimony must be coherent and explain how the accused device meets the element; repeated, unreconciled contradictions (especially ones that create a physical impossibility) will not suffice to support the verdict.

Why (the doctrine and standard)
- JMOL review (Fifth Circuit standard applied in Finesse) asks whether, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence “points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” (Finesse, citing Wi‑LAN). Finesse also reviews infringement findings for substantial evidence — i.e., whether a reasonable jury could have found for the patentee in light of the trial record.  
- Courts have held that an expert’s testimony can be the only evidence on a point, but that testimony must be sufficiently clear and non‑contradictory to support a verdict. Johns Hopkins v. Datascope is the principal precursor cited by Finesse: there the Federal Circuit held that an expert’s opinion that an accused device “contacts the vessel in three dimensions” could not support a literal‑infringement finding when the same expert elsewhere said the device contacts the vessel in only two places — the contradictions left no reasonable basis for the jury’s literal‑infringement finding. Finesse applies that principle to reverse JMOL where the patentee’s case depended on Dr. Wells’ shifting testimony. (Finesse, pp. quoted at J.A. 15010–19; opinion at pp. 10–12).

What the Finesse opinion treated as decisive (factors the court relied on)
The opinion identifies several interrelated features that made the expert’s testimony insufficient as a matter of law:

1. Centrality to a claim element: Dr. Wells’ mappings were the plaintiff’s proof that the accused radios sampled both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” — a required claim limitation. Because that mapping was essential, contradictions were dispositive. (Finesse at pp. 7–12).

2. Internal contradiction and confusion: Dr. Wells repeatedly and consistently mapped the “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, but the modeled PIM path is generated after sampling (i.e., it could not have been sampled). When confronted, Dr. Wells began saying there are “two signals” (x1 and x2) but never clearly or directly mapped x1/x2 to the claimed terms; his testimony therefore remained internally inconsistent and confused. The court characterized this as a “confusing change of course” and “nothing clear about” his testimony. (Finesse, citing J.A. 15010–19 and district court record).

3. Lack of reconciliation or detailed explanation: the expert did not give a clear, detailed reconciliation of the conflicting statements — no straightforward mapping, no explanation of how the modeled PIM path could be both generated post‑ADC and be the “interference generating signals,” nor an explicit statement that x1/x2 were meant in place of the modeled PIM path. The panel emphasized that piecing together snippets ninety pages apart is not an adequate substitute for coherent testimony. (Finesse at pp. 10–12).

4. Physical impossibility or contradiction with the device operation: the expert’s mapping, if taken at face value (modeled PIM path = interference generating signal), produced a logical impossibility (the receiver allegedly samples a signal generated only after sampling). Contradictions that create such impossibilities are especially fatal. (Finesse at pp. 21–22).

5. Exclusive reliance on the expert: the opinion stresses that the patentee “rest[ed] its case on an expert’s self‑contradictory testimony.” When the party with the burden rests on such testimony, reversal may follow. (Finesse quoting Johns Hopkins).

Practical rule of thumb (how courts will apply these considerations)
- If the expert is the sole or principal evidentiary source for a claim element, make sure the expert: (a) expressly maps each claim limitation to specific structures/operations of the accused device, (b) gives a consistent account, and (c) if there is apparent tension with device operation or documentary evidence, provides a clear reconciliation.  
- If an opponent can show the expert repeatedly made contradictory identifications, failed to reconcile them, and those contradictions go to an essential claim element (or create a physical impossibility), JMOL is likely appropriate because no reasonable juror could find for the patentee on that element.  
- By contrast, if there is other non‑contradicted evidence supporting the mapping, or the expert’s testimony can reasonably be harmonized, then credibility is for the jury.

Applying it to Finesse (concise)
Finesse reversed because (1) infringement turned on whether the RF ADC sampled the claimed interference signals; (2) Dr. Wells’ testimony was the patentee’s proof of that mapping; (3) his testimony repeatedly identified the modeled PIM path as the interference signal even though that path is generated after sampling (a clear operational contradiction); and (4) he never actually or clearly said x1/x2 were the claimed signals nor reconciled the inconsistency — the patentee asked the courts to stitch together scattered transcript fragments instead of offering coherent testimony. Under those circumstances the panel concluded no reasonable juror could rely on the expert’s testimony to find infringement.

Bottom line
There is no fixed counting formula (e.g., “three contradictions = JMOL”), but courts will grant JMOL when an expert’s testimony — which is necessary to prove a claim element — is internally contradictory, unreconciled, and produces an outcome that no reasonable juror could accept (particularly where the contradiction conflicts with the device’s actual operation). Finesse and Johns Hopkins set the governing examples of that doctrine.

### Must the '134 patent's "interference generating signals" be present in the received passband pre-ADC, or can a modeled/derived internal signal qualify?
Question:
The court found no infringement because the accused radios' RF ADC could not have sampled the modeled PIM path—it was generated downstream after sampling. Does the claim language ("over-sampling ... a passband of received signals") categorically require that the 'interference generating signals' be actual received RF content pre-ADC? Could a digitally generated model of PIM ever count as an 'interference generating signal' under this claim, and if not, is that due to claim construction, written description, or just the evidence presented?
Answer:
Short answer
The Federal Circuit treated the ’134 claim’s “over‑sampling … a passband of received signals” limitation as requiring that the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” be among the received signals actually sampled by the RF ADC. A PIM signal that is only produced later inside the radio (i.e., generated downstream of the ADC as a modeled/derived signal) will not satisfy that limitation unless the patentee proves the modeled signal corresponds to something that was in the passband at the time of sampling. In this case the court held the evidence did not do that and therefore the verdict of infringement was not supported.

Why (what the opinion says)
- The court framed limitation 1[a] of claim 1 expressly: “over‑sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals … wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals” (opinion reproducing claim language). The patentee (Finesse) bore the burden of proving the accused radios “sample a passband of signals that includes both the ‘signals of interest’ and ‘interference generating signals.’” (Op. § I.A.)
- The court relied on the undisputed operational fact in the record that the Nokia radios generate the “modeled PIM path” inside the GROOT FPGA after the RF ADC sampling. The court therefore concluded that “if the modeled PIM path is the claimed ‘interference generating signals,’ then limitation 1[a] cannot be met because the accused radios’ receiver does not sample a passband of received signals that includes interference generating signals since those signals are not generated until after sampling occurs.” (Op. pp. 21–22 (citing trial transcript).)
- The court’s decision was grounded in insufficiency of the evidence: the plaintiff’s expert repeatedly and inconsistently mapped the claim terms to the Nokia diagram (at times pointing to a modeled PIM path that is generated post‑ADC), and the court found that conflicting, confusing testimony inadequate to carry Finesse’s burden. The court reversed JMOL of noninfringement because “the jury’s infringement verdict is not supported by substantial evidence.” (Op. pp. 21–22.)

What that means for modeled/derived signals
- A modeled or computed signal will not automatically qualify as an “interference generating signal” under limitation 1[a] if that modeled signal is produced only after the ADC has sampled the passband. The claim text (and the court’s analysis) ties the limitation to “received signals” that are being over‑sampled.
- That does not foreclose every possibility that a digital/model‑derived quantity could be treated as the claimed “interference generating signal.” The opinion focuses on the evidentiary reality: if the model is simply a downstream construct with no showing that it corresponds to (or was derived from) actual components of the received passband as sampled, it cannot meet the claim. If, conversely, a patentee can show the modeled signal is nothing more than a faithful representation or extraction of components that were actually present in the received passband at the time of sampling, then the modeled signal could—factually—be shown to satisfy the limitation. The court’s holding turns on the record evidence and the expert’s mapping, not on a broader claim construction exclusion of every internally generated signal.

Is this claim construction, written‑description, or evidence?
- The opinion treated the issue as an evidentiary one: Finesse had the burden to prove the ADC sampled both kinds of signals, and the court found the expert testimony and documentary record did not substantiate that mapping. The court did not adopt a new or narrow claim construction that generically forbids any modeled signals; instead it applied the claim language (“passband of received signals”) to the facts and rejected the proof offered. (See Op. § I.A.)
- The opinion does not rest on a written‑description or enablement ground. Its reversal was for lack of substantial evidence supporting the jury’s finding that the accused devices met limitation 1[a].

Practical takeaway for litigation/claim drafting
- If your claim requires sampling a passband that “includes” interference‑generating signals, you must be able to show (with clear expert testimony and documentary support) that the accused ADC actually sampled those signals — or that any model used post‑ADC is demonstrably derived from sampled passband components so that it is functionally the same signal for purposes of the claim.
- Absent that showing, a post‑ADC modeled/derived signal will be vulnerable to a noninfringement JMOL or directed verdict challenge, as happened here.

References to the opinion
- Claim limitation and plaintiff’s burden described at Op. § I.A (reproducing claim 1[a]).  
- Court’s finding that the “modeled PIM path is generated in the GROOT FPGA” and therefore cannot have been sampled by the RF ADC, and its conclusion that if the modeled path is the claimed interference‑generating signal limitation 1[a] cannot be met (Op. pp. 21–22).  
- Court’s reversal based on insufficiency of the expert evidence and lack of substantial evidence for the jury verdict (Op. pp. 21–22).

### What exactly must be preserved in a Rule 50(a) motion to avoid waiver of a JMOL theory on appeal?
Question:
The district court deemed AT&T/Nokia to have waived the argument that S1, S2, S3 must be 'unique' because it wasn't raised at Rule 50(a). On appeal, the panel discussed prosecution history and claim scope anyway but reversed on different grounds (insufficient proof of seven multiplications). In practice, how specific must a Rule 50(a) motion be to preserve a claim construction-related noninfringement theory? Is pointing to 'failure of proof on element [d]' enough, or must the party flag the uniqueness point and its legal basis (e.g., disclaimer) to avoid waiver?
Answer:
Short answer
The opinion shows that merely saying “failure of proof on element [d]” is risky if your noninfringement theory depends on a particular claim‑construction or prosecution‑history point (e.g., that “three signals” means three unique signals). To avoid waiver you should make the specific legal theory clear in your Rule 50(a) motion: identify the claim language at issue, say why (and on what legal basis) that language cannot cover the accused product (for example, prosecution disclaimer or an argued construction), and tie that legal point to the record evidence (explain concretely why the evidence does not meet the element as you construe it).

Why the Finesse opinion points that way
- The district court held AT&T/Nokia waived the “uniqueness” theory because it was not raised in their Rule 50(a) motion. The opinion records that the district court thought the uniqueness argument had not been preserved (see J.A. 38, 40). That is the posture in which the court refused to grant JMOL on that ground.
- The Federal Circuit nevertheless examined the prosecution history / claim scope on the merits and rejected Finesse’s disavowal argument (the panel concluded the amendment did not amount to clear and unambiguous disavowal) (see J.A. 109–14). But critical for the appeal, the court reversed on a different, factual ground: the patentee’s expert never proved the seven claimed multiplications (the Nokia document showed at most three distinct multiplications and the expert did not map them to the seven claimed multiplications), so no reasonable jury could have found infringement (see J.A. 26485–86; J.A. 14966–70; discussion at J.A. 38–39, J.A. 26486). For the ’134 patent, the opinion also emphasizes that the patentee’s expert gave internally inconsistent testimony and failed to map the accused device to the claimed signals (see J.A. 15010–19; J.A. 10–11). 

What that means in practice (concrete preservation checklist)
1. Identify the element and your specific legal theory. Don’t rest on a generic “failure of proof on element [d]” if your JMOL theory turns on a particular claim‑construction or prosecution‑history point. Say, e.g., “JMOL: element 1[d] not proved because ‘three signals S1,S2,S3’ requires three unique input signals (prosecution disclaimer/amendment).”
2. State the legal basis plainly. If you rely on prosecution disclaimer, point to the amendment/exchange and explain why it is “clear and unambiguous” (cite the record pages you will rely on). The Finesse opinion focuses on the prosecution record when addressing surrender/disavowal (J.A. 109–14).
3. Tie the legal theory to the record evidence. Explain why the evidence fails under that construction: point to the specific documents, testimony, and the mapping (or lack of mapping) between the accused product and the claim terms. The Federal Circuit reversed because the expert never mapped the three listed multiplications to the seven claimed multiplications, and the Nokia documents did not show repetition (J.A. 26485–86; J.A. 14966–70).
4. If you have alternate grounds, preserve them too. If you can show both (a) claim construction/prosecution‑history noninfringement and (b) lack of evidentiary mapping, state both in Rule 50(a). The district court’s waiver ruling in Finesse illustrates the risk of not flagging the claim‑scope argument; the panel’s ultimate reversal on evidentiary grounds shows the practical importance of preserving and clearly articulating both kinds of arguments (J.A. 38–40; J.A. 26486).
5. If your theory depends on expert testimony weaknesses, point those out specifically in the motion. Finesse turned on the expert’s inconsistent or unhelpful testimony (e.g., not mapping x1/x2 to claimed terms) (J.A. 15010–19). Call that out in Rule 50(a).

Bottom line
Rule 50(a) preservation requires more than a blanket “insufficient evidence” label when your noninfringement argument rests on a discrete claim‑scope or prosecution‑history contention. The Finesse opinion illustrates two things: a district court may treat an unargued claim‑construction theory as waived (J.A. 38, 40), and an appellate court will still examine the prosecution history and evidence if presented on appeal — but you cannot rely on the appellate court to rescue an unpreserved, under‑developed theory. So be specific: identify the claim term, state the legal basis for your construction/noninfringement, and tie it to the record in your Rule 50(a) motion.

### Did amending from "two or three signals" to "three signals S1, S2, S3" create a prosecution disclaimer against two-unique-signal implementations?
Question:
Appellants argued Finesse surrendered scope by changing 'two or three' to 'three' during prosecution to overcome Filipovic. The panel said no 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal and read the claims as requiring three 'separately identifiable' signals, not necessarily three unique inputs. How is that consistent with the typical inference that narrowing amendments surrender at least some scope? What made this amendment and argument insufficient to constitute disclaimer, and how much weight did the panel give to the distinction between passive vs. active IMPs in assessing scope surrender?
Answer:
Short answer: No. The panel concluded the amendment from “two or three signals” to “three signals S1, S2 and S3” did not effect a clear and unambiguous prosecution disclaimer of two‑unique‑signal implementations. The court applied the familiar Federal Circuit rule that a narrowing amendment only surrenders claim scope if the patentee clearly and unambiguously disavows subject matter during prosecution — and found that did not happen here. See opinion at J.A. 110–11 (citing Continental Circuits).

Why that result fits the governing doctrine
- Legal standard: The Federal Circuit requires a “clear and unambiguous” disavowal in the file history to read a prosecution argument or amendment as a disclaimer of claim scope. A mere narrowing amendment does not automatically disclaim all intermediate territory unless the patentee’s prosecution statements make the surrender unmistakeable. (Opinion cites Continental Circuits, J.A. 110–11.)
- Application here: Finesse amended the claims and argued over Filipovic largely on the ground that Filipovic addressed active IMPs, not the passive IMPs the asserted claims target. The examiner’s rejection was overcome “in part by explaining ‘Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs’ and not passive IMPs” (J.A. 985; J.A. 1006–07), and the panel read that prosecutorial context as showing the amendment was directed to the passive/active distinction rather than an intent to disclaim two‑signal implementations (J.A. 110–11).
- Claim construction and extrinsic evidence: The district court had construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable” but not “unique,” and credited inventor testimony that S1, S2, S3 can be the same signal or different signals (J.A. 109–15; J.A. 115). The panel adopted that understanding: at most the amendment required three separately identifiable signals, not three distinct/unique inputs (J.A. 110–11). Because there was no explicit prosecution statement saying “we give up two‑signal implementations” or equivalent unmistakable language, the court declined to treat the amendment as a disclaimer (J.A. 110–11).

Why a narrowing amendment didn’t automatically surrender scope here
- Narrowing amendments create a risk of surrender, but that risk converts into actual disclaimer only when prosecution arguments or the amendment itself clearly and unambiguously exclude the subject matter. The panel found the amendment plus the prosecution remarks did not do so: the patentee emphasized the passive vs. active IMP distinction when distinguishing Filipovic, and never made a clear, express caveat that two‑input systems were excluded (J.A. 985; J.A. 110–11).
- Put another way: the amendment changed claim language, but the file history shows the reason for the change (distinguishing Filipovic on active/passive IMPs), and intrinsic/extrinsic evidence supports an interpretation that the claim requires three “separately identifiable” signals rather than three unique signals. That combination defeated an inference of unequivocal surrender.

How much weight the panel gave to the passive/active IMP distinction
- Substantial weight. The panel expressly relied on Finesse’s prosecution argument that Filipovic was about active IMPs, not passive IMPs, as a key reason the amendment did not amount to a clear disavowal of two‑signal systems (J.A. 985; J.A. 110–11). The court treated that factual/prosecutorial context as the operative explanation for why the amendment was made — therefore not a plain attempt to carve out two‑signal implementations from the claims.
- The panel did not treat the passive/active point as the only relevant factor; it also relied on the prior claim language, the amendment, the district court’s construction, and inventor testimony (J.A. 109–15). But the passive/active distinction was the central prosecution justification that undercut any inference of surrender.

Bottom line for practice
- A narrowing amendment can trigger surrender, but proving disclaimer requires clear, unambiguous file‑history statements. Here, the patentee’s amendment and its arguments to the examiner were framed as distinguishing passive vs. active IMPs, and the intrinsic and inventor testimony supported a non‑exclusive reading of “three signals.” Those facts were sufficient for the panel to conclude no prosecution disclaimer of two‑signal implementations occurred (J.A. 110–11).

### How specific must the plaintiff’s mapping be to show the '775 patent’s seven required multiplications are actually performed?
Question:
The Nokia document identified only three distinct multiplications and included modulus and complex conjugate operations. The panel faulted Finesse for not showing that all seven claimed multiplications were performed or explaining any repetition/mapping. What level of evidentiary detail is required to prove a 'seven multiplications' limitation—must the patentee identify each multiplication explicitly, or can they rely on mathematical equivalences/combinations if clearly explained to the jury? Do operations on |x| and x' ever qualify as 'multiplying S1, S2, S3,' and what proof is needed to equate them?
Answer:
Short answer

The patentee must present trial evidence that lets a reasonable jury conclude each claim element is met — here, that the accused product actually produces the seven third‑order products recited in the claim. That can be done either (1) by identifying each of the seven multiplications in the accused system, or (2) by presenting a clear, contemporaneous, principled mapping (e.g., algebraic equivalence or repetition) showing how the device’s listed operations correspond to the seven claimed products. Mere post‑trial argument or an unexplained inference from fewer distinct operations is insufficient. The Federal Circuit reversed because the Nokia document only showed three distinct operations (x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2', x1·|x2|·|x2|), Dr. Wells never tied those three to the seven claimed multiplications, and Finesse attempted to fix the mapping only on appeal. See Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T, No. 2024‑1039, slip op. at pp. 24–26 (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025).

Why — legal standard and how it was applied

- Burden and substantial‑evidence standard: The patentee bears the burden to prove infringement and must produce substantial evidence that the accused device meets each claim limitation. A jury verdict must have evidentiary support such that a reasonable jury could reach the same conclusion. Finesse, slip op. at pp. 6–7 (citing Wi‑LAN/Apple standards).

- Element‑by‑element proof: For a limitation that requires “generating … S1×S1×S2 and S1×S2×S2 …” the patentee must show the accused system performs the seven specific multiplications recited. The court required either direct proof that the seven multiplications occur or a trial‑record mapping that establishes equivalence or repetition that yields the seven products. Finesse, slip op. at pp. 23–26.

- Why Finesse lost: The Nokia document presented only three distinct multiplications and used modulus (|x|) and complex conjugate (x') operations rather than plain multiplications of the raw signals x1 and x2. The court held there was no evidence those three listed operations were repeated or otherwise mapped to all seven claimed products, and the expert never explained at trial how the three correspond to the seven. The court rejected the patentee’s appellate attempt to supply the missing mapping. Finesse, slip op. at pp. 24–26.

When mathematical equivalence can help — and what proof it requires

- Mathematical equivalence is allowed, but it must be shown to the jury at trial by admissible evidence — not invented on appeal. An expert may prove that certain device operations are algebraically equivalent to the claimed multiplications (for example, that multiplying x by |x| or x' yields the same third‑order term as S1×S1×S2 in the claim under the asserted claim construction), but the expert must

  1. identify which physical or software signals in the accused product correspond to S1, S2, S3;
  2. explain, step‑by‑step, how each claimed multiplication is produced by the device (pointing to code, block diagrams, signal flows, or outputs); and
  3. if using equivalences (e.g., involving modulus or conjugation), show the algebraic identity and why the claim language covers that transformed signal or why the transformed signal is equivalent for purposes of producing the claimed IMP cancellation term.

- The court specifically flagged that the Nokia document “does not disclose multiplying x1 and x2, but their complex conjugate and modulus,” and that mapping S3 onto x2, x2' and |x2| without explanation was flawed. Thus, to rely on modulus or conjugation you must expressly show (at trial) how those operations produce the same claimed products. Finesse, slip op. at pp. 24–25.

Practical checklist for trial proof (what a patentee should present)

- Explicit identification in the record of the accused device’s signals that correspond to S1, S2, S3 (no after‑the‑fact inferences).
- For each of the seven recited products, either (a) evidence that the device actually computes that product, or (b) an expert demonstration (with citations to device documents/code/hardware) that some listed device computations are algebraically identical to the claimed products or that outputs repeat to yield the seven products.
- If modulus or complex conjugate are involved, concrete testimony and analysis showing the equivalence to a plain multiplication of the claimed signals (and why the claim language embraces that form).
- Avoid relying on a chart/document that lists fewer distinct operations without also presenting the mapping or repetition evidence to the jury.

Bottom line

You do not have to literally recite seven separate lines in a document if you can prove at trial that the accused system produces all seven claimed products (by direct identification, repetition, or a clear algebraic mapping). But the proof must be presented and explained to the jury in the trial record — vague inferences from fewer listed operations or post‑trial clarifications on appeal will not satisfy the substantial‑evidence requirement. The Federal Circuit reversed here because Finesse failed to do that: the Nokia document showed only three distinct operations involving |x| and x', and the expert never tied those operations to the seven claim terms at trial. Finesse, slip op. at pp. 24–26.

### Why did the Federal Circuit vacate the damages award instead of rendering judgment of zero after finding noninfringement?
Question:
Given the court reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims, it then 'vacated' the damages award. Is vacatur just the standard label when liability falls away, or does it preserve any possibility of further proceedings on remand (e.g., different claims, different products)? In what scenarios would the Federal Circuit instead enter judgment of zero damages rather than vacate?
Answer:
Short answer
The Federal Circuit vacated the damages award because it reversed the jury’s infringement findings for all asserted claims — the damages verdict had no legally cognizable basis once liability was undone. The opinion says so plainly: “Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we vacate the damages award.” Vacatur is the routine remedy where the liability underpinning a damages award has been invalidated; it removes the jury’s money award rather than substituting a specific damages figure.

Why vacatur here (what the opinion shows)
- The court reversed JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of both patents. The jury’s $166,303,391 lump-sum award was expressly for infringement of those claims. Once the appellate court removes the infringement finding, the damages award “has no legal footing” — the opinion ties vacatur directly to that reversal.
- The panel did not say it was entering a new, final damages judgment on appeal (for example, “judgment for defendants” or “$0”); it simply vacated the award. That is the course the opinion follows.

What vacatur does (as shown by the opinion)
- Vacatur wipes out the jury’s damages verdict that depended on the now-reversed infringement finding. The opinion’s disposition — “REVERSED AND VACATED” — effectuates that result.
- Vacatur preserves the district court’s role to take whatever further action is appropriate consistent with the appellate decision (for example, to enter final judgment consistent with noninfringement, to address any remaining/ancillary issues, or to entertain such motions as parties may present). The opinion does not itself pronounce further remedies beyond vacating the award.

When an appellate court might instead enter judgment of zero
- An appellate court will sometimes render final judgment on appeal (including entering judgment for defendant/zero damages) only if the appeal resolves every legal and factual question necessary to a correct final decision on damages and no further proceedings are required. In other words, if liability is conclusively eliminated on appeal and there are no remaining factual or discretionary issues for the district court, the appellate court can and sometimes will enter judgment for the defendant rather than vacate.
- By contrast, when there are residual issues (procedural or factual), or the appellate court prefers to leave assessment of ancillary matters to the district court, the court will vacate the jury award and remand rather than substitute a damages figure itself.

Applying that to this opinion
- The panel reversed noninfringement for all asserted claims, so the damages award no longer rested on a viable infringement verdict — the opinion therefore vacated the award. The Court did not render a substitute damages judgment (e.g., $0) in this opinion, which follows the common practice shown in the opinion of removing a damages verdict that has lost its legal predicate and leaving any necessary district-court follow-up to be handled below.

### Was it proper for the district court to rely on inventor testimony to construe S1, S2, S3 as 'separately identifiable' rather than 'unique'?
Question:
The district court's construction leaned on intrinsic evidence and also credited inventor testimony that the three signals 'can be the same signal or different signals.' Under Phillips, extrinsic evidence has limited weight. Was it appropriate to use inventor testimony here, and did the Federal Circuit implicitly endorse that approach by not disturbing the construction? What would have been needed in the intrinsic record to require 'unique' instead?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — the district court acted within established claim‑construction practice in crediting inventor testimony to resolve an ambiguity once the intrinsic record did not clearly require “unique” signals. Under Phillips, extrinsic evidence (including inventor testimony) may be consulted to resolve genuine ambiguity in the intrinsic record, but it cannot be used to override a clear, unambiguous intrinsic definition or prosecution disclaimer. The Federal Circuit did not disturb the district court’s construction that S1, S2, S3 are “separately identifiable” (and not necessarily “unique”); instead it relied on that construction when explaining why there was no clear and unmistakable surrender of two‑signal embodiments and then reversed JMOL for lack of evidence on other infringement elements. To have required “unique,” the intrinsic record would have had to contain a clear and unmistakable definition or prosecution disclaimer to that effect.

Why that is the correct reading of the opinion
- The opinion reports that the district court construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to be “separately identifiable,” but not “unique,” and that the court “credited inventor testimony, as extrinsic evidence, that ‘S1, S2 and S3’ represent signals generally and can be the same signal or different signals.” J.A. 109–15, 115. That is the factual posture on which the Federal Circuit reviewed the later JMOL issues.
- Legal standard the opinion applies: the court explicitly analyzed whether Finesse had “clearly and unambiguously” disavowed claim scope over two‑signal products during prosecution, citing Continental Circuits and the well‑known requirement that a patentee’s amendment or statements must be clear and unambiguous to amount to disclaimer. The Federal Circuit concluded the amendment and prosecution history did not rise to that level, and at most required “three separately identifiable signals” without mandating a specific number of unique inputs. J.A. 110–11 (citing Cont’l Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788, 798 (Fed. Cir. 2019)).
- How Phillips and extrinsic evidence fit in (as applied by the court): Phillips teaches that intrinsic evidence (claims, specification, prosecution history) controls and extrinsic evidence is of limited value and cannot be used to contradict the intrinsic record. Where the intrinsic record is not decisive, however, courts may consider extrinsic evidence to resolve ambiguity. That is exactly what happened here: the district court found the intrinsic evidence did not mandate “unique,” and so it relied in part on inventor testimony to clarify that S1–S3 can be the same or different signals. The Federal Circuit did not reverse that construction; it treated the absence of an intrinsic, clear disavowal as dispositive on surrender and proceeded to JMOL review on other grounds. See J.A. 109–15, 110–11.
- What the Federal Circuit did (and did not) do: the court did not overturn the district court’s construction nor say the district court erred in considering inventor testimony as extrinsic evidence; rather, it concluded the prosecution history/amendment did not amount to the “clear and unambiguous” surrender that would force a reading of “unique,” and then reversed JMOL because the proof at trial was insufficient to show the accused devices performed all seven claimed multiplications. J.A. 110–11, 38–40.

What would have been necessary in the intrinsic record to require “unique”
To construe S1, S2, S3 as necessarily “unique,” the intrinsic record would have had to contain a clear, unambiguous statement or definition showing the patentee limited the claim to distinct signals. Concretely, that could have been:
- Specification language that defines S1, S2, S3 as distinct or different signals (e.g., repeatedly and consistently describing S1, S2, S3 as coming from distinct transmitters, separate sources, or always “different”); or
- Prosecution statements or claim amendments that unmistakably disclaim two‑signal embodiments — for example, an explicit response to a rejection that says the inventor limited the claims to three distinct/unique signals (not merely added “three signals” without clarifying that they must be distinct), or an amendment from “two or three signals” to “three signals” that is accompanied by a clear argument that the three must be different and that two‑signal variants are excluded; or
- Intrinsic structural/functional limitations that make the claimed result achievable only if the three inputs are distinct (so the specification and claims inevitably imply uniqueness).

Absent that kind of “clear and unmistakable” intrinsic language or prosecution history, the court properly may (and here did) rely on extrinsic inventor testimony to resolve ambiguity and adopt “separately identifiable” rather than “unique.” The Federal Circuit’s treatment — accepting that the intrinsic record did not compel “unique” and evaluating the prosecution history against the high bar for disclaimer — is consistent with Phillips and Continental Circuits as applied in the opinion.
