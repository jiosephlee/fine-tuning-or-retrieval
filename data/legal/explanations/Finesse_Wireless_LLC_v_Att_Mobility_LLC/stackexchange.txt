Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC

### Why did the Federal Circuit apply Fifth Circuit law to the JMOL standard but still talk about 'substantial evidence'?
Question:
The opinion says denials of JMOL are reviewed under the law of the regional circuit (here, the Fifth Circuit) and de novo, but then it frames the infringement question in terms of whether substantial evidence supports the jury’s verdict. How do these two fit together doctrinally? Is the 'substantial evidence' inquiry the legal standard applied in the de novo review of the JMOL ruling, or is it a separate Federal Circuit gloss specific to patent cases?
Answer:
Short answer: The two formulations are describing the same legal inquiry from two perspectives. The Federal Circuit correctly says (1) it applies the regional circuit’s standard for reviewing a district court’s denial of JMOL—here the Fifth Circuit’s de novo test of whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict, points so strongly for one party that reasonable jurors could not reach a contrary result—and (2) it also asks whether the jury’s factual finding is supported by substantial evidence. Those are not inconsistent rules but complementary ways of stating the governing standard for overturning a jury verdict on JMOL.

Why that is so (doctrinally and with citation to the opinion)
- The opinion states the controlling precedent: “We review a district court’s denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit, here the Fifth Circuit. Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple Inc., 811 F.3d 455, 461 (Fed. Cir. 2016). The Fifth Circuit reviews denials of JMOL de novo, reversing ‘only if, when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.’” (Opinion, quoting Wi‑LAN). That language is the classic Fifth Circuit JMOL test.
- The opinion then separately says, “We review a jury’s finding of infringement for substantial evidence. Apple Inc. v. Wi‑LAN Inc., 25 F.4th 960, 969 (Fed. Cir. 2022). ‘A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.’” (Opinion, citing Apple). That is the familiar substantial‑evidence formulation.

How these fit together
- A denial of JMOL is a judicial determination that the evidence was sufficient to permit reasonable jurors to find for the prevailing party. Under Fifth Circuit law (and most circuits), an appellate court reviews such a denial de novo but asks whether the evidence, viewed favorably to the verdict, could nonetheless permit no reasonable juror to reach the verdict reached. That inquiry is naturally expressed either as the Fifth Circuit’s “points so strongly and overwhelmingly” formulation or as the “substantial evidence” standard (i.e., could a reasonable jury have found for the winner?). The two formulations ask the same thing from slightly different angles: is there legally sufficient evidence to support the jury’s factual finding?
- The Federal Circuit therefore does not create a special “substantial evidence” gloss distinct from the regional circuit rule. It is simply using the substantial‑evidence test to implement the regional circuit’s JMOL standard and to review the underlying jury finding. The opinion explicitly treats both concepts as applicable: it identifies the regional law governing review of the district court’s denial of JMOL and then applies the substantial‑evidence sufficiency test to the jury’s infringement findings (see the opinion’s statements quoted above and its subsequent reasoning reversing JMOL because the jury verdict was not supported by substantial evidence).

Practical takeaway for reading the opinion
- When the court says it reviews the denial of JMOL under Fifth Circuit law de novo, read that as adopting the Fifth Circuit’s sufficiency inquiry (view the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict and ask whether reasonable jurors could differ).
- When the court says it reviews the jury’s finding for substantial evidence, read that as the evidentiary standard used to decide whether the JMOL denial should stand. They are two descriptions of the same legal sufficiency inquiry rather than two independent, conflicting standards.

In short: the Federal Circuit followed the regional‑circuit rule for appellate review of a JMOL denial and used the substantial‑evidence formulation to carry out that review. The opinion’s language reflects two conventional ways of stating the same sufficiency standard.

### What gives the Federal Circuit appellate jurisdiction here instead of the Fifth Circuit?
Question:
The court states it has jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1). My understanding is that § 1295 gives the Federal Circuit jurisdiction over civil actions 'arising under' the patent laws. Given this was an appeal from E.D. Tex. (within the Fifth Circuit), can someone explain precisely why appellate jurisdiction vests in the Federal Circuit and not the Fifth Circuit, and whether the presence of only patent claims (versus mixed claims) matters?
Answer:
Short answer
The Federal Circuit sits here because the case is a civil action “arising under” the patent laws and 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1) gives the Federal Circuit exclusive jurisdiction on appeal from such cases. The opinion itself states that jurisdictional basis: “We have jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).” (Opinion, first page / header).

Why that controls instead of the regional circuit
- Section 1295(a)(1) authorizes the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit to hear appeals “from any civil action arising under any Act of Congress relating to patents.” When a district-court case is a patent case (i.e., it arises under the patent laws), the Federal Circuit—not the regional circuit’s court of appeals—hears the appeal regardless of the trial court’s geographic location. The panel’s jurisdictional statement in this opinion reflects that statutory allocation.

How the opinion shows that rule applies here
- The opinion begins by identifying the appeal (from the Eastern District of Texas) and then explicitly states the jurisdictional grant: “We have jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).” That is the controlling statement in this decision; the court proceeds to decide patent-infringement issues (claim construction, JMOL on infringement of the ’134 and ’775 patents, and the damages award), which is precisely the sort of subject‑matter § 1295(a)(1) routes to the Federal Circuit.

What about mixed claims or only patent claims?
- In this case the disputes the panel decides are patent claims (infringement and damages for asserted patents), so § 1295(a)(1) plainly applies and federal appellate jurisdiction is in the Federal Circuit. The opinion does not analyze mixed‑claims jurisdictional edge cases; it simply states the statutory basis and proceeds to resolve the patent issues. Because the opinion is grounded on § 1295(a)(1), the fact that the district court sat in the Fifth Circuit (E.D. Tex.) does not leave the appeal with the Fifth Circuit—patent appeals go to the Federal Circuit under the statute.

Bottom line
The statutory grant in 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1) is what vests appellate jurisdiction in the Federal Circuit for this appeal; the opinion itself expressly invokes that provision.

### Did Nokia, as an intervenor-defendant, need independent Article III standing to appeal?
Question:
Nokia intervened at the district court and is listed as a defendant-appellant on appeal. Does an intervenor-defendant need to demonstrate independent standing to participate in the appeal, or is it sufficient that a party with standing (AT&T) is also appealing? Would the answer change if AT&T had not appealed?
Answer:
Short answer
- No — when a plaintiff (Finesse) lost at trial and at least one defendant-appellant with Article III standing (AT&T) timely appealed, Nokia as an intervenor-defendant did not separately need to prove independent Article III standing to be listed and participate as a co‑appellant on that appeal. The Federal Circuit’s opinion treats AT&T and Nokia collectively as “Appellants” and proceeds on the merits without any standing dispute. See opinion heading and JURISDICTION statement (noting jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1) and listing “AT&T Mobility LLC and Nokia of America Corporation (collectively, Appellants)”).

- Yes — if AT&T had not appealed, Nokia (as an intervenor) would have had to establish that it itself was a proper party to invoke appellate jurisdiction (i.e., that it was a “party aggrieved” by the district court’s denial of JMOL and satisfied Article III standing requirements). The opinion does not address that scenario and contains no holding excusing a stand‑alone appellant from Article III requirements.

Why (legal doctrine in short)
- Appellate courts have jurisdiction only over “cases” or “controversies,” so an appellant must ordinarily show it has standing to bring the appeal. When multiple defendants share the same adverse judgment and one or more defendants with Article III standing appeal, co‑defendants who were parties below and who share substantially the same legal interest are routinely included as appellants and participate in the appeal without relitigating standing as to jurisdiction. In practice, courts accept that a properly standing appellant’s invocation of appellate jurisdiction supports review of claims that affect the same judgment and legal rights of other parties who were defendants below and are co‑appellants.

- By contrast, an intervenor who did not appeal and who sought to appeal alone must show it is a “party aggrieved” by the district court’s ruling and satisfy Article III injury‑in‑fact, causation, and redressability. That is separate from, and cannot be supplied by, a different party’s appeal if that different party does not itself invoke appellate jurisdiction.

How the opinion reflects this
- The opinion lists “AT&T Mobility LLC, Nokia of America Corporation, Defendants‑Appellants” and proceeds to decide the merits and vacate damages, reversing the district court’s denial of JMOL and noting jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1). There is no discussion in the opinion of any standing defect for Nokia, and the Court treated the appeal as properly before it on the joint appellants’ briefing and arguments. That treatment is consistent with the ordinary practice that a defendant‑appellant’s proper appeal supports review of issues affecting co‑defendants who are parties below and join the appeal.

- The opinion does not address the hypothetical where AT&T did not appeal. Because the court never considered whether Nokia could independently invoke appellate jurisdiction, it does not adopt any rule excusing an intervenor from Article III requirements when it is the sole appellant. If Nokia had been the only appellant, the Court would (absent some other jurisdictional basis) require Nokia to show it had standing to appeal the district court’s adverse rulings.

Practical takeaway for a student
- If you are analyzing appeals by intervenor‑defendants, always check (a) whether at least one party with clear Article III standing framed and filed the appeal, and (b) whether the intervenor was a party below and shares the same adverse judgment/interest such that it can be joined as a co‑appellant. If so, courts typically accept the joint appeal without a separate standing inquiry into the intervenor. If not — i.e., the intervenor alone seeks review — then you must analyze Article III standing for that intervenor as you would for any appellant.  

(Disclosure: the cited Federal Circuit opinion does not discuss standing issues; the answer limits itself to what the opinion shows — Nokia was a co‑appellant and the court exercised jurisdiction without addressing any separate standing challenge.)

### How can the Federal Circuit reverse a jury’s infringement verdict under the 'substantial evidence' standard without violating the Seventh Amendment?
Question:
The court repeatedly invokes 'substantial evidence' deference but still reverses the verdict on both patents based on the insufficiency of the expert’s testimony. Where are the constitutional limits here (Reexamination Clause)? What distinguishes permissible appellate review for legal sufficiency from impermissible reweighing of credibility?
Answer:
Short answer
Appellate courts may reverse a jury infringement verdict under the substantial‑evidence / JMOL standard without running afoul of the Seventh Amendment (or the Article III “reexamination” principle) when the record would permit no reasonable juror to find for the plaintiff — for example because the plaintiff’s sole or critical proof is internally inconsistent, legally insufficient, or cannot be reasonably read to establish the claimed facts. What the court may not do is reweigh competing evidence or supplant the jury’s credibility calls when reasonable jurors could disagree.

What the opinion does (and why it’s constitutional)
1) Standard applied. The panel expressly applied the Fifth Circuit JMOL standard and the Federal Circuit substantial‑evidence test (see Opinion, pp. 4–5): the court reviews denial of JMOL de novo but will reverse only if “when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” It repeats the substantial‑evidence definition: “if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” (Opinion pp. 4–5).

2) Not credibility reweighing, but legal sufficiency review. The court did not recount competing expert testimony and then pick a winner; instead it evaluated whether the plaintiff’s proof — principally Dr. Wells’s testimony tied to the Nokia documents — could, as a matter of legal sufficiency, support the verdict. Where the plaintiff’s case hinged on an expert whose testimony was confusing and self‑contradictory about the very mapping the claims required, the court concluded no reasonable jury could have relied on that testimony to meet the claim element. The panel relied on controlling precedent for that proposition: “When the party with the burden of proof . . . rests its case on an expert’s self‑contradictory testimony, we may conclude the evidence is insufficient to satisfy that standard.” Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp., 543 F.3d 1342, 1349 (Fed. Cir. 2008) (cited at Opinion p. 9). The court applied that rule to the record: “There is nothing clear about Dr. Wells’ testimony. Dr. Wells never testified x1 and x2 correspond to the ‘signals of interest’ and ‘interference generating signals.’” (Opinion pp. 10–11). It therefore held the verdict was not supported by substantial evidence and reversed (Opinion p. 12).

3) The constitutional limits in practice. The Seventh Amendment / Article III bar prevents an appellate court from substituting its view for the jury’s when reasonable jurors could disagree about the evidence or credibility. But it does not prevent appellate courts from concluding that, even when the evidence is viewed in the non‑movant’s favor, that evidence is legally insufficient to sustain the verdict. The opinion illustrates that boundary: the court did not resolve competing versions of the facts where each was reasonably supported; instead it found the plaintiff’s only asserted mapping was internally inconsistent and inadequate as a matter of law to prove the claim element (Opinion pp. 7–12 for the ’134 patent; pp. 12–14 for the ’775 patent).

How to tell permissible legal sufficiency review from impermissible reweighing
- Permissible (what the court did): ask whether a reasonable juror could accept the proof offered to satisfy the claimed element. If the plaintiff’s evidence consists chiefly of an expert opinion that is conclusory, contradicted by other testimony of that same expert, or lacks the necessary foundation (e.g., the expert never actually tied x1/x2 to the claimed signals), the appellate court may rule that no reasonable juror could have relied on it. (See Opinion: “This sort of confusing change of course is not sufficient to support the jury verdict.”; and the Johns Hopkins citation, Opinion p. 9.)
- Impermissible (what the court must avoid): taking competing testimony and substituting the court’s assessment of witness credibility or the weight of evidence where reasonable jurors could credit either side. The opinion avoids that by pointing to the absence of any coherent, legally adequate mapping in the plaintiff’s evidence rather than by saying the defendants’ expert was more believable.

Illustrative quotations from the opinion
- Standard: “A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” (Opinion p. 4–5).
- Rule for self‑contradictory expert proof: “When the party with the burden of proof, such as Finesse, rests its case on an expert’s self‑contradictory testimony, we may conclude the evidence is insufficient to satisfy that standard.” (Opinion p. 9, citing Johns Hopkins).
- Application to record: “There is nothing clear about Dr. Wells’ testimony. Dr. Wells never testified x1 and x2 correspond to the ‘signals of interest’ and ‘interference generating signals.’ … This sort of confusing change of course is not sufficient to support the jury verdict.” (Opinion pp. 10–11).
- Conclusion: “We reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’134 patent because the jury’s infringement verdict is not supported by substantial evidence.” (Opinion p. 12; same language used for the ’775 patent, pp. 13–14).

Bottom line
Appellate reversal under the substantial‑evidence / JMOL framework is constitutional so long as the court confines itself to the narrow legal question — could reasonable jurors, viewing the record in the non‑movant’s favor, rationally find the elements proven? — and does not impermissibly reweigh witness credibility where reasonable minds could differ. This opinion stays on the permissible side by identifying the plaintiff’s critical proof as internally inconsistent and legally inadequate, and by relying on precedent that allows appellate courts to find a verdict unsupported where the sole proof is self‑contradictory or fails to establish the required factual mapping.

### Why did the court decline to reach the disputed claim constructions ('receiver' and 'co-located')?
Question:
For both the ’134 and ’775 patents, the panel notes there were claim construction disputes (e.g., 'receiver', 'co-located') but says it did not need to reach them because it reversed on JMOL for noninfringement. Is this an application of judicial restraint or mootness? Are there standards for when the Federal Circuit must address claim construction to provide guidance versus when it can avoid it?
Answer:
Short answer: The panel declined to decide the disputed constructions because it did not need to do so to resolve the appeal — it reversed on JMOL of noninfringement for both patents, and expressly said that because it reversed on the merits it would not reach the construction disputes. That is classic judicial restraint (avoidance of unnecessary rulings), not a holding that the constructions were right or wrong.

Why the panel stopped there (what the opinion says)
- The opinion repeatedly states the point succinctly. For the ’134 patent the panel notes in a parenthetical: “The parties also dispute the correct construction of ‘receiver.’ … Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement, we do not reach this issue.” (see J.A. page cited in the opinion).  
- The same formulation appears for the ’775 patent on “co-located”: “The parties also dispute the correct construction of ‘co-located.’ … Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement, we do not reach this issue.” (see opinion, pp. 11–15 and 14–15).

Legal doctrine / standard applied
- The panel applied the ordinary appellate principle of avoiding unnecessary decisions: it resolved the appeal on noninfringement (reversing the district court) and therefore there was no necessity to resolve the contested claim construction issues. In short, the constructions were unnecessary to the outcome, so the court exercised prudential restraint and left them unresolved.
- The opinion makes clear the court handled the appeal on the ground that produced a final disposition (reversal for noninfringement and vacatur of damages) and that deciding the constructions was not needed to reach that disposition.

What this means for when the Federal Circuit must address claim construction
- The opinion shows the court will decide constructions when they are necessary to the disposition of the case on appeal; conversely, it will not decide constructions when the appeal can be resolved on another dispositive ground (here, JMOL/noninfringement). The panel’s language — “we do not reach this issue” because it reversed JMOL — embodies that approach.
- The opinion does not announce a new rule requiring the court always to reach (or always to avoid) claim-construction questions; it simply demonstrates the routine appellate practice of not issuing advisory rulings when a dispositive alternative is available.

Practical takeaway for practitioners/students
- If an appellate court can dispose of an appeal on a clear dispositive ground that does not require resolving claim construction, it may (and here did) refrain from construing disputed terms. If you need an appellate construction for future litigation or precedent, be prepared that the court might avoid the question unless it is necessary to the result.

### How did the contradictions in Dr. Wells’s testimony justify JMOL of noninfringement on the ’134 patent?
Question:
The panel relies on Johns Hopkins v. Datascope to say self-contradictory expert testimony cannot sustain the verdict. Here, Dr. Wells mapped 'interference generating signals' to the modeled PIM path (generated after the ADC), yet elsewhere referenced x1/x2 as the two inputs. What makes a contradiction severe enough to render the evidence legally insufficient, instead of merely going to weight and credibility for the jury?
Answer:
Short answer: Because Dr. Wells’ testimony on the single, disputed claim element was internally inconsistent on an essential point (whether the accused receiver actually samples the “interference generating signals”), his opinions could not supply substantial evidence for the jury’s verdict. Under Johns Hopkins and the regional-circuit JMOL standard applied here, an expert’s self‑contradictory testimony on a dispositive element is legally insufficient to carry the plaintiff’s burden, and that is exactly what happened.

Why that doctrinal standard controls
- JMOL/substantial-evidence review (Fifth Circuit law here) asks whether, viewing the record most favorably to the verdict, reasonable jurors could have found for the plaintiff. The court will reverse only when the evidence “points so strongly and overwhelmingly” for the moving party that reasonable jurors could not contrary (see J.A. __ (citing Wi‑LAN)). 
- Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope, 543 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2008), is the governing precedent on expert self‑contradiction: if the party bearing the burden relies on an expert who gives irreconcilable accounts of whether an accused device meets a limitation, a reasonable jury cannot rely on that testimony to find infringement. The panel here expressly applied that rule (J.A. 10–11).

What the opinion finds factually inconsistent and why it matters
- The ’134 claim requires that the accused receiver “over-sampl[es] … a passband of received signals” that “include … interference generating signals” (claim limitation 1[a]) — so Finesse had to prove the RF ADC actually samples both the signals of interest and the interference‑generating signals (J.A. 5–6; claim text at J.A. 28:1–6).
- Dr. Wells repeatedly mapped “signals of interest” to the DL(TX) reference and “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path (e.g., testimony at J.A. 14918 50:16–19; J.A. 15009 141:10–14; expert report J.A. 4476–77 ¶343).
- Appellants’ cross-examination established the modeled PIM path is generated downstream of the RF ADC (in the GROOT FPGA) — i.e., after sampling — so it cannot be a signal the RF ADC samples (J.A. 15010–11 at 142:12–143:5; J.A. 15019 at 151:4–13; J.A. 21–22).
- Confronted with that consequence, Dr. Wells “pivoted”: he stated “there’s two signals on that red path … x1 and x2” and later said x1/x2 are “the two inputs” (J.A. 15010 at 142:15–16; J.A. 15018 at 150:16–18). But crucially he never clearly testified that x1 or x2 correspond to the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Instead he kept asserting the DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path were the two signals (J.A. 15010–11, 15017, 15011 at 143:20–22).
- The panel found that confusion material: the only explicit mapping Dr. Wells gave for the “interference generating signals” was to the modeled PIM path (which cannot have been sampled), and his later references to x1/x2 were ambiguous and uncorroborated (J.A. 10–12). The panel concluded Finesse’s case rested on that expert testimony and that testimony was self‑contradictory and unexplained — so it could not supply substantial evidence of the required sampling (J.A. 10–12; citing Johns Hopkins, 543 F.3d at 1349).

Why this was more than a credibility/weight issue for the jury
- If the expert offered two coherent, alternative explanations, the jury could weigh them. But here the expert gave conflicting identifications of the same claim‑critical signals (explicitly mapping them to a post‑ADC modeled path that cannot be sampled, then ambiguously saying “x1 and x2” without mapping them to the claim terms). The panel treated that as an internal contradiction about the single factual predicate for infringement (whether the ADC samples the interference source), not merely a matter of how persuasive the expert was.
- Johns Hopkins instructs that when the plaintiff’s entire proof of a claim element depends on an expert whose testimony is internally inconsistent on that element, the testimony cannot constitute substantial evidence. The panel applied that rule here and held no reasonable jury could have found the ADC sampled the interference signals on the record as given (J.A. 10–12).

Bottom line
Dr. Wells’ testimony on the pivotal issue (what signal the receiver actually samples) was internally inconsistent and never reconciled: he explicitly tied “interference generating signals” to a modeled PIM path that is generated after sampling, but later invoked x1/x2 without ever clearly mapping those to the claim terms. Because Finesse’s proof of the disputed limitation rested on that confusion, Johns Hopkins and the applicable JMOL standard required the court to conclude the evidence was legally insufficient to support the jury’s verdict. The panel therefore reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the ’134 patent (J.A. 10–12).

### Why wasn’t there prosecution disclaimer limiting the ’775 patent to three unique signals after the amendment?
Question:
During prosecution, the applicant changed 'two or three signals' to 'three signals' in responding to an obviousness rejection over Filipovic (which used two inputs). The court holds there was no 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal of two-signal systems. What specifically about the applicant’s arguments and the Notice of Allowance led the panel to reject disclaimer, despite the narrowing amendment?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel held that the prosecution history did not contain the “clear and unambiguous” surrender required for prosecution disclaimer. The applicant’s arguments and the Notice of Allowance focused on a different distinction (Filipovic’s teaching of active IMPs versus the claimed passive-IMP solution), not an express exclusion of two‑signal systems. Thus the amendment to recite “three signals” did not by itself — nor did the prosecution statements — clearly disclaim two-signal embodiments; at most the intrinsic and extrinsic record supports that S1–S3 must be “separately identifiable,” not necessarily three unique inputs.

Why that outcome follows from the opinion
1) Governing rule: prosecution disclaimer requires clear and unmistakable statements.
- The panel applies the settled rule that an amendment or argument during prosecution will limit claim scope only if it amounts to a “clear and unambiguous” disavowal of subject matter. The opinion cites Continental Circuits, 915 F.3d at 798, and repeatedly frames the inquiry in those terms (see J.A. 110–11).

2) What the applicant actually argued to the examiner.
- The examiner rejected claims over Filipovic (which used two inputs). Finesse responded and overcame the rejection primarily by arguing Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs, whereas the claimed invention addresses passive IMPs (J.A. 985; see also J.A. 941 (Notice of Allowance)). The panel emphasizes that the successful prosecution argument centered on that substantive distinction (active vs. passive IMPs), not on a categorical difference in the number or uniqueness of input signals (J.A. 12–13, 12–13 quoted in context).

3) The amendment plus those arguments did not equate to a disclaimer of two-signal systems.
- Finesse did amend claims to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3,” but the panel explains that an amendment alone (narrowing language) is insufficient to prove disclaimer unless the prosecution statements make an unmistakable contrary delineation. The court held the prosecution history did not say “we exclude two-signal systems” or “we surrender two-unique-signal embodiments” (J.A. 110–11). Instead, the Notice of Allowance and the response explained the claim’s difference from Filipovic on the passive/active point (J.A. 941; J.A. 985), so the narrowing amendment did not rise to the level of disavowal.

4) Intrinsic/extrinsic support for a broader reading.
- The court also relied on claim construction and inventor testimony: the district court had construed S1–S3 as “separately identifiable” but not “unique,” and inventor testimony was credited that S1–S3 can be the same signal or copies (J.A. 109–14; J.A. 115). The panel treated that record as consistent with the absence of a clear disclaimer of two‑signal systems (J.A. 110–11).

Net result (as the panel put it)
- Because the prosecution statements addressed a different patentability rationale (active vs. passive IMPs) and did not clearly and unambiguously relinquish coverage of systems using only two unique input signals, the amendment to “three signals” did not constitute prosecution disclaimer of two-signal embodiments (J.A. 110–11). The most the history supports is a requirement for three separately identifiable signals, not a prohibition on two‑input implementations (J.A. 968–69; J.A. 110–11).

Takeaway for claim drafting and prosecution
- Narrowing an element (e.g., from “two or three” to “three”) can create a risk of disclaimer, but disclaimer requires explicit, unambiguous statements that the patentee is excluding particular subject matter. Arguing a different distinguishing feature (here, passive vs. active IMPs) and securing allowance on that basis will not, by itself, prove a surrender of other embodiments unless the patentee expressly disclaims them.

### How did Rule 50(a) preservation affect the 'unique vs. separately identifiable' signals dispute under the ’775 patent?
Question:
The district court said appellants waived an argument that S1, S2, and S3 must be 'unique' because it wasn’t raised at the Rule 50(a) stage. On appeal, the panel notes appellants did not challenge the construction requiring only 'separately identifiable' signals. What exactly must be raised in a Rule 50(a) motion to preserve a claim construction or sufficiency argument, and how strictly does the Fifth Circuit apply that rule?
Answer:
Short answer
To preserve a claim‑construction or evidentiary sufficiency argument under Rule 50(a) you must make a timely, specific JMOL motion at the close of the evidence that identifies the precise legal/factual ground for relief (e.g., “the claims require three unique signals, not merely three separately identifiable signals”) and tie that ground to the evidence (or lack of evidence) presented. The Fifth Circuit enforces this preservation rule strictly: failure to present the exact argument in the Rule 50(a) motion is treated as waiver on appeal.

What the opinion shows (and why)
- The district court had construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to require only that the signals be “separately identifiable,” not “unique.” J.A. 109–15 (construction); J.A. 115 (explicit holding). Appellants did not challenge that construction on appeal. See opinion at 11–13 (noting Appellants “do not appeal the district court’s holding that ‘S1, S2 and S3’ need only be separately identifiable, not unique”).
- At JMOL the district court rejected Appellants’ contention that they had waived an argument that S1–S3 must be unique because Appellants had not raised that precise point at the Rule 50(a) stage. The court concluded using two signals plus a copy was sufficient under its construction of “separately identifiable.” J.A. 38, 40.
- The appellate panel treated that preservation failure as dispositive: it declined to overturn the district court’s construction (Appellants did not press it on appeal) and evaluated the sufficiency of the evidence against the construction actually before the jury. In short, because Appellants did not preserve a “must be unique” construction in their Rule 50(a) motion, they could not rely on that alternative theory on appeal.

Practical rule for preservation
- Be specific. A Rule 50(a) motion must identify the precise legal ground and the basis in the trial record (e.g., “JMOL: as to infringement, the jury lacked substantial evidence that the accused device uses three unique signals S1,S2,S3 as required by the claim construction because the evidence shows only two distinct signals x1,x2”).
- Make it at the right time. The motion needs to be made at the close of the evidence (or close of all the evidence, if appropriate) so the trial court and the opposing party have a chance to respond.
- Tie to the claim construction. If your argument depends on a particular construction (for example, “unique” vs. “separately identifiable”), press that construction in the Rule 50(a) motion or in prior motions for judgment/claim construction so the district court can rule on it. The panel emphasized Appellants did not challenge the district court’s construction requiring only “separately identifiable” signals, so the “unique” argument was not preserved. See J.A. 115; J.A. 38–40.

How strictly the Fifth Circuit applies the rule
- The opinion applies the preservation requirement strictly: an argument not raised in Rule 50(a) is treated as waived on appeal. The Fifth Circuit standard for reviewing denial of JMOL is de novo, but that standard does not excuse a failure to preserve an argument at trial. The panel here reversed JMOL for lack of substantial evidence under the construction that was actually before the jury and rejected the unpreserved “unique signals” theory. See J.A. 38–40 and the panel’s statement that Appellants “do not appeal the district court’s holding that ‘S1, S2 and S3’ need only be separately identifiable, not unique.” 

Bottom line
If you want to preserve on Rule 50(a) that a claim requires “unique” inputs rather than merely “separately identifiable” inputs, state that exact construction/argument in your Rule 50(a) motion and show why the evidence cannot support it. If you do not, the Fifth Circuit (as shown in this opinion) will likely treat the point as waived and will review only the theory actually presented to the jury.

### Can the Federal Circuit rely on oral argument statements and record citations not clearly presented to the jury?
Question:
The opinion cites oral argument (with an audio link) and Nokia documents. For the ’775 patent, the panel faults Finesse for only presenting three multiplications to the jury and not mapping all seven. How does the appellate court treat attorney explanations at oral argument versus trial evidence? Are new mappings or clarifications offered at oral argument considered part of the evidentiary record or disregarded?
Answer:
Short answer: The Federal Circuit relied only on the trial record — not new mappings or clarifications first offered at oral argument or on appeal. Attorney or counsel explanations made at oral argument are not a substitute for evidence presented to the jury and will not salvage a verdict when the trial record lacks the required proof.

Why (doctrine and how the opinion applies it)
- Appellate review of a verdict looks to the evidence actually presented at trial. The court framed its task as asking whether the jury’s infringement finding was supported by substantial evidence (and whether, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence could reasonably support it). See Discussion I, JMOL standard (citing Wi‑LAN, Apple). That standard necessarily limits the court to the trial record.
- The opinion repeatedly treats the mapping issue as a question of what testimony and documents were before the jury. For the ’775 patent the court held that Dr. Wells relied on Nokia documents that only showed three distinct multiplications and that “Dr. Wells did not testify how those three distinct multiplications map onto the seven claimed multiplications.” J.A. 14966–70 quoted in the opinion. The court then said plainly: “Finesse attempts to clarify the mapping on appeal, but that evidence was never presented to the jury.” J.A. __ (paragraph beginning “Finesse attempts to clarify…”).
- At oral argument Finesse advanced additional explanations (e.g., that Nokia’s documents show ten multiplications and that a subset corresponds to the claimed seven). The opinion records those arguments (citing the oral‑argument transcript/audio) but treats them as a post‑trial clarification: “Nowhere do Finesse or Dr. Wells explain which subset of the ten multiplications listed in the Nokia document corresponds to the seven claimed multiplications.” The court then reversed JMOL because the jury verdict was “not supported by substantial evidence.” J.A. __ (pp. discussing ’775).
- The same principle appears in the court’s discussion of the ’134 patent: conflicting or unclear trial testimony by the expert could not be made coherent by piecing together later assertions; the court faults reliance on inferences pieced from testimony “over ninety pages apart” and rejects post‑hoc clarifications. See discussion on Dr. Wells’ x1/x2 testimony and the court’s conclusion that the testimony was “confusing and unclear” and therefore insufficient. J.A. 15010–19; J.A. 10–11.

Practical takeaways for counsel and experts
- If a mapping, explanation, or piece of evidentiary linkage is essential to infringement, it must be presented clearly at trial (through admissible evidence and witness testimony) and not reserved as an appellate or oral‑argument explanation.
- Oral argument can clarify legal issues for the court, but it does not add facts to the evidentiary record. The Federal Circuit will not treat new factual mappings or exhibit interpretations first articulated at oral argument as part of the trial proof supporting the jury verdict.

Bottom line: The panel treated oral‑argument statements as argument (and recorded them), but did not treat new mappings or clarifications first offered there as evidence. The court based its reversals on deficiencies in the actual trial record and expert testimony, not on anything counsel said at oral argument.

### Why wasn’t it enough that the accused radios performed three distinct multiplications that could correspond to multiple of the seven claimed forms?
Question:
For the ’775 patent, the court says the Nokia docs show only three distinct multiplications (e.g., x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2', x1·|x2|·|x2|) and that Finesse did not show the full set of seven required multiplications from claim 1(d). Why can’t a single implemented multiplication (like x1·x1·x2') be treated as covering several of the enumerated monomials (e.g., S1·S1·S2 and S1·S2·S1) for literal infringement, given commutativity/associativity? Is the court requiring proof that each listed form is actually performed, even if mathematically redundant?
Answer:
Short answer: Because literal infringement requires proof that the accused device actually practices the claim limitation as written (or a convincing, trial‑presented mapping showing equivalence), and the court found no such proof or mapping in the record. The Nokia documents and Dr. Wells’ trial testimony only showed three distinct multiplications (x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2', x1·|x2|·|x2|), and the court concluded there was no evidence those three were repeated or shown to be the seven specific S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S1, … multiplications recited in claim 1(d).

Why commutativity/associativity didn’t save Finesse
- Literal infringement turns on whether the accused product performs the claimed steps/elements (or an agreed equivalent). You cannot satisfy a multi‑part claim limitation by saying “these operations are mathematically the same” unless the patentee presented evidence at trial that the accused implementation performs operations that correspond to the claim terms (or shows an accepted equivalent mapping). The panel applied the substantial‑evidence standard and found that Finesse did not carry that burden. See opinion, pp. 11–13, 12–13 (discussion of claim 1(d) and required seven multiplications).
- The court pointed to three concrete problems in the record:
  1. The Nokia doc “only lists three distinct multiplications—x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|—and there is no evidence any multiplications are repeated such that all seven claimed multiplications are performed, as required by limitation 1[d].” (Opinion at p. 12–13; reproduced citation to J.A. 26486.)
  2. Dr. Wells “did not testify how those three distinct multiplications map onto the seven claimed multiplications.” The court emphasized Finesse’s attempted mapping was first made on appeal and was not presented to the jury. (Id. at p. 13.)
  3. The Nokia implementation uses complex conjugates and moduli (x2' and |x2|), so the asserted mapping would require treating S3 as simultaneously x2, x2', and |x2|—a mapping the court described as “flawed” and unsupported in the record. (Id. at p. 13.)
- Because the jury had only Dr. Wells’ testimony and the Nokia document, and Dr. Wells did not give the necessary, non‑contradictory mapping showing how the three listed multiplications correspond to the seven enumerated in the claim, the court held no reasonable jury could find the seven claimed multiplications were performed. The court therefore reversed JMOL on noninfringement for the ’775 patent. (Id. at pp. 12–13.)

Bottom line: The court wasn’t imposing a formalistic rule that each listed monomial must be performed in some sequenced way despite algebraic redundancy. It required record evidence that the accused radios actually produced the seven claimed forms (or that the patentee put before the jury a reliable mapping showing the accused operations are the same as the claimed ones). Finesse failed to present that evidence at trial (and the Nokia doc’s use of conjugates/moduli undermined any simple algebraic equivalence), so commutativity/associativity alone could not substitute for the missing factual proof.

### Could the doctrine of equivalents have bridged the gap for modulus or complex-conjugate multiplications under the ’775 patent?
Question:
The Nokia implementation used modulus and complex conjugates instead of the exact triple products of S1, S2, and S3. The opinion treats this as defeating literal infringement. Was the doctrine of equivalents argued at trial for these limitations? If not, would DOE be foreclosed on appeal, and what would be required to show equivalence for these specific signal-processing operations?
Answer:
Short answer
- The Federal Circuit’s opinion does not describe any successful doctrine-of-equivalents (DOE) argument for the modulus / complex‑conjugate operations. Instead the court reversed for lack of substantial evidence because Finesse relied on a Nokia document that discloses modulus and conjugate operations and Finesse failed to explain (to the jury) how those operations correspond to the seven claimed third‑order multiplications. See opinion at pp. 12–14 (discussion of the Nokia document listing x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|) and pp. 13–14 (holding Dr. Wells “did not testify how those three distinct multiplications map onto the seven claimed multiplications” and that Finesse’s mapping “was never presented to the jury”).
- Because Finesse did not present the necessary mapping/equivalence evidence to the jury, DOE would be procedurally difficult to rely on for the same claim limitations on appeal: appellate courts generally will not consider new theories or evidence that were not presented to the jury. The opinion repeatedly emphasizes that Finesse’s post‑trial/appeal explanations were not before the jury and so cannot support the verdict. See pp. 13–14.

What the DOE would require here (doctrinal standard, and what Finesse needed to show)
- Legal standard: under DOE, an accused product or process infringes if it includes an element that is not literally within the claim but is equivalent — i.e., it performs substantially the same function, in substantially the same way, to achieve substantially the same result as the claimed element. The opinion does not restate the full DOE test, but that is the governing doctrine that would apply if Finesse had pursued equivalence.
- What the court faulted Finesse for failing to prove: concrete, trial‑level evidence tying the accused operations (modulus |·| and complex conjugation ' ) to the seven claimed triple multiplications (S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, …). The opinion explains that Nokia’s document lists only three distinct expressions involving modulus and conjugates and that there was no testimony or evidence showing repetition or a mapping from those expressions to the seven claimed multiplications. See pp. 12–14.
- In practice, to show equivalence for these specific signal‑processing operations Finesse would have needed at trial:
  1) a clear, non‑confusing expert explanation mapping each claimed triple product to the accused math operations (or showing that the accused operations produce the same terms as the claimed products), with demonstration that the accused operations perform the same function, in the same way, and achieve the same result; and
  2) evidence that any differences (e.g., use of magnitude or conjugate rather than a literal third product) are insubstantial — for example, a mathematical derivation or measurements showing the outputs used for cancellation are the same (or equivalent in effect) as the claimed products; and
  3) an affirmative response to any prosecution‑history or amendment‑based estoppel issues (the opinion discusses the claim amendment to “three signals S1, S2 and S3” and found no clear disavowal of two‑signal devices (pp. 11–13), but DOE arguments can still be limited by estoppel where amendments were made for patentability).
- The opinion makes clear what was missing: Dr. Wells “did not testify how those three distinct multiplications map onto the seven claimed multiplications,” and Finesse’s later on‑appeal mapping “was never presented to the jury.” Because of that evidentiary gap, the court held there was no substantial evidence to support literal infringement and reversed JMOL (pp. 12–14).

Consequences for preservation and appellate review
- If DOE was not argued and supported by evidence at trial, it is effectively waived for purposes of supporting the jury verdict on appeal. The court’s decision stresses that post‑trial or appellate explanations that were not put before the jury cannot supply the missing evidence (pp. 13–14).
- Even if DOE had been argued at trial, Finesse would still have carried the burden of proving equivalence with specific evidence mapping the accused modulus/conjugate operations to the claimed multiplications and addressing any prosecution‑history estoppel. The court’s discussion of the prosecution history amendment (pp. 11–13) shows that amendment does not automatically foreclose DOE, but that estoppel can limit equivalents when there is a “clear and unambiguous” surrender.

Bottom line
The opinion shows the problem was evidentiary and procedural: Finesse did not present the jury with the required mapping or equivalence proof for the modulus/complex‑conjugate operations, and the appellate court refused to cure that omission on appeal. To have bridged the gap under DOE, Finesse needed to put before the jury clear expert testimony and demonstrative/mathematical proof that the accused operations are equivalent to the claimed triple multiplications (and to address any estoppel).

### Why did the panel vacate the damages award instead of remanding for a new trial on damages?
Question:
After reversing JMOL of noninfringement on all asserted claims of both patents, the court vacated the $166M lump-sum award. Is vacatur automatic when liability is reversed (making damages moot), or could the court have remanded damages on a different theory? What procedural posture would justify remand for damages versus vacatur with instructions to enter judgment of noninfringement?
Answer:
Short answer: The panel vacated the damages because it reversed JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims, and an award of damages cannot stand once liability is nullified. Vacatur is the ordinary result when the underlying liability finding on which damages depend is undone; remand for a new damages trial would only make sense if some liability remained (or if the appellate ruling left a live claim that required re-quantification of damages).

Why the panel did this (doctrine and how the opinion applies)
- Damages are derivative of liability. The jury’s lump‑sum award flowed from its verdict that the asserted claims were infringed. The opinion expressly reverses JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of both patents (Opinion, Conclusion and throughout Parts I.A–I.B). Because liability was undone for all asserted claims, “we vacate the damages award.” (Opinion, II. Damages ¶—“Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we vacate the damages award.”)

- In other words, once the court held that no reasonable jury could have found infringement (i.e., liability fails as a matter of law), the award of damages based on that impossible finding is moot and must be vacated. The panel did not reach or rely on any independent flaws in the damages evidence; it disposed of the damages question by eliminating the predicate liability.

When a remand for damages would be appropriate instead
- Remand (for a new damages trial or further proceedings) is appropriate when liability remains at least in part, but the damages determination is defective or requires further factfinding. Example situations:
  - The appellate court affirms infringement (liability) but finds trial error in the damages phase (so it remands for a new damages trial).
  - The appeal upholds infringement for some claims or defendants but reverses as to others — the court can remand to determine damages attributable to the surviving liability.
  - The appellate court reverses on a legal issue that changes the legal framework for damages but leaves a live, remediable claim that requires recalculation of damages.

- By contrast, when the reversal eliminates all liability (as here), there is nothing left to compensate, so a damages retrial would have no legal basis.

Procedural posture that justifies one outcome or the other
- Vacatur with instruction to enter judgment of noninfringement: justified where the appellate court reverses liability as a matter of law for all asserted claims (exact posture here). The court’s reversal removes the legal foundation for the damages award, so vacatur is the natural remedy.

- Remand for new trial on damages: justified where (1) liability is affirmed (or at least some asserted claims remain viable), and (2) there is a legal or factual problem confined to the damages proceeding that requires further factfinding. Also justified where only the damages JMOL or new‑trial motion is reversed but the infringement verdict survives.

Tie to the opinion’s text
- The panel explicitly tied the vacatur to its liability reversal: “Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we vacate the damages award.” That is the controlling rationale in this opinion — damages fall with the reversed liability.

Conclusion
- Vacatur was not an arbitrary choice; it flowed directly from the court’s holding that the jury verdict of infringement (the predicate for damages) was unsupported as a matter of law for all asserted claims. Had the court left any liability intact, or found error confined to the damages phase, it could have remanded for further proceedings on damages instead.

### What is the precise line between 'regional circuit law' and 'Federal Circuit law' in reviewing new-trial denials and sufficiency in patent cases?
Question:
The court applies Fifth Circuit law to the denial of a new trial (abuse of discretion) but uses Federal Circuit standards for substantial-evidence infringement review. How do practitioners determine which issues on appeal are governed by the regional circuit (procedural) and which by Federal Circuit (substantive patent law)? Are there canonical examples where this allocation has been outcome-determinative?
Answer:
Short answer
- Procedure (standards of review for motions like JMOL or new trial) is governed by the regional circuit. The opinion says explicitly that the denial of JMOL and denial of a new trial are reviewed under Fifth Circuit law (citing Wi‑LAN). See Opinion, Discussion I (first paragraph) and II (damages).
- Substantive patent-law determinations (claim construction, and whether a jury’s infringement finding is supported by the record) are governed by Federal Circuit law: claim construction de novo (with underlying factual findings for clear error) and factual findings of infringement reviewed for substantial evidence. See same paragraph in the Opinion (citing Wi‑LAN and Apple v. Wi‑LAN).

How to decide in practice
1) Ask whether the question is procedural (how the district court exercised its discretion or which regional standard of review applies) or substantive patent law (what the patent means, whether the claim elements are met):
   - If the appeal asks whether the district court abused its discretion in denying a new trial or a JMOL motion (i.e., you are challenging the court’s exercise of procedural discretion or invoking the regional circuit’s standard), the regional circuit’s standard governs. The Opinion: “We review a district court’s denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit, here the Fifth Circuit.” (Discussion I, first full paragraph).
   - If the appeal asks whether the accused device meets the claim limitations (infringement) or whether claim terms were correctly construed, the Federal Circuit’s patent standards apply: claim construction de novo; factual infringement findings for substantial evidence. The Opinion: “We review a jury’s finding of infringement for substantial evidence. . . . We review claim construction de novo and any underlying factual findings . . . for clear error.” (same paragraph).

2) Translate that to the specific issues you will brief:
   - Items about whether the jury had legally sufficient evidence to find infringement are argued under the Federal Circuit’s substantial‑evidence framework (could a reasonable jury find for the patentee based on the record). In this opinion, the court applied that standard when it asked whether Dr. Wells’ testimony and the Nokia documents could support the jury’s findings (see Discussion I.A and I.B where the court concluded the verdict was not supported by substantial evidence).
   - Items about whether the district court should have granted a new trial or granted/denied JMOL procedurally (timing, Rule 50(a)/(b), Rule 59, abuse of discretion) are addressed under the regional circuit’s abuse‑of‑discretion and related standards. The Opinion states that denials of a new trial and JMOL are reviewed under Fifth Circuit law (Discussion I opening and Discussion II on damages).

Where allocation is outcome‑determinative (canonical or in this opinion)
- This opinion itself shows how the allocation matters:
   - The court framed its review of the denial of JMOL under Fifth Circuit law (procedural), but then evaluated the sufficiency of the infringement evidence under the Federal Circuit’s substantial‑evidence standard. The court reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL because the infringement verdict “is not supported by substantial evidence” — a Federal Circuit substantive assessment applied in the context of the regional circuit standard for reviewing a JMOL denial (Discussion I.A and I.B). Put another way, the appellate court used the regional circuit’s pathway (review of a JMOL denial) but reached its substantive conclusion by applying Federal Circuit law on sufficiency of the infringement evidence.
- Other illustrative (cited) examples:
   - Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple, and Apple v. Wi‑LAN are cited by the panel to make the split: Wi‑LAN for applying regional circuit law to review denials of JMOL/new trial; Apple/Wi‑LAN for the substantial‑evidence standard for infringement findings. The Opinion expressly cites both authorities in the opening paragraph of Discussion I.
   - Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp. is relied on here for the proposition that self‑contradictory expert testimony cannot support a verdict — a substantive sufficiency point the panel used to reverse (Discussion I.A, citing Johns Hopkins).

Practical checklist for practitioners preparing appeals in patent cases
- Identify whether the contested ruling is (a) a pure procedural ruling about the district court’s exercise of discretion (new trial, evidentiary rulings, timeliness, waiver) or (b) a substantive patent issue (claim construction, infringement, validity, how evidence maps to claim elements).
- If procedural: prepare to address the regional circuit’s standard (abuse of discretion, de novo review of legal questions under regional law where applicable). Cite the regional circuit’s precedents and show reversible error under that standard.
- If substantive patent law: prepare Federal Circuit precedent (claim construction law, substantial evidence for factual findings). When the appeal is procedurally framed (e.g., denial of JMOL), be prepared that the court will still ask the Federal Circuit substantive question — is there substantial evidence to support the verdict — and you must win under that Federal Circuit standard.
- Anticipate the hybrid: many appeals are packaged as procedural (denial of JMOL/new trial) but require the Federal Circuit to assess whether the record contains substantial evidence of infringement — as happened in this opinion.

Bottom line
The federal appellate court separates the two roles: regional circuit law governs review of district‑court procedural decisions (denial of JMOL/new trial); Federal Circuit law governs patent‑specific substantive questions (claim scope, infringement sufficiency, claim construction). In practice you must brief both: show procedural error under the regional standard and show that, on the Federal Circuit’s patent standards, the substantive patent findings either fail or succeed. The Finesse opinion illustrates that the court will explicitly state the allocation and then apply the Federal Circuit’s substantial‑evidence test to the record when assessing whether the jury’s infringement verdict can stand.

### How did extrinsic inventor testimony influence the construction that S1, S2, S3 need only be 'separately identifiable'?
Question:
The district court construed 'three signals S1, S2, and S3' as 'separately identifiable' but not 'unique,' crediting intrinsic evidence and also inventor testimony as extrinsic evidence. Under Teva and Phillips, when can inventor testimony sway claim construction, and how does the 'separately identifiable' concept square with the claim language that then enumerates seven specific third-order products?
Answer:
Short answer
- The district court relied on inventor testimony as extrinsic evidence to read “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as requiring that the signals be “separately identifiable” but not necessarily three different (unique) signals. See J.A. 109–14, J.A. 115.
- That use of inventor testimony is permissible under Phillips and Teva only where the intrinsic record is not dispositive and the extrinsic testimony does not contradict a clear intrinsic disavowal. See discussion infra (citing the opinion’s treatment of intrinsic vs. extrinsic evidence).
- That construction means the claim requires three separately identifiable inputs for forming the enumerated third‑order products, but it does not itself resolve the separate factual question whether the accused product actually produces the seven listed multiplications (the court accepted that the “separately identifiable” reading allows copies or reuse of signals, J.A. 38–39, but the Federal Circuit reversed the infringement verdict because the record did not show the seven claimed multiplications were performed, J.A. 26485–86; J.A. 14966–70).

Why inventor testimony was credited here (how Teva/Phillips apply)
- Phillips requires courts to rely primarily on the intrinsic evidence (claims, specification, prosecution history), but it does not forbid consideration of extrinsic evidence when the intrinsic record leaves an ambiguity. Teva v. Sandoz explains that subsidiary fact‑findings (e.g., what extrinsic evidence means) are reviewed for clear error. The opinion adopts that framework: “we review claim construction de novo and any underlying factual findings supporting the district court’s construction for clear error.” (J.A. — quoted in the opinion).
- The district court concluded the intrinsic record did not clearly require S1, S2, S3 to be distinct/unique, so it permissibly considered inventor testimony as extrinsic evidence to illuminate what “three signals” meant in the patentee’s view. The court’s construction (“separately identifiable, not unique”) is stated at J.A. 109–14 and J.A. 115.
- The Federal Circuit did not overturn that construction on appeal (Appellants did not appeal that particular ruling; see opinion), which indicates the appellate court accepted that the intrinsic record did not preclude the non‑unique reading and that the district court’s factual reliance on inventor testimony was not clearly erroneous.

What “separately identifiable” means relative to the seven enumerated multiplications
- The district court read “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to require that the three signals be separately identifiable (i.e., the claim requires three named signal inputs that can be distinguished in the process) but not to require three different physical sources. That is, the same underlying signal may be used more than once or a copy of a signal may serve as one of S1–S3 so long as the three roles are separately identifiable in the claimed multiplications. See J.A. 115 and the district court’s finding that “using two signals, plus a copy of one of those signals, was sufficient to meet its construction of three separately identifiable signals” (J.A. 38).
- The claims, however, do not merely name three signals; they require generating certain third‑order products (the seven listed multiplications). The district court treated the construction as allowing fewer than three unique physical signals to satisfy the claim if the processing treated them as three identifiable inputs to produce the required multiplications (J.A. 38–39).

Why that construction did not save the infringement verdict
- Even accepting the district court’s “separately identifiable” construction, infringement still required evidence that the accused radios actually produce the seven claimed third‑order products. The Federal Circuit reviewed the trial record and concluded the Nokia documents relied on by Finesse showed at most three distinct multiplications (x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|) and did not demonstrate that the seven claimed multiplications were produced or that the three listed operations map to the seven claimed multiplications. See J.A. 26485–86; J.A. 14966–70. Because the jury’s verdict lacked substantial evidentiary support on that element, JMOL of noninfringement was warranted despite the permissive construction. The opinion explains this at J.A. 26485–86 and J.A. 14966–70 and rejects the mapping argument as not presented to the jury (and insufficient as a matter of record).

Bottom line for claim construction practice
- Inventor testimony can be considered to resolve an ambiguity in claim meaning so long as it does not contradict a clear statement in the intrinsic record; the district court may credit such testimony and make factual findings about it (Teva/Phillips framework). See J.A. 109–14 and the opinion’s general discussion of claim construction review.
- But a claim construction that permits non‑unique but “separately identifiable” signals does not eliminate the separate factual requirement that the accused device actually performs the claimed operations (here, all seven enumerated multiplications). If the intrinsic + extrinsic record leaves open whether the accused product produces those specific multiplications, the patentee still must present competent evidence of that mapping at trial—failure to do so dooms the infringement case, as the Federal Circuit held (J.A. 26485–86; J.A. 14966–70).
