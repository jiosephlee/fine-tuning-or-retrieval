Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC

# Case Background, Technology, and Procedural Posture

## Parties and accused products: AT&T and Nokia’s PIM‑C feature in Nokia radios
The dispute pits Finesse Wireless LLC, the patent holder, against AT&T Mobility LLC and intervening Nokia of America Corporation. Finesse sued AT&T for allegedly using radios that incorporate Nokia’s PIM cancellation feature (referred to in the opinion as “PIM‑C”), asserting that the accused equipment practices methods claimed in two patents owned by Finesse: U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134 (the ’134 patent) and U.S. Patent No. 9,548,775 (the ’775 patent). Nokia intervened in the litigation and defended the operation of its radios. At trial Finesse relied primarily on testimony from its technical expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, and on internal Nokia technical documents to explain how the accused radios operate and why they allegedly satisfy the claim limitations.

## Technical backdrop: IMPs and PIM, internal vs. external PIM, and why radios care
The patents and the disputed technology address intermodulation products (IMPs), which are new frequencies that can be created when two or more radio-frequency signals mix. In practical wireless systems, transmit signals can combine—often when they interact with imperfect or non-linear elements such as metal structures, corroded connectors, or other discontinuities—to produce IMPs that may fall into the same frequency band as desired receive signals, causing interference.

Passive intermodulation (PIM) is a subset of IMPs caused by passive (non-powered) defects or mechanical issues in the signal path—examples include loose connections, rusted contacts, or foreign objects near antennas. The opinion also distinguishes internal versus external PIM: internal PIM arises from mechanical problems inside the radio itself, while external PIM has its source outside the radio (for example, nearby metallic structures). PIM matters because it can create in‑band interference that degrades receiver performance; the accused PIM‑C feature purports to mitigate such in‑band PIM by generating cancellation signals computed from transmitter signals and subtracting estimated IMPs from the received signal.

## Asserted claims: the ’134 patent (isolating signals and canceling IMPs)
The ’134 patent asserts a multi-step method for reducing IMP interference. Representative claim 1 requires, among other steps, that a receiver (i) over‑sample a passband of received signals to create a bit stream that includes both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” (ii) isolate the signals of interest and the interference generating signals using decimating filters, (iii) compute an estimate of the intermodulation products generated by the interference generating signals, (iv) cancel the in‑band intermodulation products using that estimate, and (v) perform closed‑loop phase and amplitude adjustment on the estimations—explicitly using sub‑sample phase shifts. A key factual and legal dispute about these claims centered on whether the accused radios’ receiver actually samples a passband that includes both the claimed “signals of interest” and the claimed “interference generating signals,” or whether portions of the signal path (the “modeled PIM path” in Nokia documents) are generated after sampling and thus cannot be samples of received signals as the claim requires.

## Asserted claims: the ’775 patent (generating cancellation signals via specified multiplications)
The ’775 patent claims a method for generating intermodulation product cancellation signals (ICSs) in a receiver that is co‑located with its transmitter. The representative claim requires the ICSs to be generated continuously and near real time using copies of transmitter signals, and it specifies that passive IMPs addressed by the claim are generated in passive transmitter and receiver components after the high‑power amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter. Critically, claim 1 requires that generating an n‑th order ICS be done by digitally multiplying three signals—labelled S1, S2, and S3—in a particular set of seven third‑order multiplications (for the third order example in the claim). Central disputes at trial were whether (1) the claim language “three signals S1, S2 and S3” requires three distinct (unique) input signals or simply three separately identifiable signals (which could include copies), and (2) whether the accused radios actually perform the seven specified multiplications (or the needed subset of operations) as claimed. Finesse’s infringement theory again relied on Nokia technical documents and expert testimony to show the accused radios perform the claimed multiplicative operations to generate cancellation signals.

## District court proceedings: jury verdict of validity and infringement; damages award
The case was tried in the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas. A jury found all asserted claims of both the ’134 and the ’775 patents to be valid and infringed by the accused Nokia radios used by AT&T. The jury awarded a lump‑sum damages award of $166,303,391 for the remaining life of the asserted patents. Those findings—validity, infringement, and the damages award—formed the basis for post‑trial motions from the defendants.

## Post‑trial motions: JMOLs and new‑trial motions denied
After the verdict, AT&T and Nokia moved for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement and for JMOL or a new trial on damages. The district court denied these motions. The defendants appealed the denial of JMOL on noninfringement for both patents and the denial of a new trial (and JMOL) on damages. The Federal Circuit’s opinion considers whether the jury’s infringement findings were supported by substantial evidence given the expert testimony and the Nokia documents, and whether any of the post‑trial denials were properly decided.

## Appeal posture and jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1)
The appeal was brought to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The court exercised jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1), which grants the Federal Circuit jurisdiction over appeals from final decisions of district courts in patent cases. The Federal Circuit reviewed the denial of JMOL under the regional (Fifth Circuit) law applicable to JMOL denials and reviewed the sufficiency of the evidence for infringement under the substantial‑evidence standard, setting up the opinion’s legal analysis that follows from these factual and procedural foundations.

# Standards of Review and Claim-Construction Framework

This chapter explains the appellate lenses the Federal Circuit applied in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC and how those lenses framed the court’s evaluation of the jury verdicts and the district court’s JMOL/new‑trial rulings. Two distinct review tracks are important in this opinion: (1) the standard for reviewing the denial of JMOL (and denial of a new trial) which follows regional‑circuit law (the Fifth Circuit here), and (2) the standard for claim construction, which the Federal Circuit reviews de novo with any subsidiary factual findings reviewed for clear error. Understanding how those standards operate and which party carried which burdens on appeal explains why the court reversed JMOL for both patents despite some contested procedural rulings below.

## JMOL standard (regional circuit / Fifth Circuit)

The Federal Circuit applied the Fifth Circuit’s standard in reviewing the denial of JMOL. Under that standard the denial is reviewed de novo but with the factual posture that the appellate court must view the trial evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict winner. Reversal is required only if the evidence “points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” Practically, that means an appellate court will overturn a jury verdict for insufficiency of evidence only in rare circumstances where the evidentiary record, taken as a whole (and viewed in the non‑movant’s favor), cannot support a contrary verdict. The Federal Circuit applies that regional rule but also asks whether the jury’s factual findings are supported by substantial evidence.

## Substantial‑evidence review of jury infringement findings

For factual findings underlying infringement, the court asked whether “a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” That is the substantial‑evidence standard the Federal Circuit applied when assessing whether the jury’s finding of infringement could stand. Substantial evidence review is more demanding than mere plausibility: the question is whether the record contains sufficient evidentiary support such that reasonable jurors could have reached the verdict. Where the patent owner’s case rested heavily on expert testimony that was internally inconsistent, the court may determine no reasonable juror could have relied on that testimony to find the claim limitation satisfied.

## Claim construction: de novo with clear‑error review of underlying facts

Claim construction remains a question of law reviewed de novo on appeal, but any underlying factual findings that informed the construction are reviewed for clear error. Thus, the Federal Circuit independently determines the ordinary and customary meaning of claim language, consulting intrinsic evidence first and giving deference only to the district court’s subsidiary factual findings (for example, findings about how a term was used in prosecution or about what a skilled artisan would understand) unless those findings are clearly erroneous.

## Construction specific to the ’775 patent: “three signals S1, S2 and S3”

Applying the claim‑construction rules, the district court construed the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in the ’775 patent to mean the signals must be “separately identifiable” but need not be “unique.” The Federal Circuit treated that construction as grounded in the intrinsic record and the prosecution history, and it accepted the district court’s use of inventor testimony as extrinsic evidence supporting a construction that the three labels can represent signals that are the same or different so long as they are separately identifiable in the processing. This construction matters because the infringement question turns on whether the accused systems supply three separately identifiable inputs (even if one is a copy) and whether the accused multiplications correspond to the seven claimed operations.

## Prosecution history and scope: no clear and unambiguous disclaimer to unique inputs

The opinion reiterates the usual rule for prosecution‑history disclaimer: a patentee must make a “clear and unambiguous” disavowal to lose claim scope. The Federal Circuit examined the prosecution record where the applicant originally overcame prior art (Filipovic) and amended claims to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3.” The court concluded that amendment and the prosecution arguments did not amount to the sort of clear and unmistakable surrender of claim scope that would confine the claims to embodiments having three distinct unique inputs. In short, amending to recite “three signals” did not, on the intrinsic record, amount to an express covenant that excludes systems that in practice use copies or non‑unique inputs, given the prosecution explanations. That conclusion framed the infringement inquiry for the ’775 patent: the question was not solely one of claim scope, but whether the proof at trial actually showed the accused radios performed the seven claimed multiplications using three separately identifiable signals.

## Preservation and waiver context; how the Federal Circuit approached waiver

Preservation issues affected the district court proceedings. The district court found the defendants had waived the argument that “three signals” must be unique because they did not raise that point in a Rule 50(a) motion at the close of evidence. The Federal Circuit acknowledged that waiver ruling but did not rest its decision on waiver alone. Instead, the panel addressed the merits of the prosecution‑history/scope question (rejecting a clear‑disavowal theory) and then proceeded to evaluate whether the evidence at trial was sufficient to support the jury’s findings. For both patents the court ultimately reversed because it concluded the evidentiary record—particularly the way the patentee’s expert framed and mapped signals and multiplications—was insufficient as a matter of substantial evidence to support the jury verdicts. The important practical lesson is twofold: litigants should preserve Rule 50(a) arguments to avoid waiver; and on appeal the Federal Circuit may reach the merits (or an alternative ground) if it can do so while respecting standards of review.

## How the standards steered the court’s outcome in this case

The interaction of these standards explains the result. Under the Fifth Circuit JMOL framework the Federal Circuit asked whether reasonable jurors could have found infringement given the trial evidence. Under substantial‑evidence review the court examined the expert testimony and the technical documents relied on at trial. Under claim‑construction rules it assessed the meaning of “three signals” and reviewed prosecution history for any clear disclaimer. The court concluded that, even accepting the “separately identifiable” construction and finding no clear prosecution disclaimer, the patentee’s expert testimony was internally inconsistent (for the ’134 patent) and the documentary and expert evidence did not show the seven claimed multiplications (for the ’775 patent). Because those failures meant no reasonable jury could have reached the infringement verdicts, the Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s denials of JMOL and vacated damages. The opinion thus illustrates how the interplay of regional JMOL standards, the substantial‑evidence inquiry, and de novo claim construction (with clear‑error review of underlying facts) determines appellate review of jury infringement findings.

# The ’134 Patent—Sampling Limitation and Expert Testimony Breakdown

This chapter explains why the Federal Circuit held that Finesse’s proof on the ’134 patent failed as a matter of law. The core problem was proof of limitation 1[a], which requires that the receiver “over-sampl[e], at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals.” The court concluded that Finesse (the patentee) did not present substantial evidence that the accused radios’ receiver sampled a passband containing both types of signals, because the only clear mapping its expert offered was temporally and architecturally impossible and his trial testimony was internally inconsistent.

## Key limitation 1[a]: receiver must sample a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” to create a bit stream

Limitation 1[a] is a fact-bound, element-level requirement for infringement: the accused device must perform an act (over-sampling a passband to create a bit stream) on a passband that includes two kinds of signals simultaneously—“signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Because Finesse was the plaintiff, it bore the burden at trial of proving that the accused radios met this limitation. On appeal the Federal Circuit reviewed the denial of JMOL under Fifth Circuit law (de novo), measuring whether the jury’s verdict was supported by substantial evidence—i.e., whether a reasonable jury could have found for the patentee in light of the evidence presented.

## Finesse’s proof theory: Nokia diagram; RF ADC as the sampler; mapping “signals of interest” to DL(TX) reference and “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path

Finesse relied almost entirely on one theory: its infringement expert, Dr. Wells, pointed to a Nokia technical diagram that depicted an RF analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC) and various signal paths. Dr. Wells testified that the RF ADC was the claimed sampler that creates the bit stream and that the diagram’s legend—“Red – DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path”—showed that the RF ADC sampled both the “signals of interest” (which he equated with the DL (TX) reference) and the “interference generating signals” (which he equated with the modeled PIM path). His expert report made the same mapping (DL (TX) reference = signals of interest; modeled PIM path = interference generating signals). The jury accepted the infringement verdict based on that testimony.

## Temporal/architectural mismatch: modeled PIM path is generated downstream (e.g., in FPGA) after the RF ADC; cannot be sampled before it exists

Appellants countered—and the cross-examination highlighted—that the “modeled PIM path” shown in Nokia’s documents is generated downstream in the radio’s processing chain (in the GROOT FPGA) after the RF ADC has already sampled the incoming analog passband. In other words, the modeled PIM path is not an input present in the analog passband at the time of sampling; it is computed later from sampled data. That temporal and architectural point is fatal to Finesse’s theory if the modeled PIM path is what the patentee claims is the “interference generating signals”: a device cannot sample a signal that does not yet exist. If the modeled PIM path is the mapped interference source, limitation 1[a] cannot be satisfied because the receiver did not sample that signal as part of the passband.

## Cross-examination pivot: reference to x1 and x2 without clearly mapping them to the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals”

Faced with the temporal problem on cross-examination, Dr. Wells attempted to pivot by referring to “x1” and “x2,” labels that also appear on the Nokia diagram as the two inputs on the red path. He suggested there were “two signals on that red path” and at one point said “x1 and x2 are the two inputs here that are represented by those two signals that are on the red path.” But crucially he never gave the jury a clear, consistent mapping that x1 = (signals of interest) and x2 = (interference generating signals), nor did he explain how the modeled PIM path label related to x1/x2 in a way that cured the temporal problem. Across testimony spanning many pages, he repeatedly identified the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path as the claimed signals and did not plainly correct or reconcile that identification to show x1/x2 were the claimed signals sampled by the RF ADC.

## District court’s JMOL denial: inferred that x1/x2 were the claimed signals despite expert’s repeated identification of DL(TX) and modeled PIM path

The district court denied JMOL and sustained the jury verdict by treating Dr. Wells’ “two signals” references as an affirmative identification of x1 and x2 as the sampled “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” The court emphasized testimony it read as saying that two signals enter the RF ADC and that those are the claimed signals, and thus concluded there was sufficient evidence for the jury. The Federal Circuit, however, found that reading to be unwarranted: the expert’s testimony remained confusing and internally inconsistent, and the district court’s inference required piecing together fragments of testimony spread far apart in the record rather than relying on any clear, contemporaneous mapping offered to the jury.

## Federal Circuit’s reasoning: testimony confusing and self-contradictory; no clear mapping; reliance on Johns Hopkins v. Datascope regarding insufficiency when an expert’s contradictory testimony underpins the verdict

The Federal Circuit reversed. Two points guided the court’s analysis. First, substantial-evidence review requires that a reasonable jury had a reliable evidentiary basis for its finding on each claim limitation; ambiguous or self-contradictory expert testimony that does not clearly establish an element cannot supply that basis. Second, the court relied on its prior decision in Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp., which recognized that an expert’s contradictory testimony cannot be the linchpin of an infringement finding. There, as here, when the patentee’s case rests on an expert who offers inconsistent statements about a dispositive factual point (in Johns Hopkins, whether a device contacted tissue in three dimensions; here, whether the accused receiver sampled both required signal types), the evidence is legally insufficient to support the verdict. The Federal Circuit applied that principle: Dr. Wells repeatedly equated the modeled PIM path with the interference generating signals (which are generated post-sampling), and his later ambiguous references to x1/x2 did not amount to a clear, admissible mapping that established the receiver sampled both required signals.

## Result: no substantial evidence supports limitation 1[a]; reversal of JMOL denial as to all asserted ’134 claims

Because Finesse failed to present substantial evidence that the RF ADC sampled a passband containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” the Federal Circuit concluded the jury’s infringement verdict could not stand. The court reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’134 patent. The decision underscores two practical lessons for litigators: (1) when a claim element turns on temporal or architectural facts (e.g., whether a particular signal exists at a particular point in the processing chain), the patentee must present affirmative, non-contradictory evidence mapping the claim language to the accused system; and (2) an expert’s confusion or internal inconsistency on a dispositive element can be fatal to the patentee’s case because a jury cannot be left to infer a critical mapping from scattered or equivocal testimony.

# The ’775 Patent—Failure to Prove Seven Required Multiplications from Three Signals

This chapter explains why the Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL as to the asserted claims of the ’775 patent: the trial record did not support the jury’s finding that the accused radios perform the seven specific third‑order multiplications the claim requires. It walks through the claim language at issue, the court’s construction, the evidence Finesse presented at trial, the substantive gap in that evidence, and why appellate attempts to fill the gap could not stand.

## Claim 1 structure (context for the disputed limitation)

Claim 1 of the ’775 patent claims a method performed “in a receiver, with a transmitter and the receiver being co‑located,” that generates intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) to cancel passive IMPs. The claim requires that the ICSs be generated “continuously and near real time” and be “using copies of transmitter signals of the transmitter.” The claim also locates where the passive IMPs are generated (in passive transmitter/receiver components downstream of the HPA and transmitter filter). These contextual elements matter because they tie the claimed technique to cancellation of passive IMPs using transmitter signal copies, but the appeal turned on a much narrower, algebraic limitation about how the cancellation signals are produced.

## The critical limitation — limitation 1[d]: seven enumerated third‑order products

Limitation 1[d] requires that “generating the ICSs is based on a power series description of a non‑linear process for generating the IMPs, and includes generating an n‑th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering” seven explicitly listed third‑order products:
- S1×S1×S2
- S1×S2×S2
- S1×S2×S3
- S1×S1×S3
- S2×S2×S3
- S1×S3×S3
- S2×S3×S3

The dispute focuses narrowly on whether the accused radios actually produce these seven enumerated multiplications (or otherwise satisfy that limitation) as the jury was instructed to find.

## Claim construction background: “three signals”—separately identifiable, not necessarily three unique signals

At summary judgment the district court construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to require that the three signals be “separately identifiable,” but the court declined to read the claim as requiring three distinct, unique input signals. The court relied on intrinsic evidence and inventor testimony and concluded that the amendment during prosecution to recite “three signals” did not amount to a clear and unambiguous disavowal of systems that in practice use two unique signals (for example, where one of those signals has a copy). Thus, for infringement the patent did not demand three different physical transmitter signals; it required three separately identifiable signal streams for use in the claimed multiplications.

That construction matters because Appellants argued the accused radios only use two unique signals (x1 and x2), while Finesse argued three signal terms are present in the accused system. But claim construction alone did not resolve whether the accused system actually performs the enumerated seven multiplications.

## What Finesse actually offered at trial

Finesse’s infringement opinion at trial rested principally on a Nokia technical document and Dr. Wells’ testimony interpreting it. The Nokia document, and Dr. Wells’ explanation of it, identified three distinct multiplication‑like operations appearing in the accused radios’ processing chain: examples cited include x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2' (x2 complex conjugate), and x1·|x2|·|x2| (where |·| is a modulus operation and x' denotes complex conjugation). The document also shows other algebraic terms and lists coefficients a0 through a9 in a formula, which Finesse later pointed to on appeal in arguing more multiplications exist in the code/documentation.

Dr. Wells, however, did not present testimony at trial mapping each of the seven claim‑listed products to specific operations in the Nokia document, nor did he demonstrate that the modulus and complex‑conjugate operations shown were equivalent to multiplying the three signal variables in the exact combinations recited in the claim. The district court relied on the trial testimony and the document to deny JMOL, but the Federal Circuit examined whether that proof was legally sufficient under the substantial‑evidence standard.

## The evidentiary gap: three distinct multiplications but no proof of the seven claimed products

The Federal Circuit identified the key shortfall: the Nokia material and the testimony identify at most three distinct multiplication‑type expressions, and there was no evidence that those expressions are repeated or otherwise instantiated so as to yield the seven enumerated products the claim requires. The opinion emphasized three points:

1. The claim lists seven explicit third‑order products of S1, S2, and S3. Proving infringement of limitation 1[d] required evidence that those seven specific combinations are generated (or an acceptable proof that equivalent operations produce the claimed results).

2. The Nokia document, as presented at trial, shows operations involving modulus and complex conjugation rather than raw multiplications of S1, S2, and S3 in the seven recited combinations. Converting between those listed operations and the claim’s multiplications is not self‑evident and was not shown at trial. For example, showing x1·|x2|·|x2| does not establish that S2×S2×S3 exists unless there is a record linking |x2| and x2' to the claimed S2 or S3 roles in a way that produces the required algebraic products.

3. Dr. Wells did not testify how the three distinct multiplications he identified correspond to the seven enumerated products. There was no trial‑record mapping that connected the three expressions to all seven claim terms, and there was no evidence that the accused system repeats or rearranges those three expressions to yield the full set of seven products required by the claim language.

Because the jury’s verdict depended on the expert testimony and the Nokia document, and that proof at most showed three distinct multiplications and did not tie them to the seven required combinations, the Federal Circuit concluded the evidence was insufficient as a matter of law for a reasonable jury to find limitation 1[d] met.

## Appellate attempts to supply missing mappings and why they could not help

On appeal, Finesse argued (and pointed to portions of the Nokia materials) that the accused system actually performs more algebraic operations—Finesse suggested there are ten multiplications listed in the documentation and that some subset could be the seven claimed ones. The Federal Circuit rejected this as post‑trial supplementation for two related reasons:

- The jury never heard the detailed mapping between the ten‑term list (or any particular subset) and the seven specific claim products; Finesse’s elaboration on appeal amounted to a new theory of infringement that was not supported by the trial record. An appellate court cannot affirm infringement based on arguments and mappings not presented to the jury.

- Even accepting that the document lists more algebraic terms, Finesse still failed to identify on the record which of those terms correspond to each of the seven claimed multiplications, and the document entries that were shown involved complex conjugates and moduli rather than plain multiplications of the S1/S2/S3 variables as claimed. Thus there remained no substantial evidence showing the accused radios actually generated the seven enumerated third‑order products.

The court therefore treated the appellate argument as insufficient to cure the trial‑record deficiency.

## Legal standard applied and the result

The Federal Circuit applied the regional‑circuit standard for JMOL denial review (Fifth Circuit): the court examines whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict, nonetheless points so strongly and overwhelmingly that reasonable jurors could not have reached the verdict. For factual findings the court asks whether there is substantial evidence to support the jury’s verdict. Applying these standards, the court held that no reasonable juror could find the seven specific multiplications required by limitation 1[d] were performed based on the evidence presented at trial.

Because limitation 1[d] was not supported by substantial evidence of infringement, the Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL as to all asserted claims of the ’775 patent. The court’s reversal of infringement also led it to vacate the damages award, since damages depended on a sustained infringement verdict.

## Takeaway for reading opinions about claim‑element proof

When a claim enumerates specific algebraic combinations or other discrete operations, a patentee must present evidence at trial that ties the accused device’s operations to those specific items. Expert testimony that points to similar but non‑identical operations (for example, expressions involving modulus or conjugation rather than the exact multiplications listed) or that offers new mappings only on appeal will not suffice. If the patent claims explicit, enumerated products, the record must show either that those exact products are produced or that the evidence admitted at trial proves equivalence in the manner actually argued at trial; absent that, JMOL is appropriate because the substantial‑evidence requirement is not met.

# Damages, Disposition, and Doctrinal Takeaways

## Damages award
The jury awarded Finesse a lump-sum damages verdict of $166,303,391 for the remaining life of the ’134 and ’775 patents. After the verdict, AT&T and Nokia moved for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) and, alternatively, for a new trial on damages; the district court denied both motions. On appeal the Federal Circuit reversed the denials of JMOL of noninfringement for both patents and vacated the damages award. Because the court found the jury’s infringement findings for both patents were not supported by substantial evidence, the damages award could not stand.

## Disposition
The Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s refusal to enter JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’134 patent and the ’775 patent. Because those reversals eliminate the predicate for any damages, the court vacated the $166,303,391 award. The opinion awards costs to the defendants–appellants. The court did not reinstate or remand a different damages finding; the damages judgment was vacated in light of the noninfringement holdings.

## No need to reach new‑trial‑on‑damages arguments
Although the appellants had sought a new trial on damages (a standard the Fifth Circuit reviews for abuse of discretion), the Federal Circuit did not decide those arguments on the merits. Once the panel concluded that the jury verdicts of infringement lacked substantial evidentiary support and reversed JMOL of noninfringement, the damages award was rendered moot and was vacated. In practice, if a court reverses infringement on JMOL, downstream issues about the size or form of a damages award need not be reached unless the case is remanded with a surviving infringement determination.

## Key lessons for litigating complex signal‑processing patents
The opinion illustrates several practical and doctrinal points that are especially important in cases in which liability depends on technical expert mappings and on careful alignment of claim language with device operation.

First, experts must map claim elements to accused devices clearly and consistently at trial. The Federal Circuit reversed the ’134 infringement verdict in large part because the patentee’s expert gave internally inconsistent testimony about which signals corresponded to claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Self‑contradictory expert testimony that is essential to the patentee’s case can be treated as insufficient to carry the plaintiff’s burden.

Second, respect the physical signal flow and timing constraints of the accused systems. A recurring factual flaw here was attempting to treat as “sampled” a signal that, according to the defendants’ technical documents and the district court record, is generated downstream of the analog‑to‑digital converter. The court emphasized that sampling cannot logically occur before a signal is generated; if a claim requires sampling a passband that includes a given source signal, the patentee must prove that the receiver actually samples that source signal rather than a later, internally generated model or estimate.

Third, documentary evidence must actually disclose the claimed operations, not merely something that can be stretched into correspondence on appeal. For the ’775 patent, the Nokia materials relied on at trial listed operations involving moduli and complex conjugates and showed only three distinct multiplications, whereas the claim required seven specific third‑order multiplications of three signals. The Federal Circuit held there was no trial evidence showing the seven claimed multiplications were performed (and the patentee’s attempt to supply a mapping on appeal could not cure that gap). Put simply: if a claim recites particular mathematical operations or a particular sequence of steps, the patentee’s trial record must show how the accused implementation performs those same operations.

Fourth, do not expect appellate briefing to substitute for a developed trial record. The court repeatedly noted that mappings or explanations first offered on appeal (or late in briefing) were not presented to the jury and therefore could not sustain the verdict. Appellate courts review the evidence that was actually adduced at trial; novel theories or detailed mappings must be presented at trial and tested by opposing counsel and the finder of fact.

Fifth, be careful in prosecution and claim drafting about the scope you accept or argue for, but recognize that narrowing amendments do not automatically equate to clear disavowal. The panel declined to find a “clear and unambiguous” surrender of two‑signal systems merely because the applicant amended claims to recite “three signals” during prosecution. Nevertheless, prosecution history can matter, so patentees should be mindful that explicit statements to overcome prior art can be construed as limiting.

Taken together, these points underscore fundamental trial‑level obligations in technically complex cases: present an internally coherent expert story, tie every element to documents or demonstrable device behavior, honor the system’s physical causation and timing, and preserve your theories in the trial record rather than relying on appellate reconstruction.
