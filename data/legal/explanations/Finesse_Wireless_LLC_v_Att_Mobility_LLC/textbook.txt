# Framing the Doctrine: Proving Patent Infringement and Standards of Review

This chapter sets out the doctrinal and procedural frame that governs jury trials and appellate review in patent cases. It explains the elements a patent plaintiff must prove to establish infringement, the burden of proof and how the jury and the trial judge divide responsibility, and the mechanics and standards of judgment as a matter of law under Rule 50. The recent Federal Circuit opinion in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC, reversing the denial of JMOL and vacating a large damages award, provides the principal vehicle for illustrating how these rules operate at the interface of expert proof, claim language, and appellate review.

## Elements of patent infringement and the all‑elements rule

Patent infringement analysis is claim‑driven. A patent claim defines the metes and bounds of the patentee’s exclusionary right; infringement exists only if the accused product or method contains every limitation of an asserted claim, either literally or under the doctrine of equivalents. This is the “all‑elements” or “all‑limitations” rule: the patentee bears the burden of proving, element by element, that the accused device practices the claim language as construed by the court.

In practice, the all‑elements rule shapes both expert proof and litigation strategy. The plaintiff must identify, and the jury must be able to infer, how each claim limitation is present in the accused device or acts. That mapping is often performed by expert testimony that connects technical disclosure about the accused product to the court’s claim constructions. If the plaintiff fails to identify a required limitation or shows it only conjecturally, the absence of proof on that element is fatal to an infringement verdict.

Finesse illustrates these principles clearly. The asserted claims in the ’134 patent required that the accused receiver “over‑sample[] . . . a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals.” The jury was entitled to consider whether the accused radios satisfied that limitation, but only if the evidence—which in this case centered on expert mapping of signals in a Nokia technical document—supported the requisite showing. The Federal Circuit reversed because the plaintiff’s proof on the claimed “interference generating signals” element depended on an expert’s shifting and internally inconsistent testimony about which signals were present at the analog‑to‑digital converter. The court treated the inconsistency as more than a credibility dispute; because the plaintiff had rested its case on that expert mapping, and that mapping was contradictory and legally insufficient to establish the limitation, the all‑elements rule was not satisfied as a matter of law.

The lesson is concrete: a claim limitation is a discrete evidentiary hurdle. Even if other evidence suggests infringement in a general sense, failure to prove a single claim limitation—because the evidence does not establish that particular factual predicate—dooms the claim. Expert testimony is an important tool for satisfying the rule, but expert opinions must be coherent, traceable to the intrinsic claim language, and anchored in admissible, consistent factual foundations.

## Burden of proof: preponderance of the evidence on infringement

The patent plaintiff carries the burden of proving infringement by a preponderance of the evidence—a standard less than proof beyond a reasonable doubt but still requiring that the asserted proposition be more likely true than not. This burden is assigned to the patentee at trial and remains theirs throughout the litigation unless the defendant admits a limitation or stipulates to facts establishing infringement.

Because the preponderance standard is fact‑based, its application turns on the sufficiency and persuasiveness of the evidence submitted to the jury. The plaintiff cannot rely on jury sympathy or general technical plausibility; the evidence must enable a reasonable juror to conclude more likely than not that every claim limitation is met. This often means presenting demonstrative evidence, technical documentation, and expert testimony that identifies specific structures or steps in the accused devices corresponding to the claim limitations.

The Finesse opinion underscores the allocation of this burden. The court emphasized that Finesse “bore the burden of proving the accused radios sample a passband of signals that includes both the ‘signals of interest’ and ‘interference generating signals.’” Because Finesse’s proof on the relevant limitation depended on an expert who equivocated about which items in a technical schematic corresponded to the required signals, the evidence did not suffice to meet the preponderance standard for that claim element. When a patentee’s case turns on one expert’s testimony and that testimony is internally contradictory on a critical point, the preponderance standard may not be satisfied as a matter of law.

## Jury role versus judge role in patent trials

The separation of roles between judge and jury in patent trials is well settled. Claim construction—determining the meaning and scope of the claim language—is a question of law for the court. The court’s constructions frame the jury’s task. The jury’s role is to find the underlying historical and technical facts—e.g., what the accused device does, how the accused device operates, whether a given component corresponds to a claim limitation—applying the court’s claim constructions. Credibility determinations, weighing of conflicting expert opinions, and resolution of factual disputes are classic jury functions.

This allocation produces two recurring practical points. First, the judge’s claim construction is the linchpin of the infringement inquiry; parties must think early about how claim language will be read by the judge because that reading dictates the content of expert testimony and jury instructions. Second, because the jury decides fact questions, appellate review is limited; courts will not ordinarily substitute their view of contested factual inferences for those of jurors unless the evidentiary record cannot support the jury’s finding.

Finesse highlights the interplay. The district court had previously construed certain claim terms (for example, determining that “S1, S2 and S3” in the ’775 patent need be “separately identifiable” but not “unique”), and that construction set the parameters for the jury’s fact‑finding. The jury concluded the accused radios satisfied the constructions, but the Federal Circuit reversed for lack of substantial evidence because the plaintiff’s proofs—again primarily an expert mapping—were legally deficient under the court’s claim constructions. The opinion thus shows how claim construction (judge) and factual mapping (jury) work together, and how an appellate court polices the sufficiency of the jury’s factual conclusions against that combined legal–factual framework.

## JMOL under Rule 50(a)/(b): timing, preservation, and grounds

Rule 50 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure governs judgment as a matter of law (JMOL). The Rule has two critical stages that litigants must understand to preserve appellate rights.

First, Rule 50(a) permits a party to move for JMOL during trial—specifically, after the opposing party has been fully heard on an issue but before submission to the jury. A Rule 50(a) motion must identify the law and the fact issues on which the moving party seeks JMOL; it serves two functions: it affords the trial judge a chance to grant immediate relief and it preserves the issue for appeal if the motion is later renewed following a jury verdict. If the moving party fails to make a timely Rule 50(a) motion, it typically waives the right to pursue a renewed motion after the verdict.

Second, Rule 50(b) allows a renewed motion for JMOL after the jury returns its verdict. The Rule 50(b) motion can be considered only if the moving party made a proper Rule 50(a) motion. When reviewing a Rule 50(b) motion, the trial court applies the same legal standard as in the Rule 50(a) context—asking whether reasonable jurors could reach a contrary conclusion in light of the evidence—and may enter judgment as a matter of law if it concludes no reasonable jury could have found for the nonmoving party.

Substantively, JMOL is appropriate when there is legally insufficient evidence to support a jury verdict on an essential element of the claim. That insufficiency may take several forms: the plaintiff’s evidence might be purely speculative, internally inconsistent, unsupported by a factual basis, or otherwise incapable of supporting a reasonable juror’s verdict. Importantly, JMOL is not an invitation to weigh evidence or reassess credibility; rather, it tests whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict winner, is such that reasonable minds could not differ.

Finesse demonstrates the mechanics and stakes of Rule 50. Appellants moved for JMOL of noninfringement after trial. Their motions raised the foundational argument that Finesse had failed to prove specific claim limitations. The Federal Circuit considered whether the district court’s denial of those motions could stand in light of the evidentiary record. The court concluded that where the patentee’s proof rests on an expert whose testimony is internally contradictory as to a critical limitation, JMOL must be granted because no reasonable jury could have found the element proved. The opinion also touches on preservation issues: for some arguments (for example, the character of “S1, S2 and S3”), the appellants contended waiver for failing to move at Rule 50(a) stage; the panel analyzed waiver but ultimately resolved the case on the merits for the elements at issue.

Practically, Rule 50 timing is a strategic imperative. Moving parties must identify the precise insufficiency during trial; plaintiffs must anticipate and shore up weak evidentiary links where they intend to rest their case on expert testimony.

## Regional‑circuit standard for reviewing denial of JMOL (Fifth Circuit “overwhelming evidence” formulation)

When a district court denies a Rule 50(b) motion, the appellate standard of review is governed by the law of the regional circuit. The Federal Circuit canvasses the controlling regional standard and applies it when reviewing denials of JMOL. In the Fifth Circuit—the regional circuit applicable to Finesse—the denial of JMOL is reviewed de novo, but with an exacting formulation: reversal is warranted only if, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict winner, the evidence “points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” That language reflects a high threshold: the appellate court must conclude that no reasonable juror could have reached the verdict based on the evidence, even when all reasonable inferences and credibility choices are resolved in favor of the prevailing party.

This regional standard differs in texture from the Federal Circuit’s own “substantial evidence” test applied to review of jury findings (discussed in the next section), but the two are related. The regional “overwhelming evidence” formulation preserves deference to jury factfinding and limits appellate reversal to cases of clear evidentiary insufficiency.

In Finesse, the Federal Circuit applied Fifth Circuit law to review the district court’s denial of JMOL. The panel articulated the regional standard and then applied it to the record, concluding that the plaintiff’s expert testimony was so contradictory and insufficient on essential limitations that reasonable jurors could not have found infringement on the asserted claims. Where the patentee’s proof rested on an expert’s equivocation about which signals were present at the sampling stage, and where the technical documents demonstrated that the particular “modeled” signal was generated after sampling, the court found the evidence tipped overwhelmingly in favor of noninfringement on that element. Under the Fifth Circuit formulation, this kind of internally inconsistent, legally inadequate evidence fails even when viewed most favorably to the verdict winner.

## Federal Circuit standards: substantial‑evidence review of jury findings; de novo review of claim construction

The Federal Circuit applies two principal standards on appeal from patent trials. First, claim construction is a question of law reviewed de novo, with the trial court’s subsidiary factual findings at construction reviewed for clear error. This means the appellate court reexamines the judge’s legal interpretation of claim terms without deference, but it will respect any factual findings that undergird those constructions unless they are clearly erroneous. Because claim meaning controls infringement analysis, de novo review of claim construction is often outcome determinative.

Second, factual findings by the jury are reviewed under the substantial‑evidence standard. A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have reached the conclusion in light of the evidence presented at trial. This standard is deferential: appellate courts do not reweigh evidence or reassess witness credibility. However, substantial evidence is not a rubber stamp. If the record lacks any competent evidence on an essential element, or if the only evidentiary support is plainly inadequate—such as conclusory expert testimony with no factual anchoring, testimony that is self‑contradictory on a key point, or an absence of proof as to an indispensable claim limitation—an appellate court will reverse.

Finesse demonstrates the interplay of these standards. The panel noted the court of appeals reviews claim construction de novo but did not need to reexamine certain constructions because its reversal rested on the insufficiency of evidence to prove the court’s own constructions. For the jury’s factual findings, the court applied the substantial‑evidence inquiry and concluded the record could not sustain the verdict. The court relied on its precedent (for example, Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.) recognizing that where a patentee’s case depends on an expert who gives internally inconsistent testimony about a dispositive fact, the testimony may be insufficient as a matter of law to support the verdict. The Federal Circuit therefore balanced deference to jury determinations with the obligation to ensure that factual findings rest on legally sufficient evidence.

Two procedural corollaries follow from this framework. First, where the patentee’s evidence is narrowly focused—say, a single expert mapping the accused device to claim language—the expert’s testimony must be coherent and adequately supported; otherwise appellate courts can and will overturn a jury verdict. Second, because claim construction is legal, litigants must preserve construction arguments at the district court stage and develop the record on how construction affects the mapping to the accused device; absent a preserved construction dispute, appellate review is truncated.

Conclusion and practice points

The Finesse decision exemplifies how evidentiary sufficiency, expert testimony, and the procedural mechanics of Rule 50 converge in patent litigation. The patentee must prove each claim limitation by a preponderance of the evidence; if the proof rests on expert testimony, that testimony must be internally consistent, rooted in factual record evidence, and expressly tied to the court’s claim constructions. Trial counsel should anticipate Rule 50 timing requirements, present clear element‑by‑element mappings in trial evidence and jury instructions, and prepare to challenge at the Rule 50(a) stage any theory the opponent advances as the linchpin of its case. Appellate counsel should remember that the standard governing reversal of a district court’s denial of JMOL is determined by the regional circuit and that the Federal Circuit will police the sufficiency of the jury’s verdict under a substantial‑evidence standard while reexamining claim scope de novo.

For law students, the key takeaways are these: (1) infringement is a claim‑by‑claim, element‑by‑element undertaking; (2) the preponderance burden rests on the patentee; (3) the judge decides claim meaning, the jury decides whether the accused device meets those meanings; (4) Rule 50 motions are technical but potent—move early and precisely; and (5) appellate review affords deference to juries but will not sustain verdicts grounded in legally insufficient expert testimony or where the evidence cannot, as a matter of law, support an essential claim limitation. Finesse is a cautionary illustration of those principles in action.

# Technical Primer: Intermodulation Products, PIM, and DSP Concepts

This chapter supplies the radio-frequency (RF) and digital-signal-processing (DSP) vocabulary and conceptual grounding necessary to understand the technical disputes in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC (the Federal Circuit opinion summarized above). The case turns on what signals are present where in a radio, when they are sampled, and what mathematical operations are performed on copies of those signals. Those are questions of engineering that translate directly into claim-element disputes (e.g., whether a receiver “samples a passband that includes” particular interference-generating signals, or whether a receiver “generates . . . n-th order” products from three signals). A lawyer who can translate between the legal claim language and the underlying signal flow and DSP operations will be better prepared to analyze infringement and invalidity theories and to evaluate expert testimony.

The sections below review the key concepts and then explain why those concepts mattered in the dispute before the court. Each section aims to be practically useful to a law student preparing to read expert reports and to craft or evaluate claim-chart evidence.

## Intermodulation products (IMPs) and passive intermodulation (PIM): internal vs. external sources

Intermodulation products (IMPs) arise when two or more signals mix in a nonlinear element and generate new frequencies that are algebraic combinations of the original frequencies (for example, f1 ± f2, 2f1 ± f2, 2f2 ± f1, etc.). In RF systems this can happen deliberately (e.g., mixers) or inadvertently in any non‑ideal component that exhibits nonlinear behavior. IMPs are particularly problematic when the new frequencies fall “in band” — that is, into the passband carrying desired signals.

The subset called passive intermodulation (PIM) refers specifically to IMPs produced by otherwise passive structures and connections: corroded connectors, loose metal contacts, metal fences near antennas, rusted junctions, or mechanical defects in the radio chain. Those elements are “passive” because they do not contain active semiconductor devices or amplifiers that intentionally mix signals, yet they can behave nonlinearly under high power and produce intermodulation.

Two distinctions are important for litigation and claim construction:

- Internal (or internal-to-the-radio) PIM: PIM generated by components that are physically inside the radio or inside the transmitter/receiver enclosure — for example, a bad connector in the transmit chain after the high-power amplifier (HPA) or in the antenna feed. When PIM is internal to the radio, it is often produced in or near the transmit chain itself and can be proximate in time and space to the receiver front end.
- External (or outside-the-radio) PIM: PIM generated by external objects or connectors outside the radio housing — for example, a metal fence near the antenna or a corroded adapter in the antenna line outside the radio. External PIM is still a source of interference, but its physical origin is outside the radio hardware.

The distinction matters for infringement when claims specify where and how the IMPs are generated (the ’775 patent focused on passive IMPs generated after an HPA and transmitter filter). It also matters for the practical feasibility of certain cancellation approaches: if the interference source is external and variable in time or location, a model-based cancellation scheme that depends on fixed channel estimates may be less effective than when PIM is produced by a stationary internal component.

In Finesse, the patents address mitigation of PIMs that are generated in “passive transmitter components of the transmitter and receiver components of the receiver after a high powered amplifier (HPA) and transmitter filter of the transmitter.” Whether the accused implementation relied on signals that represent the actual interference-generating physical processes at the time of sampling (versus a modeled representation generated later in the digital chain) became a dispositive factual question.

## Transmit/receive paths, co-located transmitter and receiver architectures

Modern wireless systems commonly colocate transmitter and receiver hardware (for example, in a base-station cabinet). Colocation raises the problem that transmitter power can leak into the receiver path and cause or exacerbate PIM. To reason about this you need a mental model of the common blocks and where signals originate:

- Transmit chain: baseband signal generation → digital-to-analog conversion (DAC) → upconversion to RF → power amplifier (HPA) → transmitter filter → antenna feed → antenna.
- Receive chain: antenna → receive filter(s) → low-noise amplifier (LNA) → downconversion to intermediate frequency or baseband → analog-to-digital converter (ADC) → digital baseband processing.

If the transmitter and receiver are physically close, powered transmit signals can interact with passive elements in either the transmit line, the antenna, or the receive line and generate PIM that then enters the receiver front end. The location of the nonlinear element (after the HPA, in the antenna feeder, at an external connector, etc.) and the point at which signals are sampled or copied determines the information available to any cancellation algorithm.

Two practical consequences for patent disputes are worth stressing:

- Timing and location of sampling: A claim that requires sampling “a passband of received signals that include signals of interest and interference generating signals” is only satisfied if the ADC (or a receiver sampling function) actually samples a waveform that contains both those components at the same time. If the alleged “interference generating signal” is only reconstructed or modeled later in a digital block (after sampling), an accused device does not literally sample that modeled signal at the RF ADC.
- Copies of transmit signals: Many cancellation schemes rely on a copy of the transmit waveform (obtained via couplers, taps, or by taking a copy from the baseband transmit path) so that the device can model how nonlinearities would create IMPs from those transmit signals. The provenance of the copy (analog tap vs. digital copy vs. post‑ADC reconstructed signal) affects whether the copy is an actual sampled input or a derived estimate.

The Federal Circuit opinion turned on precisely this: whether the “modeled PIM path” that an expert identified was a physical interference-generating signal that was sampled by the RF ADC, or whether that “modeled” signal was produced inside an FPGA after sampling and therefore not literally present at the sampling instant.

## Oversampling and decimation; creating and filtering bit streams

Understanding the terms “oversampling,” “decimation,” “passband,” and “bit stream” is essential to parsing claims and diagrams in RF/DSP patents.

- Oversampling: Sampling a continuous-time signal at a rate substantially higher than the Nyquist rate (twice the bandwidth of interest). Oversampling can simplify analog anti-aliasing filter design and enable digital filtering and decimation that more cleanly isolate narrow bands inside a wide RF passband.
- Passband and baseband: A passband is a frequency band containing signals of interest (for example, a carrier band at RF). Baseband refers to the signal spectrum shifted to be centered at zero frequency (I/Q representation). Many receivers downconvert the RF passband to baseband before or after sampling.
- Bit stream / sample stream: The ADC converts an analog waveform to a digital stream of samples (commonly called a bit stream or sample stream). Those samples are then processed by digital circuits (filters, decimators, mixers, multipliers).
- Decimation: Reducing the sampling rate by integer factors, typically performed by first applying an anti-aliasing digital filter (lowpass or bandpass) and then downsampling. Decimating filters are often used to isolate a narrower band (e.g., the “signals of interest”) from a wideband sampled stream.

Crucially for patent proof, whether the ADC samples a waveform that includes particular components depends on the order and placement of these stages. If the ADC samples the full RF passband (oversampling), then later digital decimation can separate different components into separate digital streams. But if a putative “interference generating signal” is only constructed by combining digital samples after the ADC stage (i.e., it is not a physical analog input at the ADC), then the ADC did not sample that particular waveform.

In practice, block diagrams in vendor documents often annotate multiple conceptual “paths” on the same chart. The legal problem is that labels like “modeled PIM path” can describe a software-generated estimate derived in the FPGA; such a modeled path is not the same as an analog waveform that was present on the antenna and sampled by the RF ADC. The case illustrates how an expert’s testimony must tie the claim terms to specific physical or sampled signals, not merely to diagram labels.

From an evidence perspective, a party seeking to prove literal infringement of a claim limitation like “oversampling . . . to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals” should produce: (a) the ADC functional description showing what analog spectrum was at the ADC input; (b) timing/ordering of blocks showing whether the alleged interference signal exists at or prior to the ADC; and (c) signal captures, oscilloscope traces, or other measurements demonstrating the two components are present in the sampled stream. Absent that, a modeled reconstruction generated only after sampling cannot satisfy a literal “sampling” claim element.

## Phase and amplitude adjustment; sub-sample phase shifts

Many cancellation schemes require aligning an estimate of an interference waveform to the observed interference so that subtraction is effective. Two basic alignment dimensions are amplitude (scaling) and phase (timing). Claims that require “phase and amplitude adjustment . . . in a closed loop manner” and that specifically require “sub-sample phase shifts” implicate concrete DSP techniques.

What these phrases mean in engineering terms:

- Amplitude adjustment: Multiplying the modeled interference samples by a scalar (or by a slowly time-varying gain) so the cancellation signal has the same magnitude as the unwanted component.
- Phase adjustment: Rotating the phase of the modeled samples so the cancellation signal lines up in time with the unwanted component. At baseband this is often implemented as complex multiplication by a phasor exp(jθ) or by applying a delay.
- Sub-sample phase shifts: A “sample” is a discrete-time tick. A sub-sample shift means aligning signals with a precision finer than the sample period: fractional delays. One cannot implement a non-integer-sample delay simply by shifting the sample index. Instead, one uses fractional delay techniques:
  - Interpolation / fractional-delay filters: Apply an FIR or IIR filter that approximates a time shift smaller than one sample period. These are standard DSP constructs (e.g., using Lagrange interpolation or Farrow structures).
  - Complex phasor multiplication (frequency-domain): For narrowband signals represented in complex baseband, a fractional phase shift corresponds to multiplying by a complex exponential whose phase varies continuously; this is equivalent to a fine-tuned time delay for the tone(s) of interest.
  - All-pass filters: Phase-response-shaping filters can impart frequency-dependent delays and are sometimes used to fine-tune phase without altering amplitude.
- Closed-loop adjustment: The cancellation system measures residual error (the remaining interference after subtraction) and adjusts amplitude/phase parameters adaptively to minimize that residual. Common adaptive algorithms include LMS (least-mean-square) and RLS (recursive least squares). Closed-loop here means an iterative feedback process that updates parameters in near‑real time.

For litigation, the engineering upshot is twofold. First, “sub-sample phase shifts” is a specific technical limitation that is not satisfied by coarse sample-aligned delays; expert testimony should explain what implementation accomplishes sub-sample shifts and point to code, filter coefficients, or design documents showing fractional‑delay filters or equivalent. Second, the location of the adjustment loop matters: if the estimate is adjusted in an FPGA after sampling, an accused system may still satisfy a claim that requires phase/amplitude adjustment — but that depends on whether the claim requires that the adjustments be performed on estimations that are themselves formed from sampled signals that included the interference-generating sources at the ADC.

In Finesse, claims required performing phase and amplitude adjustment on estimations of the interfering signals in a closed‑loop manner and performing sub‑sample phase shifts. Assessing whether the accused radios met those limitations required identifying the exact point where the estimates were formed and whether fractional-delay mechanisms were present.

## Model-based PIM cancellation (PIM‑C) features in commercial radios

Model-based cancellation is a practical, widely used approach to mitigate PIM in colocated transmit/receive systems. The steps of a typical model-based PIM cancellation (PIM-C) implementation are:

1. Obtain copies of the transmit signals. This can be done:
   - Analog tap: a small proportion of the analog RF transmit waveform is tapped off and fed into the receiver or into a modeling circuit.
   - Digital copy: copies of the baseband transmit samples (before DAC) are available in the digital domain.
   - Post-sampling reconstruction: some designs reconstruct a modeled version of a PIM source using signals that have already been sampled by ADCs.

2. Choose a mathematical model of the nonlinear process. Common choices include:
   - Power-series (polynomial) models: assume the PIM generation is approximated by a finite-order polynomial (e.g., third-order terms dominate).
   - Volterra series or memory-polynomial models: model nonlinearities with memory (so past samples influence the present nonlinearity).
   - Parametric or measured lookup-table models.

3. Generate candidate intermodulation terms (basis functions) from the transmit signal copies. For a third-order model with three input signals S1, S2, S3, the basis functions include triple products such as S1·S1·S2, S1·S2·S2, and so on (more detail in the next section). These basis terms are filtered and scaled to produce an estimated PIM waveform.

4. Align the estimated waveform to the observed interference (amplitude/phase/time) using the techniques discussed earlier (fractional delays, complex phasor adjustment).

5. Subtract the aligned estimate from the received samples (cancelation), and measure the residual error for closed-loop adaptation.

Practical radios implement subsets of this architecture. Vendor documents (as in the Nokia documentation cited in the case) often show flowcharts or formulae indicating which basis functions are used (for example, complex conjugate or modulus operations) and how many multiplications are performed. Translating those vendor documents into a claim chart requires careful mapping: which signals in the vendor diagram correspond to claim terms like S1, S2, S3? Are the required multiplications present? Are they performed on the exact signals recited by the claim or only on transformed versions (conjugate/modulus)? And crucially, were the signals used to produce the modeled PIM themselves sampled by the ADC, or are they derived later?

The Federal Circuit rejected the jury’s infringement finding in part because the plaintiff’s expert relied on a “modeled PIM path” that the record showed was generated inside an FPGA after sampling — so it could not have been sampled by the RF ADC as the ’134 claim required. The broader lesson: model-based blocks in vendor diagrams are not ipso facto “signals of interest” or “interference-generating signals”; the difference between an analog sampled waveform and a digitally constructed model must be carefully teased out.

## Multiplication of signals in DSP: baseband/complex representations, modulus (|x|), complex conjugate (x′)

Many PIM models and cancellation schemes operate in complex baseband. A short primer on complex signal operations clarifies why labels and operations matter when mapping to claim limitations that recite multiplications among named signals.

- Complex baseband representation: A real RF sinusoid can be represented in complex baseband as x(t) = I(t) + jQ(t), where I and Q are the in‑phase and quadrature components. Operations that are simple in the RF domain (e.g., frequency translation) correspond to complex multiplication at baseband.
- Complex conjugate x′ or x*: For x = I + jQ, the complex conjugate x′ = I − jQ. Multiplying x by its conjugate gives x·x′ = I^2 + Q^2 = |x|^2, which is real and equals the instantaneous power (magnitude squared) of the complex sample.
- Modulus |x|: Commonly used to mean magnitude (√(I^2 + Q^2)). In some DSP shorthand, |x| is used when the algorithm uses the magnitude waveform; in others, operations use |x|^2 (magnitude squared) because it is simpler to compute (no square root). It is essential to know which is intended because multiplication by |x| versus by |x|^2 or by x′ yields different algebraic terms.
- Multiplying signals: When claims recite multiplying three signals S1·S2·S3 (third-order products), in a complex baseband implementation the actual operations might be S1·S2·S3, or S1·S2·x2′, or S1·|x2|·|x2|, etc. Those are not algebraically equivalent in general, and the mapping must be shown explicitly.

The combinatorics for the third-order terms in the ’775 claim are concrete. Given three signals S1, S2, S3, the claim lists seven third-order products that form the basis set for third-order cancellation:

- S1·S1·S2
- S1·S2·S2
- S1·S2·S3
- S1·S1·S3
- S2·S2·S3
- S1·S3·S3
- S2·S3·S3

(There are seven distinct combinations if order and permutation symmetries are collapsed appropriately; the patent lists these representative groupings.) To prove infringement of a claim that requires producing those seven distinct multiplications, an accused device must be shown to form functions that are algebraically equivalent to those seven terms or to produce a set of products that can be shown to span the same function space used by the patentee’s model.

Two pitfalls arise in practice:

1. Two-versus-three-signal dispute: If the accused device only has two distinct input signals (call them x1 and x2), but the claim requires three separately identifiable signals S1, S2, S3, the question is whether two signals sufficed under the claim language. Some claim constructions permit “three separately identifiable” signals to include two distinct signals plus a copy or duplicate (for example, S2 = S3 or S1 = S2). Whether that satisfies the claim depends on prosecution history and claim language. If the patentee amended claims to say “three” to distinguish over prior art that used only two inputs, courts will scrutinize whether there was a narrowing disclaimer that excluded two-input implementations. The technical reality is that if one substitutes S3 = S2 in the list above, some distinct algebraic products collapse (e.g., S1·S2·S3 becomes S1·S2·S2), reducing the number of algebraically distinct products. The patent required seven distinct multiplications in the ’775 claim; an accused product that produces only three unique combinations cannot satisfy a claim that requires seven unless the record shows that the three listed products are carried out in such a way or repeated to produce the seven required products.

2. Conjugate/modulus versus plain multiplication: Vendor documentation often expresses basis functions in terms of conjugates or absolute values: x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2′, x1·|x2|·|x2|, etc. These are not the same as S1·S1·S2 in algebraic form unless one shows that the modulus or conjugate operations map to particular signals or that the product set spans the same subspace. For example, x1·x1·x2′ uses a conjugate on x2, which flips the sign of the Q component; x1·|x2|·|x2| uses magnitudes and yields a non-linear but real-valued scaling on x1. An expert must explain how the vendor’s combination of conjugate and modulus terms corresponded to or generated the seven algebraic third‑order terms recited in the claim, and whether any algebraic identities or filterings produce the claimed products.

In Finesse, the court concluded that the Nokia document relied on by the plaintiff listed three distinct multiplications and also used conjugate and modulus operations, and there was no evidence that the accused radios actually performed the seven claimed multiplications or repeated the three distinct operations so as to generate all seven. The expert testimony did not supply the necessary algebraic mapping at trial; on appeal the court found the record insufficient to support that aspect of infringement.

## Applying the technical concepts to legal proof: practical pointers

Translating the engineering ideas above into effective evidence for claim elements (or for defenses) requires attention to three recurring themes:

1. Tie claim terms to physical/sampled signals precisely. Labels in vendor diagrams (e.g., “modeled PIM path” or “DL (TX) reference”) are shorthand; an expert should explain whether the labeled path is (a) an analog waveform present at the ADC input, (b) a digital copy of a transmit baseband sample, or (c) a digitally reconstructed model generated after sampling. For a claim element that requires sampling the interference-generating signal, evidence must show the ADC input included that signal at the sampling time.

2. Show exact operations and algebraic equivalence. If a claim requires specific multiplicative terms among S1, S2, S3, an expert should map each claimed product to an explicit operation performed by the accused device (pointing to source code, fixed-point arithmetic blocks, DSP diagrams, or hardware multipliers). If the vendor’s documentation uses conjugates or magnitudes, the expert must show how those operations correspond to the claimed algebraic terms (or explain why the claim covers those transformed operations).

3. Explain timing and placement of adaptive loops and fractional delays. For claim limitations invoking closed-loop phase and amplitude adjustment or sub-sample phase shifts, a competent expert will identify the algorithm (LMS, RLS, or other), show where in the processing chain the adjustments occur, and document the implementation of fractional-delay filters, interpolators, or phasor adjustments. Demonstrating that an adjustment is “sub-sample” requires pointing to an implemented fractional delay or equivalent; mere assertion that a loop “tightens timing” is insufficient.

For litigators, the case teaches that confusing, internally inconsistent, or ambiguous expert testimony that does not cleanly map claim language to concrete elements in the accused system creates vulnerability to JMOL. When the accused architecture uses modeled signals constructed after sampling, claim elements requiring sampling of particular signals often fail on the record, because sampling occurs only once at the ADC. Similarly, when vendor documents list a set of DSP operations, cursory mapping to claim language without algebraic demonstration of equivalence or repetition will invite appellate reversal.

Conclusion

The Federal Circuit’s reversal in Finesse underscores that seemingly minor technical distinctions — whether a PIM estimate is “modeled” downstream of sampling, whether a multiplication uses a conjugate or an absolute value, whether a three‑signal product actually appears or is realized as fewer products — can be decisive in patent cases. This chapter has provided the core DSP and RF concepts you will encounter in such litigation and the practical ways those concepts intersect with claim construction and infringement proof. A careful lawyer will use this vocabulary to audit expert reports, demand precise mappings from claim terms to device elements, and make the timing and algebraic nature of operations a central focus of fact development and cross‑examination.

# How to Prove Infringement of Method Claims

Method claims require the patentee to show that the accused party actually performs the claimed sequence of operations. That seemingly simple requirement generates a host of evidentiary and doctrinal wrinkles: each claim step must be proven performed by the accused actor, the steps must be performed in the claimed order when the claim requires ordering, and the proof must demonstrate that the accused system operates with the physical signals and at the moments the claim contemplates. Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC, decided by the Federal Circuit in 2025, provides a compact illustration of these rules in practice and of the ways in which evidentiary gaps — unclear expert testimony, shaky mappings from vendor documents, and a failure to show temporal feasibility — can be fatal at the JMOL stage. This chapter uses Finesse as a vehicle to explain what a plaintiff must prove and how to prove it.

## Method claims: proving performance of each step and the sequence of operations

A method claim is a sequence of steps. To prove infringement, the patentee bears the burden of proving that the accused device or actor performed every claim limitation. That burden is strict: omission of a single required step—or proof that a step cannot be satisfied by the accused system—dooms a finding of literal infringement. The question is a factual one for the jury (or the court on bench trial), but it must be supported by substantial evidence; appellate courts review denials of JMOL de novo under regional-circuit standards and will reverse when the evidence points so overwhelmingly for one side that no reasonable juror could disagree.

Finesse underscores two aspects of that rule. First, the Federal Circuit emphasized that Finesse (the patentee) had to prove the accused radios sampled “a passband of received signals that includes signals of interest and interference generating signals.” That was not asserted as an abstract architectural match but as a temporal fact: the receiver must sample a passband containing those signals. Second, the court demonstrated the danger of relying on ambiguous testimony. Finesse’s expert repeatedly identified certain labels in a vendor diagram as corresponding to claimed signals, but his testimony was internally contradictory and, more importantly, failed to show the required temporal relation: the entity the expert identified as the “interference generating signals” (the “modeled PIM path”) was not present at the time of sampling because it was generated after the analog-to-digital conversion. The Federal Circuit held that the jury’s verdict lacked substantial evidence because the plaintiff, relying on that expert, failed to show the accused radios satisfied the sampling limitation.

Practical consequences follow. The patentee must present evidence sufficient to establish every step, and, where the claim speaks in temporal terms (e.g., “sampling a passband of received signals”), the patentee must show the claimed events actually occur in that temporal order in the accused system. It is not enough to show that, at some later point in the processing chain, the system produces a representation of a signal that the patentee wishes to map onto a claim limitation if the claim requires the signal to exist at an earlier point.

## Using design documents, source code, and testing as proof of performance

How does a patentee prove that a device performs a method? Typical sources of proof are (1) design documents and architecture diagrams from the vendor, (2) source code or firmware and documentation that explains algorithmic steps, (3) live or lab testing and captured signal traces, and (4) testimony from engineers who built or work with the system. Each evidence type has strengths and weaknesses, and the most persuasive cases use multiple types in combination.

Design documents and block diagrams are often the starting point: they show the intended signal flow, the location of key blocks (ADCs, filters, FPGAs, DSPs), and where signals appear and are transformed. But a diagram alone rarely proves what operations actually run in shipped units or what data values exist at a particular physical interface at a particular time. Source code or firmware is stronger because it shows the concrete operations the device executes; code comments, function names, and the code itself can disclose the exact mathematical operations (e.g., multiplications, conjugations) and their ordering. Hardware description language (HDL) used to program FPGAs can similarly show what blocks are synthesized and when they operate.

Testing and captured traces are decisive for temporal questions. If a claim requires that an ADC sample a passband that includes two signals, the best evidence is a captured analog or digital waveform showing the presence of those signals at the ADC input at sampling time, or a test configuration that demonstrably places both signals into the ADC’s passband and then captures the ADC output. Logs and telemetry showing that a user enabled a feature and that the system executed a particular processing chain during specific times also help connect user action to method steps.

Finesse relied heavily on vendor documents and an expert’s interpretation of those documents. The Federal Circuit’s critique shows the limits of doing so without corroborating contemporaneous technical evidence and clear, consistent explanation. Vendor diagrams that use compact legends (for example, “red – DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path”) can be ambiguous about whether two things are separate signals or a single labeled path, and an expert’s inconsistent mapping from diagram labels to claim terms is a weak foundation for a verdict. When relying on vendor materials, the patentee should (a) produce the actual versions of code or hardware that were shipped, (b) tie the diagram elements to code modules or hardware blocks, and (c) use captures or demonstrations to show the required signals exist at the stated points in time.

## Linking technical blocks to claim limitations: receivers, ADCs, filters, multipliers

A recurring task in method-claim litigation is mapping elements of an accused system’s architecture to the claim language. Courts and juries must translate engineering concepts—ADCs, decimating filters, FPGA-generated models, digital multipliers—into the more abstract words of claims: “receiver,” “isolating signals,” “computing an estimate,” “digitally multiplying S1×S1×S2,” and so on. This mapping must be explicit and consistent.

Start from claim construction. Courts construe claim terms to determine the legal meaning to be applied at trial. Once those constructions exist, the patentee must show the accused system contains components that satisfy the constructions. For example, if “receiver” is construed to include a particular set of elements or operations, the plaintiff must show that the accused device’s RF ADC and downconversion chain meet that construction. The mapping should identify (i) the component in the accused device (e.g., the RF ADC, an FPGA module, a digital filter block), (ii) the function that component performs (e.g., sampling, decimation, complex conjugation, multiplication), and (iii) the claim limitation it satisfies.

Documentary and demonstrative proof is useful: point to a block in a vendor diagram and then to the specific module name in source code or HDL, explain the code that implements the operation, and show a simulation or captured output demonstrating the result. Where the claim recites a particular mathematical operation (e.g., multiply S1×S1×S2), identify the exact code or hardware multiplier, the operand sources (which variables or signals supply S1, S2), and the timing at which those operands are valid. If the accused device uses conjugates or magnitudes (|x|, x') rather than the raw signal, explain why that is or is not within the claim’s meaning; do not assume a jury or judge will infer it.

Finesse illustrates why such explicit mapping matters. For the ’775 patent, the claimed limitation required generating seven specific third-order products from three signals S1, S2, S3. The Nokia document Finesse relied on listed three distinct multiplications (and possibly more numeric coefficients), but did not show the seven claimed products or how three listed operations corresponded to the seven claimed combinations. The Federal Circuit reversed because the plaintiff never provided the jury with a mapping showing that the listed operations equaled the seven claimed multiplications. The lesson is straightforward: when a claim requires a set of specific mathematical combinations, produce evidence that the accused device produces those exact combinations (or acceptable equivalents under claim construction), and show how the operations in the product’s code or hardware translate into the claimed algebraic expressions.

## Temporal feasibility: a step cannot operate on a signal that does not yet exist

Method claims frequently embody temporal relationships among steps. A sampling step, for instance, may require that a receiver sample a passband containing certain signals; a modeling step may require computing an estimate “from source signals” that exist at that time. Time matters: an operation cannot literally act on a signal that does not yet exist, and proof that an operation uses a later-generated representation will not satisfy a limitation that requires earlier presence or sampling.

Finesse’s treatment of the ’134 patent shows this issue in stark relief. One claim required “over-sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals.” Finesse’s expert mapped “interference generating signals” to a “modeled PIM path,” but that modeled path was generated in an FPGA after the RF ADC had already sampled the RF input. The modeled PIM was therefore not present at the time of ADC sampling. Because the claim expressly required that the received signals (including interference-generating signals) be sampled, a later-generated model cannot satisfy that limitation. The appellate court reversed the jury verdict because the plaintiff failed to show the necessary temporal fact.

Practically, a patentee must be able to show not only that a signal exists somewhere in the processing chain but that it exists where and when the claim requires it. Useful evidence includes oscilloscope captures at component inputs, timestamps on logs demonstrating when data streams are produced, and annotated signal-flow timelines that connect physical inputs to claim steps. Where an accused device generates internal “models” or derived signals downstream of sampling, the patentee must explain how those downstream items relate to the claimed “received signals” and must avoid conflating a post-sampling computational artifact with a physical received signal present at sampling time.

## Direct infringement by users of vendor features (e.g., carriers using PIM-C in supplied radios)

Proving that a user — such as a carrier — directly infringes a method claim requires showing the user (not merely the vendor or the device) performed every claimed step. This is often straightforward when the user directly operates the device and the device performs the steps automatically upon user activation. It is harder when steps are split between parties (the classic divided-infringement problem) or when the user only passively receives a device with capabilities but does not actively cause the claimed steps to be performed.

Finesse involved both carriers (AT&T) and a vendor (Nokia). The district court allowed a jury to find infringement as to both. The appellate opinion does not restate in detail the facts the jury credited about which party performed which steps, because the Federal Circuit reversed for lack of substantial evidence that any party performed certain claim steps. But the case highlights two points of general applicability.

First, a vendor’s provision of a device capable of performing method steps does not automatically translate into direct infringement by a user; the patentee must show that the user actually caused the device to execute the steps. Evidence of user activation (configuration records, operational logs, billing or maintenance records) and contemporaneous telemetry showing the device performed the steps under the user’s control is the typical proof required.

Second, where the user performs some steps and the vendor performs others, the patentee must show that the user exercised the necessary direction or control over the vendor’s performance so that a single actor can be attributed with performing all steps. Absent such direction or a contractual obligation, liability theories shift to inducement or contributory infringement (which have their own elements) or fail entirely. While the Federal Circuit did not reach divided-infringement issues in Finesse, students should remember that establishing a single actor is essential for method claims and that proof of user activation and ongoing control is usually necessary to prove direct infringement by a user.

## Common pitfalls: ambiguity in what constitutes a "signal" versus a transform of a signal

One of the most frequent problems in method-claim cases involving signal processing is conflating different representations of information. A “signal” in common engineering parlance can be a physical RF waveform, a digitized bitstream, a complex-valued baseband sample, a magnitude (absolute value) of a complex number, or a model computed in firmware. Claims that use the word “signal” must be mapped carefully to the specific representation the claim intends; otherwise, the patentee can find itself arguing that a downstream transform (a conjugation, a modulus, a modeled path) counts as the original signal, even when the claim requires the original.

Finesse shows the problem in two ways. For the ’134 patent, the modeled PIM path was an internal model generated post-sampling; treating that model as the “interference generating signal” that the receiver must have sampled was a temporal mismatch and a categorical conflation of a modeled representation with a received signal. For the ’775 patent, the Nokia documents showed multiplications of x1 with |x1| and x2 with |x2| and operations involving complex conjugates. The claim required particular algebraic triplets S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, and so on. Mapping S2 to x2 in one place and to |x2| or x2' (the conjugate) in another without an explanation of why those variants are equivalent to “S2” for claim purposes is a risky strategy. The Federal Circuit rejected that approach where the plaintiff failed to show how the accused device’s list of operations corresponded to the claim’s seven permutations.

Avoid these pitfalls by defining, at the outset, what “signal” means for the claim construction and then showing concrete links between that definition and the accused device’s representations. If the accused device uses transformed versions of a signal (modulus, conjugate, filtered version), be prepared to explain whether and why those transforms are covered by the claim language or to show that the device in fact uses the raw signal at the claim point. Expert testimony can bridge the engineering/legal gap, but it must be internally consistent and tied to documentary or experimental corroboration; courts will discount inconsistent or speculative testimony.

## Avoiding evidentiary and doctrinal traps — lessons and a practical checklist

Finesse crystallizes a number of “how-to” lessons for proving method-claim infringement and for avoiding reversal on JMOL grounds. The following checklist consolidates those lessons into practical steps a patentee should take before trial and at trial:

1. Know your claim constructions and work from them. Tie every mapped component to the court’s constructions; anticipate disputes on contested terms.

2. Prove each step, literally. Identify the accused component or actor that performs each step, and produce contemporaneous evidence (code, logs, HDL, hardware files, test captures) showing the operation actually occurs.

3. Demonstrate temporal ordering. If a step must occur before another (e.g., sampling before modeling), show timestamps, oscilloscope traces, or system logs that establish the order. Do not rely on post hoc models as substitutes for earlier physical signals.

4. Provide explicit mappings for mathematical operations. If a claim specifies particular multiplications or combinations, show the device produces those exact algebraic forms or explain why an observed operation is equivalent under the claim language.

5. Use multiple evidence types. Diagrams are fine as guides; they must be backed by code or hardware descriptions and by dynamic evidence such as captures or test results that show real-world execution.

6. Keep expert testimony consistent, detailed, and tethered to documents. The Federal Circuit will reject verdicts based on self-contradictory expert testimony or testimony that lacks documentary anchors.

7. For user-based infringement theories, show user actions. Logs showing that a carrier enabled a feature, configuration records, and telemetry proving that the feature ran under the carrier’s control help establish that the user — not just the vendor — performed the steps.

8. Anticipate alternative interpretations. If a diagram legend groups multiple concepts under one label (e.g., “DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path”), anticipate and address possible confusions in advance; do not expect a jury to infer distinctions from snippets of testimony.

9. Address prosecution history issues preemptively. If claim amendments during prosecution might suggest narrowing, be prepared to explain whether and why an amendment did or did not create a disavowal of claim scope. Continental Circuits and similar precedent require “clear and unmistakable” disclaimers to limit claim scope.

10. Be precise with jargon. Establish, for the trier of fact, what engineering terms mean in the context of your claim language and the accused product. Avoid assuming highly technical shorthand will translate to legal claim terms without explication.

In sum, proving infringement of method claims is a fact-intensive task that demands explicit, consistent, and temporally precise proof. Finesse Wireless illustrates how reliance on ambiguous diagram labeling and inconsistent expert mapping — and a failure to show that an alleged “interference generating signal” existed at the time of sampling — can result in reversal even after a jury verdict. The disciplined approach above reduces the risk that a verdict will be undone on appeal for lack of substantial evidence.

# Claim Construction Fundamentals

A central task in patent litigation is construing the patent claims — the legally operative descriptions of the patentee’s monopoly. Courts undertake claim construction to determine the scope of the patent right, and that construction often proves dispositive of infringement and validity disputes. This chapter explains the governing methodology for construing claim language, the hierarchy and role of intrinsic and extrinsic evidence, the standard of appellate review, and how constructions shape the patentee’s evidentiary burdens at trial. The recent Federal Circuit decision in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC, reversing denial of JMOL of noninfringement, supplies concrete illustrations of these principles: the opinion addresses the interplay of claim language, specification, and prosecution history; the permissible role of inventor and expert testimony; and how ambiguous or internally inconsistent expert testimony can doom a patentee’s proof when claim construction requires a clear mapping between claim elements and accused features.

## Intrinsic evidence hierarchy: claim language, specification, prosecution history

The starting point for claim construction is the intrinsic record: the claim language itself, followed by the patent specification, and then the prosecution history. Phillips v. AWH Corp. remains the touchstone: claim terms are given the meaning a person of ordinary skill in the art (POSITA) would ascribe in the context of the intrinsic record. Courts must therefore read the claims in the full context of the patent, not in isolation.

Claims control. The text of the claims is the primary determinant of claim scope. Ordinary words used in ordinary fashion generally carry their ordinary meanings to a POSITA, unless the patentee has acted to redefine terms or used them in a special sense. If the claim term is clear on its face, the courts need not look further to construe it.

The specification is the primary source for determining whether a claim term needs special definition or clarification. It explains how the inventor describes the invention and often includes preferred embodiments, examples, and detailed explanations of the operation and purpose of claimed features. The specification can act to narrow a claim term: expressly adopting a particular definition or repeatedly and unmistakably describing the invention in a limited way may amount to a “definitional” or “disavowal” narrowing the ordinary meaning. But courts must be careful: the specification should not be used to read limitations from preferred embodiments into the claims absent clear intent.

Prosecution history is the third rung of intrinsic evidence. Statements made to the Patent Office during examination — claim amendments, arguments, and distinguishing remarks — can limit claim scope by way of prosecution disclaimer or estoppel. When a patentee clearly and unambiguously disclaims a particular meaning or range during prosecution to obtain allowance, that disavowal bargains away claim scope and binds the patentee in later litigation. However, the bar for disavowal is high: the disclaimer must be “clear and unmistakable.” Merely amending claims to recite particular language does not always amount to a disavowal if the amendment is not accompanied by clear statements disclaiming alternatives.

The Finesse Wireless opinion applies this hierarchy. There, the Federal Circuit evaluated a dispute over whether the prosecution amendment and examiner dialogue produced a “clear and unambiguous” surrender of claim scope over techniques using only two signals. The court recognized that the amendment to require “three signals S1, S2 and S3” altered the claim language but concluded that, on the intrinsic record, the amendment did not amount to the kind of unequivocal disavowal that would categorically exclude systems using two distinct inputs. The opinion thus illustrates the common pattern: claim language controls, the specification and prosecution history inform deviation from ordinary meaning, and prosecution statements are scrutinized for clarity before they are treated as disclaimer.

## Extrinsic evidence: expert and inventor testimony as context, not control

Extrinsic evidence — dictionaries, treatises, expert testimony, and inventor statements outside the intrinsic record — may help courts understand technical matters or confirm the meaning a POSITA would attach to claim language. But extrinsic evidence plays a subordinate role. It cannot be used to contradict the claim language or the specification or to create a narrower construction that conflicts with the intrinsic record.

Expert testimony is indispensable at times because claim construction involves technical subject matter and factual predicates about how a POSITA would understand terms. Teva Pharmaceuticals USA, Inc. v. Sandoz, Inc. clarified that when a district court makes subsidiary factual findings about extrinsic matters (for example, the meaning of a term to a POSITA based on expert evidence), those findings are reviewed for clear error on appeal. The Federal Circuit in Finesse relied on expert testimony at trial to attempt to map claim terms to accused devices, but the case also underscores the limits of expert evidence when it conflicts with the intrinsic record or is internally inconsistent.

Inventor testimony is a particular species of extrinsic evidence that courts will consider but treat with caution. Testimony by an inventor can shed light on how certain terms were intended or on the technical operation of embodiments, but it cannot be used to rewrite claim language or to create a narrower claim construction absent supporting intrinsic evidence. In Finesse the district court credited inventor testimony and allowed a claim construction (three signals could be the same or copies) consistent with that testimony. The Federal Circuit emphasized, however, that such extrinsic support cannot substitute for the intrinsic record when the intrinsic evidence is contrary or when the patentee must meet an evidentiary burden at trial to map claim terms to accused features.

Two practical principles emerge from the interplay of extrinsic evidence and the intrinsic record. First, extrinsic evidence is most helpful when it supplements and illuminates the intrinsic record — for example, when a claim term is ambiguous and experts explain how the term is used in the art. Second, litigants should not rely on extrinsic evidence to overcome a clear specification statement or to manufacture a mapping at trial that the specification and prosecution history do not support. Courts will treat such efforts skeptically, and inconsistent or equivocal expert testimony can prove fatal to a patentee’s claim of infringement.

## Standard of review: de novo construction; clear-error review of subsidiary facts

On appeal, claim construction is reviewed under a hybrid standard. The Federal Circuit reviews claim construction de novo when the district court’s construction rests purely on legal analysis of intrinsic evidence. Where the district court makes subsidiary factual findings — for example, resolving disputes about extrinsic evidence or POSITA understandings — those factual findings are reviewed for clear error (Teva). The Finesse Wireless opinion exemplifies this division: the court applied de novo review to the construction issues but invoked clear-error principles when addressing any factual underpinnings the district court relied on (for instance, factual findings about what the prosecution statements meant or what signals were “separately identifiable” in the accused system).

Understanding how appellate review operates shapes trial strategy. A party challenging a district court’s construction must identify whether the district court made factual determinations and, if so, whether they were clearly erroneous. Conversely, the party advocating for a particular construction should create a record that ties factual predicates (POSITA testimony, dictionary definitions, inventor statements) coherently and consistently to the intrinsic evidence to survive appellate scrutiny.

The Finesse panel also applied the regional-circuit standard for review of denial of JMOL and new-trial motions (Fifth Circuit law in that case), but the key claim-construction review was governed by the Federal Circuit’s hybrid approach. When the Federal Circuit reverses on claim construction grounds, the determination often entails legal interpretation rather than substitution of appellate judgment for the district court’s factual role.

## Construing "three signals S1, S2, S3": separately identifiable vs. unique

A concrete and instructive dispute in Finesse concerned the correct meaning of the claim phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3.” The district court construed that phrase to require that the signals be “separately identifiable,” but not “unique” — meaning that, in the court’s view, the claim requires three identifiable signal inputs but does not prevent those three signals from being copies or otherwise not distinct in content. Appellants argued that the claim language and prosecution history required three unique signals, and that Finesse had surrendered a broader scope in prosecution when it amended the claims to require three signals in order to distinguish Filipovic, a prior-art reference that used only two signals.

The Federal Circuit set out the analytical steps that govern such disputes. First, the court looked to the claim language: the plain reading could support either interpretation (three labeled signals versus three distinct sources). Second, the court turned to the specification and prosecution history to see whether the patentee expressly or implicitly defined the term or disavowed alternatives. Third, the court considered extrinsic evidence (inventor testimony) that the district court had credited in construing the term.

On the prosecution-history question, the key inquiry is whether the patentee’s amendment or argument during prosecution amounts to a “clear and unmistakable” surrender of claim scope. The Federal Circuit reiterated that amendment to add “three signals” does not automatically represent a categorical abandonment of any system that operates with two inputs. The court emphasized that the patentee had successfully distinguished Filipovic on substantive grounds — that Filipovic addressed active IMPs rather than the passive IMPs claimed — rather than expressly stating “we surrender systems that use two signals.” Because the intrinsic record did not contain an explicit, unequivocal disclaimer that would bar two-signal systems, the court concluded the patent owner had not disclaimed such systems as a matter of law.

That analysis teaches several doctrinal points. “Separately identifiable” is a narrower interpretive nuance than “unique.” A signal can be separately identifiable — for example, a signal and its copy or a signal and its complex conjugate — without being intrinsically unique in content or source. Whether the claim requires “uniqueness” depends on the language and the intrinsic evidence. When the patentee amended to recite three signals, that change narrowed the claims in the sense of adding a new literal requirement; but such an amendment is not ipso facto a prosecution disclaimer of two-signal embodiments unless accompanied by unambiguous prosecutorial statements that trade away that scope.

Practically, this distinction matters at trial. If the court construes “three signals” to mean three unique signals, the patentee must prove the accused system supplies three distinct inputs. If the court construes the term to mean “three separately identifiable” signals, then copies, replicas, or derivations of a signal may satisfy the element if the accused device produces or receives three identifiable signals. The Finesse court accepted the district court’s claim construction but then turned to whether the patentee had actually presented substantial evidence at trial that the accused radios performed all the required multiplications or used the requisite signal inputs. Even under the district court’s more permissive construction, the patentee’s trial proof failed to demonstrate the seven claimed multiplications. Thus, claim construction alone does not conclude the analysis; the patentee’s proof must map the accused system to the construed claims.

## Other contested terms not reached on appeal (e.g., "receiver," "co-located"): why appellate courts sometimes bypass them

Not every disputed term in a patent necessarily requires appellate resolution. Appellate courts often decline to reach other contested constructions once they have resolved the appeal on an independent, dispositive ground. In Finesse the Federal Circuit reversed for lack of substantial evidence supporting infringement of key elements and therefore did not reach disputes over terms such as “receiver” or “co-located.” This avoidance serves sensible judicial economy and doctrinal caution: courts will not announce a binding construction on peripheral issues if the appeal can be resolved without deciding them.

A related reason to defer or avoid construction on appeal is to prevent issuing advisory opinions. When the resolution of a case does not turn on a particular term, providing an authoritative construction could be unnecessary and potentially misleading in later cases. Appellate courts therefore generally construe only the terms necessary to resolve the dispute before them. Litigants should anticipate that courts may avoid construing collateral terms and should prepare to litigate them at the district level, where a full evidentiary record can inform subsidiary factual determinations.

Finally, parties sometimes strategically focus claim construction efforts on the terms most likely to be dispositive. If the court rules for one side on a key term, remaining terms may become moot. The lesson for practitioners is to prioritize clarity in claim terms likely to determine infringement or validity, and to preserve issues for appeal by ensuring constructions are developed in the record when necessary.

## Impact of constructions on evidentiary burdens at trial

Claim construction does more than set legal meaning — it shapes the patentee’s burden of proof at trial and the form of expert testimony and documentary mappings required to meet that burden. The patentee bears the burden of proving infringement by a preponderance of the evidence; that proof consists of showing, element by element, that the accused product or method meets every limitation of the properly construed claim.

When a claim element is construed narrowly, the patentee must tailor its trial evidence to the construal. For example, if a construction requires three unique signals, the patentee must present evidence — often expert testimony corroborated by documents or source code — that the accused system actually produces or uses three distinct signal sources. If the construction allows copies or derived signals, the patentee can proceed with evidence showing that three separately identifiable signals exist even if they derive from a common source.

The Finesse opinion demonstrates how the failure to present a clear mapping between claimed elements and accused-device features — and inconsistent or self-contradictory expert testimony — can defeat the patentee at the JMOL stage. The court was particularly critical of Dr. Wells’ testimony on the ’134 patent, which the court characterized as internally inconsistent: on some parts of the record he equated “interference generating signals” with the “modeled PIM path,” which, according to the accused-system documentation, is generated downstream from the ADC and therefore cannot be a sampled input as the claim requires; on other parts he suggested that the RF ADC received two inputs (x1 and x2) that corresponded to the claimed elements, but he never clearly mapped x1 and x2 onto “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” The Federal Circuit held that where a patentee rests its case on an expert’s testimony that is equivocal or self-contradictory, the evidence is insufficient as a matter of law to sustain a jury verdict. Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp., cited by the court, stands for a similar proposition: an expert’s assertion cannot, by spin, create literal infringement where the testimony contradicts itself or the intrinsic record.

Likewise, on the ’775 patent the court found that even if the “three signals” construction did not require uniqueness, the patentee failed to show the accused radios performed all seven claimed third-order multiplications. Relying on a Nokia document, the patentee’s expert identified only three distinct multiplications and did not explain how those three distinct computations corresponded to the seven claimed multiplications. Moreover, the Nokia materials used complex conjugates and modulus operations, which the expert did not reconcile with the claim language. Because the mapping between accused processes and the claim limitations was not established, the Federal Circuit reversed.

These outcomes illustrate three practical evidentiary lessons:

- Establish a clear element-by-element mapping at trial. Each claim limitation must be tied to specific features or operations in the accused system. Experts should be prepared to show in detail how accused components perform the claimed functions, referencing documents, code, schematics, or device outputs.

- Keep expert testimony consistent and rooted in the intrinsic record. Confusion or contradictions in expert testimony (e.g., identifying a downstream signal as an input to a sampling ADC) undermine the patentee’s case. Experts should avoid making assertions that cannot be reconciled with the accused-product documentation or the claim construction.

- Anticipate and address prosecution history. If a claim was amended, be ready to explain why the amendment does not constitute a disclaimer of certain embodiments — or, if the amendment does narrow the claim, be ready to prove the accused device fits within the narrowed scope. As the Finesse court emphasized, amendment alone is not always fatal, but prosecution arguments that unequivocally disclaimed certain subject matter will bind the patentee.

Conclusion

Claim construction is both a legal and a fact-sensitive task. The intrinsic record — claim language, specification, and prosecution history — remains the principal source of meaning, with extrinsic evidence serving a supplementary role. The standard of review on appeal reflects this division, with de novo review for legal determinations and clear-error review for subsidiary factual findings. The Finesse Wireless decision offers concrete lessons: prosecution amendments do not automatically translate into total surrender of related scope unless the record shows an unambiguous disclaimer; inventor and expert testimony can illuminate but not control claim meaning; and, crucially, a patentee must present clear, non-contradictory evidence at trial to map claim limitations to accused devices. For practitioners and students, the case underscores the centrality of precise claim drafting, a careful prosecution record, and disciplined, consistent expert testimony in meeting the element-by-element evidentiary burden at trial.

# Prosecution History and Disavowal

Prosecution history—the written record of interactions between the patent applicant and the United States Patent and Trademark Office (PTO)—is an essential component of claim construction and of the limits to which a patentee may later press its asserted claims. Courts treat statements and amendments that a patentee makes during prosecution as part of the intrinsic record, and those statements can narrow the ordinary meaning of claim language when they amount to a clear and unmistakable surrender of claim scope. But not every amendment or argument in the file wrapper effects such a narrowing. The Federal Circuit’s decision in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC (Fed. Cir. 2025) illustrates the limits of prosecution history disavowal and how courts analyze whether a patentee “clearly and unambiguously” relinquished claim scope.

This chapter explains the controlling doctrines—when prosecution history can and cannot be used to narrow claim scope—examines the interpretive consequences of amendments made to obtain allowance, applies the doctrines to the facts and prosecution history in Finesse, and offers practical drafting and prosecution guidance to avoid unintended narrowing.

## Doctrinal test: clear and unmistakable disavowal required to narrow claim scope

Two separate but related doctrines govern the impact of the prosecution record on claim scope. First, prosecution disclaimer (sometimes simply called “disavowal”) is a limit on claim construction: statements by the patentee during prosecution that are “clear and unmistakable” can narrow the ordinary meaning of claim terms. Second, prosecution history estoppel, developed principally in the context of the doctrine of equivalents (Festo), restricts the patentee’s ability to assert equivalents for claim elements when the patentee narrowed claim scope by amendment for reasons related to patentability.

The Federal Circuit has repeatedly emphasized the threshold for either doctrine: the patentee must have made a clear and unambiguous surrender. For claim construction, the patentee’s statements during prosecution are part of the intrinsic record and may be read to limit the canonically ordinary and customary meaning of claim terms only where prosecution statements show that the patentee “unequivocally disavowed” certain subject matter. This standard protects the notice function of claims—third parties should be able to rely on the claim language—but it also protects patentees against the application of an unduly narrow construction based on ambiguous or merely tactical remarks made to secure allowance.

When a patentee narrows claims through amendment, the amendment itself is strong evidence that the patentee gave up subject matter. Yet even a narrowing amendment does not always produce a narrow construction. Courts look to the intrinsic record—including the specification, the prior claim language, the examiner’s rejections, and the patentee’s arguments—and ask whether those materials demonstrate that the patentee clearly and unambiguously surrendered a particular interpretation that would otherwise be consistent with the claim language and specification.

The two doctrines operate at different stages and for different consequences. Prosecution disclaimer controls the literal meaning of the claims as construed. Prosecution history estoppel affects the availability of the doctrine of equivalents as an avenue of recovery. Both doctrines require a showing that the patentee’s words or amendment were made with an unmistakable intent to exclude a particular meaning or territory; ambiguous or equivocal statements do not suffice.

## Amendments for patentability and their interpretive consequences

Amendments made to overcome prior-art rejections are a frequent source of litigation over claim scope. When a claim is amended to add a limitation, the amendment is prima facie a narrowing: the claim as issued covers less than the claim as filed. That narrowing can have two different interpretive consequences.

First, for claim construction, courts will consult the prosecution history to determine whether the patentee’s arguments in support of the amendment should be read as a disclaimer. A patentee who says in explicit, unequivocal terms that a disputed term excludes a particular embodiment or arrangement has disclaimed that subject matter for purposes of claim construction. But if the amendment was made without explicit, unambiguous statements disavowing a particular scope—if the patentee’s statements were ambiguous, equivocal, or merely descriptive—then the amendment alone may not create a limitation beyond what the plain language and specification require.

Second, for the doctrine of equivalents, when an applicant narrowed a claim to obtain allowance, the patentee is presumptively estopped from recapturing that surrendered territory under equivalents. The Supreme Court’s Festo line holds that a narrowing amendment made for reasons related to patentability creates a presumption of surrender of the territory between the original and amended claim, although that presumption may be rebutted in certain circumstances (for example, if the rationale for the amendment was tangential to patentability or if the equivalent was unforeseeable at the time of amendment).

Putting these principles together, an amendment for patentability often signals a patentee’s willingness to narrow claim scope. But whether the amendment amounts to a “clear and unmistakable” disavowal for claim construction depends on how the patentee described the amendment and the relationship between the added language and the prior art. Courts will not impute a narrow meaning based purely on the fact of amendment; they examine the particular words and arguments in the file wrapper.

## Finesse’s amendment from “two or three signals” to “three signals”: what it did and did not concede

The Finesse patents at issue claimed methods that, among other things, required “three signals S1, S2 and S3” used in multiple third-order multiplications. During prosecution, the applicant had previously drafted claims (or proposed claims) that recited “two or three signals,” and the examiner cited prior art—Filipovic—that taught use of two inputs to generate cancellation signals. To overcome the rejection, the patentee amended the claims to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3” rather than “two or three signals,” and made accompanying arguments distinguishing Filipovic.

On appeal, the defendants argued that this amendment effected a clear and unambiguous surrender of coverage for an accused device that used only two input signals (x1 and x2). The district court, however, construed the claim term “three signals S1, S2 and S3” not as requiring three unique signals but as requiring three separately identifiable signals, which could, under the district court’s view, be copies or otherwise non-unique signals. The Federal Circuit examined the prosecution record and concluded that the patentee’s amendment to “three signals” did not rise to the level of a clear and unambiguous disclaimer of anything that could reasonably be described as two-input systems. In short, the patentee’s choice to amend to “three signals” to secure allowance did not, on the record, amount to an explicit, unequivocal disclaimer of two-signal implementations.

Finesse therefore stands for the useful proposition that an amendment from “two or three” to “three” during prosecution—without more—does not automatically disprove that the patentee intended to exclude configurations in which the “three signals” may be represented by two distinct physical or logical sources (for example, two unique signals plus a copy of one of them). The court explained that whether the amendment creates a disavowal depends on how the patentee characterized the distinction from the prior art; here, the patentee’s arguments emphasized a difference in the type of intermodulation product addressed (passive versus active IMPs), not a categorical surrender to devices that use fewer signal sources.

Finesse therefore illustrates a common outcome: an amendment to the claims made for patentability may be probative of a patentee’s intent to narrow, but the court will look to the entire prosecution record—examiner rejections, applicant arguments, and the specification—to determine whether there was a “clear and unmistakable” disavowal of a particular scope. Where the patentee’s remarks bear on a different distinguishing feature than the one urged by the accused infringer, the amendment may not produce the narrow construction sought by the accused.

## Distinguishing subject-matter scope from input-signal uniqueness

A recurring source of dispute in claim construction is the distinction between subject-matter scope—what the claim, properly construed, covers—and particular theories about whether claimed inputs must be distinct or “unique” as physical entities. The Finesse litigation shows how that distinction operates.

The ’775 patent recited “three signals S1, S2 and S3” used in specified multiplications. The district court read “three signals” to require three “separately identifiable” signals, but not three “unique” signals—meaning that, in the court’s view, the claim could be satisfied by two unique signals plus a duplicate (a separately identifiable copy) of one of them. That construction focuses on the functional identity of the inputs as logical signal streams that can be distinguished in the processing chain, rather than on each input mapping to a distinct physical transmitter or source.

Why does that matter? Because if claim scope is interpreted to require three unique sources, then an accused device implementing the functions with only two unique sources cannot literally infringe. But if the claim requires only that three input streams be separately identifiable for the subsequent multiplications (even if two streams are identical or derived from the same origin), then a device with two physical sources but three separately identifiable streams (e.g., x1, x2, and a copy of x1) might literally fall within the claim.

When the prosecution history is examined to evaluate which of these interpretations is correct, courts will look for explicit statements by the applicant that “three signals” means three distinct sources or, conversely, that the numerical requirement was introduced merely to distinguish types of IMPs or other substantive features. If the applicant’s arguments in the file wrapper emphasize the problem solved (e.g., passive vs active IMPs) and do not say that three must mean three unique signal sources, then a construction that permits non-unique but separately identifiable signals may be appropriate.

Finesse underscores that numerical recitations in claims do not automatically require uniqueness of physical sources unless the intrinsic record makes that requirement unmistakable. Numerical language can refer to separately identifiable functional streams rather than to distinct physical entities, and the prosecution record is the principal source to decide which reading the patentee intended.

## Continental Circuits v. Intel and related authority on disavowal

The Federal Circuit’s jurisprudence contains numerous examples of how prosecution statements and amendments have been treated. One instructive case is Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788 (Fed. Cir. 2019), invoked by the Finesse court. Continental Circuits reaffirmed that prosecution arguments can limit claim scope, but only when those arguments are clear and unmistakable. In Continental Circuits, the court examined the prosecution history to decide whether arguments made to overcome prior art had amounted to an unequivocal surrender of territory; the decision emphasized the need for explicitness.

The controlling principle from Continental Circuits is consistent with long-standing doctrinal rules: prosecution disclaimer exists to ensure patentees cannot assert a claim scope that contradicts clear representations made during prosecution, but courts will not construe ambiguous or equivocal statements as disavowals. Phillips v. AWH Corp. (the en banc Phillips decision) remains the leading precedent for incorporating prosecution history into claim construction and for directing courts to view the specification and file history together. For equivalents, Festo Corp. v. Shoketsu Kinzoku Kogyo Kabushiki Co. (the Festo line of cases) governs when amendments create a presumption of surrender of equivalents.

Taken together, this body of authority requires courts to be careful: treat the prosecution record as an integral part of claim construction, but require precision before holding that the patentee has relinquished subject matter. The patentee’s arguments must be deliberate and unambiguous to be binding.

## Strategic drafting and response practices to avoid unintended narrowing

The Finesse litigation yields practical lessons for patent drafters and prosecutors. Because a patentee’s words during prosecution can later be read as limiting, careful drafting and cautious prosecution strategy are essential.

First, in drafting original claims and the specification, anticipate the likely rejections and draft multiple alternative claim formulations and claim dependency structures that preserve fallback positions. When numerals are used (e.g., “three signals”), consider whether the specification should explicitly describe that the numeral refers to separately identifiable streams and whether those streams may be derived from fewer physical sources. If the inventor intends to cover copies or derived signals, describe examples where one physical source is copied or where a signal is duplicated in the processing chain. The specification can thus supply intrinsic support for a broader reading of numerals.

Second, when responding to examiner rejections, carefully consider whether to amend claim language or to rely on substantive argument. An amendment should be framed narrowly and precisely—if an amendment is necessary, avoid surrendering more than is required to overcome the rejection. Explain clearly why the amendment is needed, and, if appropriate, expressly state that the amendment is intended to recite the specific distinguishing feature being relied upon without disclaiming other interpretations. For example, if the distinction to the prior art is the type of IMP addressed (passive vs. active), emphasize that technical distinction and avoid characterizing the amendment as excluding particular device architectures unless you intend that result.

Third, when making arguments to secure allowance, avoid categorical and sweeping statements that could be read as a disavowal. Do not use phrases like “the claim does not cover X” unless you are prepared for a court to treat that as a disavowal. If you must identify features that distinguish the invention from the cited art, tie the discussion to the specific feature—as in Finesse, focusing on “passive IMPs” rather than on numeric aspects of inputs—so the record shows a targeted reason for allowance rather than a broad abandonment of claim territory.

Fourth, maintain careful records of the reasons for amendments and why certain limitations were added. If the amendment is made for reasons only tangentially related to patentability (for example, to clarify ambiguous language rather than to avoid a prior art reference), document that rationale. Such documentation can be important later if a court must decide whether a narrowing amendment created a prosecution history estoppel with respect to equivalents.

Finally, litigators should remember that even if a prosecution amendment does not produce a conclusive disavowal for claim construction, it may still have consequences under the doctrine of equivalents. Amendments made for patentability reasons create a presumption that the patentee intended to relinquish the related territory; the patentee must be prepared to rebut that presumption if it wants to assert equivalents covering the surrendered territory.

Conclusion

The prosecution record is a double-edged sword. It can secure allowance and provide convincing distinctions over the prior art; the same words, however, can later be used by an accused infringer to show the patentee clearly and unambiguously surrendered claim scope. Finesse Wireless v. AT&T illustrates that a narrowing amendment—by itself—does not inevitably produce a clear and unmistakable prosecution disclaimer; courts will look to the content and context of the patentee’s statements and amendments. For practitioners, the lesson is practical: draft and prosecute with care, describe alternative embodiments and the intended scope of numerical or functional claim limitations in the specification, and avoid broad, categorical disclaimers in the file wrapper unless you intend to limit your claim territory accordingly.

# Case Study Setup: Finesse Wireless v. AT&T & Nokia

This chapter situates the Federal Circuit’s decision in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC and Nokia of America Corp. within the litigation timeline and highlights the patents, the accused functionality, the trial outcome, and the post-trial and appellate issues. The opinion provides a useful vehicle to study how trial proof—particularly expert testimony tied to vendor documents—interacts with Rule 50 motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), the appellate standards of review for JMOL denials, and related claim‑scope questions that were argued but not reached on appeal.

## The patents-in-suit: ’134 (sampling and cancellation pipeline) and ’775 (seven multiplications of three signals)

The litigation involved two patents that address passive intermodulation (PIM) interference in radio systems. The ’134 patent claims a signal-processing pipeline that begins by “over-sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream” that includes both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” isolates those two categories with decimating filters, computes an estimate of the intermodulation products produced by the interference-generating signals, and cancels those estimated intermodulation products, including closed-loop phase and amplitude adjustments using sub-sample phase shifts. The technical problem is familiar: transmit signals can mix (when encountering passive defects or obstacles) to generate new frequencies—PIM—that may fall inband of desired receive signals; the claimed method samples a passband that contains both desired and interference‑generating energy, models the interfering products, and cancels them in the digital domain.

Claim 1 of the ’134 patent thus places at its front end a fact‑intensive limitation: the receiver must sample a passband that contains both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” At trial Finesse’s infringement theory depended on identifying particular signals in Nokia technical documents and tying those signals to the claim terms. That mapping—and whether the accused receiver actually samples the claimed interference‑generating signals—proved pivotal on appeal.

The ’775 patent addresses cancellation of passive IMPs in a co‑located transmitter/receiver arrangement using a power‑series description of a nonlinear process. The representative claim requires “generating intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) . . . using copies of transmitter signals” and—critically—generating an n‑th order ICS by digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2, and S3 to produce seven distinct third‑order products (S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, etc.). During prosecution the claims were amended to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3,” and the district court construed those three signals to be “separately identifiable” but not necessarily “unique.” The appellate dispute turned on whether the accused radios in fact use three separately identifiable signals and whether the system performs the seven claimed multiplications.

Both patents thus turned on technical mappings: (1) whether the receiver samples the claimed mix of signals in the ’134 pipeline, and (2) whether the accused implementation performs the seven third‑order multiplications from three inputs required by the ’775 claim language. These are typical issues in software/hardware patent cases: translating vendor documentation and expert testimony into concrete proof that accused products meet each claim limitation.

## Accused functionality: Nokia’s PIM‑C feature used in AT&T’s network

The accused functionality was a PIM cancellation (PIM‑C) feature implemented in certain Nokia radios deployed in AT&T’s network. The parties introduced internal Nokia technical documents showing signal flows and mathematical operations performed by the accused implementation. Key components of that documentation included a receiver analog‑to‑digital converter (RF ADC), downstream FPGA processing blocks (including what Nokia labeled a “modeled PIM path” and processing in a GROOT FPGA), and blocks performing modulus and complex‑conjugate operations and multiplications related to modeled cancellation signals.

Finesse relied on a Nokia diagram that placed a “DL (TX) reference” and a “modeled PIM path” on a diagrammed red path leading toward the RF ADC. Finesse’s expert, Dr. Wells, initially testified that the RF ADC samples both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Appellants pointed out—and repeatedly questioned at trial—that the Nokia documentation indicated the “modeled PIM path” is a digitally generated signal created downstream in FPGA logic after the RF ADC, so it could not be sampled by the RF ADC. That temporal/sequencing fact—whether a putative interference‑generating signal exists at the RF ADC input at sampling time—was decisive for the ’134 claim: the claim requires that the RF ADC sample a passband that contains the interference generating signals.

For the ’775 patent, the Nokia documents showed certain multiplications, some involving complex conjugates or moduli (e.g., x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|), and other documentation listed a sequence of coefficients or polynomial terms. Finesse’s infringement theory equated the Nokia listed multiplications or polynomial coefficients with the seven third‑order products claimed by the ’775 patent. The defense emphasized that the Nokia documents disclosed only three distinct multiplications in the relevant portion, and that there was no evidence the asserted system actually executed the seven distinct multiplications required by the claim language.

In short, the accused functionality was not in dispute as deployed in AT&T’s network; the contested matter was how the internal signals in Nokia’s implementation corresponded, in time and kind, to the limitations recited in the patents.

## Jury verdict: liability and lump-sum damages award

After a bench and jury trial in the Eastern District of Texas (Judge J. Rodney Gilstrap presiding), the jury found all asserted claims of both patents valid and infringed. The asserted claims included claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and a set of claims from the ’775 patent (claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36). The jury then awarded a lump‑sum, reasonable‑royalty damages award of $166,303,391 for the remaining life of the patents.

The damages phase was a single, lumped royalty calculation for the asserted patents rather than a running royalty or disgorgement. Finesse’s damages theory therefore depended on proving a reasonable royalty base and royalty rate and connecting the value of the patents to the Nokia PIM‑C feature’s contribution to the accused products’ value. The district court entered judgment on the jury verdict, and the defendants filed post‑trial motions seeking JMOL of noninfringement, JMOL on damages, and a new trial on damages.

The jury’s liability findings and substantial damages award are the touchstone for appellate review: when a patent plaintiff wins a jury verdict based largely on expert testimony and vendor documentation, appellate courts scrutinize whether the evidentiary foundation is legally sufficient to permit reasonable jurors to conclude infringement and to sustain the damages award.

## Post-trial motions: JMOLs and motion for new trial

Following the verdict, defendants filed Rule 50(b) motions for judgment as a matter of law and Rule 59 motions for a new trial. The defendants asked the district court to enter JMOL of noninfringement for both patents, arguing that Finesse had failed to meet its burden of proof on critical limitations: for the ’134 patent, that the receiver samples a passband including the interference‑generating signals; and for the ’775 patent, that the accused radios perform the seven required third‑order multiplications of three signals. Defendants also sought JMOL and/or a new trial on damages, challenging the sufficiency and admissibility of evidence supporting the jury’s lump‑sum award.

The district court denied the motions. On the ’134 patent the court concluded the jury had a factual basis to find that the RF ADC sampled the relevant signals (crediting inferences drawn from the expert testimony and Nokia’s documentation). For the ’775 patent the court accepted the district construction that S1, S2, and S3 need be “separately identifiable,” not necessarily unique, and concluded the record permitted a jury to find infringement by mapping Nokia’s listed operations to the claimed multiplications. The court also denied relief on damages.

These interlocutory denials preserved the defendants’ right to appeal because a Rule 50(b) JMOL motion is a condition precedent to raising JMOL grounds on appeal; the defendants renewed their JMOL arguments in this procedural posture and then pursued appeal to the Federal Circuit after final judgment. The defense also preserved challenges to the damages award via the district court motions and the appellate record.

## Appellate issues: noninfringement JMOL, damages/new trial, and constructions not reached

On appeal the parties preserved multiple issues, and the Federal Circuit narrowed its decision to those properly before it and supported by the appellate record. The main issues the court resolved were: (1) whether the district court erred in denying JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’134 patent; and (2) whether the district court erred in denying JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent. Because the Federal Circuit concluded infringement was not supported by substantial evidence for both patents, it reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacated the damages award.

Doctrinally, the opinion is instructive on several points that students should note. First, the standard of review for a denial of JMOL is governed by the regional circuit’s law; here the Federal Circuit applied Fifth Circuit standards, reviewing the denial de novo but reversing only where, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence “points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” The Federal Circuit also reviews factual findings for substantial evidence and claim constructions de novo (with underlying factual findings supporting constructions for clear error). These standards shaped how the court treated the trial record.

Second, the court emphasized the plaintiff’s burden to prove each claim limitation and focused on the sufficiency of the evidentiary support—particularly expert testimony tied to corporate documents. On the ’134 claim the court found the plaintiff’s expert testimony internally inconsistent and inconclusive as to whether the RF ADC actually samples the modeled PIM path. The expert repeatedly identified the “modeled PIM path” as the interference‑generating signals and simultaneously relied on a diagram legend that grouped the DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path together; but the vendor documents and the expert placed the modeled PIM path as generated downstream of the RF ADC. The court concluded that when a patentee’s entire case rests on such self‑contradictory testimony, it may not constitute substantial evidence supporting the jury’s verdict; it cited precedent holding that internally inconsistent expert testimony may be insufficient as a matter of law to satisfy the plaintiff’s burden.

On the ’775 patent the court handled two discrete legal contentions. Defendants argued Finesse had surrendered claim scope over two‑signal implementations during prosecution and therefore could not now assert claims against an implementation that used only two signals. The Federal Circuit applied the familiar rule that prosecution disclaimer or surrender must be “clear and unmistakable” to narrow claim scope; it concluded that the prosecution history and amendment to recite “three signals” did not rise to such a clear and unambiguous disavowal and so did not bar Finesse’s theory that three “separately identifiable” signals might include copies of signals or signals that are not unique. However, even accepting the district court’s construction that the signals need only be separately identifiable, the court found no substantial evidence that the accused radios actually performed the seven claimed multiplications. The Nokia documents cited at trial listed only three distinct multiplications and involved modulus and complex‑conjugate operations; there was no evidence the particular seven third‑order terms claimed were performed or repeated in the manner required by the claims, and Finesse’s appellate clarifications were impermissible post‑hoc reconstructions of the trial record. As a result, the Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL for the ’775 claims as well.

The Federal Circuit declined to reach other disputed constructions that had been briefed (for example, constructions of “receiver” or “co‑located”) because its reversal on noninfringement obviated the need to resolve those issues. That practice—deciding only what is necessary to resolve the appeal—is a reminder that appellate courts often dispose of cases on narrower record-supported grounds rather than reach all disputed claim‑construction or contention issues when they are not outcome‑determinative.

Finally, because the court reversed JMOL for noninfringement, it vacated the damages award as moot; without liability, there can be no damages. The court therefore did not resolve the separate damages/new‑trial arguments substantively.

## Jurisdictional path to the Federal Circuit

The procedural posture is straightforward and exemplifies the route by which patent appeals reach the Federal Circuit. The case was tried in the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas (Judge Gilstrap). After final judgment—entry of a judgment incorporating the jury’s verdict—the defendants timely appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction over appeals from final decisions in patent cases is statutory, under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

Because JMOL was properly preserved by filing Rule 50 motions at the district court stage (Rule 50(a) at the close of evidence and Rule 50(b) after verdict), the defendants were able to press JMOL arguments on appeal. The Federal Circuit applied the regional (Fifth Circuit) standard for reviewing denials of JMOL but applied its own precedent for patent‑specific questions (substantial evidence for infringement findings, de novo review for claim construction with clear‑error review of underlying factual determinations). The opinion illustrates the interplay between regional procedural standards and Federal Circuit patent doctrine.

Conclusion

Finesse Wireless v. AT&T & Nokia is a paradigmatic example of how an infringement case that relies heavily on expert testimony tied to vendor documentation can founder if the mapping between documents, expert opinion, and claim language is internally inconsistent or insufficiently developed for a jury. The Federal Circuit’s reversal demonstrates the appellate court’s willingness to grant JMOL where, even accepting the jury verdict, the evidentiary record does not contain substantial evidence to support infringement of particular claim limitations. For students, the case is an instructive study of (1) how claim elements must be proved with coherent, non‑contradictory evidence; (2) how prosecution history affects claim scope (and the high bar for disavowal); and (3) how appellate standards of review apply when district courts deny JMOL and damage challenges.

# Teaching the ’134 Patent: Sampling the Passband and Identifying the Claimed Signals

This chapter uses the Federal Circuit’s decision in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC to teach principles that recur in patent trials where the patentee’s case depends on expert testimony that maps claim limitations to components or signals in an accused product. The focal patent limitation—claim 1(a) of U.S. Patent No. 7,346,134—requires “over-sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals.” That language looks simple, but proving it at trial required (and in this case failed to achieve) a careful, coherent linkage between the claim language, the accused radios’ signal path, and the timing of when particular signals are generated and sampled. The Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement because the patentee’s expert testimony was internally inconsistent and failed to supply substantial evidence for the jury verdict. The lessons are (1) how to map claim terms to an accused device, (2) the importance of temporal feasibility (can the accused device possibly perform the claimed act?), and (3) the limits of equivocal or self-contradictory expert testimony.

## Claim 1(a): oversampling a passband that includes both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals"

Claim 1(a) requires sampling a passband that contains two categories of signals: signals of interest and interference-generating signals. This is a classic example of a claim that is partly descriptive of an operation (sampling at a desired frequency) and partly definitional of the content whose presence is required in the sampled passband (both categories must be present). In litigation, the patentee bears the burden of proving that the accused device performs the act described (here, over-sampling) and that the act is performed on a passband that contains the required kinds of signals.

Two doctrinal points arise immediately. First, because the claim requires the presence of certain signals in the passband, evidence must show that those signals exist in the radio front end at the time — and in the place — where sampling occurs. It's not enough to show that some signal with some relationship to the claimed categories exists somewhere in the system at some time. Second, a patentee commonly meets this burden by having an expert present a mapping: the expert identifies particular physical paths, stages, or labeled signals in vendor documents and testifies that those are the claimed signals. The sufficiency of this mapping turns on its clarity, internal consistency, and foundation in record evidence.

The Federal Circuit treated claim 1(a) as a factual showing that the accused RF ADC (the analog-to-digital converter in the radio) samples a passband that includes both categories. Because Finesse bore the burden of proof, their expert’s testimony had to reliably establish that mapping. The court’s analysis highlights that when a claim requires the simultaneous or co-located presence of two things (here two kinds of signals in a sampled passband), proving their co-presence requires evidence that they actually exist at the sampling point at the relevant time.

## Core factual dispute: whether the RF ADC samples the interference-generating signals

Central to the appeal was a straightforward factual question: does the RF ADC sample the interference-generating signals? The accused Nokia radios implement a PIM cancellation feature. Finesse’s expert, Dr. Wells, relied on Nokia technical documentation that diagrammed a “red path” and a legend that stated “Red – DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path.” Dr. Wells repeatedly identified the RF ADC as the component that samples the signals on that red path, and he repeatedly identified the DL (TX) reference as the “signal of interest” and the modeled PIM path as the “interference generating signals.”

On cross-examination the defense established a temporal mismatch: the modeled PIM path is generated in digital logic (a downstream FPGA) after the RF ADC has already sampled the analog passband. Because the modeled PIM path is a computed/modelled signal created after sampling, it cannot have been present in the analog passband that the RF ADC sampled. If the “interference generating signals” that the claim requires are the modeled PIM path, then the accused radios do not meet claim 1(a) because the ADC did not sample those signals.

This is the crux of the case: whether the jury could reasonably infer that the modeled PIM path (a post-sampling modeled signal) was the required interference-generating signal, or whether Dr. Wells meant something else—specifically, that the ADC had sampled two analog signals (which he later called x1 and x2) that constitute the “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” The defendant argued that, as a matter of logic, a signal generated downstream of sampling cannot be part of the sampled passband; therefore, the patentee’s mapping asserting that the modeled PIM path was the interference source was temporally impossible and could not support infringement. The Federal Circuit agreed that the record supported that conclusion.

The take-away for students: when a claim requires that some content be present in sampled data, you must anchor that content to the physical signals or waveforms that exist before or at the sampling instant. Any mapping that rests on signals that arise only after sampling is fatal unless the patentee can show those downstream signals are faithful, pre-existing representations of incoming analog content (and such a showing itself requires evidence).

## Expert mapping reliance on a vendor legend: DL(TX) reference vs. modeled PIM path

The patentee in Finesse relied heavily on a vendor diagram and its legend. Diagrams and vendor documentation are common trial exhibits and can be powerful persuasive tools, but they must be interpreted carefully. The diagram in this case labeled a red path and explained in the legend that the red color corresponded to “DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path.” Dr. Wells testified, repeatedly, that the red path contained both the signal of interest (DL (TX) reference) and the modeled PIM path (the interference-generating signal). Read plainly, that testimony tied the interference-generating signal to the modeled PIM path.

Two lessons follow. First, expert testimony that simply repeats or parrots a label in a vendor document is not enough if the label is ambiguous and the expert does not explain how the diagram maps onto the real-time signal flow. Experts need to explain not only what the diagram says but how the labeled entities correspond to signals that exist at the claimed sampling point and at the claimed time. Second, trial counsel must anticipate ways an adversary can show that a diagram’s labels do not reflect reality as to timing or location; the defendant cross-examined effectively by showing that the modeled PIM path was a downstream computed signal and therefore could not have been sampled by the upstream ADC.

In short, vendor legends are evidence, not magic; the expert’s role is to build a reasoned, non-contradictory bridge from the vendor’s artifacts to the claim elements. If the bridge is missing steps or contains obvious contradictions, it is unlikely to sustain a jury verdict on appeal.

## Temporal impossibility: modeled PIM path generated downstream of the RF ADC

Temporal feasibility is decisive when a claim refers to actions that necessarily occur in a particular sequence. The county of events matters: sampling occurs at a point in time; signals generated later cannot retroactively be part of a passband that was sampled earlier.

In Finesse, the defense introduced testimony and documentation showing that the modeled PIM path is produced inside the GROOT FPGA after the ADC has sampled the analog passband. That fact creates a logical impossibility for the proposition that the ADC sampled the modeled PIM path. The Federal Circuit treated this as dispositive absent a reliable alternative mapping from the patentee’s expert tying the “interference generating signals” to analog signals that were actually present before sampling.

This analysis parallels many other cases where a claim requires a device to act upon or process some native input and the defendant shows the accused device only produces that input via internal computation. A post-processed or modeled signal is not the same as the raw, pre-sampling analog input. Thus, when a claim requires processing of an input that must be physically present at sampling, a patentee must show the input existed at the sampling point; demonstrating only that the device produces a model or representation afterwards is insufficient.

From a litigation strategy perspective, the implication is clear: defendants should interrogate the timing of signal generation and sampling and seek to expose any post-sampling generation of purportedly “sampled” signals. Patentees should prepare expert testimony that can show either (a) the modeled signal is merely a faithful representation of an analog input that was sampled (and show how), or (b) if the modeled signal is created after sampling, then identify the true analog interference-generating signal that was sampled, with coherent mapping.

## The attempted pivot to x1/x2: ambiguity and contradictions in expert testimony

Confronted with the temporally impossible identification of the modeled PIM path as the interference-generating signal, Finesse’s expert attempted to pivot. At various times Dr. Wells described the red path as “the DL (TX) reference and the modeled PIM path,” and at other times referred to two signals called x1 and x2 entering the ADC. The patentee later argued that Dr. Wells’s references to the legend were mere shorthand and that he really meant x1 and x2, with x1 being the signal of interest and x2 being the interference-generating signal—both of which the ADC sampled.

The Federal Circuit treated these shifts as vague and contradictory. The opinion emphasizes several related points that are instructive for students:

- Consistency matters. An expert may offer alternative theories, but if the expert presents contradictory statements without explanation, a court may discount the testimony. Here Dr. Wells repeatedly said the modeled PIM path was the interference-generating signal, then later suggested x1 and x2 were the two inputs—but never clearly and directly mapped x1/x2 to the claimed terms in a way that resolved the timing problem.

- Piecing together testimony from disconnected parts of a long transcript to create a mapping is risky. The patentee asked the appellate court to infer that scattered utterances, made many pages apart, showed the expert intended x1 and x2 to be the claimed signals. The Federal Circuit rejected that exercise as insufficient because the expert never clearly testified to that effect in a single, coherent, grounded explanation.

- The expert must bridge gaps in the record. If the vendor legend is ambiguous or suggests a downstream signal, the expert must either (a) provide a contemporaneous explanation tying particular analog waveforms pre-sampling to the claimed “interference generating signals,” or (b) concede non-infringement. An attempt to create such a bridge by a “mental leap” or after-the-fact appellate argument is not a substitute for coherent trial testimony.

The practical lesson: trial experts must give a clear, contemporaneous mapping between an accused product diagram and the claim terms, especially when timing undermines the patentee’s theory. Vague pivots are unlikely to survive appellate scrutiny.

## Johns Hopkins v. Datascope: why self-contradictory expert opinions cannot support a verdict

The Federal Circuit relied on Johns Hopkins University v. Datascope Corp. to articulate a general rule: when the patentee’s case rests solely on an expert’s testimony that is internally contradictory or fundamentally at odds with documentary evidence, the testimony cannot create substantial evidence support for a verdict.

In Johns Hopkins, the patentee’s expert made incompatible factual assertions about how the accused device made contact with a vessel; the court concluded that no reasonable jury could find infringement on that testimony. The same reasoning applies here: Dr. Wells’s testimony contained incompatible statements about whether the modeled PIM path was the interference-generating signal (and thus post-sampling), or whether the ADC sampled two analog inputs x1 and x2. Because the defense established that the modeled PIM path could not have been sampled, the jury could only reach its verdict by accepting an interpretation of the expert’s testimony that the expert never clearly gave and that contradicted other parts of his testimony. The Federal Circuit refused to stretch testimony into a coherent mapping where the expert had not provided one.

The broader doctrinal point for students is this: courts will not sustain a verdict where the only purported evidentiary support is an expert’s ambiguous, self-contradictory testimony that, if interpreted one way, is irreconcilable with other record facts. An expert’s opinion must be internally consistent, adequately explained, and supported by the underlying record; otherwise it is not substantial evidence.

## Substantial-evidence application: when the record compels JMOL for noninfringement

Two legal standards converge on appeal. For a motion for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) moved after trial, the regional circuit standard requires viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict and reversing only if the evidence “points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” On appeal to the Federal Circuit, infringement findings are reviewed for substantial evidence: whether a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the patentee in light of the evidence presented.

Applying those standards in Finesse, the Federal Circuit found the evidence did not meet the threshold of substantial evidence. The record showed that (i) the modeled PIM path was generated downstream of the ADC (so it could not have been sampled), (ii) the expert repeatedly identified the modeled PIM path as the interference-generating signal, and (iii) the expert’s later, ambiguous references to x1 and x2 did not constitute clear testimony mapping those signals to the claimed elements. The appellate court concluded that no reasonable jury could find infringement based on the testimony as presented.

For law students, the approach to applying substantial evidence review is methodical:

- Identify the claim limitation that is dispositive. Here, claim 1(a)’s requirement that the sampled passband include the interference-generating signals was dispositive.

- Pinpoint the evidentiary route the patentee used for that limitation—usually expert testimony mapping claim terms to device features or to items in vendor documents.

- Look for contradictions between the expert’s mapping and the physical operation of the device (e.g., temporal impossibility that a downstream-generated modeled signal could have been sampled).

- If the expert’s testimony contains unexplained contradictions or depends on layered inferences (piece together testimony across widely separated record locations), determine whether any reasonable jury could have resolved those contradictions in the patentee’s favor. If the contradictions are central and unresolved, JMOL is appropriate.

Finally, appellate reversals on JMOL grounds are not routine; the record must be such that reasonable jurors could not have reached a contrary conclusion. Here the court concluded that the temporal impossibility combined with the expert’s inconsistent testimony produced exactly that situation.

## Practical implications for trial practice and expert preparation

This case offers practical admonitions for both patentee and defendant counsel and for experts:

- For patentee counsel and experts: anticipate timing issues. When a claim requirement depends on the presence of particular signals at a point in the device, your expert must be able to point to where those signals are located in the physical signal chain before sampling. If the best evidence is a vendor diagram that uses labels that could indicate post-processing, the expert must explain why the labeled items on the diagram correspond to pre-sampling analog signals. Prepare demonstratives and deposition testimony that create a fixed, internally consistent mapping; avoid shifting positions at trial.

- For defendants: focus cross-examination on temporal relations and the physical feasibility of the patentee’s mapping. If you can show a modeled signal was computed after sampling, you create a hard-to-overcome logical gap in an infringement theory that depends on the modeled signal being sampled.

- For the district court: insist on clarity. When an expert’s testimony is internally inconsistent, rule evidence accordingly and give a careful JMOL assessment if the jury’s finding depends on resolving the contradiction in the patentee’s favor without record support.

- For appellate counsel: preserve the record. If you intend to rely on a particular mapping (e.g., x1/x2), elicit an unambiguous, clear mapping in testimony and in expert reports; appellate courts are less sympathetic to theories pieced together from scattered transcript fragments.

## Conclusion

Finesse teaches that claim proving is factual, and experts who serve as the patentee’s bridge between the claim language and the accused product must build that bridge with clear, consistent planking. When a claim requires that certain signals be present at the time of sampling, evidence must show those signals are indeed present at the sampling point—signals generated downstream do not qualify. Vendor documentation can be persuasive, but an expert cannot safely rely on a legend’s shorthand if a careful analysis reveals a temporal impossibility. Finally, courts will not sustain a verdict supported only by self-contradictory expert testimony that, if read consistently with other record evidence, clearly undermines the asserted theory of infringement.

# Teaching the ’775 Patent: "Three Signals" and the "Seven Multiplications" Requirement

The Federal Circuit’s decision in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC offers a compact lesson in how courts treat mathematical and signal-processing claim limitations, how prosecution history and claim construction constrain proof of infringement, and what proof is necessary at trial to show that an accused system actually performs every asserted mathematical operation. The opinion is most pedagogically useful on three fronts: (1) what the court means when it requires a patentee to identify which physical or logical signals correspond to claimed variables (here S1, S2, S3); (2) how amendments and statements during prosecution can — but need not — narrow claim scope; and (3) what evidence is required to prove that a device performs a multi-part arithmetic or algebraic limitation (here, seven specific third-order multiplications). The following sections develop those lessons and the doctrinal principles that govern them.

## Construction of S1, S2, S3: separately identifiable signals need not be unique

One of the central claim-construction issues in the ’775 patent concerned the phrase “three signals S1, S2 and S3.” The district court adopted a construction requiring that S1, S2 and S3 be “separately identifiable,” but it declined to read “three” to mean “three different” or “three unique” signals. The Federal Circuit accepted that construction on appeal. The legal point is straightforward: claim language that recites numerals (for example “three”) does not always impose a requirement that the three items be distinct in kind or origin; it may require only that the claimed elements be separately identifiable within the accused system or within the relevant processing context.

There are two important practical corollaries. First, the construction that permits duplicated signals (for example, “two unique signals plus a copy of one”) changes the universe of accused devices that can infringe: devices that operate with fewer unique sources may still satisfy a claim if the claim requires separate, identifiable signal inputs rather than strictly distinct inputs. Second, even when the court allows non-unique inputs, patentee still bears the burden of proving the mapping from the accused device to the claim terms. That means a patentee must tell the factfinder which physical or logical quantity in the accused device is the thing the claim calls “S1,” which is “S2,” and which is “S3” — and must do so consistently and coherently.

Finesse’s trial evidence illustrates the point. Because the court construed “three signals” as “separately identifiable” rather than “unique,” the reported decision acknowledges that the asserted claims might be met by using two distinct signals where one of those signals is duplicated or copied. But that permissive construction did not relieve Finesse of showing the accused radios actually produced the seven specific third-order products formed from S1, S2 and S3. The lesson for practitioners and students is that a permissive construction about uniqueness does not eliminate the need for a precise mapping between claim variables and accused-system signals.

## Prosecution history did not clearly disavow two unique inputs plus a copy

When a patentee amends claims during prosecution, the amendment can operate as a disclaimer or disavowal of claim scope. But courts apply a demanding standard: prosecution disclaimer that narrows the claim’s scope must be “clear and unmistakable.” The Federal Circuit reiterated this principle in the Finesse decision. Although the patentee amended the claims to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3” during examination, the court concluded that amendment did not amount to a clear and unmistakable surrender of claims covering devices that use two unique inputs plus a copy. The amendment was made in the context of distinguishing a prior-art reference (Filipovic) that addressed active rather than passive IMPs; the examiner’s rejection and the patentee’s remarks did not clearly state that any device with only two distinct inputs (even if one is copied) was excluded from claim scope.

The doctrinal takeaway is that narrowing amendments do not automatically produce prosecution disclaimer. The patentee’s words and the context in which they were spoken matter. When a prosecutor distinguishes prior art on a substantive ground that is unrelated to the precise numerical composition of inputs, that factual context weakens the argument that the patentee clearly disavowed coverage of two-input systems. Practical advice follows: when a patentee intends to surrender particular claim scope during prosecution, the safest course is to make an explicit and unmistakable statement to that effect. Conversely, if a patentee seeks to narrow but preserve coverage over certain embodiments (for example, two unique signals plus a copy), the record should contain clear explanations of why such embodiments remain covered despite the amendment.

## Limitation 1(d): proving seven third-order multiplications are actually performed

At the heart of the ’775 patent’s asserted claim is limitation 1(d), which requires that generating an n-th order cancellation signal include forming a set of third-order products — specifically, the seven distinct third-order multiplicative combinations that can arise from three signals S1, S2 and S3 (S1×S1×S2; S1×S2×S2; S1×S2×S3; S1×S1×S3; S2×S2×S3; S1×S3×S3; S2×S3×S3). Proving this kind of limitation is a paradigmatic example of an “every-element” proof requirement: a patentee must prove that the accused device performs each claimed element, and for mathematical combinations, the patentee must show that each specified algebraic product is produced by the accused system.

The Federal Circuit’s reversal underscores two evidentiary principles. First, it is not enough to point to components or processing blocks whose operations are superficially similar; the patentee must show by understandable, non-speculative evidence that the specific algebraic products exist in the accused device. Second, the patentee’s expert must map the accused operations to the claim’s required algebraic terms in a manner that a reasonable jury could follow and accept. Where the alleged infringement rests on documents that list multiplicative expressions, and those expressions do not correspond one-to-one to the claim’s required products, the patentee must supply additional evidence — test data, design documents, code, or clear expert demonstration — showing how the listed operations produce the claimed products.

In Finesse, the Nokia document the expert relied upon listed operations that the court characterized as three “distinct multiplications” (x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|) and elsewhere a larger vector of coefficient-based terms. The court concluded that the document, and Dr. Wells’ testimony tying it to the claimed seven multiplications, were insufficient as a matter of law to show that the accused radios actually perform all seven specific products. The decision thus reinforces that, for algebraic limitations, the patentee must either point to definitive evidence in the accused device that the algebraic products are generated, or else provide expert testimony grounded in reliable evidence that explains, step by step, how the accused system constructs each claimed product.

## Distinguishing multiplications from operations on modulus and complex conjugates

A deceptively technical, but legally important, aspect of the Finesse dispute is the difference between forming algebraic products of signals and forming operations that involve moduli and complex conjugation. The Nokia documents referenced by Finesse used constructs such as |x1| (modulus) and x2' (complex conjugate). To translate those constructs into the claim’s required S1×S1×S2-type products, Finesse needed to show that the presence of |x1| or x2' in an expression equates to the particular algebraic combination the claim requires.

Signal- and complex-number algebra teaches that modulus and conjugation are not mere renamings of a base signal. The modulus |x| of a complex signal x is the nonnegative real number sqrt(x * x'), and the complex conjugate x' reverses the sign of the imaginary component. Thus, expressions that involve |x| and x' are algebraically different from simple multiplicative products of the underlying complex signal x. Whether those algebraic differences matter for infringement depends on whether the claim requires the precise algebraic form or whether a court adopts a broader interpretation. In Finesse, the claim required particular multiplicative combinations; consequently, a document that lists x1×|x1|×|x1| is not the same as a document listing x1×x1×x1, and mapping requires explanation.

The practical consequence is: when an accused system uses modulus or conjugation in its internal computations, a patentee cannot assume that those operations satisfy a claim that recites raw multiplications of base signals without proof. The patentee must establish either algebraic equivalence in the context at hand or demonstrate how the accused device’s use of modulus and conjugation produces the very algebraic combinations required by the claim. Absent that demonstration, the court will treat the two forms as different and will require proof that the accused form actually yields the claimed products.

## Why identifying only three distinct multiplications is insufficient

The Nokia document and Dr. Wells’ testimony, at best, identified three distinct multiplicative terms in the accused processing pipeline. The Federal Circuit made clear that pointing to three distinct multiplications does not satisfy a claim that requires seven distinct third-order products unless the patentee shows that some of the three are repeated or expanded in a manner that covers all seven claimed combinations. It is not sufficient to assert in post-trial briefing that the three can be “stood for” the seven; the mapping must be made explicit and supported by the record presented to the jury.

Why is three insufficient? Because the claim does not seek “some” or “at least” third-order products; it specifies a set of combinations — seven distinct forms — that, taken together, define the claimed method of generating an n-th order interference-cancellation signal. If the accused device produces only a subset of those forms, infringement does not follow. The analytic burden is similar to any case where a claim recites a specific list of structural or functional elements: omission of any listed element means no literal infringement. Thus, an expert who points to only three products and then assumes without record evidence that the others are present has failed to satisfy the patentee’s proof burden.

The Federal Circuit’s decision shows the appellate court will perform that element-by-element check on the record. Where the factual showing is limited to three terms, and where no admissible evidence demonstrates that any of those terms are repeated or algebraically expanded to produce the remainder of the claimed set, the evidence is insufficient as a matter of law.

## The necessity of a faithful mapping from accused operations to all seven claimed products

The most important practical rule that emerges from Finesse is this: a patentee asserting infringement of a claim that recites a specific list of algebraic operations must present a faithful, demonstrable mapping from the accused system’s operations to each item in that list. “Faithful” means three things. First, the mapping must be internally coherent: the expert must consistently identify which signal in the accused device is S1, which is S2, which is S3 (or which is the copy), and must explain why each identification is reasonable. Second, the mapping must be algebraically defensible: if the accused device uses derived signals (conjugates, moduli, filtered versions), the expert must show how those derived signals correspond to the claimed variables or how they produce the exact claimed algebraic products. Third, the mapping must be presented to the jury with sufficient clarity and evidentiary support (documents, code, test results) so that reasonable jurors can find that the accused device actually does what the expert claims.

Failure in any of those three respects will likely doom the infringement claim. In Finesse, Dr. Wells’ testimony was contradicted in places and ambiguous in others; he did not provide a clear mapping of the three distinct multiplications in the Nokia document to the seven claimed multiplications; and no evidence was introduced showing actual repetition or recombination to create the missing ones. The appellate court therefore held that a reasonable jury could not find for Finesse on that limitation based on the record below.

For litigators, this is a lesson in presentation. When a claim requires a set of algebraic outcomes, the patentee should prepare demonstrative exhibits, interrogatory responses, expert charts, and demonstrable experiments that produce each claimed product from the accused device or its published design, and should elicit testimony that traces the product’s origin in the accused system. Vague or equivocal testimony, or reliance on unexplained documents, is an invitation to JMOL.

## Substantial-evidence analysis: the insufficiency of post-hoc mapping not presented to the jury

The Federal Circuit’s reversal also reinforces a critical appellate principle: the appellate court reviews whether there was substantial evidence supporting the jury’s verdict, and it will not credit formulations that first appear on appeal. Substantial evidence means such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion. That standard does not permit appellants (or appellees) to invent or clarify mappings on appeal that were not presented to the jury.

Finesse tried to shore up its trial presentation by offering revised mappings on appeal and by invoking additional portions of the Nokia documents. The court refused to rely on that post-hoc rationalization. The remedy was reversal of the denial of JMOL because, viewing the record in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence did not meet the substantial-evidence threshold. The case thus illustrates two discrete rules: (1) appellants may obtain JMOL where the patentee’s proof at trial was inadequate as a matter of law; and (2) a patentee cannot rely on explanations that were never introduced at trial to save a verdict.

This principle has procedural consequences. A patentee who relies heavily on technical documentation and expert explanation must ensure that its theory is consistently and clearly presented at trial and well-documented in pretrial disclosures and expert reports. If the mapping is only articulated for the first time in appellate briefing, an appellate court will likely deem it unavailable to sustain the jury’s verdict.

## Practical and doctrinal lessons

Finesse is a cautionary tale for patentees and their experts in cases involving algorithmic, mathematical, or signal-processing claims. Several concrete lessons flow directly from the decision.

- Burden of proof and element-by-element analysis: The patentee bears the burden of proving infringement, and that requires proof that every claimed limitation is present in the accused device. For mathematical lists of products, that means demonstrating the presence of each algebraic combination the claim recites.

- Clarity in claim construction: When claims specify numbers (three signals), litigants should be mindful of whether the specification and prosecution leave room for non-unique embodiments. If the patentee wants to preserve coverage over duplicate-input embodiments, the record should reflect that intent.

- Prosecution statements matter — but beware the high bar for disclaimer: Narrowing amendments will not necessarily produce prosecution disclaimer unless the patentee’s words are clear and unmistakable.

- Expert testimony must be coherent, consistent, and tied to evidence: Courts will discount testimony that is self-contradictory or that relies on unstated assumptions. Experts must map accused-system signals to claim variables and must explain how each claimed algebraic term is produced.

- Be explicit about derived operations: If an accused system uses modulus or conjugation, do not assume they will be treated as equivalent to raw multiplications. Explain algebraically or empirically how those operations correspond to the claimed combinations.

- Do not save the mapping for appeal: The mapping from accused operations to claim elements must be presented to the jury; appellate courts will not credit new arguments or mappings advanced for the first time on appeal.

In sum, Finesse emphasizes that when claims recite specific algebraic products derived from multiple signals, proof of infringement is not a metaphorical exercise: it is a literal, element-by-element demonstration that the accused device outputs the asserted products. The decision affirms that doctrinal guards — clear-and-unmistakable prosecution disclaimer, the requirement of substantial evidence, and the element-by-element infringement rule — remain robust when the contested elements are mathematical in character.

# Appellate Disposition and Remedies: JMOL Reversal and Damages Vacatur

This chapter examines the Federal Circuit’s disposition in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC, focusing on the court’s reversal of the district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on noninfringement and the consequent vacatur of a large damages award. The opinion illustrates three connected appellate doctrines: (1) the standards and posture for JMOL and appellate review of denials of JMOL in the regional-circuit context, (2) the role and limits of expert testimony in proving infringement and how contradictions in expert testimony can defeat a plaintiff’s burden, and (3) the ordinary remedy — vacatur of damages — when appellate reversal eliminates liability. The opinion also highlights collateral matters that become moot after reversal (claim-construction disputes) and the routine allocation of costs to the successful appellant.

## Reversal of the denial of JMOL: standards and application to both patents

The vehicle by which the Federal Circuit disposed of this case was a reversal of the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for every asserted claim of both patents. To understand the import of that disposition, students must parse two distinct but related standards and how they interact in patent cases.

First, JMOL (Rule 50 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure) is a trial-stage device. A defendant seeking JMOL of noninfringement ordinarily must move at the close of the plaintiff’s evidence (Rule 50(a)) and renew the motion after verdict (Rule 50(b)) if the motion is denied. The district court’s initial grant or denial is made against the backdrop of a jury verdict built from the trial record; the record includes testimony (often expert testimony), demonstratives, technical documents, and claim-construction rulings.

Second, appellate review of a district court’s denial of JMOL is governed by the law of the regional circuit in which the district court sits. In Finesse, the Fifth Circuit’s standards apply: a denial of JMOL is reviewed de novo, but the court will reverse only if, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so overwhelmingly to the moving party that reasonable jurors could not have reached a contrary conclusion. The Federal Circuit, for its part, reviews the factual findings underlying infringement for substantial evidence — that is, whether a reasonable jury could have found for the patentee in light of the evidence. These formulations are complementary: the appellate court asks whether any reasonable jury could have returned the verdict in favor of the plaintiff, given the trial evidence.

Finesse is instructive in how those standards play out. The asserted claim limitations required, among other things, that the accused radio’s analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC) sample a passband that “includes signals of interest and interference generating signals” (’134 patent) and that the accused apparatus perform seven specified third‑order multiplications of three signals (’775 patent). The jury found infringement on both patents based principally on the testimony of the patentee’s expert, Dr. Wells, and on Nokia technical documents he relied upon.

The Federal Circuit reversed because the trial evidence — viewed in the record — was insufficient as a matter of law. Two recurring problems explain the court’s conclusion. First, Dr. Wells’ testimony was internally inconsistent and equivocal on critical mappings from the accused system to the claim language. For the ’134 patent, he sometimes identified the “interference generating signals” as a modeled PIM path depicted in Nokia materials, and at other points relied on the existence of two inputs labeled x1 and x2 without consistently mapping them to the claimed terms. The court emphasized that when the patentee relies on self‑contradictory expert testimony to meet its burden, the testimony may be insufficient to sustain a jury verdict — a principle previously applied in Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp. Second, factual demonstration of performance of the claimed seven multiplications was missing. The Nokia document at most listed three distinct multiplication expressions (involving complex conjugates and moduli), and Dr. Wells did not credibly map those to the seven enumerated multiplications required by the claim. The appellate court will not entertain new mappings posited for the first time on appeal; the verdict must be supported by trial evidence.

Students should take from this that (a) appellate courts will examine whether the trial record contains evidence, presented to the jury, that reasonably permits the jury to find that each claim limitation is met; (b) contradictory or evasive expert testimony undermines the sufficiency of the proof; and (c) the patentee cannot cure evidentiary gaps by arguing different factual mappings on appeal.

## Damages vacatur as the routine consequence of reversal of liability

When an appellate court reverses a jury’s finding of liability, it typically vacates any damages award that depended on that finding. The reasoning is straightforward: damages flow from liability. An award predicated on infringement can have no legal effect once the infringement determination itself is overturned. In Finesse, the jury had awarded $166,303,391 as a lump-sum for the remaining life of the patents. The Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of both patents and vacated the damages award.

Vacatur is the usual and appropriate remedy for two basic reasons. First, the damages award presupposes a binding verdict of liability; without liability, the award lacks a legal predicate. Second, vacatur avoids advisory recalculation: with liability eliminated, the appellate court should not attempt to assess damages in a case the patentee no longer legally won. If the patentee wishes to pursue damages further, the appropriate course is to seek a new trial on damages only if the liability result is upheld or the appellate court remands with instructions. In the typical sequence, reversal of liability ends the case for defendants and removes any monetary judgment against them.

This principle also reflects practical judicial economy. The court should not expend its limited resources to refine or upset a damages analysis that proves irrelevant because liability has been negated. Likewise, district courts normally do not proceed to calculation of damages unless and until liability is established.

## Motions for a new trial on damages — standard of review and posture

Although the Federal Circuit vacated damages in this case because of its reversal of liability, the opinion also touches on the standard for new‑trial motions on damages, which is important for practice. Motions for a new trial on damages are governed by Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 59; a district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial is reviewed under regional‑circuit law for abuse of discretion. In the Fifth Circuit, that means a reviewing court will reverse only if the district court’s decision was manifestly unreasonable, based on legal error, or a clear abuse.

A litigant seeking a new trial on damages typically argues that the damages award is excessive, unsupported by the evidence, tainted by improper evidence or argument, or infected by legal error in the jury charge or admissibility rulings. In Finesse, Appellants preserved challenges to the damages award post‑verdict, moving both for JMOL on damages and for a new trial. The district court denied both requests. Although the Federal Circuit reviewed the denial of the new trial application under the Fifth Circuit’s abuse‑of‑discretion standard, it did not reach the merits because the dispositive reversal of noninfringement rendered the damage issues moot. The rule is simple: if the underlying liability finding is reversed, a new trial on damages becomes unnecessary unless the plaintiff secures a reinstatement of liability.

Practitioners should note the strategic interplay: robust preservation of damages objections helps preserve appellate review, but even a meritorious damages argument becomes academic if liability falls on appeal. Conversely, a defendant may seek conditional relief (e.g., request for remittitur or for a new trial on damages only if liability is affirmed) to protect its interests should liability survive.

## Unreached issues and mootness — claim construction disputes and collateral questions

Appellate courts commonly decide only those questions necessary to resolve the appeal. In Finesse the Federal Circuit expressly declined to resolve certain disputed claim‑construction issues, including the proper construction of terms such as “receiver” and whether the claim limitation “co-located” imposed particular geographic or functional constraints. Those issues became moot once the court reversed for lack of substantial evidence of infringement. This practice follows Article III limits on advisory opinions and the prudential avoidance of unnecessary rulings.

Two lessons follow. First, litigants should expect that the court will not reach subsidiary or alternative issues if the dispositive grounds dispose of the appeal. Second, careful preservation of claim-construction positions at the district‑court stage remains essential—because if liability is upheld, those unresolved constructions will become critical on remand or in subsequent appeals.

Relatedly, the opinion touches on waiver doctrines. Appellants contended that the patentee had disclaimed a particular scope during prosecution (i.e., that the claims no longer covered two-signal embodiments) and that the patentee had waived certain theories by failing to raise them at the proper Rule 50(a) stage of trial. The district court and the Federal Circuit reviewed those contentions, finding no clear and unambiguous disavowal during prosecution but being careful to recognize that a failure to raise issues at trial can sometimes forfeit them. These procedural rules matter: an appellate court is more likely to reach and decide a preserved argument about claim scope or waiver than one raised for the first time on appeal.

## Allocation of costs on appeal

Finally, appellate disposition often includes an award of costs to the prevailing party. In Finesse the Federal Circuit directed costs to Defendants‑Appellants. The award of costs on appeal follows Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 39 and Circuit rules; typically, the prevailing party may file a bill of costs for taxable costs such as printing and copying of necessary records. The appellate court’s indication that costs “to Defendants‑Appellants” is a routine allocation reflecting the outcome. Students should understand that an appellate victory may carry fiscal consequences for the losing party beyond the substantive judgment reversal.

## Practical implications and litigation tips

Several pragmatic takeaways flow from the Finesse decision:

- Build an incontrovertible evidentiary mapping from accused products to each asserted claim element at trial. Appellate courts will demand that the jury’s verdict have a credible, non‑speculative evidentiary foundation.

- Avoid reliance on equivocal or internally inconsistent expert testimony to carry essential elements of proof. Cross‑examination that exposes inconsistencies can neutralize that witness on appeal.

- Present any alternative mappings or legal theories to the jury at trial; do not reserve them for appeal. Courts are reluctant to accept new theories for the first time on appeal.

- Preserve damages objections and seek conditional relief when appropriate (for example, a conditional motion for remittitur or a contingent new‑trial motion) so that relief remains available if liability is sustained.

- Recognize that appellate reversal of liability will ordinarily vacate associated damages, which often ends the case in favor of defendants — an important strategic consideration in settlement and post‑verdict litigation.

In sum, Finesse underscores the appellate court’s role in policing the sufficiency of the trial evidence that supports a jury’s finding of infringement and demonstrates the predictable remedial consequence — vacatur of damages — when that sufficiency fails.

# Litigation Strategy Lessons: Building and Defending Complex Technical Infringement Cases

Technical patent litigation requires lawyers and experts to think like engineers while thinking like advocates. The Federal Circuit’s decision in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC (reversing the district court’s denial of JMOL on infringement and vacating damages) offers a compact, practical lesson plan about how to build—and how to defend against—a complex technical infringement case. The opinion is useful not only for its holdings (e.g., reversing because the plaintiff’s expert testimony was internally inconsistent and unsupported with necessary mappings), but for the procedural and evidentiary failures it highlights.

The sections that follow extract specific, actionable lessons illustrated by Finesse. Each section explains the doctrine or standard at play, then translates that law into courtroom strategy for both plaintiffs and defendants. Where appropriate, the text points to what the Federal Circuit found critical in Finesse and how different choices—by counsel or experts—would likely have produced a different result.

## Prepare precise, consistent expert mappings of every claim element to concrete accused operations

A patent plaintiff carries the burden of proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the accused product practices each element of an asserted claim. That burden is largely carried by expert testimony in complex technical cases. Finesse illustrates the danger of failing to produce a clear, consistent mapping: the Federal Circuit reversed because the plaintiff’s expert repeatedly equivocated about which signals corresponded to the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” and never gave a reliable mapping from the claim language to the accused radio’s operations.

Practical guidance
- At the outset, require the expert to produce a claim chart that maps each claim element, word-for-word, to discrete, labeled locations in the accused system (source document, line of source code, block in a vendor diagram, FPGA module, firmware routine, timing log). This is not just a helpful demonstrative; it is the expert’s legal work product and the evidentiary spine of the infringement case.
- Make the mapping granular. Map by element and sub-element. For functional claim elements (e.g., “isolating signals of interest”), identify the precise module or routine, the configuration or mode in which the product must operate, and the version of the firmware or build.
- Avoid implicit or circular mappings. An expert should not say “the red path contains both signals” if the red path in the vendor’s diagram legend itself conflates two distinct things (as the Nokia legend did in Finesse). If the vendor legend is ambiguous, the expert must say why: identify the underlying data showing two distinct signals (e.g., two ADC inputs, two buffers with different timestamps).
- Maintain consistency across reports, depositions and trial testimony. If the expert changes an argument, explain why in an updated report or supplemental declaration before trial so the change is in the record. Unexplained shifts are often fatal on JMOL.

What to do pre-trial
- Serve a “detailed infringement contentions” document with claim-element-to-evidence mapping and cite to specific materials (source code lines, diagrams with callouts, logs).
- Use expert reports to anchor the mapping—reports are admissible evidence; a jury verdict cannot rest on post hoc mappings developed only in argument.
- When new evidence changes the mapping, supplement interrogatory responses and expert reports promptly and preserve the explanation in deposition.

What the Finesse court condemned
- Vague legend reliance (saying the red path includes two signals) without explicit testimony tying x1 and x2 to the claim elements; repeated, unexplained contradictions in the expert’s testimony; and lack of a clear, contemporaneous mapping. The Federal Circuit cited Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope for the proposition that when a party’s case rests on an expert’s self-contradictory testimony, a reasonable jury cannot rely on it.

## Avoid reliance on ambiguous vendor legends; corroborate with timing diagrams and source code

Vendor documents, internal diagrams, and marketing or product manuals are often central pieces of evidence. They are also frequently opaque, ambiguous, and misleading when used alone. In Finesse, a Nokia technical diagram’s legend (“Red – DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path”) created ambiguity that the plaintiff’s expert failed to resolve credibly.

How to handle vendor materials
- Do not let a legend stand alone as the mapping. Treat vendor diagrams as pointers to underlying facts, not as self-explanatory proof. If a diagram groups two items in a single legend entry, interrogate and test whether those are logically separate signals (e.g., separate electrical nodes, separate timestamps, distinct bits in a data stream).
- Seek the underlying artifacts. For software-defined radios and other programmable devices the underlying artifacts include source code, firmware image, release notes, FPGA bitstream descriptions, synthesis reports, HDL source, module-level documentation, and developer comments. Request these in discovery and cut the vendor’s diagram into traceable pieces in the expert’s chart.
- Corroborate with timing diagrams and execution traces. A vendor drawing that shows a “modeled PIM path” is not a substitute for an oscilloscope trace, timestamped log, or packet capture showing when a signal is created relative to sampling. These timing artifacts are often dispositive in sequencing disputes (see next section).
- Use reverse-engineering and black-box testing where appropriate. If source code or low-level firmware is unavailable, controlled tests that produce input-output correlations can be persuasive. But those tests must be documented, repeatable, and reproducible.

Preservation tip
- When relying on vendor materials, elicit admissible foundation at deposition and in the expert report: who authored the diagram, when, and what data supported it. A vendor diagram without foundation is easily discredited.

## Explain temporal sequencing to show feasibility (or impossibility) of steps like sampling

Many claimed inventions depend on a particular time order of operations (sample then compute; compute then inject; filter then decimate; etc.). The Finesse case turned on timing: the jury’s verdict failed to properly account for the fact that the “modeled PIM path” in Nokia’s architecture was generated downstream of the ADC, meaning it could not have been sampled at the ADC as the claim required.

Why sequencing matters
- Patent claims’ function often depends on what happens first. Demonstrating that an accused product cannot perform a claimed operation because the data is not available at the claimed time is a powerful noninfringement argument. Conversely, the plaintiff must show that the accused system provides the necessary signals at the right time and place.

How plaintiffs should prove sequencing
- Produce timestamped, low-level traces showing the lifecycle of the signals: where they originate, when they are created, how they are passed through buffers, and when the ADC sampling occurs. Use oscilloscope captures, logic analyzer outputs, or detailed software logs. If sampling is an explicit hardware event, a test bench reproduction that triggers sampling while logging signals is compelling.
- Identify the exact sampling clock domain and show the signals of interest are present in that domain. If the claim element says “over-sampling ... a passband of received signals,” the expert must show what that passband is and what enters the ADC at the sampling instant.
- When the vendor architecture separates “modeled” signals (derived later) from physical inputs, explain how the model might nevertheless be reconstructable at sampling time (if it can), or explain why it cannot be. Unsupported assertions that a downstream-generated modeled path was sampled are legally insufficient.

How defendants should disprove sequencing
- Emphasize offsets and clock domains. If the accused product uses multiple clock domains, or if some processing happens in a different FPGA stage after the ADC, show the clock and buffering delay that prevents the asserted temporal alignment.
- Use deposition and vendor testimony: ask engineers where signals are generated and whether they exist at the ADC inputs at the time of sampling. Force the expert to reconcile their mapping with known hardware flows.

A courtroom example
- In Finesse, the defendant elicited from the plaintiff’s expert that the modeled PIM path was generated in the GROOT FPGA (downstream), and thus could not have been part of the ADC’s sampled passband. That temporal mismatch—not overcome by consistent evidence—was fatal.

## Account for mathematical transforms (|x|, xʹ) and justify why they qualify as the claimed "signals"

Modern DSP systems routinely use transforms: complex conjugates, magnitudes, filters, FFTs, and other nonlinear operations. Claims that require “three signals S1, S2 and S3” can be trapped into sloppy argument if those transforms are called “signals” without support. The Finesse court found that the Nokia materials showed operations like modulus and complex conjugate; the plaintiff’s attempt to treat those transforms as the same signals as x1 and x2 was not sufficiently explained or mapped.

Key doctrinal point
- Whether a transformed quantity counts as a separate “signal” for claim purposes is an evidentiary and claim-construction question. The plaintiff must show either (a) that the claim term encompasses such transforms under the agreed construction, or (b) that the transform is the same signal (i.e., functionally equivalent in context) as one of the claimed signals.

Practical steps for plaintiffs
- Explicitly argue and explain how the transforms map onto claim terms. If the claim requires three separately identifiable signals, and the accused device only explicitly stores x1 and x2 but computes |x1| and x1ʹ and |x2| on the fly, the expert must explain why |x2| (the modulus) is “a signal” in the sense the claim requires. That explanation should: (i) identify where |x2| is computed; (ii) show that it exists as a distinct data item (a buffer, register, or stream) at the time it is used; and (iii) show whether it is produced in real-time or retroactively.
- If the transform is computed, show that the computation is performed in a way that satisfies any “continuously and near real time” or “generating ... using copies of transmitter signals” limitations. Tie the computation to precise code or hardware blocks.

Practical steps for defendants
- Point out the logical differences between x2, x2ʹ (complex conjugate), and |x2| (magnitude). If the claim requires independent signals S1, S2, S3, show that a magnitude or conjugate is a function of an original signal and not a separately generated source signal as claimed.
- Use the vendor document: call out the entry that lists x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1| or x1⋅x1⋅x2ʹ, and require the expert to explain how those terms satisfy the claim’s requirement of separate multiplications of distinct signals. If the expert cannot map the conjugate and modulus to distinct S1/S2/S3 categories, emphasize the evidentiary gap.

Rules of thumb
- A transform can be a “separately identifiable” signal if (and only if) it exists as a separable data item at the point in the processing pipeline required by the claim. Mere mathematical representation in a document is insufficient.

## Use demonstratives that enumerate and align all required operations (e.g., seven multiplications)

A clear demonstrative can win or lose a case. The Federal Circuit criticized Finesse because the Nokia document listed only three distinct multiplications while the claim required seven, and the plaintiff’s expert did not explain the mapping from the document’s multiplications to the seven claimed ones.

Designing effective demonstratives
- Create a demonstrative that enumerates, one-by-one, every asserted claim element and shows the accused operation that allegedly satisfies it, with a direct citation to the underlying evidence (e.g., source-code line, diagram callout, log line).
- For claims that require multiple enumerated operations (like the seven third-order multiplications), the demonstrative should explicitly list the claimed operations and then, next to each, identify which multiplication in the accused system corresponds to it. If the accused system produces fewer distinct multiplications, the demonstrative must show either repeated execution or repeated use that reaches the claimed number (with objective proof).
- If the defendant’s architecture lists operations differently (for example, using complex conjugates or moduli), the demonstrative must also show how those operations correspond, step-by-step, to the claimed operation, not merely assert equivalence.

Best practices
- Demonstratives are persuasive only if they are anchored to evidence. Each cell of a mapping demonstrative should incorporate a citeable source: a page/figure number in vendor docs, a line in the source code, a deposition page and line, a timestamp in a capture, or an expert’s report paragraph.
- Anticipate cross-examination. Keep the underlying documents available and organized so that any challenged mapping can be immediately supported with the source artifact.

How Finesse went wrong
- The Nokia document showed three distinct multiplications, and the plaintiff’s expert did not testify how the three mapped to the seven claimed multiplications (or whether repetitions could or did produce additional multiplications). The absence of a detailed alignment demonstrative made the jury’s verdict unsupportable on appeal.

## Preserve arguments at Rule 50(a) to avoid waiver; build the record with pinpoint citations

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 50(a) requires a party to move for JMOL at the close of the opposing party’s case (or at the close of all evidence) to preserve the argument for appeal. The Finesse opinion demonstrates that district courts sometimes decline to entertain arguments raised only post-verdict, and appellate courts will apply a strict standard of review that requires a robust and specific record.

What to preserve and how
- If you believe the plaintiff has failed to prove a claim element, move for JMOL under Rule 50(a) at the appropriate time and be precise. A generic motion is less likely to preserve specific grounds for appeal. For example, argue “no reasonable jury could find element 1[a] is met because the alleged ‘interference generating signals’ are generated downstream of the ADC and cannot have been sampled,” and cite the precise deposition pages, the specific vendor schematic, and the expert report paragraphs that support that proposition.
- For each challenged claim element, provide pinpoint citations in your Rule 50(a) motion to the trial record, depositions, and exhibits. The more specific you are about the evidentiary gaps, the more forceful any later JMOL or appellate argument will be.
- Do not assume that a Rule 50(a) move is merely a formality. Failure to make a timely, specific Rule 50(a) motion can result in waiver of the argument on appeal.

Creating an appellate record
- Complement Rule 50(a) with contemporaneous objections and offers of proof during trial. If an expert offers a novel theory on redirect or in summation that was not in the report, object and request a proffer or strike the testimony. Preserve your objections in the transcript.
- Seek limiting instructions or curative measures if an expert testifies to facts outside the record. If the court permits it, make a record about why admission of the new theory prejudices your client.

The Finesse lesson
- Appellants in Finesse challenged specific evidentiary points at JMOL, pointing to the expert’s testimony and the evidence that the modeled PIM path was generated downstream. The court reviewed those arguments de novo (under Fifth Circuit law). If the appellants had failed to present these distinct points at Rule 50(a), reversal on appeal would have been harder to achieve.

## Cross-examination strategies: exposing contradictions and unperformed steps

Cross-examination of opposing experts and fact witnesses is the most powerful trial tool in a technical case. Finesse shows that contradicting testimony by an expert can be decisive—if the contradiction is exposed and made meaningful to the jury and the court.

Techniques to use
- Prepare impeachment material that documents inconsistencies: deposition testimony, prior expert reports, vendor emails, code comments, and earlier demos. Present these excerpts in side-by-side form in demonstratives and ask the witness to confirm or explain the inconsistency. If the witness cannot reconcile the discrepancy, emphasize the absence of reliable testimony.
- Use step-by-step questioning to force the expert to map claim elements live on the stand. A sequence such as: “Identify claim element 1; point to the line in the code that shows it; show where that data exists at time T0; if it does not exist at T0, say so” can pin down otherwise vague testimony.
- Ask binary, forced-choice questions on key points. Instead of “Is the modeled PIM path sampled?” ask “At the instant the ADC samples, is the modeled PIM path present at the ADC input? Yes or No?” Repeatedly forcing the expert to answer can either produce admissions or highlight evasions.
- Bring in the vendor engineer who authored the diagram or code to testify about timing, buffering or data paths, or use their deposition testimony to impeach.

How to challenge unperformed steps
- Seek a lab demonstration or black-box test in front of the jury (if feasible and admissible) to show an operation is not performed under realistic conditions.
- If the plaintiff claims the product produces repeated multiplications to reach a required count (e.g., seven), demand evidence of repetition: logs showing that the same multiplication runs individually, time-stamped traces, or code that unambiguously does the repeated operations. Absent such evidence, emphasize the gap.

Use of authoritative precedent
- The Federal Circuit’s reliance on Johns Hopkins in Finesse is instructive: when the plaintiff’s expert gives contradictory testimony about a factual predicate required for infringement, a court may conclude the evidence is legally insufficient. Cross-examiners should aim to create those contradictions.

## Checklist: What plaintiffs must do and what defendants must do (summary)

Plaintiff’s checklist
- Produce detailed infringement contentions and an expert report that maps every claim element to specific pieces of evidence with pinpoint citations.
- Corroborate vendor diagrams with underlying code, firmware, FPGA design documents, timing traces, or lab reproductions showing signals present at the claimed point in the pipeline.
- Explain how transforms (modulus, conjugates, filters) qualify as “signals” under the construction; show where they exist in the pipeline.
- Build demonstratives that enumerate, one-by-one, each claimed operation and the accused operation that matches it, with citations.
- Anticipate and address prosecution-history issues (e.g., whether amendments narrowed claim scope) in the expert report.
- Preserve noninfringement grounds with precise Rule 50(a) motions and contemporaneous objections.

Defendant’s checklist
- During discovery, seek source code, firmware, vendor schematics, design reviews, and internal testing results; depose the engineers who designed the signal flow.
- Prepare targeted deposition questions to pin down where signals are generated, whether they exist at the relevant sampling event, and whether transforms are stored or computed later.
- Create cross-examination scripts that force experts to map claim elements on the stand and to reconcile any inconsistencies with their reports.
- Move for JMOL at the appropriate time with specific citations to the testimony and exhibits establishing noninfringement or absence of proof on individual elements.
- If the plaintiff’s claim rests solely on vendor diagrams, emphasize the absence of corroborating low-level evidence.

## Final observations about risk management and damage implications

The Finesse opinion does more than instruct on infringement mappings; it underscores the consequence of weak evidence—a reversal and vacatur of a large damages award. In technical cases, the record that supports infringement also underpins damages. Demonstratives, code, logs, and expert testimony that are airtight for infringement help justify royalty bases and apportionment. Conversely, a fragile infringement case risks not only a loss on liability but also reversal of any damages award and exposure to costs on appeal.

A few closing tips
- Coordinate technical discovery, expert preparation, and trial demonstratives early, and insist on drafts of the expert’s mapping well in advance of depositions. Give the opponent little room to say an argument is “new” at trial.
- For plaintiffs, consider running bench tests or lab reproductions designed both to replicate accused operation and to produce objective artifacts the jury can see (traces, oscilloscope screenshots, packet captures).
- For defendants, preserve the record for appeal: make precise Rule 50(a) motions, preserve objections to new expert theories, and use deposition excerpts and vendor testimony to build an appellate-friendly record that can be cited in JMOL briefing.

Conclusion

Finesse Wireless v. AT&T is a cautionary tale and a practical manual. Courts will not sustain jury verdicts that rest on equivocal expert testimony or ambiguous diagrams when the record lacks a coherent mapping of claim elements to specific, time-stamped operations in the accused product. Lawyers and experts who invest in clear, evidence-based mappings, corroborating artifacts, and disciplined testimony—and who preserve noninfringement arguments at the right moments—dramatically improve their odds at trial and on appeal.

# Comparative Case Law: Substantial Evidence and Expert Testimony in the Federal Circuit

This chapter uses the Federal Circuit’s decision in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC, and a sequence of companion decisions, to explore how the appellate court treats the sufficiency of trial evidence in patent cases where expert testimony is essential to map claim limitations onto accused products or processes. Finesse, a 2025 Federal Circuit decision, reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacated a damages award because the plaintiff’s case rested on equivocal, internally inconsistent expert testimony and on documentary materials that did not, as a matter of record, show the required element-by-element operations. That outcome is a useful vehicle for extracting doctrine about: (1) the standards of review applicable to JMOL and substantial-evidence challenges in patent trials; (2) the particular dangers posed by self-contradictory or speculative expert testimony; (3) how prosecution history amendments and statements can — but do not always — limit claim scope by clear and unmistakable disclaimer; and (4) the recurring appellate pattern of reversal when plaintiffs fail to prove each claim limitation with adequate precision at trial.

The remainder of the chapter situates Finesse alongside key authorities—Wi-LAN/Apple decisions, Johns Hopkins University v. Datascope, and Continental Circuits v. Intel—and draws practical and doctrinal lessons for litigators, experts, and judges.

## Apple v. Wi-LAN: substantial evidence review of infringement and damages

Two related sets of decisions supply the Federal Circuit’s approach to appellate review of factual findings in patent trials. When this court reviews a jury’s factual rulings—most prominently whether a jury could reasonably have found infringement or a particular damages amount—it asks whether those findings are supported by substantial evidence. Substantial evidence means such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support the conclusion; put differently, whether a reasonable jury, viewing all the evidence presented at trial, could have returned the verdict reached.

In practical terms for patent cases, this standard carries three implications:

- The plaintiff must produce enough evidence at trial, and that evidence must be such that reasonable jurors could conclude each claim limitation is met. Documentary evidence, demonstratives, and expert testimony all count, but the evidence must be enough that a jury could reasonably connect the dots.
- The Federal Circuit will not weigh credibility anew on appeal; it will consider whether, given the evidence actually presented, a reasonable jury could have done what the jury did. But that standard is not toothless. Where the only testimony supporting infringement is speculative, internally inconsistent, or unmoored from the record, the finding may fail the substantial-evidence test.
- For damages, the same substantial-evidence rule applies to underlying factual findings (for example, that a particular licensing theory was adopted by the hypothetical negotiators), though legal conclusions (e.g., the proper measure of damages) are reviewed de novo.

Finesse illustrates these points: although a jury found infringement and awarded a large lump-sum damages figure for the life of two patents, the Federal Circuit reversed because the evidence—principally the plaintiff’s expert testimony and a set of Nokia technical documents—did not permit a reasonable jury to find the asserted claim limitations were met. The lesson is clear: substantial evidence review is forgiving of reasonable inferences, but it requires that the inferences be tethered to evidence actually introduced at trial.

## Wi-LAN v. Apple: regional-circuit JMOL standards and Federal Circuit deference

A distinct but related layer of review concerns JMOL (judgment as a matter of law, also called JNOV). The Federal Circuit applies the law of the regional circuit governing JMOL motions. In Finesse the Fifth Circuit’s standard controlled: when considering a motion for JMOL, the district court must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the non-moving party (the verdict winner), and the appellate court will reverse denial of JMOL only if the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that reasonable jurors could not have arrived at a contrary conclusion.

Two interacting points follow from this regional-circuit rule:

- The district court’s denial of JMOL is reviewed with the same deference embodied in the regional-circuit standard; the Federal Circuit asks whether, viewing the evidence as the district court was supposed to view it, the denial was legally defensible.
- That deference does not insulate fundamentally unsupported verdicts. If the record contains only equivocal, contradictory, or post-hoc explanations for how claim elements map onto the accused product, an appellate court can conclude that no reasonable jury could find for the patentee.

In practice, then, litigators must remember that the JMOL inquiry is not an invitation to reweigh credibility—the court must still ask whether the totality of the admissible evidence could have supported a reasonable jury verdict. Finesse demonstrates that, when the only plausible reading of the expert record is internally inconsistent (for example, the expert repeatedly says an accused signal is both generated before and after the receiver’s analog-to-digital converter), a reversal is appropriate because the evidence does not point in the way required by the standard.

## Johns Hopkins v. Datascope: effect of contradictory expert testimony

Johns Hopkins University v. Datascope Corp. (543 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2008)) is one of the Federal Circuit’s most frequently cited authorities on the limits of expert testimony as a basis for a jury’s verdict. In Johns Hopkins the expert’s testimony was internally inconsistent about how an accused medical device contacted a blood vessel—testifying at different times that the device contacted the vessel in either three dimensions or in only two places. The court held that the expert’s contradictory testimony could not, as a matter of law, support a finding that the device literally met a claim limitation requiring three-dimensional contact.

Johns Hopkins establishes a practical rule: when an expert’s testimony is the centerpiece of a patentee’s mapping of claim elements onto an accused device, internal inconsistency in that testimony can defeat the sufficiency of the evidence. The Federal Circuit will not assume the jury resolved the inconsistency in favor of the patentee if the record shows the inconsistency is fundamental to whether a claim limitation is satisfied.

Finesse applies Johns Hopkins directly. There, the plaintiff’s expert (Dr. Wells) repeatedly identified particular signals in a Nokia schematic as (i) the “signals of interest” and (ii) the “interference generating signals.” On cross-examination, Appellants showed the modeled PIM path that the expert had identified as the interference-generating signal was generated downstream of the RF analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC)—i.e., after sampling—so it could not be something the RF ADC had sampled, as the claim required. The expert then equivocated and pointed to two signal labels (x1 and x2) as “the two signals” on the path, but never clearly mapped x1 and x2 onto the claimed terms. The Federal Circuit treated this change of footing as the type of contradictory and unclear evidence Johns Hopkins condemns: the patentee had the burden of proof and rested it on an expert whose testimony was internally inconsistent and insufficiently precise to support an element-by-element finding of infringement. As in Johns Hopkins, the appellate court reversed for lack of substantial evidence.

Doctrine distilled from Johns Hopkins and demonstrated in Finesse:

- An expert may testify to mapping claim limitations to accused structures, but the testimony must be consistent, comprehensible, and sufficiently precise that a reasonable jury could find for the patentee without resorting to speculation.
- If an expert’s core mapping is contradicted by other record evidence (for example, timing or signal-flow relationships shown in schematics or admitted at trial), and the expert cannot reconcile the contradiction clearly, the testimony will not sustain a verdict.
- Ambiguous references to diagrams or legends—without contemporaneous explanation linking labels on a diagram to the claim language—are fragile forms of proof.

## Continental Circuits v. Intel: disavowal/disclaimer standards and scope control

Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788 (Fed. Cir. 2019), addresses how prosecution history can limit claim scope by disavowal. The Continental Circuits decision reiterates a familiar principle: for prosecution statements to produce a narrowing of claim scope (disclaimer), the patentee must make clear and unmistakable statements during prosecution that would lead a reasonable competitor to conclude that the patentee surrendered claim scope.

Continental Circuits sets a demanding standard for disavowal:

- An amendment or argument that narrows claim language for reasons of patentability does not automatically constitute a clear and unmistakable disavowal of broader claim scope. The examiner’s rejection and the patentee’s response must be read in context.
- The patentee’s statements must be sufficiently specific to demonstrate an intention to confine the claim to a particular meaning; general arguments that distinguish prior art on a particular ground—e.g., that a reference addresses a different problem such as active rather than passive interference—will not ordinarily produce a binding disclaimer of a claim feature unless phrased with the requisite clarity.

Finesse applied this doctrine in the following way: during prosecution the patentee amended claims to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3” and argued against a prior reference (Filipovic) that used two signals. Appellants argued Finesse had disclaimed two-signal embodiments; the Federal Circuit concluded the prosecution history did not contain the sort of clear and unmistakable surrender required by Continental Circuits. Instead, the amendment and accompanying arguments were reasonably read as addressing a patentability distinction (passive vs. active intermodulation) rather than as a clear disclaimer of any claim scope that might encompass two non-unique inputs that are separately identifiable in the claim sense.

Two practical takeaways from Continental Circuits and Finesse:

- Amendments that add limitations can invite an inference of narrowed scope, but courts will not infer forfeiture or disavowal lightly. The inquiry focuses on what the patentee actually said, and whether that communication shows an unmistakable intent to surrender particular subject matter.
- Conversely, patentees should be careful in prosecution statements. If a patentee intends to keep claim scope broad, it is safer to present distinguishing arguments in a way that does not suggest a categorical surrender of alternatives.

## Themes: the all-elements rule, evidentiary precision, and the dangers of post-hoc rationalization

Several themes thread these decisions together and inform litigation practice.

The all-elements rule and the burden of proof. The Federal Circuit repeatedly emphasizes that infringement requires proof that the accused product or process meets every claim limitation, whether literally or under the doctrine of equivalents. Because infringement is a question of fact when disputed, a jury may find infringement based on direct or circumstantial evidence, but the plaintiff bears the ultimate burden to prove each limitation. This “all-elements” requirement is not a formalistic checklist; it is the practical obligation to provide fact-based support—experimental data, contemporaneous documents, or consistent expert explanation—that a factfinder could accept without speculative leaps.

Evidentiary precision. Expert testimony often provides the crucial bridge between complex technical materials and claim language. But that bridge must be stable. Courts demand that experts do more than assert mappings; they must explain how and why particular components or operations correspond to the claim terms. When claimed elements involve temporal or causal relationships (as in Finesse, where the claim required sampling a passband that includes certain signals), experts must reconcile timing shown in accused-device schematics with the claim’s temporal language. Failure to do so—or testimony that reflects confusion about where signals are generated or sampled—will leave the record wanting.

The dangers of post-hoc rationalization. A recurring appellate fault line is reliance on theories developed or clarified only on appeal. Appellate courts will not credit a new mapping or explanation that was not presented to the jury. In Finesse the plaintiff attempted to shore up its case on appeal by offering a clearer mapping of the three multiplications required by the claim to multiplications listed in a Nokia document. Because that mapping was not presented to the jury, the appellate court would not accept it as support for the jury verdict. The basic rule is that appellate courts review the record as made at trial; strategies and explanations that did not exist then cannot be used to cure evidentiary insufficiency on appeal.

Concrete implications of these themes:

- Plaintiffs should ensure their experts present direct, non-contradictory mappings at trial, supported by demonstratives and, if possible, corroborating engineering documents or test data.
- Defendants should focus on exposing temporal or causal contradictions between claimed operations and the accused product’s design—differences in where something is generated vs. where something is sampled are classic, potent attacks.
- Trial courts should police expert testimony for clarity and consider precluding testimony that is speculative or that fails to provide an adequate factual basis to permit reasonable jurors to draw the asserted inferences.

## Patterns in reversals arising from missing or speculative element-by-element proof

Surveying the Federal Circuit’s decisions over the past two decades reveals predictable patterns where appellate reversal follows a jury verdict.

Pattern 1 — reliance on a single, equivocal expert. When the plaintiff’s case depends heavily on one expert and that expert’s testimony is inconsistent, conclusory, or flatly contradicted by contemporaneous documents, the appellate court often concludes that no reasonable jury could have found infringement. Johns Hopkins and Finesse exemplify this pattern.

Pattern 2 — failure to map each claim limitation to concrete operations or structures. A patentee may show that a device generally performs a function, but if it does not show how the device performs every claimed sub-step (especially when the claim enumerates discrete sub-steps), the evidence will be deemed insufficient. In Finesse the claims required that the radio sample a passband containing both signals of interest and interference-generating signals; the record did not permit a clear mapping of both labels to signals actually sampled by the RF ADC.

Pattern 3 — acceptance of ambiguous schematics without contemporaneous explanation. Technical diagrams are helpful, but legends and abbreviated notations can be misleading unless an expert clearly ties labels and signal flows in the diagram to the claimed elements. The Finesse expert’s reliance on a diagram legend that labeled two items together (“DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path”) without a reliable explanation of how those labels corresponded to claimed, distinct signals was fatal.

Pattern 4 — post-hoc mappings on appeal. Appeals courts will not retrofit trial records with arguments made for the first time on appeal. Thus, litigants who rely on strategic obfuscation at trial cannot later rescue their positions by advanced mapping on appeal.

Pattern 5 — prosecution history pitfalls. Where a patentee’s prosecution statements plainly and unmistakably limit claim scope, courts will hold the patentee to those statements. But where the patentee’s arguments were contextualized to distinguish a reference on a different ground (e.g., active vs. passive IMPs), courts will not read a disavowal into the record without a clear and unmistakable statement. Finesse demonstrates that adding a limitation during prosecution (e.g., “three signals S1, S2 and S3”) does not necessarily mean the patentee intended to surrender all embodiments that could be read to involve only two unique physical signals.

Practical checklist to reduce appellate risk

- For each claim element, require the expert to prepare a demonstrative that (a) quotes the claim limitation, (b) shows the accused product’s component(s) or signal(s), and (c) explains, with citations to documents or test data, why the component or operation meets the limitation.
- If a claim requires temporal or causal order (e.g., sampling happens before processing), ensure the expert addresses timing expressly, with reference to architecture diagrams and signal flow.
- Anticipate cross-examination points that show the diagram’s labels are generated at different places/times and prepare reconciliations grounded in evidence (not supposition).
- Avoid relying only on legends or unexplained labels in technical documents; corroborate with source code, firmware, design documents, or test results where feasible.
- Preserve any necessary alternative mappings at trial; do not reserve the key explanation for appellate briefing.

## Conclusion

Finesse Wireless v. AT&T illustrates the boundaries of permissible proof of infringement in patent trials. The Federal Circuit’s reversal underscores that substantial-evidence review is meaningful: it protects defendants from jury verdicts grounded in speculative or self-contradictory expert testimony and from plaintiffs’ post-hoc theories advanced only on appeal. Johns Hopkins and Continental Circuits supply complementary guardrails—Johns Hopkins on the limits of inconsistent expert testimony, and Continental Circuits on when prosecution history narrows claim scope.

For practitioners, the message is practical and stark: win (and keep) infringement verdicts by presenting clear, contemporaneous, element-by-element evidence at trial. For judges and jurors, the decisions remind us that technical complexity cannot be used as a cover for ambiguity; the more complicated the subject matter, the more precise the evidentiary foundation must be.

# Implications for Patent Drafting, Prosecution, and Product Design

The Federal Circuit’s decision in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC crystallizes practical lessons at the intersection of claim drafting, prosecution strategy, product engineering, and litigation readiness where signal identity and numerical mathematical operations are central to patent scope. The opinion resolved two recurring problems that ensnare patentees and engineers alike: ambiguous identification of what, precisely, counts as a “signal” for claim purposes, and the risks of asserting that a device performs a specified set of mathematical operations when the accused implementation performs related but not identically framed computations. The court’s reversal of the jury verdict for lack of substantial evidence (and the attendant vacatur of damages) shows how those drafting and evidentiary gaps can be dispositive. This chapter draws out doctrinal rules the opinion applied and converts them into concrete guidance for patent drafters, prosecutors, and product designers.

## Claiming "signals" versus "functions of signals": drafting to reduce ambiguity

At the heart of the ’134 patent dispute was whether the accused radios “sampled a passband of received signals that includes signals of interest and interference generating signals.” The patentee relied on an expert who mapped claim terms to labels appearing on a vendor diagram (e.g., “DL (TX) reference” and “modeled PIM path”). The expert’s testimony, however, was internally inconsistent: he sometimes referred to the vendor labels, and sometimes to internal variables x1 and x2, without an unambiguous on-the-record mapping. The Federal Circuit found that ambiguity fatal: a verdict based largely on an expert’s contradictory mapping could not be sustained.

This aspect of the opinion underlines an essential drafting principle: when a claim turns on the presence, timing, or identity of “signals,” the specification and claims should clearly define what is meant by a “signal” and the universe of signal-related representations that should be covered. There are several dimensions to this clarity:

- Temporal and physical locus. Specify where and when the claimed “signal” is present. Does the claim require the original analog electromagnetic waveform present at an antenna? Or does it encompass subsequent digital representations—samples, filtered versions, down-converted duplicates, modeled or synthesized signals generated later in the processing chain? The Finesse dispute hinged on whether a modeled PIM (generated after ADC) could count as an “interference generating signal” that had been sampled. If the patentee’s intended scope was to include modeled or synthesized versions of a waveform, the specification should say so in concrete terms.

- Functional formulations. If the invention is meant to cover processes applied to or functions of signals—e.g., “a representation of a transmitter signal,” “a sampled and filtered version of x(t),” “a modeled estimate of an interference signal”—use functional claim language in addition to nominal labels. Functional claiming can avoid later disputes about whether a particular internal signal bears the same name as a claimed “signal.” But do so carefully: functional language can invite means-plus-function treatment under 35 U.S.C. § 112(f) if not coupled with sufficient structure, especially in mechanical or mixed domains; when possible, couple functional recitations with particular structures or process steps and explicit examples.

- Multiple embodiments and equivalents. Draft claims and the specification to encompass a range of representations—analog, sampled digital, complex-conjugated, modulus, filtered, upsampled and downsampled variants. Describe explicitly that the claimed “signal” may be the original waveform, a sampled bitstream, a demodulated baseband sample, a complex representation, or a modeled estimate derived from other signals. The more explicit the specification is about these equivalences and about the point in the processing chain at which the claim element may be met, the less likely a later court will read an accused implementation out of the claim because it uses a different representation.

- Link names to role and provenance. Names like “x1,” “DL(TX) reference,” and “modeled PIM path” are convenient in internal documents, but they do not substitute for an explicit explanation of which of them are originals, copies, references, or computed estimates. If a claim requires “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” the specification should show examples mapping real-world source signals to internal labels and should identify the direction of data flow and the temporal relationship among them.

The Federal Circuit’s critique of inconsistent expert testimony highlights that even with imperfect drafting, litigation success often depends on the ability to point to contemporaneous, consistent documentation that links claim language to real-world artifacts. That documentation begins with careful drafting.

## Enumerating operations (e.g., seven multiplications) and providing alternative formulations

The ’775 patent required “generating an n-th order [intermodulation] ICS by, given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering S1×S1×S2 and S1×S2×S2 …” and thus contemplated seven specific third-order products. The accused implementation’s documentation, however, set out three distinct algebraic forms (involving modulus and complex conjugation) or, in other vendor materials, as many as ten coefficients in a power series. The patentee’s expert failed to show how the documented three or ten computations mapped to the claimed seven multiplications. The Federal Circuit concluded there was no substantial evidence that the seven claimed multiplications were performed.

This teaches two drafting lessons about enumerated operations:

- When claims enumerate a required number of algebraic operations, the specification should give multiple ways to meet that limitation and explain when different syntactic forms are to be considered equivalent for claim purposes. If a claim recites seven multiplications, the specification should explain whether other algebraic representations (e.g., use of modulus |x|, complex conjugates x*, or linear combinations of products) are considered equivalent, and should give representative examples of mappings between mathematical expressions. Absent such explanatory material, a defendant can point to a mathematically different—but functionally similar—implementation and credibly argue the claim element is missing.

- Provide alternative claim formulations and dependent claims. If the invention’s inventive core is the generation of cancellation signals that correspond to particular polynomial terms of transmitted signals, consider multiple claim approaches in the same family: (a) a claim that enumerates particular algebraic products (for clarity and to stake out specific scope); (b) a claim reciting generation of ICSs “corresponding to” terms in a power series expansion (a functional phrasing that captures alternate mathematically equivalent implementations); and (c) a claim reciting a mathematical basis (e.g., “based on an n-th order power series description of a non-linear process”) coupled with structural steps for forming the basis. The presence of multiple claim styles gives both prosecution flexibility and fallback positions in litigation.

- Use plain-English explanatory text for algebraic equivalences. Engineers reading the patent should be able to see, for example, that x⋅|x|⋅|x| can be produced by multiplying x with two magnitude terms, and how that relates to x⋅x⋅x in a particular coordinate representation. If such equivalences require particular assumptions (e.g., real-valued vs complex-valued signals, presence of conjugate symmetry, or other transformations), set those assumptions out carefully.

Finally, be cautious about claiming a precise number of operations when the art commonly implements equivalent functionality using coefficient tables, lookup approaches, or transform-domain approximations. If the claim must specify a count for patentability reasons, include dependent or alternative claims that capture the broader ways practitioners might implement the asserted function.

## Prosecution responses that preserve breadth while distinguishing prior art

A second critical lesson concerns prosecution strategy. During examination the patentee amended claims to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3” and argued distinctions from prior art that used two signals. On appeal the Federal Circuit refused to treat that amendment as a clear and unambiguous disavowal of coverage over two-signal implementations—because the intrinsic record and the patentee’s arguments did not amount to unmistakable surrender. Nonetheless, the court relied on the lack of evidence mapping accused implementations to the seven multiplications and rejected the jury verdict.

From a prosecution standpoint, the ideal is to craft arguments and claim changes that distinguish the prior art while minimizing the risk of inadvertent disclaimer or unintended narrowing. That balance can be struck as follows:

- When amending, state clearly whether the amendment is intended to be narrowing and, if possible, explain why the narrower wording is necessary to overcome the cited art without stating that embodiments outside the narrowed scope are relinquished. If an amendment is genuinely necessary, record why the distinguishing feature is essential for patentability but also present alternative unamended claim sets (continuations) that preserve broader coverage.

- Avoid absolute language that can be read as an unequivocal surrender. For example, rather than saying the prior art “fails to disclose three signals,” an applicant might state: “Filipovic discloses generation of cancellation signals using two transmitter inputs; the present claim, by contrast, requires three separately-identifiable signal inputs or equivalent distinct components used to derive the claimed third-order products, which permits cancellation of passive IMPs occurring after the HPA—a feature that Filipovic’s active-IMP-focused method does not address.” The point is to draw a principled, technical distinction without asserting that the broader scope has been given up except insofar as necessary.

- Preserve fallback claim paths. During prosecution, maintain dependent claims and divisional/continuation filings that keep alternative claim formats alive—e.g., claims that recite “at least two signals” or claims that recite “three separately-identifiable signals but wherein one or more may be copies or processed versions of another.” Having those continuations gives options later if a court construes the amended claims narrowly.

- Use specification statements that anticipate prosecution positions. If the invention is intended to cover two-signal and three-signal variants (or the use of modeled versus original signals), include that clarity in the specification. Put examples in the specification showing how two originals plus a modeled copy can be used to produce third-order products that are equivalent to the explicit S1×S1×S2, etc. Those written examples can mitigate arguments of disavowal later.

- Use careful claim construction trap-avoidance. Because courts construe claims de novo but give deference to factual findings, ensure your prosecution statements are not ripe for being read as clear disavowal. Ambiguous, offhand remarks in prosecution history can be seized upon in litigation; maintain a consistent record.

The prosecution record is part of the intrinsic evidence. If you need to distinguish an examiner’s prior art by reference to a narrow, technical difference, do so in a way that aims to persuade without narratively cutting off broader coverage you might later want.

## Design-around opportunities: restructuring DSP pipelines, ordering of operations, and transforms

The factual core of the ’134 patent decision illustrates a straightforward design-around principle: if a claim requires that an accused device “sample a passband of received signals that includes signals of interest and interference generating signals,” then an implementer who ensures the alleged “interference generating signals” are created after the sampling point has a credible noninfringement argument. The accused radios’ documentation showed the modeled PIM path was generated in an FPGA downstream of the ADC, so the RF ADC did not sample that modeled path; that timing relationship was fatal to infringement as claimed.

Engineers can exploit such timing, ordering, and representation distinctions when designing systems to avoid the literal scope of claims. The patent-aware design choices include:

- Move computations out of the sampling path. If a claim requires the receiver to sample the “interference generating signals,” implementers can place PIM modeling or cancellation computations in a module that receives only post-sampled data or computed estimates, after the ADC, making it harder for a patentee to show the ADC sampled the claimed signals.

- Change representation and domain. Many claims are written to cover particular algebraic forms (products, polynomials). Implement a transform-domain approach (e.g., FFT-based correlation, adaptive filtering operating on spectral amplitudes rather than multiplicative time-domain products) that achieves the same end but does not perform the enumerated products literally. But beware: courts sometimes apply the doctrine of equivalents, so one should seek to design around both literal and equivalent coverage where feasible.

- Reorder operations. Some claims require multiplication of three signals prior to filtering or combination. If the system instead generates the requisite correction term by first combining signals in a different order (e.g., computing magnitudes, squared magnitudes, or linear combinations, and then applying a single multiplicative coefficient), the precise enumerated multiplication may be absent. Again, document the changed sequence carefully.

- Use approximate or parametric models. Rather than computing the exact set of algebraic cross-products, generate coefficients in a parametric model (e.g., identify coefficients in a polynomial approximation) and feed those coefficients into a filter. If the claim requires explicit per-term products, a parametric coefficient approach may avoid literal infringement.

Because patent doctrines also include equivalents and doctrine-specific fact patterns vary, design-around choices should be evaluated by patent counsel in light of the specification and claim scope. But the Finesse decision demonstrates that ordering and locus of operations—what is sampled, when—is a practical lever.

## Evidentiary preparedness: creating documentation that accurately reflects signal paths and timing

Finesse’s loss underscores how crucial contemporaneous, explicit documentation is to proving infringement when signal identity and timing are disputed. The Federal Circuit emphasized that the patentee’s expert testimony was inconsistent and that Finesse failed to provide a clear mapping from the accused product’s internal variables (x1, x2) to the claimed signals. In practice, litigation outcomes often turn on whether an expert can point to crisp, unambiguous documentary evidence.

To maximize the odds of surviving JMOL and reverse engineering defenses, patent owners and their patent prosecutors should ensure the following documentation practices during product development:

- Annotated signal flow diagrams. Maintain up-to-date diagrams that show signal sources, copies, sampling points, and the transformations applied at each stage. Each diagram should explicitly label where sampling occurs (ADC entry), where modeled signals are generated, and the relationships between raw inputs and processed outputs.

- Datasheets and interface specifications. Vendor materials often use terse labels; supplement them with internal documentation that explains what each label denotes, its provenance, and its timing relative to sampling (e.g., “DL(TX) reference: downlink transmit reference waveform present at the baseband input prior to sampling,” versus “modeled PIM path: estimated PIM constructed from sampled baseband data in FPGA stage GROOT after ADC”).

- Code, firmware comments, and commit logs. A software repository with clear comments showing variables (x1, x2) and the equations that generate derived signals is strong evidence. Give particular attention to commit messages and architecture documents that correlate variable names with the physical channel they represent.

- Test vectors and measurement logs. Empirical evidence that a particular signal exists at a given node at a given time—oscilloscope captures, logic analyzer traces, ADC sample dumps, annotated timestamps—can be proved in court and used to corroborate expert testimony.

- Formal verification and traceability matrices. Maintain traceability from requirements to implementation, mapping claim elements to code modules or hardware blocks, with dates. This reduces the risk that an expert will have to adduce after-the-fact explanatory testimony without documentary corroboration.

- Expert-ready demonstratives. Produce diagrams and annotated excerpts before litigation that directly map claim terms (e.g., “signals of interest,” “interference generating signals,” “S1, S2, S3”) to the documented artifacts. Consistency between the expert’s courtroom mapping and these demonstratives reduces cross-examination vulnerability.

In Finesse, the jury verdict depended on expert testimony that was later found contradictory. A robust documentary trail would have made it easier to present a consistent mapping and to resist cross-examination attacks on the reliability of that mapping.

## Risk management in venues with high patent dockets (e.g., E.D. Tex.)

Finally, Finesse was tried in the Eastern District of Texas, a venue long associated with patent litigation. The Federal Circuit’s decision to reverse JMOL notwithstanding demonstrates that venue selection does not insulate a patentee from appellate reversal, and that careful preparation is required irrespective of forum.

From a risk-management perspective, patentees and defendants should weigh the following practical considerations:

- Early claim construction and Markman preparation. Because claim interpretation can be outcome-determinative, litigants should prioritize early, comprehensive claim construction briefing and the development of a record on how claim terms map to the accused product. The Federal Circuit reviews claim construction de novo (with deference to subsidiary factual findings), so a well-developed Markman record helps preserve positions on appeal.

- Anticipate expert cross-examination and interrogatory discovery. In high-volume patent venues, juries are common. Prepare expert witnesses with a consistent, documented mapping between claim elements and product artifacts. Counsel should run mock cross-examination scenarios that test whether experts can sustain their mappings under pressure.

- Consider early dispositive motions where risk is high. If there is a plain mismatch—e.g., the claimed “signals” were not present at the required sampling point, or the claimed enumerated computations are absent—seek summary judgment or early JMOL on those discrete issues. Finesse shows that even strong jury sympathy cannot save a case that lacks substantial evidentiary support for essential elements.

- Portfolio and damages strategy. The Finesse damages award was vacated only after the Federal Circuit reversed on infringement. Where high-value damages are sought, ensure your infringement case is particularly robust on the technical linkage between claims and product; otherwise, a defendant can focus appellate attention on discrete technical failures that may undermine the entire award.

- Preserve alternatives for global enforcement. Maintain continuations and international filings that provide flexibility for enforcement if particular claims are vulnerable in one jurisdiction. The litigation environment changes over time; keeping alternative claim sets available is valuable.

These considerations do not substitute for substantive drafting and engineering changes but reflect how litigation planning should incorporate technical minutiae.

## Concluding checklist for practitioners and engineers

The Finesse decision offers a compact set of actionable takeaways:

- For drafters: define “signal” with attention to time, location, and representation; include examples of equivalent mathematical forms and where they are present in the processing chain; include multiple claim styles (structural, functional, numerical).

- For prosecutors: when amending claims to overcome art, avoid unequivocal statements of disavowal; preserve continuations and dependent claims; explicate why differences matter technically without conceding broader scope.

- For product designers: know the claim elements and, where possible, arrange ordering/timing/representation of operations to avoid meeting the literal claim elements; document why design choices differ—do not rely solely on obsolescent or informal internal labels.

- For litigators: develop a documentary chain mapping claim elements to product artifacts; prepare expert testimony that is consistent with contemporaneous documentation; consider early dispositive motions on clear technical mismatches.

Finesse Wireless is a cautionary tale: ambiguous signal identifications, enumerated mathematical operations, and inconsistent expert mappings are not merely theoretical risks. They are concrete vulnerabilities that can undo a favorable jury outcome on appeal. For patent drafters, prosecutors, engineers, and litigators, the lesson is to make the technical record unequivocal long before the courtroom.

# Notes, Questions, and Problems

This chapter gives exercises and hypotheticals to rehearse the doctrinal lessons from Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC and to practice translating those lessons into litigation strategy when the claims at issue are mathematical or signal-processing methods. The Federal Circuit’s decision highlights three recurring themes in patent trials over algorithmic and signal-processing patents: (1) the need for a clear, consistent element‑by‑element mapping from claim language to accused device operations; (2) the limits of what can be counted as a “signal” or a “copy” of a signal for claim purposes; and (3) the evidentiary consequences when an expert’s testimony is internally inconsistent. The problems below ask you to apply those themes, suggest trial tactics, and provide concrete drafting and cross‑examination tools that would reduce the risk of reversal on JMOL.

## Problem: Can |x2| or x2′ count as S3 where the claim recites "three signals"? Explain your mapping and limits.

The ’775 claim language requires “three signals S1, S2 and S3” and further recites generating third‑order ICS terms such as S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, and S1×S2×S3, etc. The district court construed “three signals” to mean “separately identifiable” signals, not necessarily “unique.” The accused system, however, exposes a practical question: can mathematical transforms of a base signal—for example, the modulus |x2| or the complex conjugate x2′—be treated as distinct signals S3 for purposes of satisfying the claim?

Analytic approach. Begin with claim construction and intrinsic evidence. The starting point is the claim text: does the claim require “copies of transmitter signals” or simply “signals” generically? Here the ’775 patent repeatedly discusses generating ICSs “using copies of transmitter signals,” which suggests the claimed inputs should be signals that correspond in a straightforward way to transmitter outputs. That phrase will be important: a “copy” normally means a representation that preserves the essential information content of the original signal, not necessarily an arbitrary mathematical transform. Next examine the specification and prosecution history for any limiting statements addressing whether transforms like modulus or conjugation are contemplated.

Arguments supporting counting |x2| or x2′ as S3. One can argue that |x2| and x2′ are “signals” in any ordinary sense: they are time‑series functions (complex or real) derived deterministically from x2 and carried through the system as data. If the district court accepts the “separately identifiable” construction, then a modulus or conjugate is a separately identifiable stream (it has its own storage or register, and the system operates on that stream independently). From a functional perspective, the claim language identifying S1, S2, S3 does not explicitly preclude derived versions of the same underlying physical waveform. If the specification or prosecution history used language like “S1, S2, S3 represent signals generally” or an inventor testified transforms are contemplated, that supports counting |x2| or x2′ as separate signals.

Arguments against counting |x2| or x2′ as S3. On the other hand, if the claim or intrinsic record emphasizes “using copies of transmitter signals” then a transform that discards phase or otherwise alters the physical relation to the original may not be a “copy” in the sense meant by the patentee. The modulus |x2| loses the phase information of x2; the conjugate x2′ changes the phase sign. If the claimed invention is premised on multiplicative combinations of the original transmitter waveforms, equating those combinations with combinations that involve modulus or conjugates may change the physics of the cancellation mechanism. Further, if the prosecution history shows that the applicant added “three signals” to distinguish over prior art that used two real baseband signals, an argument can be made that the patentee intended three distinct physical or informational inputs, not repeated or transformed versions of only two underlying signals.

How to present the mapping in court. If you are the patentee, make the mapping explicit in the expert report and in testimony. Show (1) where in the accused system the stream |x2| or x2′ is produced and stored, (2) how that stream is functionally used as an input to the multiplicative operations, and (3) how the system treats |x2| or x2′ as distinct (e.g., separate buffer, different timing, different application). Tie this to the claim: explain why an operation that uses x1, x1, and |x2| corresponds to S1×S1×S3. If you are the accused infringer, emphasize that “separately identifiable” does not mean “separately originating” and show that every candidate S3 is merely an algebraic manipulation of x2 and not a distinct transmitter copy contemplated by the patent.

Limits and risk. Even where the court’s construction permits “separately identifiable” signals, mapping transforms to separate S‑labels carries risk. The mapping must be documented and explained repeatedly and consistently; a late or improvised mapping (for example, only developed at trial or on appeal) will be unpersuasive and can be characterized as litigation‑driven. Moreover, contrived mappings that require a single physical signal to play simultaneously the role of S2, S3, and S2’s conjugate invite the very kind of skepticism the Federal Circuit expressed when experts flip between inconsistent descriptions.

Practical conclusion. Whether |x2| or x2′ can count as S3 depends on claim construction and the quality of the evidentiary record. If the patent and prosecution history do not clearly exclude transforms, and the patentee provides corroborating system documentation and a consistent expert mapping showing the derived stream is treated as a distinct input in the accused device, a court may accept the mapping. Absent that, and especially where the claim or intrinsic record emphasizes “copies of transmitter signals,” the safer litigation posture for a patentee is to identify true, independently generated transmitter‑derived streams (or show the transform preserves the “copy” character) rather than rely on strained algebraic identifications.

## Problem: An accused radio computes ten third‑order terms. How would you prove which seven satisfy the claim?

This problem arises directly from the ’775 analysis, where Nokia documentation listed multiple multiplicative terms (ten, perhaps), and the jury was left without a clear explanation of which subset of those terms corresponded to the seven third‑order multiplications claimed. Proving the required seven is an exercise in precise, documentary, and experimental evidence.

Step 1 — identify the claimed algebraic pattern. Begin by writing out the claim’s required third‑order products explicitly: S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, S1×S1×S3, S2×S2×S3, S1×S3×S3, S2×S3×S3. This is the baseline. Make a canonical representation so that you can map the accused device’s terms to these canonical products.

Step 2 — map candidate device terms. Next, collect every source that documents the accused radio’s third‑order computations: design documents, firmware or DSP source code, internal logs, DSP flow diagrams, comment blocks, mathematical expressions in white papers, and testbench outputs. For each documented term (for example, x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2′), prepare a table (for internal use) that shows the term, its algebraic form, and whether it can be rewritten to match a claimed product under reasonable algebraic identities or under the court’s claim construction (e.g., is |x1| equivalent to a separately labeled S1?).

Step 3 — demand operational evidence, not just labels. A label in a document is not enough. You must show the device actually computes the term in an operational sense and that the term is used to generate ICSs that are applied in the cancellation path. Good evidence here includes: code execution traces that show the operation performed at run time; synthesized hardware netlists or FPGA bitstreams indicating the multiplier trees and how many distinct multiplications are instantiated; runtime counters that show how many product outputs are produced per frame; and captured intermediate numeric vectors showing the products computed. When the accused system uses transforms like modulus or conjugation, the expert needs to explain how these map to the claimed products and whether substituting a conjugate for an S‑term preserves the intended cancellation effect.

Step 4 — show multiplicity and repetition. The claim requires seven multiplications. If the device’s documentation lists ten potential terms, you must (a) select which seven correspond to the claim’s set and (b) show that those seven are indeed produced and used. Demonstrate either that exactly those seven are computed (by tracing code or FPGA resources), or if more are computed, show that at least the claimed seven are among them and are selected/used for ICS formation. Where multiplications are produced by reusing a computed result rather than recomputing separate multipliers, demonstrate that the resulting outputs are identical to the claimed products and are used at the relevant points in the cancellation algorithm.

Step 5 — provide incontestable examples. Produce a worked numeric example where the accused radio’s inputs are known and the outputs of the multiplication stage are recorded. Show a concrete three‑sample period: list x1[n], x2[n], and x3[n] (or their derived forms), compute the accused device’s internal products as the device would, and show that the numerics equal the claimed products. A test where the device is forced to run with known inputs (test mode) and where the internal vectors can be logged is the most persuasive.

Step 6 — anticipate and neutralize alternative interpretations. Opposing counsel will likely point to alternative decompositions of the device’s ten terms that exclude some claimed multiplications or show that certain listed terms are mathematical intermediates never used in cancellation. Preempt by: (1) producing code comments or commit history showing the author intended those terms for cancellation; (2) showing signal paths where those outputs are fed forward; and (3) explaining why transforms (modulus/conjugate) do or do not defeat literal matching.

Trial presentation. Present the mapping in the expert report in a clear, consistent manner: numbered claim element, mapped device component, documentary exhibit citation, and demonstration artifact (log or trace). Use demonstratives to show the numeric example side‑by‑side with the claim product. Avoid equivocation or multiple inconsistent mappings; the Federal Circuit reversed on the basis that the expert’s testimony was confusing and internally inconsistent.

Standard of proof and litigation timing. Remember to meet the patentee’s burden to prove infringement by a preponderance of the evidence. Establish the mapping at summary judgment or at the Rule 50(a) stage if possible; otherwise, the record at trial must be coherent enough to survive JMOL review. If you are the accused infringer, focus your Rule 50(a) motion on pointing out gaps: absence of runtime evidence, lack of mapping between the device’s terms and the claim’s seven listed products, or logical impossibility (e.g., device only generates three distinct products).

## Problem: The ADC samples x1 and x2; a PIM model is computed later. Can plaintiff still meet ’134 claim 1(a)?

Claim 1(a) of the ’134 patent requires “over‑sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals.” The litigation posture in Finesse shows that where the accused radio’s modeled PIM path is generated after the ADC, the plaintiff’s expert mapped the “interference generating signals” to that modeled path, which created an obvious temporal problem: a claim that requires the receiver to sample signals that include the interference‑generating signals cannot be met if those interference‑generating signals do not exist until after sampling.

Legal and factual axes to consider. Two distinct questions must be addressed: (1) what do the claims mean by “interference generating signals” and (2) at what point must those signals exist for the sampling limitation to be met? The words “received signals” and “to create a bit stream” suggest that the receiver must sample actual analog signals present at the RF front end. If the “interference generating signals” are artifacts produced later in a digital model, they arguably are not “received” in the sense the claim requires. The intrinsic record and prosecution history can refine this: if the patent contemplates modeling the interference using copies of transmitted signals or using internal digital estimates derived from sampled signals, then a later‑computed model might nevertheless be sufficiently tied to sampled phenomena to satisfy 1(a). But the typical reading is that 1(a) looks to sampling of signals present on the analog passband.

How a plaintiff can succeed. To meet 1(a) despite the PIM model being computed later, the patentee should do one of the following:

- Show that the ADC actually samples analog evidence of the interference‑generating phenomenon. For external PIM, the IMPs originate from passive defects that cause spurious in‑band energy; if the RF ADC receives those spurious analog components, they will be present in the sampled bit stream. Produce spectrum captures, pre‑ADC analog traces, or bitstream captures that show the in‑band IMP energy is present in the samples.

- Show that one of the “signals of interest” or transmitter references is itself the “interference generating signal.” For example, if the transmitter leakage or downlink reference is sampled (or its effect is), then those sampled streams can count as the claimed “interference generating signals” even if the explicit PIM model is computed later.

- Demonstrate that the model is not an after‑the‑fact fabrication but is instead an algorithmic transformation that the receiver computes deterministically from signals it sampled. If the PIM model is computed by applying an invertible deterministic transform to an already‑sampled signal, then one can argue that the sampling limitation is satisfied because the underlying physical signal was sampled and the derivative model is simply a post‑processing representation.

- Produce device documentation showing that the modeled PIM path is a notional label for pre‑existing signals routed internally before the claimed sampling stage, i.e., show that what appears at trial as a “modeled PIM path” is in fact rooted in signals that exist on the red path before the ADC.

How a defendant should attack. If you are defending, emphasize the temporal sequence: sampling occurs first; the purported “interference generating signals” are output of a model produced later; signals cannot be sampled before they exist. Cross‑examine the plaintiff’s expert to force a clear mapping and to lock down timing. If the expert pivots mid‑testimony (as Dr. Wells did), use that to argue the evidence is insufficient as a matter of law.

Doctrinal takeaway. The patentee can only satisfy a sampling limitation that is temporally bounded at the analog, pre‑ADC stage by proving the claimed types of signals are present and sampled at that stage. Post‑processing models will not normally retroactively satisfy a limitation that requires sampling unless the model is shown to be a representation of signals actually present and sampled. The lesson of Finesse is practical: make the temporal mapping explicit in the record and be wary of relying on after‑the‑fact conceptual “paths” unless you can show the physical sampling included the relevant energy.

## Problem: Draft cross‑examination questions to test whether seven distinct multiplications are performed

The cross‑examination should aim to (1) show lack of proof that the claimed seven distinct products are produced, (2) expose any inconsistency between the expert’s report and trial testimony, and (3) force the expert to concede that core record documents do not show the seven multiplications. Below are sample questions organized to move from foundational facts to impeachment. Tailor each to the specific facts and exhibits.

Foundational identity and source
- Doctor, you agree that your infringement opinion is based principally on Nokia documents A, B, and C; correct?
- Exhibit B lists a set of multiplicative terms the implementation nominally uses; do you see that?
- The terms in Exhibit B are labeled as complex conjugates and moduli for certain variables; correct?

Mapping and algebraic equivalence
- You say that x1·|x1|·|x1| corresponds to the claim term S1×S1×S1; is that right?
- Do you agree that |x2| (the modulus) removes phase information that is present in x2?
- Do you agree that x2′ (the complex conjugate) is not algebraically identical to x2 unless x2 is real-valued?
- Which of the claim’s listed products require phase information from S3 to produce the asserted cancellation effect?

Counting and multiplicity
- Exhibit B lists ten possible product terms; is that your Exhibit 3?
- Of those ten, which seven do you contend correspond to the claim’s seven required multiplications? (Pause for an answer.)
- Can you point me to a single line of code, a single runtime trace, or a single DSP register dump where each of those seven outputs is computed and assigned to a distinct internal signal name?
- Is there any document in the product design that explicitly labels the outputs as S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, and so on—the seven claim products? If so, please point to it.

Usage and relevance
- Even if the device computes those ten intermediate terms, can you identify where in the cancellation path the claimed seven are selected and applied to cancellation? Point to the evidence.
- Is it possible that some of the ten terms are merely intermediate calculations that are subsequently discarded or combined into a fewer number of outputs?
- Do you know whether the hardware instantiates seven distinct multiplier units or whether it reuses multiplier hardware to compute multiple products by time‑multiplexing? Show me the evidence.

Prior testimony and consistency
- At your deposition you testified [cite page] that the modeled PIM path is generated in an FPGA downstream of the ADC; correct?
- Today you testified that the modeled PIM path is one of the “signals” sampled by the ADC; is that a fair read?
- Which of those two statements is correct? (If the witness hedges:) Isn’t it true that both statements cannot be simultaneously correct?
- You have not produced a testbench trace in which the numeric values of the seven claimed products are captured side‑by‑side; is that correct?

Hypothetical forcing questions
- Let us suppose the system uses only x1 and x2 as inputs and computes only three algebraically distinct products. Would that satisfy the literal claim requirement for seven distinct third‑order multiplications? (Goal: get witness to concede “no.”)
- If the system uses modulus and conjugates to generate ten variants, but none of those variants are labeled or used in a way that corresponds to the claim’s specific product set, would you still contend literal infringement? (Goal: make witness commit to necessity of mapping evidence.)

Impeachment with documentary contradiction
- Earlier you cited Document D as showing “x1·x1·x2′” is computed; Document D, however, shows simultaneous storage of only three product buffers. How do you reconcile that?
- You earlier told the court that x1 and x2 are the only signals sampled by the ADC; yet today you say the modeled PIM path is sampled. Which is true? (Use the inconsistency to undermine credibility.)

Effective cross‑examination will be crisp: demand specific document and line citations; request to mark precise code or netlist lines; and force the expert to admit the absence of runtime evidence when it does not exist. An admission that the expert lacks a single runtime trace for each claimed product is powerful JMOL fodder.

## Short answer: What record showing would avoid the "self‑contradictory expert" problem?

Conciseness matters here. To avoid the reversal seen in Finesse, a patentee should build an early, coherent, and corroborated record that ties each claim element to specific, contemporaneous artifacts. The minimal elements of that record are:

- A clear, element‑by‑element mapping in the expert report that cites specific exhibits (design docs, source code, FPGA bitstream, block diagrams) next to each claim limitation.

- Demonstrable runtime evidence: logs, register dumps, DSP vector captures, testbench traces, or oscilloscope captures that show the claimed signals exist and that the claimed computations are performed with numeric examples.

- Code or hardware documentation showing the exact arithmetic operations performed (for example, the multiplier tree or the portion of firmware that computes the products), with line numbers or netlist references.

- Demonstrative examples and small numeric test vectors in which the inputs are known, the intermediate products are recorded, and the final cancellation output is shown to use those products.

- Consistent testimony: the expert’s deposition, report, and live testimony should use the same mapping and should explain any necessary assumptions up front; if the expert must change a theory, the change should be disclosed and explained before or at summary judgment, not first raised at trial.

- Corroboration by company witnesses or authors of the documents, where possible, to show that the named document truly reflects the implementation rather than a high‑level conceptualization.

In short, avoid the prospect of an expert improvising at trial. The stronger and more contemporaneous the documentary and experimental evidence that map claim language to accused operations, the less likely the testimony will be characterized as self‑contradictory and thus insufficient as a matter of law.

## Checklist: Building an element‑by‑element mapping for math‑heavy method claims

Below is a practical checklist for counsel and experts preparing infringement proof for mathematical or signal‑processing method claims. Use it as a template when preparing reports, demonstrations, and deposition testimony.

1. Claim parsing
   - Transcribe each asserted claim and number each limitation.
   - Highlight temporally bounded actions (e.g., “over‑sampling … to create a bit stream”) and quantifiers (e.g., “three signals,” “seven multiplications”).

2. Claim construction liaison
   - Record the court’s claim construction for disputed terms.
   - Note whether constructions require “separately identifiable,” “unique,” “copies of transmitter signals,” or other qualifiers.

3. Identify candidate device artifacts for each limitation
   - For each claim limitation, list the device component(s) that could perform the limitation (hardware block, firmware function, FPGA module, software routine).
   - Cite the specific exhibit(s) (document name, version, page/line numbers, code lines, netlist label).

4. Temporal flow and causation
   - Produce a timeline diagram showing when each signal exists relative to sampling, filtering, and modeling.
   - Explicitly tie “receive,” “sample,” “compute,” and “apply” events to device clock cycles or frame indices.

5. Algebraic mapping
   - Write canonical algebraic forms for the claimed operations and for the accused system’s operations.
   - If transforms are present (conjugate, modulus), include algebraic identities and explain whether and how they permit mapping (and whether the mapping preserves required properties like phase).

6. Runtime corroboration
   - For every claimed computation, acquire runtime traces, logs, or bench captures showing the computation occurring.
   - If hardware reuses multipliers by time‑multiplexing, capture the scheduling and buffer names to show output identity.

7. Numerical example
   - Create one or more concrete numeric examples: pick specific input sample values, run the device (or a faithful model), and capture intermediate and final outputs.
   - Present the example in the expert report as an appendix.

8. Source and code evidence
   - Produce the minimal code or RTL snippet where the multiplication is implemented; annotate line numbers.
   - If code is obfuscated or optimized, obtain a developer declaration or a corroborating design note.

9. Usage evidence
   - Show that the outputs of the claimed computations are actually used to generate ICSs or other claimed outputs (not merely computed and discarded).
   - Trace the signal path from multiplication output to applicator (e.g., adder, cancellation injection point).

10. Address prosecution history and claim scope
    - Identify any amendments or statements arguably narrowing claim scope.
    - Prepare counterarguments or evidence showing no clear and unmistakable disavowal.

11. Consistency and disclosure
    - Ensure the expert report, deposition testimony, and trial testimony use identical mappings. If you must refine a mapping, supplement disclosures and explain why the refinement is based on new evidence (cite the new exhibits).

12. Anticipate attack
    - Prepare responses to likely defense points: lack of runtime evidence, use of transforms instead of raw signals, reuse of multipliers, and difference between “listed” vs. “used” terms.

13. Litigation readiness
    - Be ready to produce demonstrative animations showing data flow step‑by‑step.
    - Prepare for cross‑examination: have precise citations ready and know where your evidence is weakest.

Cross‑check this checklist with the GM of the case: if you cannot produce a runtime trace for a critical mapping by the close of fact discovery, consider whether the claim should be asserted or whether to seek technical stipulations.

---

These problems and solutions should help you practice the application of Finesse’s principles: clear mapping, temporal fidelity for sampling claims, and the necessity of contemporaneous documentary and runtime evidence when mathematical operations and signal transforms are in issue. In preparing for trial or summary judgment, treat mapping as a technical‑and‑litigation project equally: the courthouse will want concrete artifacts, not after‑the‑fact labels.
