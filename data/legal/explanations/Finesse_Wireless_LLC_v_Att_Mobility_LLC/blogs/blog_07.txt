Title: Blog about the opinion: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC

# Customer‑Suit Dynamics in the 5G Era: OEM Intervention, Evidence Control, and Strategic Risk

## Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in Finesse Wireless v. AT&T/Nokia is not just another routine patent reversal. At base it is a vivid reminder that as networks become more complex and OEMs increasingly embed sophisticated signal‑processing features in radios, the interplay among carrier defendants, equipment manufacturers, and the evidentiary record can decisively shape infringement outcomes. The court reversed the denial of JMOL on infringement for asserted claims of two patents directed to PIM (passive intermodulation) cancellation and vacated the damages award. The reversals were driven by the court’s view that the plaintiff’s infringement case rested on confused or insufficient expert testimony tied to OEM technical documents — documents that can be simultaneously indispensable and dangerous.

Practitioners in telecom litigation should take note: OEM technical materials and the expert narratives built around them can make or break a case. How parties handle OEM intervention, coordinate experts across defendants, and curate technical exhibits for trial increasingly determines both liability and damages exposure.

## What the court did — the analytical core

Two issues drove the Federal Circuit’s analysis.

First, for the ’134 patent, claim 1 required that the accused receiver “over-sample[] . . . a passband of received signals” that includes both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Finesse’s infringement theory leaned on a Nokia diagram and its expert testimony mapping signals on that diagram to the claimed terms. But the expert’s testimony was internally inconsistent and, crucially, the diagram’s “modeled PIM path” appeared to be generated after the radio’s ADC sampling. If the “interference generating signals” are only generated after sampling, they are not “received” signals within the meaning of the claim. The Federal Circuit concluded that Finesse’s expert testimony was too equivocal and self‑contradictory to support the jury verdict — when the party bearing the burden of proof rests its case on such testimony, JMOL is appropriate.

Second, for the ’775 patent, the claim required generating seven distinct third‑order products from three separately identifiable signals (S1, S2, S3). The court accepted the district court’s construction that “separately identifiable” does not necessarily mean “unique,” but it nevertheless found that the Nokia documents and the plaintiff’s expert only showed three distinct multiplications; there was no evidentiary bridge showing the seven claimed multiplications were actually produced. The Federal Circuit therefore held the evidence was insufficient as a matter of law.

In short: (1) ambiguous expert testimony tied to ambiguous or time‑sequenced elements of OEM diagrams is legally dangerous; and (2) pointing to block‑diagram labels, modulus/complex‑conjugate operations, or lists of coefficients in OEM materials without a clear mapping to claimed mathematical operations will often fall short.

## Tactical lessons on OEM intervention and evidence control

This case surfaces a cluster of strategic issues that litigants in telecom and software‑defined hardware disputes should confront.

First, OEMs are likely to intervene when a carrier is sued over equipment features. Intervention gives the OEM the ability to control technical records, provide declarants, and present its own expert to rebut or refine the carrier’s case. That can help defendants — but it also introduces coordination challenges. Carrier counsel and OEM counsel must align on defensive themes (e.g., noninfringement mappings, sequence of operations relative to sampling) and on how to present technical diagrams without overreaching interpretive inferences that the plaintiff can attack at trial.

Second, technical documents are double‑edged. Diagrams and legends are indispensable to explain complex signal paths. But as the Federal Circuit shows, they can be fatal when an expert leans on a legend or block label that, upon closer inspection, does not support a literal claim element (e.g., a signal “modeled” post‑ADC cannot be one of the “received” signals sampled by the ADC). Practitioners should therefore vet OEM materials early for any sequence or causation issues that a plaintiff can exploit. If an OEM device produces intermediate, modeled, or calculated signals downstream of the alleged point of sampling, that temporal sequence must be anticipated and integrated into the defendants’ expert narrative.

Third, the coordination of expert testimony matters. The court faulted the plaintiff for relying on an expert whose testimony appeared contradictory — at different points mapping labels to different signals and not offering a clear, consistent mapping to claimed elements. Defendants should strive for crisp, reproducible expert explanations that connect operation diagrams to claim constructions with precision: identify where signals exist in time, where they are measured or sampled, and show (if true) how mathematical operations in code/algorithms correspond to claimed multiplications or filtering steps. Avoid relying on inferences pieced together from testimony “ninety pages apart,” as the court expressly discounted that approach.

Fourth, consider the evidentiary chain for mathematical claims. The Federal Circuit did not accept an appeal‑time mapping from three listed operations to the seven claimed products. If claims require a specific number or pattern of algebraic operations, defendants and plaintiffs alike need to present concrete traceable derivations: if an OEM document lists coefficients or ten possible multiplier terms, explain to the jury which subset maps to the claim and why any remaining terms are not cumulative or repeated versions of the claimed operations.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Doctrinally, the decision underscores two recurring appellate themes. One is the court’s willingness to reverse juries when the patentee fails to carry its burden with reliable, consistent expert testimony that ties the accused product to claim language. That reinforces Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope and related authorities: expert ambiguity can be fatal where the patentee has the burden. Second, the opinion illustrates the limits of relying on extrinsic OEM materials without a rigorous evidentiary bridge to the claims. Block diagrams, legends, and internal technical notes are not magic: labels alone do not substitute for proof that the device performs the claimed steps at the claimed times.

For defendants, the decision will encourage aggressive early focus on the temporal sequencing of signals (what exists before and after ADC sampling, what is modeled downstream) and on identifying gaps in a patentee’s mapping from technical exhibits to claim elements. For plaintiffs, the lesson is blunt: do not rest on convenient readings of legends or inconsistent expert testimony — be prepared to show an unambiguous, step‑by‑step mapping from OEM documentation (and from code/firmware where available) to each claim limitation.

## Practical takeaways

- When an OEM intervenes, use that opportunity to introduce declarants and engineering witnesses who can explain sequence and causation around signal generation and sampling.
- Coordinate expert testimony tightly: produce demonstratives that map specific signals and operations to claim limitations with a temporal narrative (before sampling/after sampling).
- Audit OEM documents for downstream “modeled” or simulated signals that could be mischaracterized as “received” or “sampled” inputs; prepare fallback theories supported by firmware, logs, or unit tests.
- For claims requiring specific arithmetic operations or counts (e.g., “seven multiplications”), build a clear evidentiary path — do not rely on labels, lists of coefficients, or post hoc mappings.

Finesse Wireless v. AT&T/Nokia is a cautionary tale for an era where radio functionality is implemented in firmware, FPGAs, and DSP chains. As devices get smarter and signal chains more abstract, litigation will hinge less on broad characterizations and more on fine‑grained proof about when a given signal exists, where it is sampled, and what arithmetic operations actually occur. The lawyers who appreciate that complexity and translate it into tightly controlled evidence and expert narratives will fare best.



## From PIM to JMOL: Proving Interference Cancellation in Complex Radios

Why this case matters
Finesse Wireless v. AT&T (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025) is a useful reminder that technical sophistication cannot substitute for clear, claim‑by‑claim proof at trial. The Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for two patents directed to passive intermodulation (PIM) cancellation in radios because the patentee’s infringement case rested on confusing expert testimony and inadequate mappings between the claimed elements and the accused products. For practitioners who litigate patents in fields that intermingle hardware, firmware, and digital signal processing (DSP), the decision crystallizes a practical rule: when you sue over signal‑processing algorithms and mixed analog/digital systems, precision in the record — not rhetorical flourish — is what carries the jury’s verdict through appellate review.

What the court did and why
The appeals turned on two separate claim packages: the ’134 patent, which claims a method that begins with sampling a “passband of received signals” containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” and the ’775 patent, which claims generating intermodulation cancellation signals by computing seven specific third‑order multiplications of three signals S1, S2, and S3. The Federal Circuit reviewed whether substantial evidence supported the jury’s finding of infringement and whether the denial of JMOL was proper under Fifth Circuit standards.

On the ’134 patent, the patentee’s expert repeatedly pointed to a Nokia technical slide in which a red path in the diagram was labeled “DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path,” and testified that the receiver’s RF ADC samples both the signal of interest and the interference‑generating signal. But cross‑examination showed the modeled PIM path in the document is generated downstream of the ADC — in the FPGA — and thus could not have been sampled by the ADC. The expert’s testimony wavered: at times he appeared to equate the two red‑path boxes with two inputs x1 and x2, but he never clearly mapped x1 and x2 to the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” The court held that this contradictory and confusing testimony could not support the jury’s verdict; where the patentee bears the burden of proof, an expert’s self‑contradiction can doom the case.

On the ’775 patent, claim scope required that seven particular third‑order multiplications be produced from three separately identifiable signals. The district court had construed “three signals” as separately identifiable but not necessarily unique. The patentee’s expert relied on Nokia materials that showed three distinct multiplications (involving complex conjugates and moduli) and argued — without a clear, trial‑record mapping — that those operations satisfied the seven claimed multiplications. The Federal Circuit held that the evidence showed only three distinct operations and no support that the seven claimed multiplications were actually produced; the patentee’s post‑hoc mappings were not presented to the jury and could not be supplied on appeal.

Analytical perspective: why precision mattered here
Finesse underlines two related evidentiary truths in DSP‑heavy patent litigation. First, when a claim turns on where in a signal chain a particular signal exists — pre‑ADC versus post‑ADC, inside an FPGA versus on an analog path, internal versus external PIM sources — the plaintiff must put on unambiguous evidence that ties the claimed signal to the accused device at the right physical or logical point. A diagram with a conflated legend (e.g., “A & B”) plus equivocal expert testimony is a brittle foundation once the defendant shows that a labeled element is actually produced downstream of the claimed sampling action.

Second, when claims recite algorithmic specifics (here, seven distinct third‑order multiplications of three signals), the patentee must walk the jury through each claimed computational step with demonstrative clarity. It is not enough to show the accused product performs “something like” the claimed math; the jury record must identify which signals correspond to S1, S2, S3, which multiplications are computed, and whether any listed operation is repeated or transformed (e.g., via modulus or conjugation) in a way that preserves the claimed structure. The Federal Circuit’s approach is not hostile to jury fact‑finding, but it requires that the technical mapping be presented coherently so the court can find substantial evidence on appeal.

Doctrinal implications
Finesse sits at the intersection of a few well‑established doctrines. The decision applies the familiar substantial‑evidence standard for jury findings of infringement, but it also reminds litigants of the Johns Hopkins line of cases in which inconsistent expert testimony can be fatal when the patentee bears the burden of proof. The court did not announce new claim‑construction rules or attempt to tighten inventorship or prosecution‑history estoppel doctrines; rather, it enforced the practical consequence that amorphous expert narrative cannot fill gaps in the evidentiary record.

A secondary doctrinal point concerns claim amendments during prosecution. The Federal Circuit declined to find clear and unambiguous disclaimer where the patentee amended claims to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as opposed to “two or three signals,” finding the prosecution history did not amount to surrender of coverage over two signals. But that was not enough to save the verdict because the patentee still failed to prove that the accused radios in fact produced the seven specific multiplications required by the claims. So the takeaways are twofold: claim amendments can be decisive if prosecution history clearly disavows scope; and even absent clear disavowal, the patentee still must present concrete trial proof that the accused device practices the claimed elements.

Practical downstream effects for litigators and experts
Finesse should prompt plaintiffs and their experts to make deliberate evidentiary choices in complex electronics cases. Two practical consequences stand out.

First, demonstratives and device‑level evidence must tie the claim language to the device’s real signal flow. Diagrams that conflate labels or rely on high‑level legends are dangerous unless the expert can point to hardware traces, software blocks, logs, firmware code, or oscilloscope captures that show where each claimed signal exists and when relative to sampling and processing events. If a claimed element depends on whether something exists pre‑ or post‑ADC, get the oscilloscope screenshots, block‑diagram exports, and firmware flow that show the timing and locus of those signals.

Second, for algorithmic claims, have a trial‑ready mapping that explains each claimed arithmetic operation. This means preparing exhibits that break the accused algorithm into discrete steps, show the actual data representations used (complex samples, conjugates, magnitudes), and demonstrate how each of the claimed multiplications is realized (including any repetition or permutation). If your theory relies on interpreting conjugates or magnitudes as satisfying multiplicative terms in the claim language, build before trial an admissible chain of evidence (expert linear algebraic explanation plus device evidence) showing that those transformations are functionally equivalent to the claim’s multiplications, and present that mapping to the jury rather than saving it for appellate briefing.

A practitioner’s checklist, derived from Finesse
Prove signal locus relative to sampling: If the claim requires sampling a passband that contains particular signals, show through device artifacts and timing evidence that those signals exist at the sampling point. Don’t rely on a slide legend or on downstream model‑signals; instead, demonstrate the physical or logical presence of the claimed “interference generating signals” at the ADC or other sampling block with data captures, firmware traces, or authenticated design documentation that the expert can point to confidently on direct examination.

Demonstrate closed‑loop adjustments concretely: For claims that require closed‑loop phase and amplitude adjustment (especially when the claim calls for sub‑sample phase shifts), put on cumulative evidence of the control loop: the sensing path, the feedback path, the digital implementation of phase‑shifting (e.g., fractional‑delay filters or interpolation used to effect sub‑sample shifts), and logs or test vectors showing the loop operating. Explain how the claimed sub‑sample shifts are implemented in the accused system (what algorithm, what interpolation order), and show any timing or convergence data that supports the “closed‑loop” characterization.

Map each algorithmic operation to an artifact: For claims that enumerate specific multiplications or other arithmetic operations, produce a trial exhibit that lists the claimed operations and shows, one by one, where each operation is performed in the accused device. If the accused product computes only a related set of operations (conjugates, moduli, repeated terms), show by admissible analysis whether and how those operations correspond to the claimed terms, and make that correspondence a part of the expert’s direct testimony. Don’t leave such mappings to appellate argument.

Avoid internally inconsistent expert narratives: Prepare experts to testify with a single, coherent mapping throughout direct and cross. If an expert must change a theory, refresh the record on the new mapping and make sure the definitive mapping is stated plainly and repeatedly in testimony and demonstratives. The Federal Circuit is likely to treat equivocation — particularly on core, dispositive issues — as fatal when the patentee carries the burden of proof.

Preserve critical issues at the right stages: If you plan to rely on a particular claim construction (for example, whether the “three signals” must be distinct or unique), raise and preserve that at the claim‑construction and 50(a) stages. But remember that preserving a construction is necessary, not sufficient: you must also build the evidentiary record that ties the accused product to that construction.

Conclusion: precision beats persuasion in DSP cases
Finesse is not a rejection of sophisticated technical theories; it is a reminder that the judicial system requires those theories to be translated into a clear, coherent record at trial. When your case turns on the existence, timing, or mathematical form of signals inside a radio, don’t trust a colorful slide or a persuasive sound bite. Prepare the artifacts, prepare the expert to map claim term to device element unambiguously, and walk the jury — and the appellate court — through each required step. In DSP‑heavy patent litigation, precision wins where persuasion alone may fail.
