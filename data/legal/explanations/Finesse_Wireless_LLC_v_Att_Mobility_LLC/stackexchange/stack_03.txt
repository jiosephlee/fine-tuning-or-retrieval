### Did Nokia, as an intervenor-defendant, need independent Article III standing to appeal?
Question:
Nokia intervened at the district court and is listed as a defendant-appellant on appeal. Does an intervenor-defendant need to demonstrate independent standing to participate in the appeal, or is it sufficient that a party with standing (AT&T) is also appealing? Would the answer change if AT&T had not appealed?
Answer:
Short answer
- No — when a plaintiff (Finesse) lost at trial and at least one defendant-appellant with Article III standing (AT&T) timely appealed, Nokia as an intervenor-defendant did not separately need to prove independent Article III standing to be listed and participate as a co‑appellant on that appeal. The Federal Circuit’s opinion treats AT&T and Nokia collectively as “Appellants” and proceeds on the merits without any standing dispute. See opinion heading and JURISDICTION statement (noting jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1) and listing “AT&T Mobility LLC and Nokia of America Corporation (collectively, Appellants)”).

- Yes — if AT&T had not appealed, Nokia (as an intervenor) would have had to establish that it itself was a proper party to invoke appellate jurisdiction (i.e., that it was a “party aggrieved” by the district court’s denial of JMOL and satisfied Article III standing requirements). The opinion does not address that scenario and contains no holding excusing a stand‑alone appellant from Article III requirements.

Why (legal doctrine in short)
- Appellate courts have jurisdiction only over “cases” or “controversies,” so an appellant must ordinarily show it has standing to bring the appeal. When multiple defendants share the same adverse judgment and one or more defendants with Article III standing appeal, co‑defendants who were parties below and who share substantially the same legal interest are routinely included as appellants and participate in the appeal without relitigating standing as to jurisdiction. In practice, courts accept that a properly standing appellant’s invocation of appellate jurisdiction supports review of claims that affect the same judgment and legal rights of other parties who were defendants below and are co‑appellants.

- By contrast, an intervenor who did not appeal and who sought to appeal alone must show it is a “party aggrieved” by the district court’s ruling and satisfy Article III injury‑in‑fact, causation, and redressability. That is separate from, and cannot be supplied by, a different party’s appeal if that different party does not itself invoke appellate jurisdiction.

How the opinion reflects this
- The opinion lists “AT&T Mobility LLC, Nokia of America Corporation, Defendants‑Appellants” and proceeds to decide the merits and vacate damages, reversing the district court’s denial of JMOL and noting jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1). There is no discussion in the opinion of any standing defect for Nokia, and the Court treated the appeal as properly before it on the joint appellants’ briefing and arguments. That treatment is consistent with the ordinary practice that a defendant‑appellant’s proper appeal supports review of issues affecting co‑defendants who are parties below and join the appeal.

- The opinion does not address the hypothetical where AT&T did not appeal. Because the court never considered whether Nokia could independently invoke appellate jurisdiction, it does not adopt any rule excusing an intervenor from Article III requirements when it is the sole appellant. If Nokia had been the only appellant, the Court would (absent some other jurisdictional basis) require Nokia to show it had standing to appeal the district court’s adverse rulings.

Practical takeaway for a student
- If you are analyzing appeals by intervenor‑defendants, always check (a) whether at least one party with clear Article III standing framed and filed the appeal, and (b) whether the intervenor was a party below and shares the same adverse judgment/interest such that it can be joined as a co‑appellant. If so, courts typically accept the joint appeal without a separate standing inquiry into the intervenor. If not — i.e., the intervenor alone seeks review — then you must analyze Article III standing for that intervenor as you would for any appellant.  

(Disclosure: the cited Federal Circuit opinion does not discuss standing issues; the answer limits itself to what the opinion shows — Nokia was a co‑appellant and the court exercised jurisdiction without addressing any separate standing challenge.)
