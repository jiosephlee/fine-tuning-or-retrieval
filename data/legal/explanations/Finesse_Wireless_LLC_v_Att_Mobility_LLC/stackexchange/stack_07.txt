Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC

### Why did the Federal Circuit vacate the damages award instead of rendering judgment of zero after finding noninfringement?
Question:
Given the court reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims, it then 'vacated' the damages award. Is vacatur just the standard label when liability falls away, or does it preserve any possibility of further proceedings on remand (e.g., different claims, different products)? In what scenarios would the Federal Circuit instead enter judgment of zero damages rather than vacate?
Answer:
Short answer
The Federal Circuit vacated the damages award because it reversed the jury’s infringement findings for all asserted claims — the damages verdict had no legally cognizable basis once liability was undone. The opinion says so plainly: “Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of the ’134 and ’775 patents, we vacate the damages award.” Vacatur is the routine remedy where the liability underpinning a damages award has been invalidated; it removes the jury’s money award rather than substituting a specific damages figure.

Why vacatur here (what the opinion shows)
- The court reversed JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of both patents. The jury’s $166,303,391 lump-sum award was expressly for infringement of those claims. Once the appellate court removes the infringement finding, the damages award “has no legal footing” — the opinion ties vacatur directly to that reversal.
- The panel did not say it was entering a new, final damages judgment on appeal (for example, “judgment for defendants” or “$0”); it simply vacated the award. That is the course the opinion follows.

What vacatur does (as shown by the opinion)
- Vacatur wipes out the jury’s damages verdict that depended on the now-reversed infringement finding. The opinion’s disposition — “REVERSED AND VACATED” — effectuates that result.
- Vacatur preserves the district court’s role to take whatever further action is appropriate consistent with the appellate decision (for example, to enter final judgment consistent with noninfringement, to address any remaining/ancillary issues, or to entertain such motions as parties may present). The opinion does not itself pronounce further remedies beyond vacating the award.

When an appellate court might instead enter judgment of zero
- An appellate court will sometimes render final judgment on appeal (including entering judgment for defendant/zero damages) only if the appeal resolves every legal and factual question necessary to a correct final decision on damages and no further proceedings are required. In other words, if liability is conclusively eliminated on appeal and there are no remaining factual or discretionary issues for the district court, the appellate court can and sometimes will enter judgment for the defendant rather than vacate.
- By contrast, when there are residual issues (procedural or factual), or the appellate court prefers to leave assessment of ancillary matters to the district court, the court will vacate the jury award and remand rather than substitute a damages figure itself.

Applying that to this opinion
- The panel reversed noninfringement for all asserted claims, so the damages award no longer rested on a viable infringement verdict — the opinion therefore vacated the award. The Court did not render a substitute damages judgment (e.g., $0) in this opinion, which follows the common practice shown in the opinion of removing a damages verdict that has lost its legal predicate and leaving any necessary district-court follow-up to be handled below.
