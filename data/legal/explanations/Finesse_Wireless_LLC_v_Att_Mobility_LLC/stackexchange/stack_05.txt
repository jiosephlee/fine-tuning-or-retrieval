Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC

### Did amending from "two or three signals" to "three signals S1, S2, S3" create a prosecution disclaimer against two-unique-signal implementations?
Question:
Appellants argued Finesse surrendered scope by changing 'two or three' to 'three' during prosecution to overcome Filipovic. The panel said no 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal and read the claims as requiring three 'separately identifiable' signals, not necessarily three unique inputs. How is that consistent with the typical inference that narrowing amendments surrender at least some scope? What made this amendment and argument insufficient to constitute disclaimer, and how much weight did the panel give to the distinction between passive vs. active IMPs in assessing scope surrender?
Answer:
Short answer: No. The panel concluded the amendment from “two or three signals” to “three signals S1, S2 and S3” did not effect a clear and unambiguous prosecution disclaimer of two‑unique‑signal implementations. The court applied the familiar Federal Circuit rule that a narrowing amendment only surrenders claim scope if the patentee clearly and unambiguously disavows subject matter during prosecution — and found that did not happen here. See opinion at J.A. 110–11 (citing Continental Circuits).

Why that result fits the governing doctrine
- Legal standard: The Federal Circuit requires a “clear and unambiguous” disavowal in the file history to read a prosecution argument or amendment as a disclaimer of claim scope. A mere narrowing amendment does not automatically disclaim all intermediate territory unless the patentee’s prosecution statements make the surrender unmistakeable. (Opinion cites Continental Circuits, J.A. 110–11.)
- Application here: Finesse amended the claims and argued over Filipovic largely on the ground that Filipovic addressed active IMPs, not the passive IMPs the asserted claims target. The examiner’s rejection was overcome “in part by explaining ‘Filipovic is limited to mitigating active IMPs’ and not passive IMPs” (J.A. 985; J.A. 1006–07), and the panel read that prosecutorial context as showing the amendment was directed to the passive/active distinction rather than an intent to disclaim two‑signal implementations (J.A. 110–11).
- Claim construction and extrinsic evidence: The district court had construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to mean “separately identifiable” but not “unique,” and credited inventor testimony that S1, S2, S3 can be the same signal or different signals (J.A. 109–15; J.A. 115). The panel adopted that understanding: at most the amendment required three separately identifiable signals, not three distinct/unique inputs (J.A. 110–11). Because there was no explicit prosecution statement saying “we give up two‑signal implementations” or equivalent unmistakable language, the court declined to treat the amendment as a disclaimer (J.A. 110–11).

Why a narrowing amendment didn’t automatically surrender scope here
- Narrowing amendments create a risk of surrender, but that risk converts into actual disclaimer only when prosecution arguments or the amendment itself clearly and unambiguously exclude the subject matter. The panel found the amendment plus the prosecution remarks did not do so: the patentee emphasized the passive vs. active IMP distinction when distinguishing Filipovic, and never made a clear, express caveat that two‑input systems were excluded (J.A. 985; J.A. 110–11).
- Put another way: the amendment changed claim language, but the file history shows the reason for the change (distinguishing Filipovic on active/passive IMPs), and intrinsic/extrinsic evidence supports an interpretation that the claim requires three “separately identifiable” signals rather than three unique signals. That combination defeated an inference of unequivocal surrender.

How much weight the panel gave to the passive/active IMP distinction
- Substantial weight. The panel expressly relied on Finesse’s prosecution argument that Filipovic was about active IMPs, not passive IMPs, as a key reason the amendment did not amount to a clear disavowal of two‑signal systems (J.A. 985; J.A. 110–11). The court treated that factual/prosecutorial context as the operative explanation for why the amendment was made — therefore not a plain attempt to carve out two‑signal implementations from the claims.
- The panel did not treat the passive/active point as the only relevant factor; it also relied on the prior claim language, the amendment, the district court’s construction, and inventor testimony (J.A. 109–15). But the passive/active distinction was the central prosecution justification that undercut any inference of surrender.

Bottom line for practice
- A narrowing amendment can trigger surrender, but proving disclaimer requires clear, unambiguous file‑history statements. Here, the patentee’s amendment and its arguments to the examiner were framed as distinguishing passive vs. active IMPs, and the intrinsic and inventor testimony supported a non‑exclusive reading of “three signals.” Those facts were sufficient for the panel to conclude no prosecution disclaimer of two‑signal implementations occurred (J.A. 110–11).
