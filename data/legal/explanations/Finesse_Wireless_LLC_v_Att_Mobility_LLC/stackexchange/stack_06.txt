Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC

### How specific must the plaintiff’s mapping be to show the '775 patent’s seven required multiplications are actually performed?
Question:
The Nokia document identified only three distinct multiplications and included modulus and complex conjugate operations. The panel faulted Finesse for not showing that all seven claimed multiplications were performed or explaining any repetition/mapping. What level of evidentiary detail is required to prove a 'seven multiplications' limitation—must the patentee identify each multiplication explicitly, or can they rely on mathematical equivalences/combinations if clearly explained to the jury? Do operations on |x| and x' ever qualify as 'multiplying S1, S2, S3,' and what proof is needed to equate them?
Answer:
Short answer

The patentee must present trial evidence that lets a reasonable jury conclude each claim element is met — here, that the accused product actually produces the seven third‑order products recited in the claim. That can be done either (1) by identifying each of the seven multiplications in the accused system, or (2) by presenting a clear, contemporaneous, principled mapping (e.g., algebraic equivalence or repetition) showing how the device’s listed operations correspond to the seven claimed products. Mere post‑trial argument or an unexplained inference from fewer distinct operations is insufficient. The Federal Circuit reversed because the Nokia document only showed three distinct operations (x1·|x1|·|x1|, x1·x1·x2', x1·|x2|·|x2|), Dr. Wells never tied those three to the seven claimed multiplications, and Finesse attempted to fix the mapping only on appeal. See Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T, No. 2024‑1039, slip op. at pp. 24–26 (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025).

Why — legal standard and how it was applied

- Burden and substantial‑evidence standard: The patentee bears the burden to prove infringement and must produce substantial evidence that the accused device meets each claim limitation. A jury verdict must have evidentiary support such that a reasonable jury could reach the same conclusion. Finesse, slip op. at pp. 6–7 (citing Wi‑LAN/Apple standards).

- Element‑by‑element proof: For a limitation that requires “generating … S1×S1×S2 and S1×S2×S2 …” the patentee must show the accused system performs the seven specific multiplications recited. The court required either direct proof that the seven multiplications occur or a trial‑record mapping that establishes equivalence or repetition that yields the seven products. Finesse, slip op. at pp. 23–26.

- Why Finesse lost: The Nokia document presented only three distinct multiplications and used modulus (|x|) and complex conjugate (x') operations rather than plain multiplications of the raw signals x1 and x2. The court held there was no evidence those three listed operations were repeated or otherwise mapped to all seven claimed products, and the expert never explained at trial how the three correspond to the seven. The court rejected the patentee’s appellate attempt to supply the missing mapping. Finesse, slip op. at pp. 24–26.

When mathematical equivalence can help — and what proof it requires

- Mathematical equivalence is allowed, but it must be shown to the jury at trial by admissible evidence — not invented on appeal. An expert may prove that certain device operations are algebraically equivalent to the claimed multiplications (for example, that multiplying x by |x| or x' yields the same third‑order term as S1×S1×S2 in the claim under the asserted claim construction), but the expert must

  1. identify which physical or software signals in the accused product correspond to S1, S2, S3;
  2. explain, step‑by‑step, how each claimed multiplication is produced by the device (pointing to code, block diagrams, signal flows, or outputs); and
  3. if using equivalences (e.g., involving modulus or conjugation), show the algebraic identity and why the claim language covers that transformed signal or why the transformed signal is equivalent for purposes of producing the claimed IMP cancellation term.

- The court specifically flagged that the Nokia document “does not disclose multiplying x1 and x2, but their complex conjugate and modulus,” and that mapping S3 onto x2, x2' and |x2| without explanation was flawed. Thus, to rely on modulus or conjugation you must expressly show (at trial) how those operations produce the same claimed products. Finesse, slip op. at pp. 24–25.

Practical checklist for trial proof (what a patentee should present)

- Explicit identification in the record of the accused device’s signals that correspond to S1, S2, S3 (no after‑the‑fact inferences).
- For each of the seven recited products, either (a) evidence that the device actually computes that product, or (b) an expert demonstration (with citations to device documents/code/hardware) that some listed device computations are algebraically identical to the claimed products or that outputs repeat to yield the seven products.
- If modulus or complex conjugate are involved, concrete testimony and analysis showing the equivalence to a plain multiplication of the claimed signals (and why the claim language embraces that form).
- Avoid relying on a chart/document that lists fewer distinct operations without also presenting the mapping or repetition evidence to the jury.

Bottom line

You do not have to literally recite seven separate lines in a document if you can prove at trial that the accused system produces all seven claimed products (by direct identification, repetition, or a clear algebraic mapping). But the proof must be presented and explained to the jury in the trial record — vague inferences from fewer listed operations or post‑trial clarifications on appeal will not satisfy the substantial‑evidence requirement. The Federal Circuit reversed here because Finesse failed to do that: the Nokia document showed only three distinct operations involving |x| and x', and the expert never tied those operations to the seven claim terms at trial. Finesse, slip op. at pp. 24–26.
