Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC

### What is the precise line between 'regional circuit law' and 'Federal Circuit law' in reviewing new-trial denials and sufficiency in patent cases?
Question:
The court applies Fifth Circuit law to the denial of a new trial (abuse of discretion) but uses Federal Circuit standards for substantial-evidence infringement review. How do practitioners determine which issues on appeal are governed by the regional circuit (procedural) and which by Federal Circuit (substantive patent law)? Are there canonical examples where this allocation has been outcome-determinative?
Answer:
Short answer
- Procedure (standards of review for motions like JMOL or new trial) is governed by the regional circuit. The opinion says explicitly that the denial of JMOL and denial of a new trial are reviewed under Fifth Circuit law (citing Wi‑LAN). See Opinion, Discussion I (first paragraph) and II (damages).
- Substantive patent-law determinations (claim construction, and whether a jury’s infringement finding is supported by the record) are governed by Federal Circuit law: claim construction de novo (with underlying factual findings for clear error) and factual findings of infringement reviewed for substantial evidence. See same paragraph in the Opinion (citing Wi‑LAN and Apple v. Wi‑LAN).

How to decide in practice
1) Ask whether the question is procedural (how the district court exercised its discretion or which regional standard of review applies) or substantive patent law (what the patent means, whether the claim elements are met):
   - If the appeal asks whether the district court abused its discretion in denying a new trial or a JMOL motion (i.e., you are challenging the court’s exercise of procedural discretion or invoking the regional circuit’s standard), the regional circuit’s standard governs. The Opinion: “We review a district court’s denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit, here the Fifth Circuit.” (Discussion I, first full paragraph).
   - If the appeal asks whether the accused device meets the claim limitations (infringement) or whether claim terms were correctly construed, the Federal Circuit’s patent standards apply: claim construction de novo; factual infringement findings for substantial evidence. The Opinion: “We review a jury’s finding of infringement for substantial evidence. . . . We review claim construction de novo and any underlying factual findings . . . for clear error.” (same paragraph).

2) Translate that to the specific issues you will brief:
   - Items about whether the jury had legally sufficient evidence to find infringement are argued under the Federal Circuit’s substantial‑evidence framework (could a reasonable jury find for the patentee based on the record). In this opinion, the court applied that standard when it asked whether Dr. Wells’ testimony and the Nokia documents could support the jury’s findings (see Discussion I.A and I.B where the court concluded the verdict was not supported by substantial evidence).
   - Items about whether the district court should have granted a new trial or granted/denied JMOL procedurally (timing, Rule 50(a)/(b), Rule 59, abuse of discretion) are addressed under the regional circuit’s abuse‑of‑discretion and related standards. The Opinion states that denials of a new trial and JMOL are reviewed under Fifth Circuit law (Discussion I opening and Discussion II on damages).

Where allocation is outcome‑determinative (canonical or in this opinion)
- This opinion itself shows how the allocation matters:
   - The court framed its review of the denial of JMOL under Fifth Circuit law (procedural), but then evaluated the sufficiency of the infringement evidence under the Federal Circuit’s substantial‑evidence standard. The court reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL because the infringement verdict “is not supported by substantial evidence” — a Federal Circuit substantive assessment applied in the context of the regional circuit standard for reviewing a JMOL denial (Discussion I.A and I.B). Put another way, the appellate court used the regional circuit’s pathway (review of a JMOL denial) but reached its substantive conclusion by applying Federal Circuit law on sufficiency of the infringement evidence.
- Other illustrative (cited) examples:
   - Wi‑LAN, Inc. v. Apple, and Apple v. Wi‑LAN are cited by the panel to make the split: Wi‑LAN for applying regional circuit law to review denials of JMOL/new trial; Apple/Wi‑LAN for the substantial‑evidence standard for infringement findings. The Opinion expressly cites both authorities in the opening paragraph of Discussion I.
   - Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp. is relied on here for the proposition that self‑contradictory expert testimony cannot support a verdict — a substantive sufficiency point the panel used to reverse (Discussion I.A, citing Johns Hopkins).

Practical checklist for practitioners preparing appeals in patent cases
- Identify whether the contested ruling is (a) a pure procedural ruling about the district court’s exercise of discretion (new trial, evidentiary rulings, timeliness, waiver) or (b) a substantive patent issue (claim construction, infringement, validity, how evidence maps to claim elements).
- If procedural: prepare to address the regional circuit’s standard (abuse of discretion, de novo review of legal questions under regional law where applicable). Cite the regional circuit’s precedents and show reversible error under that standard.
- If substantive patent law: prepare Federal Circuit precedent (claim construction law, substantial evidence for factual findings). When the appeal is procedurally framed (e.g., denial of JMOL), be prepared that the court will still ask the Federal Circuit substantive question — is there substantial evidence to support the verdict — and you must win under that Federal Circuit standard.
- Anticipate the hybrid: many appeals are packaged as procedural (denial of JMOL/new trial) but require the Federal Circuit to assess whether the record contains substantial evidence of infringement — as happened in this opinion.

Bottom line
The federal appellate court separates the two roles: regional circuit law governs review of district‑court procedural decisions (denial of JMOL/new trial); Federal Circuit law governs patent‑specific substantive questions (claim scope, infringement sufficiency, claim construction). In practice you must brief both: show procedural error under the regional standard and show that, on the Federal Circuit’s patent standards, the substantive patent findings either fail or succeed. The Finesse opinion illustrates that the court will explicitly state the allocation and then apply the Federal Circuit’s substantial‑evidence test to the record when assessing whether the jury’s infringement verdict can stand.
