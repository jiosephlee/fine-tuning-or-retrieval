### How can the Federal Circuit reverse a jury’s infringement verdict under the 'substantial evidence' standard without violating the Seventh Amendment?
Question:
The court repeatedly invokes 'substantial evidence' deference but still reverses the verdict on both patents based on the insufficiency of the expert’s testimony. Where are the constitutional limits here (Reexamination Clause)? What distinguishes permissible appellate review for legal sufficiency from impermissible reweighing of credibility?
Answer:
Short answer
Appellate courts may reverse a jury infringement verdict under the substantial‑evidence / JMOL standard without running afoul of the Seventh Amendment (or the Article III “reexamination” principle) when the record would permit no reasonable juror to find for the plaintiff — for example because the plaintiff’s sole or critical proof is internally inconsistent, legally insufficient, or cannot be reasonably read to establish the claimed facts. What the court may not do is reweigh competing evidence or supplant the jury’s credibility calls when reasonable jurors could disagree.

What the opinion does (and why it’s constitutional)
1) Standard applied. The panel expressly applied the Fifth Circuit JMOL standard and the Federal Circuit substantial‑evidence test (see Opinion, pp. 4–5): the court reviews denial of JMOL de novo but will reverse only if “when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” It repeats the substantial‑evidence definition: “if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” (Opinion pp. 4–5).

2) Not credibility reweighing, but legal sufficiency review. The court did not recount competing expert testimony and then pick a winner; instead it evaluated whether the plaintiff’s proof — principally Dr. Wells’s testimony tied to the Nokia documents — could, as a matter of legal sufficiency, support the verdict. Where the plaintiff’s case hinged on an expert whose testimony was confusing and self‑contradictory about the very mapping the claims required, the court concluded no reasonable jury could have relied on that testimony to meet the claim element. The panel relied on controlling precedent for that proposition: “When the party with the burden of proof . . . rests its case on an expert’s self‑contradictory testimony, we may conclude the evidence is insufficient to satisfy that standard.” Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp., 543 F.3d 1342, 1349 (Fed. Cir. 2008) (cited at Opinion p. 9). The court applied that rule to the record: “There is nothing clear about Dr. Wells’ testimony. Dr. Wells never testified x1 and x2 correspond to the ‘signals of interest’ and ‘interference generating signals.’” (Opinion pp. 10–11). It therefore held the verdict was not supported by substantial evidence and reversed (Opinion p. 12).

3) The constitutional limits in practice. The Seventh Amendment / Article III bar prevents an appellate court from substituting its view for the jury’s when reasonable jurors could disagree about the evidence or credibility. But it does not prevent appellate courts from concluding that, even when the evidence is viewed in the non‑movant’s favor, that evidence is legally insufficient to sustain the verdict. The opinion illustrates that boundary: the court did not resolve competing versions of the facts where each was reasonably supported; instead it found the plaintiff’s only asserted mapping was internally inconsistent and inadequate as a matter of law to prove the claim element (Opinion pp. 7–12 for the ’134 patent; pp. 12–14 for the ’775 patent).

How to tell permissible legal sufficiency review from impermissible reweighing
- Permissible (what the court did): ask whether a reasonable juror could accept the proof offered to satisfy the claimed element. If the plaintiff’s evidence consists chiefly of an expert opinion that is conclusory, contradicted by other testimony of that same expert, or lacks the necessary foundation (e.g., the expert never actually tied x1/x2 to the claimed signals), the appellate court may rule that no reasonable juror could have relied on it. (See Opinion: “This sort of confusing change of course is not sufficient to support the jury verdict.”; and the Johns Hopkins citation, Opinion p. 9.)
- Impermissible (what the court must avoid): taking competing testimony and substituting the court’s assessment of witness credibility or the weight of evidence where reasonable jurors could credit either side. The opinion avoids that by pointing to the absence of any coherent, legally adequate mapping in the plaintiff’s evidence rather than by saying the defendants’ expert was more believable.

Illustrative quotations from the opinion
- Standard: “A factual finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial.” (Opinion p. 4–5).
- Rule for self‑contradictory expert proof: “When the party with the burden of proof, such as Finesse, rests its case on an expert’s self‑contradictory testimony, we may conclude the evidence is insufficient to satisfy that standard.” (Opinion p. 9, citing Johns Hopkins).
- Application to record: “There is nothing clear about Dr. Wells’ testimony. Dr. Wells never testified x1 and x2 correspond to the ‘signals of interest’ and ‘interference generating signals.’ … This sort of confusing change of course is not sufficient to support the jury verdict.” (Opinion pp. 10–11).
- Conclusion: “We reverse the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’134 patent because the jury’s infringement verdict is not supported by substantial evidence.” (Opinion p. 12; same language used for the ’775 patent, pp. 13–14).

Bottom line
Appellate reversal under the substantial‑evidence / JMOL framework is constitutional so long as the court confines itself to the narrow legal question — could reasonable jurors, viewing the record in the non‑movant’s favor, rationally find the elements proven? — and does not impermissibly reweigh witness credibility where reasonable minds could differ. This opinion stays on the permissible side by identifying the plaintiff’s critical proof as internally inconsistent and legally inadequate, and by relying on precedent that allows appellate courts to find a verdict unsupported where the sole proof is self‑contradictory or fails to establish the required factual mapping.
