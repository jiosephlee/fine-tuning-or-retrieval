Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC

### What exactly must be preserved in a Rule 50(a) motion to avoid waiver of a JMOL theory on appeal?
Question:
The district court deemed AT&T/Nokia to have waived the argument that S1, S2, S3 must be 'unique' because it wasn't raised at Rule 50(a). On appeal, the panel discussed prosecution history and claim scope anyway but reversed on different grounds (insufficient proof of seven multiplications). In practice, how specific must a Rule 50(a) motion be to preserve a claim construction-related noninfringement theory? Is pointing to 'failure of proof on element [d]' enough, or must the party flag the uniqueness point and its legal basis (e.g., disclaimer) to avoid waiver?
Answer:
Short answer
The opinion shows that merely saying “failure of proof on element [d]” is risky if your noninfringement theory depends on a particular claim‑construction or prosecution‑history point (e.g., that “three signals” means three unique signals). To avoid waiver you should make the specific legal theory clear in your Rule 50(a) motion: identify the claim language at issue, say why (and on what legal basis) that language cannot cover the accused product (for example, prosecution disclaimer or an argued construction), and tie that legal point to the record evidence (explain concretely why the evidence does not meet the element as you construe it).

Why the Finesse opinion points that way
- The district court held AT&T/Nokia waived the “uniqueness” theory because it was not raised in their Rule 50(a) motion. The opinion records that the district court thought the uniqueness argument had not been preserved (see J.A. 38, 40). That is the posture in which the court refused to grant JMOL on that ground.
- The Federal Circuit nevertheless examined the prosecution history / claim scope on the merits and rejected Finesse’s disavowal argument (the panel concluded the amendment did not amount to clear and unambiguous disavowal) (see J.A. 109–14). But critical for the appeal, the court reversed on a different, factual ground: the patentee’s expert never proved the seven claimed multiplications (the Nokia document showed at most three distinct multiplications and the expert did not map them to the seven claimed multiplications), so no reasonable jury could have found infringement (see J.A. 26485–86; J.A. 14966–70; discussion at J.A. 38–39, J.A. 26486). For the ’134 patent, the opinion also emphasizes that the patentee’s expert gave internally inconsistent testimony and failed to map the accused device to the claimed signals (see J.A. 15010–19; J.A. 10–11). 

What that means in practice (concrete preservation checklist)
1. Identify the element and your specific legal theory. Don’t rest on a generic “failure of proof on element [d]” if your JMOL theory turns on a particular claim‑construction or prosecution‑history point. Say, e.g., “JMOL: element 1[d] not proved because ‘three signals S1,S2,S3’ requires three unique input signals (prosecution disclaimer/amendment).”
2. State the legal basis plainly. If you rely on prosecution disclaimer, point to the amendment/exchange and explain why it is “clear and unambiguous” (cite the record pages you will rely on). The Finesse opinion focuses on the prosecution record when addressing surrender/disavowal (J.A. 109–14).
3. Tie the legal theory to the record evidence. Explain why the evidence fails under that construction: point to the specific documents, testimony, and the mapping (or lack of mapping) between the accused product and the claim terms. The Federal Circuit reversed because the expert never mapped the three listed multiplications to the seven claimed multiplications, and the Nokia documents did not show repetition (J.A. 26485–86; J.A. 14966–70).
4. If you have alternate grounds, preserve them too. If you can show both (a) claim construction/prosecution‑history noninfringement and (b) lack of evidentiary mapping, state both in Rule 50(a). The district court’s waiver ruling in Finesse illustrates the risk of not flagging the claim‑scope argument; the panel’s ultimate reversal on evidentiary grounds shows the practical importance of preserving and clearly articulating both kinds of arguments (J.A. 38–40; J.A. 26486).
5. If your theory depends on expert testimony weaknesses, point those out specifically in the motion. Finesse turned on the expert’s inconsistent or unhelpful testimony (e.g., not mapping x1/x2 to claimed terms) (J.A. 15010–19). Call that out in Rule 50(a).

Bottom line
Rule 50(a) preservation requires more than a blanket “insufficient evidence” label when your noninfringement argument rests on a discrete claim‑scope or prosecution‑history contention. The Finesse opinion illustrates two things: a district court may treat an unargued claim‑construction theory as waived (J.A. 38, 40), and an appellate court will still examine the prosecution history and evidence if presented on appeal — but you cannot rely on the appellate court to rescue an unpreserved, under‑developed theory. So be specific: identify the claim term, state the legal basis for your construction/noninfringement, and tie it to the record in your Rule 50(a) motion.
