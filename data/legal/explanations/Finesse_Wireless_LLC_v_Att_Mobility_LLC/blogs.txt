Title: Blog about the opinion: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC

# Red Lines and Red Flags: The Federal Circuit Tosses the ’134 Verdict over an Expert’s Muddled Mapping

## Why this decision matters
The Federal Circuit’s decision in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC is a sobering reminder of how fragile infringement verdicts can be when the plaintiff’s case relies principally on an expert’s mapping of claim elements onto a defendant’s system. The court reversed the jury verdict of literal infringement on the ’134 patent because Finesse’s expert repeatedly tied the claim term “interference generating signals” to a “modeled PIM path” that, crucially, is generated downstream of the accused receiver’s analog‑to‑digital converter (RF ADC). Because a receiver cannot have sampled signals that did not yet exist at the time of sampling, the expert’s mapping—at least as explicitly offered at trial—could not support the jury’s verdict. The Federal Circuit’s ruling underscores that element‑by‑element proof matters, and ambiguous or self‑contradictory expert testimony can doom a plaintiff’s case on appeal.

## The factual and evidentiary hinge
The asserted claim in the ’134 patent requires, at step 1[a], “over‑sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream, wherein the received signals include signals of interest and interference generating signals.” Finesse’s expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, pointed the jury to a Nokia technical diagram and repeatedly testified that the RF ADC (the receiver) samples the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” But the same diagram’s legend labels the red path as “DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path,” and Dr. Wells also testified (more explicitly elsewhere) that the modeled PIM path is generated in the GROOT FPGA—i.e., after sampling.

AT&T and Nokia seized on that inconsistency. If the “interference generating signals” are the modeled PIM path, they cannot have been sampled by the RF ADC because they are generated later in the processing chain. At trial Dr. Wells attempted to pivot, asserting that the red path really reflected two inputs—x1 and x2—and that those are the sampled signals underlying the claim. The district court, trying to sustain the verdict, accepted an inference that Dr. Wells must have meant x1 and x2 when he referred to the red path. The Federal Circuit rejected that rescue.

## The Federal Circuit’s reasoning
The panel focused on the actual trial record and the explicit mappings the expert offered. It found that Dr. Wells “consistently and repeatedly” mapped the claimed “interference generating signals” to the modeled PIM path, and that his later suggestion that “x1 and x2 are the two inputs here” never amounted to a clear, contemporaneous testimony that x1 and x2 correspond to the claimed elements. The court stressed the point that the modeled PIM path is created after the RF ADC, and so could not satisfy the claim’s sampling requirement. Because the plaintiff bore the burden of proving that the receiver sampled both the signals of interest and the interference‑generating signals, the court concluded the evidence did not permit a reasonable jury to find infringement.

The opinion cites Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope to support the proposition that a verdict cannot rest on an expert’s self‑contradictory testimony. In that precedent, the court reversed a verdict where the expert’s conflicting descriptions of how an accused device worked made it impossible for a reasonable jury to find the required claim elements satisfied. The same logic carried the day here: where the expert offered conflicting and unclear mappings, the appellate court could not uphold the verdict as supported by substantial evidence.

## Doctrinal implications
Two doctrinal points stand out. First, this decision reaffirms that element‑by‑element proof is not a mere trial‑technique nicety but a substantive requirement for infringement. It is not enough to point to a diagram and assert that something “must” be what the claims require; plaintiffs must tie each claim limitation to evidence showing the accused product or process actually performs that limitation.

Second, the ruling clarifies the limits of a district court’s power to salvage a verdict by drawing inferences from ambiguous testimony. Under the Fifth Circuit’s (and thus the Federal Circuit’s) JMOL standard, appellate review asks whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict, nonetheless points so strongly in favor of the moving party that no reasonable jury could have ruled otherwise. That standard does not allow fuzzy, after‑the‑fact reinterpretations of expert testimony to stand when the expert’s explicit testimony mapped a claim element to a part of the system that could not fulfill the limitation.

Finally, the decision underscores that expert testimony cannot be saved on appeal by arguments developed post‑trial. The Federal Circuit criticized Finesse for trying to clarify or reframe the mapping on appeal in ways the jury never heard.

## Practical lessons for practitioners
Plaintiffs: If your infringement case depends on expert testimony, make sure the expert offers a clear, consistent, and contemporaneous element‑by‑element mapping at trial. Avoid reliance on ambiguous demonstratives or diagram legends without a solid, corroborating chain of testimony showing how the diagram entries correspond to actual signals or components in the accused system. Anticipate cross‑examination aiming to expose any timing or causation gaps (for example, “were these signals present at the sampling stage?”).

Defendants: Cross‑examination that pinpoints temporal or causal impossibilities (e.g., “how could the ADC have sampled a signal that’s generated downstream?”) can create an appellate record favorable to JMOL/X‑overturn. When you can show that an expert’s mapping places a claimed element downstream of a required event (sampling, emission, etc.), you put the plaintiff to the hard burden of reconciling the timing.

Judges and experts: Demonstratives and block diagrams are indispensable, but they cannot substitute for precise testimony. Experts should be prepared to explain what each label means, where each signal originates, and—critically—when each signal exists relative to the claim steps. Judges should be wary of saving jury verdicts by inferring an unspoken clarification that the expert never actually provided on the stand.

## Bottom line
Finesse lost not because the underlying science was hopelessly complex, but because the proof—an expert’s repeated mapping of a claim term to a modeled signal that by the expert’s own testimony did not exist at the time of sampling—was logically inconsistent with the claim language. The Federal Circuit’s reversal is a crisp reminder: in patent trials, clarity wins. Ambiguous expert mappings, especially those that rely on diagram legends or post‑hoc reinterpretations, risk being stripped of probative force on appeal.



# Seven Means Seven: Why the ’775 Patent Failed on Proof, Not Construction

## Why this case matters

Finesse Wireless v. AT&T (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025) is a useful corrective for litigants in the signal‑processing and software patent world: when a claim spells out a specific mathematical recipe, courts will demand a jury‑ready, element‑by‑element showing that the accused product actually carries out that recipe. The Federal Circuit declined to import a narrow prosecution‑history estoppel to limit “three signals S1, S2 and S3” to three distinct, unique inputs, but nonetheless reversed the jury’s infringement verdict because Finesse’s proof simply did not show that the accused Nokia radios performed the seven enumerated third‑order multiplications recited in the ’775 patent. In short, claim scope survived challenge, but the patentee’s evidence did not.

## What the court did

The ’775 patent claims an interference‑cancellation method that, for third‑order terms, requires generating seven specific products from three signals S1, S2, S3: in the patent’s words, S1×S1×S2; S1×S2×S2; S1×S2×S3; S1×S1×S3; S2×S2×S3; S1×S3×S3; and S2×S3×S3. At summary judgment the district court had construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as “separately identifiable” but not necessarily “unique.” On appeal the Federal Circuit agreed that the prosecution history did not clearly and unambiguously disclaim two‑signal embodiments when the patentee added “three signals” to distinguish Filipovic; the amendment did not amount to the sort of unequivocal surrender that would bar coverage of an accused product with only two unique inputs.

That was the doctrinal win for Finesse. It was, however, a Pyrrhic one. The jury verdict could not survive scrutiny because the only documentary evidence Finesse offered—a Nokia technical document relied on by its expert—listed only three distinct multiplication terms and used operations like complex conjugation and modulus, not literal third‑order multiplications of three separate S‑signals. The expert did not show how those three listed operations mapped to the seven claimed products, nor did he walk the jury through any repetition or algebraic equivalence that would yield the full set of seven terms. Finesse’s later attempts (on appeal) to point to a ten‑term polynomial in Nokia materials and retroactively assign which of those terms corresponded to the seven claimed products came too late; the mapping was never presented to the jury in a way that would permit a reasonable juror to find infringement. The Federal Circuit therefore held the evidence was not substantial and reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for the ’775 claims.

## Analytical perspective: claim drafting survives, proof must follow

There are two lessons layered here. First, the Federal Circuit continues to police prosecution disclaimer with a high bar: an amendment or statement during prosecution must be “clear and unambiguous” to shrink claim scope. Adding “three signals” to fend off Filipovic did not, by itself, equate to a clear disavowal of embodiments that use fewer unique physical signals (for example, reusing a signal or employing a copy). That is consistent with recent Federal Circuit authority that refuses to read unambiguous disclaimers into ordinary narrowing amendments.

Second—and more practically decisive for litigants—the court reiterates that where claims recite precise mathematical operations, patentees must provide equally precise proof. The words of the claim mattered: the claim sets out seven enumerated third‑order products, and the patentee’s burden at trial was to show that the accused system performs those products (or equivalents) in the claimed manner. A vendor document that lists only three distinct terms, and which uses modulus and conjugate notation, is not a substitute for an expert’s element‑by‑element demonstration that those operations are the functional or algebraic equivalents of each claimed multiplication. The Federal Circuit refused to allow appellant‑side re‑labeling or post hoc mappings on appeal to bootstrap the inadequate trial record.

The court’s invocation of Johns Hopkins—where the patentee’s contradictory expert testimony doomed the infringement case—underscores the evidentiary point: inconsistent or conclusory expert testimony cannot carry the patentee’s burden. And the court’s reliance on Continental Circuits for the prosecution history point shows the two ideas are separable: a patentee can avoid an estoppel finding but still lose if it fails to prove infringement.

## Doctrinal and practical implications

Doctrinally, the decision draws a clean line between claim‑scope fights and sufficiency of proof. The Federal Circuit avoided creating a new rule of prosecution disclaimer while reaffirming that precise claim language imposes an exacting proof obligation. That distinction matters: patentees may breathe easier about routine claim amendments during prosecution, but they should not assume that relaxed claim construction will rescue a weak evidentiary record.

Practically, the ruling will be cited by defendants in cases involving algorithmic, mathematical, or signal‑processing claims. When a claim explicitly enumerates algebraic terms, defendants should focus on forcing patentees to prove the mapping from the accused product’s implementation to each recited term. Vendor or standards documents that use different notation (conjugates, moduli, filters) are fertile grounds for cross‑examination: those differences can be fatal unless the patentee’s expert supplies a clear algebraic equivalence and ties the operations to the accused product’s executed code or hardware flow.

For patentees and drafters, the case suggests two sensible precautions. First, consider drafting claims (or dependent claims) that capture alternative representations of the same mathematical operations—e.g., allow for conjugate or modulus‑based implementations, or include functional language about generating specific frequency components—if multiple mathematical formulations are foreseeable. Second, build the trial record: if the infringement theory relies on algebraic equivalence, have the expert show step‑by‑step how the accused system’s computations produce each claimed term, and introduce low‑level technical evidence (code excerpts, FPGA block diagrams, test output) that corroborates the mapping.

For defense counsel, the decision proves the value of a narrow, forensic attack: isolate a single claim element that is amenable to documentary refutation (here, “seven specific multiplications”), and force the patentee to produce a jury‑ready mapping. If the patentee’s proof is inconsistent, equivocal, or dependent on undocumented inferences, ask for JMOL and press the appellate record.

## Downstream effects

We should expect two immediate litigational effects. First, patentees will sharpen expert presentations in trials involving mathematical claims—more algebra on the board, more demonstrable linkage between source code/firmware and claimed operations, and, where possible, demonstrable test vectors or outputs. Second, defendants will more aggressively pursue JMOLs (and Rule 50 motions) when the patentee’s case rests on inferential leaps from high‑level vendor documents. The Federal Circuit’s decision incentivizes early, element‑specific attacks and makes it harder for juries to speculate their way to infringement on technical claims that require precise arithmetic operations.

Finally, the decision is a reminder that “seven means seven” in patent proof. Claim construction can be generous; proof cannot be. Where a patentee chooses to claim a particular numerical combination of operations, it must prove that combination was performed (or that the accused product practices the claimed mathematical equivalence) with a clarity that matches the claim’s precision.



# Substantial Evidence, Fifth‑Circuit Style: Undoing a $166 Million E.D. Tex. Verdict

## Why this case matters
Finesse Wireless v. AT&T/Nokia is a reminder that large jury verdicts cannot survive on fuzzy expert testimony or appellate reconstructions. The Federal Circuit reversed the Eastern District of Texas’ denial of JMOL and vacated a $166 million damages award after concluding that, viewed under the Fifth Circuit’s JMOL framework and the Federal Circuit’s substantial‑evidence standard, no reasonable jury could have found infringement of either asserted patent. The decision is important not because it rewrites patent law, but because it underscores how closely appellate courts will scrutinize the evidentiary glue — particularly expert mappings of accused systems to claim elements — that supports jury verdicts.

## What the court held and how it reasoned
The appeal involved two patents aimed at canceling passive intermodulation (PIM) products in radios. For the ’134 patent, the claim required sampling a passband that includes both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Finesse’s case depended entirely on its expert, Dr. Wells, who tethered the claimed terms to elements shown in Nokia technical documents. But the Nokia documentation placed the “modeled PIM path” downstream of the radio’s analog‑to‑digital converter (ADC), meaning it was generated after sampling and therefore could not be a sampled “interference generating signal.” The expert’s testimony was internally inconsistent: at times he described the red path in the diagram as two signals (x1 and x2), but he repeatedly and unambiguously mapped the claim terms to the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path. The Federal Circuit concluded that this contradictory testimony — and the absence of a clear, unequivocal mapping of x1/x2 to the claim elements — failed to provide substantial evidence of infringement.

For the ’775 patent, the claim required generating intermodulation cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2, S3 in seven specified third‑order products. The district court construed “three signals” as “separately identifiable” but not necessarily unique. On the evidence, however, the Nokia documents showed only three distinct multiplications (and involved moduli and complex conjugates rather than plain signal multiplications). Dr. Wells did not explain how those three operations corresponded to the seven claimed products, nor did he present a mapping that the jury could rely on. The Federal Circuit held that, absent an evidentiary showing that the seven claimed multiplications actually occur, the jury verdict lacked substantial evidentiary support.

In both instances the panel emphasized that post‑hoc clarifications on appeal cannot substitute for the missing or contradictory trial record, and it cited precedent (e.g., Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope) for the proposition that courts may find expert testimony self‑defeating when it contains irresolvable inconsistencies.

## The procedural architecture: Rule 50, regional standards, and substantial evidence
A technical but pivotal feature of this decision is the interplay between regional‑circuit JMOL standards and the Federal Circuit’s substantial‑evidence review of infringement findings. The Federal Circuit reviews a district court’s denial of JMOL under the law of the regional circuit (here, the Fifth Circuit), while separately reviewing factual findings of infringement for substantial evidence. That procedural architecture produces two overlapping constraints on the patentee: first, the need to preserve discrete Rule 50(a) (and (b)) grounds at trial; and second, the need to produce a trial record that, even viewed most favorably to the verdict, contains enough coherent evidence to meet the substantial‑evidence bar on appeal.

Finesse’s loss illustrates both constraints. The court did not adopt a new theory of preservation; it reversed because the record — not the standard of review — was inadequate. But because Federal Circuit review must be tethered to the regional standard for the initial JMOL denial, district‑court practice (including preservation of issues at Rule 50(a)) remains crucial to shaping appellate review.

## Analytical perspective: why the Federal Circuit pushed back
Two themes animate the opinion. First, appellate courts will not accept creative appellate reconstructions when the trial record is internally contradictory or silent. Finesse tried to stitch together testimony and later appellate argument to salvage its case, but the panel would not let those patchwork inferences substitute for a clear, admissible mapping presented to the jury. That principle protects the Seventh Amendment jury role while also ensuring that verdicts rest on evidence, not post‑verdict spin.

Second, the opinion reflects skepticism toward experts who rely on diagrams, legends, or manufacturer documents without a contemporaneous, explicit explanation tying each claimed limitation to an identified physical or digital element. Diagrams that label a path “DL(TX) reference & modeled PIM path” do not do the work for an expert if the expert cannot say — without contradiction — which signals on that path satisfy the claim limitations, and whether those signals are even present at the relevant point in the device’s processing chain (e.g., before vs. after an ADC).

Finally, on prosecution history and claim scope, the court reiterated a familiar point: narrowing amendments do not automatically amount to clear and unmistakable disavowal. Finesse’s reduction to “three signals” during prosecution did not, in the panel’s view, incontrovertibly disclaim claims over implementations with fewer unique inputs where the claimed language was satisfied in another way. But lack of disclaimer did not save Finesse, because the evidentiary showing on the seven multiplications was still missing.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects
This decision will be cited by defendants and appeals courts as authority for vigorous scrutiny of expert testimony and documentary mappings. Expect more JMOL motions that focus not just on legal standards but on the internal coherence of expert narratives. Plaintiffs will find it harder to rely on diagrams and legends without contemporaneous, precise testimony linking each claim limitation to specific signals or operations in the accused product.

The ruling also reinforces appellate reluctance to credit new theories or mappings introduced for the first time on appeal. That constraint tightens the spotlight on trial counsel to adduce a complete record. For claim‑construction and prosecution strategy, the case underlines that amendments can be risky, but that courts will still require clear disavowal to find prosecution disclaimer; plaintiffs should be cautious about over‑reliance on ambiguous claim amendments to fend off § 102/103 rejections while later arguing broad scope at trial.

## Practice pointers for patent trials in E.D. Tex. (and elsewhere)
Trial teams should take away several practical lessons. First, when your case turns on mapping technical diagrams to claimed elements, lock down those mappings in expert reports, deposition testimony, and demonstratives. Don’t rely on the implication of a legend or on a passing sentence of testimony; require your expert to state plainly which internal signal is S1, S2, or the “interference generating signal,” and to explain timing issues (e.g., whether a signal exists pre‑ or post‑ADC).

Second, watch for internal inconsistencies in expert testimony. If an expert offers two descriptions of the same path, reconcile them before trial or eliminate the inconsistency on cross‑examination and redirect. Jurors and judges alike are more comfortable with a straightforward, replicable mapping than with elaborate technical finesse that cannot be pinned down.

Third, preserve Rule 50(a) objections with specificity. If you think an essential element lacks evidence, make that argument expressly at the close of evidence; that both gives the district court a chance to rule and preserves the point for appeal.

Fourth, avoid leaning on post‑verdict affidavits, argument, or appeal‑only mappings. If you need to respond to a perceived evidentiary gap, do it at trial — supplementing the record later will likely fail.

## Conclusion
Finesse Wireless is not a turn in patent doctrine so much as a cautionary tale: a seven‑figure jury verdict will not survive if the evidentiary thread tying accused systems to claim limitations is ambiguous or internally inconsistent. The Federal Circuit’s opinion is a clear exhortation to patentees to present crisp, unequivocal expert testimony and to defendants to seize on any uncertainty at the JMOL stage. For trial counsel in E.D. Tex. and elsewhere, the safe course is obvious — make your mappings plain, your expert testimony coherent, and your Rule 50 preservation precise.



# After the Static: Implications for PIM‑Cancellation Patents and Proving DSP Infringement

## Why this case matters

Finesse Wireless v. AT&T (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025) is a reminder that in patents whose claims turn on discrete digital‑signal‑processing steps, the proof must be element‑by‑element and technically precise. The Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for all asserted claims of two PIM‑cancellation patents and vacated a $166 million lump‑sum damages award. The reversal turned on two familiar but increasingly important themes in modern patent litigation: (1) for sampling‑related limitations, the accused device must actually have the claimed signals present at the claimed sampling point; and (2) for multiplication‑based claims, the patentee must show the accused product performs each required mathematical operation as claimed, not merely something that looks similar at a high level (e.g., modulus or conjugate operations). For practitioners, the case highlights how expert testimony and technical documents can make or break a jury verdict in DSP‑heavy suits.

## What the court held and why

The opinion addressed two patents with different technical hooks. The ’134 patent required, among other things, “over‑sampling … a passband of received signals” that “include signals of interest and interference generating signals.” Finesse’s expert relied on a Nokia block diagram and testimony that pointed to two signals on a red path in the diagram—what he variously called the “DL(TX) reference” and the “modeled PIM path,” and sometimes referred more abstractly to x1 and x2. The problem, as the Federal Circuit framed it, was that the modeled PIM path in the Nokia materials was generated downstream of the RF analog‑to‑digital converter (RF ADC). In other words, the thing the expert identified as the “interference generating signal” did not exist at the claimed sampling point; it was created later in the receiver chain inside the FPGA. The expert’s testimony was internally inconsistent and unclear about whether x1/x2 or the labeled DL/ modeled PIM paths were the mapped signals. Because Finesse bore the burden to prove the presence of both kinds of signals at the sampling point, and its expert’s testimony could not supply that element with substantial evidence, the court concluded no reasonable jury could have found infringement of the ’134 claims.

The ’775 patent claimed generating IMP cancellation signals by digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2, and S3 in seven specific third‑order products. The district court had construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as “separately identifiable” but not necessarily “unique,” and the Federal Circuit agreed that prosecution history did not amount to a clear and unambiguous surrender of two‑signal implementations. That said, the central problem at trial was evidentiary: Finesse’s expert pointed to a Nokia document that listed three distinct operations—one involving a signal multiplied by its modulus twice, others involving a conjugate—and then asserted those map onto the seven claimed multiplications. The Federal Circuit found the Nokia material showed only three distinct multiplications and did not demonstrate that the seven claimed multiplications were actually performed (nor did the expert explain how to map the defendant’s modulus/conjugate operations onto the claimed products). Because the patentee failed to show the seven claimed multiplications were performed, the infringement verdict for the ’775 claims could not stand.

Because both infringement findings failed for lack of substantial evidence, vacating damages followed naturally.

## Analytical perspective

Two features of the opinion are worth emphasizing. First, this is an archetypal expert‑driven case. The jurors do not read FPGA source code or parse analog front‑end timing diagrams themselves; they rely on experts to translate block diagrams into “meets limitation X” statements. The Federal Circuit’s willingness to treat internally inconsistent or equivocal expert testimony as insufficient evidence is straightforward law, but it has renewed force in DSP contexts: when claims involve timing, sampling order, or signal identity, the expert must tie the asserted claim language to a concrete point in the signal chain with clarity.

Second, the court’s technical strictness about mathematical operations matters. Modulus operations and complex conjugation are not ipso facto equivalent to multiplying literal copies of time‑domain signals S1, S2, S3 in the precise combinations a claim recites. The Federal Circuit demanded a mapping from what the device actually computes to the claim’s algebraic products. In short, similarity of purpose (the device is trying to cancel PIM) does not substitute for identity of operations when the claims specify particular mathematical steps.

The appellate court was careful not to re‑write claim construction. It rejected the argument that amendment to recite “three signals” constituted a clear, disallowing surrender of two‑signal implementations. But that concession did not save the verdict because Finesse still could not produce evidence that the accused radios performed the seven listed multiplications.

## Doctrinal implications

For patent law doctrine, the decision reinforces several settled propositions while sharpening their application in high‑tech cases:

- Proof requirement and JMOL/substantial evidence: The decision is a textbook application of the standard that a jury verdict must rest on substantial, non‑contradicted evidence. In DSP cases, that means the patentee’s expert must provide a consistent, element‑by‑element narrative mapping claim language to device behavior.

- Claim construction and prosecution history: The Federal Circuit continued to adhere to the “clear and unambiguous” rule for prosecution disclaimer. Amending to recite “three signals” did not necessarily exclude implementations with repeated or copied signals unless the record shows an unmistakable surrender. But claim drafting that leaves ambiguous whether signals must be distinct invites dispute and makes infringement harder to prove.

- Mathematical claim elements: The court’s insistence on mapping actual operations—e.g., real multiplications of signal copies—onto claimed algebraic forms highlights that courts will look to the exact mathematics underlying alleged infringement. Extrinsic materials like block diagrams, modulus/computational descriptions, and source/FPGA code will be parsed with algebraic precision.

## Practical takeaways for litigants and drafters

For patentees and litigators: prepare to prove the math. When claims hinge on when a signal exists (before or after an ADC, pre‑ or post‑FPGA) or on performing precise algebraic operations, the record must show that identity and timing clearly. That often means obtaining and exposing low‑level artifacts: FPGA logic, firmware, timing diagrams tied to physical sampling points, signal traces, and deterministic mappings from implemented operations to the claimed mathematical products. Expert narratives should avoid equivocation; demonstratives should show the one‑to‑one correspondence between accused operations and claim elements. If an expert introduces multiple, inconsistent characterizations of the same signal path (as Dr. Wells did here), the jury verdict will be vulnerable to JMOL.

For defendants and vendors: internal block diagrams and marketing materials can be turned into decisive noninfringement evidence—if you can show that the claimed signal simply does not exist at the claimed sampling point or that the implemented math differs from the claimed math (e.g., modulus and conjugation rather than literal multiplicative combinations). Cross‑examination that exposes ambiguity in an opposing expert’s mapping is a proven tactic.

For drafters: be explicit about signal identity, sampling points, and the algebraic nature of claimed operations. If a claim requires “three signals” consider defining whether those must be distinct or may be copies, and whether modulus or conjugate operations count as the same “multiplication” as multiplying distinct time‑domain copies. Dependent claims that specify signal generation location (e.g., pre‑ADC versus post‑ADC), number of unique inputs, or exact mathematical forms will give patentees fallback positions and reduce the risk that an ambiguous claim construction dooms a case.

For both sides, the decision suggests an increased role for early technical discovery and, potentially, early dispositive motions. When the accused device’s implementation details can be shown (or excluded) with technical artifacts, summary judgment or JMOL may be more attractive and more successful.

## Conclusion

Finesse v. AT&T is not a doctrinal revolution, but it is a crisp lesson about the evidentiary realities of modern patent litigation where signal processing and algebra are the contested terrain. Plaintiffs must prove, with clear, noncontradictory expert testimony and documentary mapping, that the accused device has the claimed signals at the claimed point and performs the claimed mathematical operations. Defendants should watch their internal diagrams and implementation details—those same materials that help them innovate can, if unaddressed, undercut infringement theories. The long‑term signal to industry is straightforward: in DSP‑heavy patents, prove the math, or risk having the verdict swept away with the static.
