Title: Blog about the opinion: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC

# When Legends Mislead: Federal Circuit Reverses on Conflicting Signal Mapping in Finesse v. AT&T

## Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit's decision in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC is a textbook lesson in the perils of shaky expert testimony and of leaning too heavily on vendor schematics and ambiguous legends to prove infringement of signal-processing patents. The panel reversed the Eastern District of Texas jury verdict of infringement for both the asserted ’134 and ’775 patents. For the ’134 patent, the reversal turned squarely on the plaintiff’s expert, whose inconsistent mappings of claimed terms to elements in a Nokia schematic left the jury with no substantial evidence to support infringement. The court invoked Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope to emphasize that self-contradictory expert testimony cannot sustain a verdict. The practical upshot is straightforward but consequential: in patents claiming particular signal paths, sampling behavior, or element-by-element processing, parties must anchor their infringement case in clear, coherent testimony and demonstrative evidence; otherwise the verdict risks being undone on appeal.

## The Federal Circuit’s reasoning in brief

The ’134 patent claims a method that, among other things, requires "over-sampling ... a passband of received signals" that "include signals of interest and interference generating signals." At trial, Finesse’s infringement theory rested on a Nokia technical document and the testimony of Dr. Jonathan Wells. The Nokia figure used a legend stating “Red – DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path” and showed a red path through the RF ADC, which Finesse told the jury contained both the claimed "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals."

On cross-examination, however, Apple’s (AT&T/Nokia’s) counsel highlighted a crucial timing detail: the modeled PIM path identified in Nokia’s materials is generated downstream of the RF ADC, i.e., after sampling. If the modeled PIM path is the thing the expert calls the “interference generating signals,” then the receiver could not literally be sampling it, and the claim’s sampling limitation would not be met.

Faced with that point, Dr. Wells repeatedly gave conflicting testimony. At times he said the interference generating signal was the modeled PIM path; at other times he suggested the red path really denoted two inputs, x1 and x2, and that those corresponded to the claimed signals. Critically, he never clearly mapped x1 or x2 to the claim language in the way the claims require. The district court denied JMOL and let the jury verdict stand; the Federal Circuit reversed. It found Dr. Wells’s testimony “confusing and unclear,” and concluded that where a plaintiff rests its case on an expert’s self‑contradictory testimony, courts may find the evidence insufficient to support a jury’s finding of infringement (citing Johns Hopkins).

The court applied similar evidentiary skepticism to the ’775 patent: Finesse used the same Nokia sources to claim the accused radios perform seven specific third‑order multiplications (S1×S1×S2, etc.). The Nokia document, however, only explicitly listed three distinct multiplications and used operations like complex conjugation and modulus. Dr. Wells did not provide a clear mapping of those three—or the purported ten operations elsewhere in Nokia materials—onto the seven claimed multiplications. The Federal Circuit held that, for the ’775 patent too, the record lacked substantial evidence that the accused devices performed the seven multiplications required by the claims.

Because the court reversed JMOL of infringement for all asserted claims, it vacated the jury’s $166 million damages award.

## Analysis: evidence, experts, and the dangers of "legend-based" proof

Two intertwined themes run through the opinion: the evidentiary requirement for coherent mapping between claim language and accused technology, and the limits of expert testimony when internal inconsistency creeps in.

First, the court reaffirms that patent infringement is a fact question that depends on substantial evidence—i.e., evidence from which a reasonable jury could have found for the patentee. When the nexus between claim limitations and the accused product is an annotated OEM schematic and expert explanation of that schematic, the jury’s ability to find infringement depends on clarity and internal consistency. A legend that groups items together (“Red – A & B”), without precise callouts or timing context, can be a trap if the underlying documents actually reflect a temporal or logical separation (here, sampling vs. post-sampling model generation).

Second, experts must be careful. The Federal Circuit’s citation to Johns Hopkins is instructive: conflicting testimony that creates more heat than light can be treated as insufficient to carry the patentee’s burden. That is especially true when the contradiction affects an elemental limitation (e.g., what the receiver actually samples). An expert may offer alternative theories, but those theories must be presented with clear mappings to claim terms, supported by demonstrative or documentary evidence, and resilient to straightforward cross-examination points like “when is this signal generated relative to sampling?”

Third, reliance on vendor materials—especially complex signal‑processing schematics that mix mathematical notation, diagrammatic legends, and conceptual models—requires careful translation. Plaintiffs must not only point to a figure and assert “these are the claimed signals,” they must explain unambiguously why the figure’s signals meet each claim limitation, including temporal ordering and whether the physical hardware actually performs the claimed operations.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

The decision is not a new rule of claim construction or a major redefinition of the sufficiency standard for infringement. But it tightens practical requirements. Lower courts and litigants should take away that:

- Expert testimony that wobbles between two inconsistent mappings risks being deemed insufficient as a matter of law on JMOL. A patentee cannot rely on an expert who alternates between literal and conceptual identifications without reconciling the inconsistency for the jury.

- When accused features hinge on the timing or flow of signals (e.g., whether something is sampled or generated post-sampling), documentary clarity is critical. Ambiguities in vendor schematics or legends that enable competing inferences will tend to doom a patentee whose expert testimony is equivocal.

- This decision will likely encourage defendants to probe timing and generation issues in cross-examination and to seek JMOL where the patentee’s proof rests on ambiguous legends and inconsistent expert mappings.

## Practical trial lessons

For patentees: prepare experts to offer a single, defensible mapping of claim terms to device elements, with demonstratives that make temporal relationships and signal flows unmistakable. If you rely on vendor docs, corroborate them with hardware-level evidence, firmware readings, or design depositions that nail down when a signal exists relative to sampling and processing stages.

For defendants: focus discovery and cross-examination on timing and causation questions that force the expert to choose a consistent mapping. Use the vendor documents’ own words against the expert when they reveal generation steps downstream of the asserted sampling.

For courts: the opinion is a reminder to police the line between reasonable expert interpretation and speculative or internally inconsistent testimony. Where a patentee’s entire proof of an elemental limitation depends on an expert who equivocates, judgment as a matter of law is a proper safety valve.

Finesse v. AT&T is not about the technical merits of PIM cancellation algorithms. It is about foundational proof: show your signals, show when they exist, and show how the accused device literally practices each claim element. If your expert’s legend-based mapping can be unstitched by one well-aimed cross-examination, the verdict may not survive appellate scrutiny.



# Counting to Seven: Numeric Claim Limits and the Perils of Partial Proof in Signal-Processing Patents

## Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in Finesse Wireless v. AT&T/Nokia is a crisp lesson in a recurring theme of modern patent litigation: when claims prescribe specific numerical or structural requirements for algorithmic operations, courts will demand proof that the accused product actually performs those precise steps — not something that looks like them, or could be mapped to them by post hoc reasoning. For practitioners who prosecute or litigate patents in digital signal processing (DSP), telecommunications, or any field where math blocks and high-level diagrams stand in for running code, the opinion emphasizes that expert testimony tied to documentation must bridge the gap between representation and implementation. Partial proof — a few analogous products of an operation, or a chart of computations that uses conjugates or magnitudes rather than literal multiplies of raw signals — will often fall short.

The decision reverses the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement for both asserted patents, including the ’775 patent whose claim required “generating an n-th order ICS by, given three signals S1, S2 and S3, digitally multiplying and filtering S1×S1×S2 and S1×S2×S2 …” (seven explicit third-order products). The Federal Circuit held that the evidence at trial — principally Nokia design documents showing three distinct multiplications (involving complex conjugates and moduli) — did not supply substantial evidence that the accused radios actually performed the seven claimed multiplications or that the jury could reasonably infer the remaining multiplications were produced.

Read through the opinion and its practical takeaway is straightforward: numeric claim limits and enumerated algebraic operations invite literalness. When claim language counts to seven, a jury verdict needs to be supported by evidence that the accused product counts to seven, not by extrapolation from three related computations.

## What the court did and why

The court’s analysis of the ’775 patent focused on two linked issues: (1) whether the claim required three “separately identifiable” signals that must be unique (it did not, by the district court’s construction); and (2) whether the accused radios actually performed the seven third-order multiplications recited by the claim. The Federal Circuit rejected appellee Finesse’s argument that claim scope had been surrendered to exclude two-input implementations; it accepted the district court’s narrower construction that three signals need only be separately identifiable, not necessarily unique.

But that construction did not save the verdict. The evidentiary underpinning of Finesse’s infringement theory rested on a Nokia technical document and expert testimony that identified three distinct multiplications present in the design: x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', and x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2| (using modulus and complex conjugate notation). The Federal Circuit observed that the document did not show the seven enumerated multiplications required by the claim, nor did the expert map those three documented operations to the seven claimed products in a way that was presented to the jury. The court emphasized that the arithmetic in the Nokia documents involved modulus and conjugate operations rather than literal multiplications of S1, S2, and S3, and that Finesse’s attempt to show mapping on appeal was not evidence the jury saw. On that basis, the court concluded the jury’s infringement finding was not supported by substantial evidence and reversed JMOL denial.

In short: the court demanded proof of performance of the specific, enumerated algebraic steps recited in the claims and refused to permit a jury to infer compliance from high-level blocks or from undocumented mappings proposed for the first time on appeal.

## An analytical perspective: literalness versus functional equivalence

The Finesse decision sits at the intersection of two perennial tensions in patent law: literalism in claim reading and the functional, implementation-focused nature of modern computing systems. Courts long have allowed some flexibility — for example, claims can be met by functionally equivalent structures under certain doctrines, and claim construction can use intrinsic and extrinsic evidence. But Finesse underscores that where claims specify explicit enumerations or counts of operations, the patentee carries a heavy burden to show those enumerations are met.

There are two distinct but related evidentiary failures here. First, the Nokia materials enumerated only three distinct operations whereas the claim required seven; Finesse needed to show either that those three correspond to the seven (e.g., by showing duplicates or repetitions in implementation) or that the design in fact carried out seven operations. Second, the operations in the Nokia document were not literal S1×S2×S3 products but involved modulus and conjugates — mathematically related but not identical forms. The court reasonably insisted that an expert explain how those different algebraic forms are coextensive with the claimed multiplications, and that the jury be given that explanation in the form of admissible evidence. Post hoc appellate mappings are no substitute for trial proof.

This reflects an important evidentiary principle: when claims recite a sequence of algebraic transformations or a finite enumerated set of computations, high-level block diagrams or symbolic summaries in vendor documentation need to be translated into concrete, traceable evidence of operations — for example, firmware listings, execution traces, test vectors, or design logs that show the steps actually being performed. Experts can and should perform that translation, but the translation must be presented clearly and consistently at trial; self-contradictory or belated expert mappings are an unreliable bridge.

## Doctrinal implications and potential downstream effects

Practically, this opinion will have ripple effects in several arenas:

- Litigation strategy in DSP, telecom, and software patents: Plaintiffs must prepare to prove the precise algorithmic steps they claim. Reliance on vendor white papers or block diagrams without corroborating evidence of implementation will be vulnerable to JMOL. Defendants should press early for source code, simulations, execution logs, and other hard artifacts showing what the accused system actually computes.

- Claim drafting and prosecution: Patent drafters who anticipate enforcement against complex systems should consider alternative claim drafting strategies. When the invention is algorithmic, including claim language that captures both the concrete enumerated operations and broader functional equivalents can help. But broad functional claims increase the risk of invalidity challenges; the safer path is to include dependent claims that enumerate permutations and alternative algebraic formulations that the inventor contemplates.

- Expert testimony standards: The opinion reinforces that expert testimony must clearly map the accused product to claim elements, and that contradicting testimony or fuzzy mappings will undercut the plaintiff’s case. Experts should prepare demonstrative, reproducible mappings (with step-by-step transformations) that the jury can follow — and that can survive Daubert/Rule 702 scrutiny if challenged.

- Appeal posture and preservation: The court rebuffed Finesse’s attempt to shore up evidentiary gaps on appeal. That’s a reminder that crucial expert mappings and technical exegeses must be front-and-center at trial; appellate courts will not credit new mappings absent a trial record.

Doctrinally, Finesse does not mark a dramatic new rule. Rather, it reinforces existing principles: claims control, proof must match claims, and substantial evidence requires a reasonable basis in the trial record. But in contexts where “math blocks” and symbolic notations are the lingua franca, the opinion is a clear warning that courts will not permit elegant but speculative mathematical equivalences to substitute for proof of performance.

## Best practices for proving algorithmic DSP claims

For patentees and their experts, the Finesse decision suggests a checklist to avoid the pitfalls that doomed the jury verdict:

- Gather and preserve low-level implementation artifacts. Source code, FPGA bitstreams, firmware release notes, compiler outputs, design commit logs, test vectors, unit-test outputs, and packet captures can show what the system actually computes and when. Vendor diagrams are helpful but insufficient.

- Produce traceable execution evidence. If the claim requires seven distinct multiplications, run tests or instrumented builds that log or demonstrate those seven computations during operation. Controlled test benches that exercise claimed modes make for persuasive demonstratives.

- Prepare a clear 1:1 mapping. Have the expert prepare a line-by-line mapping between claim limitations and specific code sections or signal flow elements, explaining any algebraic transformations (e.g., why a modulus-and-conjugate expression is equivalent to the claimed product in context), with reproducible steps.

- Avoid hindsight mappings. Mapping arguments that are developed only on appeal or in briefing, rather than presented at trial, are risky. Present the mapping early, tie it to demonstrable evidence, and be ready to defend it on cross-examination.

- Use test vectors and numerical examples. For signal-processing claims, numerical instantiation can be compelling: show concrete inputs and the computed outputs that match the claimed operations. That reduces the risk the jury treats the mapping as speculative.

- Consider claim alternatives. In prosecution, consider dependent claims that cover different canonical mathematical forms (conjugate forms, modulus-based formulations) if those are plausible implementations, so that the claim language aligns better with how real systems are engineered.

For defendants, the decision underscores robust defense tactics: obtain and leverage implementation artifacts; press to have experts dissect the precise computations; target any gaps where the plaintiff is asking a jury to infer repeated or unshown operations; and seek JMOL where the evidence is partial and the plaintiff rested on ambiguous expert testimony.

## Conclusion

Finesse Wireless v. AT&T/Nokia is a reminder that when patents require counting — whether it is seven multiplications, a fixed sequence of transforms, or a finite set of operations — courts will ask for evidence that the accused product actually performs that count. In the dense world of DSP and telecom, where diagrams, symbolic math, and block design docs are common, mapping those materials onto claim language is a technical and evidentiary exercise that must be done squarely at trial. For practitioners, the lesson is practical and stark: if you claim seven, be ready to show seven — and show it with artifacts that the jury can see and an expert who provides a consistent, reproducible mapping.



# No Clear Disavowal: “Three Signals” as Separately Identifiable, Not Unique—Drafting and Litigation Lessons

## Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in Finesse Wireless v. AT&T (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025) is an instructive microcosm of two perennial issues in patent litigation: what a prosecution amendment means for claim scope, and how fact-bound testimony and expert mapping can make or break an infringement verdict. Although the court reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL and vacated a nine-figure damages award on noninfringement grounds, the panel expressly rejected AT&T and Nokia’s contention that Finesse gave up claim scope to “two signals” during prosecution simply by amending to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3.” Citing Continental Circuits, the Federal Circuit held that the intrinsic record did not constitute a “clear and unambiguous” disavowal; at most, the prosecution history required three “separately identifiable” signals, not three unique inputs. That interpretive posture has immediate repercussions for drafters, prosecutors, and litigators working in the DSP/communications space (and beyond) where claims often turn on whether inputs are distinct or may be copies/derivations of the same signal.

Below I unpack the court’s reasoning, assess its doctrinal posture, and offer practical drafting and litigation takeaways to help practitioners preserve or challenge “uniqueness” limits when they matter.

## What the court said (and why it rejected the surrender argument)

The ’775 patent claims a method that generates intermodulation cancellation signals by digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in a set of third-order products. At summary judgment the district court construed “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as “separately identifiable,” not necessarily “unique.” On appeal, AT&T and Nokia argued Finesse had disclaimed two-signal implementations during prosecution to overcome the Filipovic reference and thus should be limited to devices that actually take three unique inputs.

The Federal Circuit disagreed. It acknowledged that the applicant amended the claims to recite three signals and overcame an obviousness rejection, but concluded the prosecution history did not rise to the level of a clear and unambiguous surrender of two-signal territory. Finesse’s arguments to the examiner focused on differences in the type of IMPs addressed (passive versus active IMPs) rather than a categorical statement that the claims require three unique physical inputs. The court thus held the amendment supported a reading that the three signals must merely be separately identifiable (i.e., distinguishable in the claimed method), not that they must be three different physical signals in the accused device. The panel relied on Continental Circuits’ framework for disavowal: to narrow claim scope through prosecution statements, the applicant’s remarks must be clear and unmistakable.

That doctrinal holding, though favorable to patentees in the abstract, did not save Finesse overall. The court reversed the infringement verdicts because it found the infringement proof was legally insufficient in other respects—principally, that the patentee’s expert had not shown the accused products in fact performed the seven specific multiplications required by the claims and gave confusing, self-contradictory testimony mapping signals in Nokia documents to the claimed signals.

## Doctrinal implications: disavowal remains narrow; prosecution focus matters

Two doctrinal lessons stand out.

First, the decision reaffirms that prosecution-based narrowing is a high bar. Continental Circuits and the Federal Circuit’s precedents require “clear and unmistakable” statements to find disavowal. That principle is doing the work here: an amendment to add “three signals” and some argument distinguishing prior art on functional or contextual grounds do not automatically equate to a surrender of two-signal embodiments unless the applicant explicitly and unambiguously told the examiner that the claims exclude two-signal implementations.

Second, the case illustrates that the content and focus of the applicant’s remarks matter. When patent applicants distinguish prior art on substantive grounds such as the kind of problem solved (e.g., passive vs. active IMPs) rather than on a structural point (e.g., “we require three distinct input signals and therefore the two-signal reference is out”), courts will usually treat the prosecution history as narrowing only insofar as the applicant expressly tied the amendment to a specific limiting feature. In short: context and framing during prosecution determine whether an amendment will be read as a surrender.

Third, the opinion also reflects how even favorable claim constructions can be insufficient if the patentee’s proof at trial is weak or inconsistent. The panel affirmed that construction but reversed infringement because the evidentiary record—chiefly the expert’s mapping and the accused-product documentation—did not reliably show the claimed seven multiplications were performed.

## Limits on inventor testimony and extrinsic evidence

The Federal Circuit’s opinion recognized—but did not overvalue—extrinsic evidence (inventor testimony). While the district court credited inventor testimony that S1, S2, S3 “represent signals generally” and could be the same or different, the appeals court was skeptical that this extrinsic material could overcome the intrinsic record to the extent of creating an unambiguous surrender. This is consistent with the standard approach: intrinsic evidence—claims, specification, prosecution history—controls; extrinsic evidence can illuminate but not rewrite clear intrinsic meanings.

For practitioners, the takeaway is two-fold. For patentees: inventor testimony can be helpful to prevent a narrow construction, but it should be buttressed by clear specification language and prosecution arguments. For defendants: relying on inventor statements to show disavowal is difficult unless the record contains unequivocal admissions.

## Drafting lessons — how to write claims and spec to capture or avoid “uniqueness”

If “uniqueness” of inputs matters to your client, the drafting phase is the best time to make that explicit.

- If you want claims to require distinct physical inputs: say so. Use clear claim language (e.g., “wherein S1, S2, and S3 are distinct signals” or “three distinct input signals”) and reiterate in the specification why distinctness is required for the invention’s operation. Provide embodiments and figures showing and describing three separate signal sources.

- If you want broader coverage that allows copies/derivations or repeated inputs: use language that emphasizes identifiability rather than uniqueness. Define terms like “separately identifiable” or “independently distinguishable” in the spec, and show examples where copies or delayed/filtered versions are used as multiple inputs. Explicit examples of using the same physical signal in multiple roles (e.g., a copied, delayed, or conjugated version counted as a separate “signal” for the method) will reduce the chance of an adverse construction.

- Use dependent claims strategically. If there is doubt over whether three unique inputs are required, include dependent claims that recite “three unique input signals” or “three physically distinct transmitters” to preserve a narrower fallback in prosecution and litigation.

- In the specification, explain why the number of distinct inputs is significant (or not). The more the specification explains that functional distinguishability suffices, the better positioned a patentee will be to argue that “three signals” need only be separately identifiable.

- Don’t rely solely on examples. Broad claim language supported by multiple embodiments is stronger than depending on a solitary example to show flexibility.

## Prosecution strategy — how to amend without unintentionally surrendering scope

When you amend claims to overcome prior art, be very careful about the language you use and the explanations you give to the examiner.

- If you must add a numerical limitation (e.g., “three signals”), explain why the number is essential in terms that are specific and functional if you intend to require it, or explain that “three” is intended as a “minimum” or an “identifiable-set” to preserve coverage of copies/derivatives.

- When distinguishing prior art, avoid language that could be read as an explicit disclaimer unless you intend to narrow the scope. For example, instead of saying “the prior art uses only two signals and thus fails to teach three signals,” explain the technical difference you rely on (signal processing context, types of IMPs, timing relationships) to emphasize that the amendment addresses a substantive limitation rather than excluding a class of embodiments.

- Preserve fallback claim language when possible. If you must narrow, add dependent claims with broader formulations or alternative phrasing to leave room for interpretation later.

- Record: place in the file history clear statements if you intend the amendment merely to identify signals conceptually rather than to require three distinct transmitters. The Federal Circuit’s reliance on context in Finesse shows that carefully worded prosecution remarks can avoid unintended disavowal.

## Litigation strategy — preserving and proving “uniqueness” (or its absence)

Litigation hinges on the evidence you present at Markman, summary judgment, and trial. The Finesse opinion highlights several tactical imperatives.

- Preserve your claim construction arguments. If “unique” versus “identifiable” is dispositive to infringement, raise and litigate that dispute at the Markman stage. Make the prosecution history record as explicit as possible in briefs and at argument.

- Build a clear chain from claim language to accused product. The Court reversed on grounds separate from the Continental Circuits question: Finesse’s expert failed to map the accused radios’ actual processing onto the claimed seven multiplications and, in other testimony, created confusion about which signals the RF ADC sampled. To withstand JMOL and appellate review, your expert must provide a coherent, non-contradictory mapping from accused-product documents and operation to each claim limitation.

- Use device documentation effectively. If you argue the accused product uses only two unique inputs, produce internal docs, block diagrams, or firmware descriptions that show the signal chain and timing that demonstrate that only two distinct physical inputs exist. Conversely, if you represent the patentee and want to avoid a uniqueness requirement, produce specification passages and inventor statements showing how copies, conjugates, or modulus operations can serve as distinct “signals” in the claimed sense.

- Avoid confusing or self-contradictory expert testimony. The Federal Circuit explicitly faulted Finesse’s expert for inconsistent testimony regarding whether the modeled PIM path was generated before or after sampling and whether x1/x2 corresponded to claimed signals—contradictions that doomed the sufficiency of the evidence. Carefully prepare experts to give consistent, document-backed mappings, and rehearse likely cross-examination points that could expose ambiguity.

- Preserve Rule 50(a) challenges. The opinion underscores that appellate review of sufficiency requires showing that no reasonable juror could have found infringement. Make sure JMOL/regulatory motions are timely and fully developed (including pointing to any affirmative weaknesses in the patentee’s proof).

## Downstream effects and closing thoughts

Finesse is a useful reminder that prosecution amendments alone do not lightly narrow claim scope absent clear, unmistakable statements to that effect. For drafters and patentees, the decision says: if you want broader coverage, hedge with careful spec language and fallback claims; if you want to limit scope, say so plainly so the public notice function of patent claims is effective. For litigators, the opinion spotlights evidence quality: even if claim construction is favorable, a shaky or inconsistent evidentiary record can be fatal at JMOL.

Finally, the decision reinforces a broader theme in modern patent law: courts will protect the public’s entitlement to reasonable claim breadth where the record does not plainly show an applicant intended otherwise, but patentees must still prove infringement with clear, consistent mappings from paper claims to silicon and firmware. Where uniqueness of inputs matters, the safest route is prevention—clear drafting, careful prosecution statements, and, in litigation, unimpeachable expert testimony grounded in the accused device’s own documentation.



# Substantial Evidence with Teeth: The Federal Circuit’s Renewed Policing of Expert Testimony

## Why Finesse matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in Finesse Wireless v. AT&T/Nokia is a compact but consequential reminder that appellate courts will not defer to jury verdicts that rest on fuzzy or self-contradictory expert testimony. Applying the Fifth Circuit’s standard for denying JMOL at the district level but reviewing infringement findings for substantial evidence under Federal Circuit precedent, the panel reversed the denial of JMOL on infringement for asserted claims of two patents and vacated a large damages award. The opinion is notable less for new doctrinal rules than for its emphatic application of long-standing principles: when a patentee’s case turns on an expert’s mapping of claimed elements to accused systems, that mapping must be coherent, consistent, and actually presented to the jury. Otherwise, the appellate court will step in.

Finesse thus sits in a line of decisions — including Apple v. Wi-LAN and Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope — in which the Federal Circuit polices the sufficiency of the evidentiary record underlying jury findings of infringement. For litigants who regularly litigate in the Eastern District of Texas or other patent hot spots, Finesse is a practical signal: technical complexity does not insulate a case from appellate reversal when the record is sparse, internally inconsistent, or relies on speculative inferences by experts.

## What the court decided and why

Two patents were at issue. The ’134 patent claimed a method in which a receiver must sample a passband that includes both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” The ’775 patent claimed a method that depends on generating seven specific third‑order multiplications of three signals S1, S2, and S3. At trial, Finesse’s expert relied heavily on Nokia technical documents and his testimony to map the claimed inputs and operations onto features of the accused radios. The jury found infringement and awarded more than $166 million in lump‑sum damages.

On appeal, the Federal Circuit concluded the evidence supporting both infringement findings was not substantial. On the ’134 patent, the expert repeatedly identified the “modeled PIM path” as the interference‑generating signal, but the record showed that modeled PIM signals were generated downstream of the ADC — i.e., after sampling — so they could not be the “interference generating signals” that the claim requires the receiver to have sampled. The expert’s testimony was at best internally inconsistent, at worst incoherent: he vacillated between saying the red path in a diagram contained two signals (x1 and x2) that mapped to the claimed inputs and saying the red path was the DL(TX) reference and the modeled PIM path. The panel relied on Johns Hopkins v. Datascope to hold that where a party bears the burden of proof, an expert’s self‑contradictory testimony cannot sustain a jury verdict.

On the ’775 patent, the court held (1) prosecution history did not amount to a clear and unmistakable surrender of two‑signal implementations; but (2) independent of any surrender argument, there was insufficient evidence that the accused radios actually performed the seven claimed multiplications. The technical document relied on by Finesse listed three distinct multiplications (and possibly up to ten items in a larger table), but the expert never tied those entries, coherently and in evidence, to the seven claimed multiplications. The panel emphasized that merely asserting a mapping on appeal is not enough; the mapping must have been presented to the jury and supported by the record.

Across both patents, the court applied the Fifth Circuit’s deferential standard for JMOL at the district court, but then reversed under the Federal Circuit’s substantial‑evidence review because the record could not reasonably sustain the verdicts.

## Analytical perspective: what the opinion is doing

Finesse does not announce a new limitation on expert testimony nor does it change claim construction law. Instead, it enforces existing limits on how much speculative, equivocal, or internally inconsistent expert opinion can do at trial. Two features of the opinion are worth underscoring.

First, the court is insisting on a clear chain of evidentiary linkage: (a) the expert must identify what, concretely, in the accused device corresponds to each claim limitation; (b) the mapping must be internally consistent and not undercut by the same expert’s testimony; and (c) the mapping must be presented to the jury in admissible, comprehensible form. In both challenged claims, the expert failed on one or more of those points.

Second, the decision reinforces that appellate courts will not swallow after‑the‑fact clarifications offered on appeal. Finesse’s attempt to supply a mapping in briefing and oral argument — for example, to explain how ten listed multiplications might correspond to the seven claimed ones — could not cure the absence of a clear, contemporaneous record before the jury. That is a practical, and doctrinal, rule: substantial‑evidence review looks to the actual trial record, not to conceptions formulated later.

The Federal Circuit’s reliance on Johns Hopkins is also telling. There, the court reversed where an expert’s contradictory trial testimony could not credibly support a finding of infringement. Finesse applies that template in a modern, signal‑processing context: the technical complexity of a device does not excuse sloppy or contradictory testimony.

## Doctrinal implications and likely downstream effects

Finesse crystalizes a pattern that litigants and district courts should note:

- Experts must do the heavy lifting at trial. When infringement turns on a complex mapping of claim elements to device internals, the expert should (1) identify the precise structures/signals/software modules at issue; (2) explain how those elements meet each claim limitation with a clear, step‑by‑step mapping; and (3) reconcile any documentary or demonstrative evidence that might appear ambiguous. Passing off important gaps as “diagram labels” or “implicit mappings” risks appellate reversal.

- Trial demonstratives and technical documents matter. If a party intends to rely on a vendor’s slide or internal doc to show performance of a claim limitation, the expert must translate that document into a coherent, non‑speculative explanation for the jury — ideally with a demonstrable chain showing where and how an operation is performed (and when, in time, in cases like sampling). Ambiguous legends and shorthand notations in vendor materials do not substitute for explicit, admissible explanation.

- Appellate courts will not permit reclamation of missing proof on appeal. One recurrent theme in Finesse is that counsel’s appellate clarifications — even if plausible — do not remedy deficiencies that existed at trial. That will affect both litigation strategy and settlement calculus: a patentee with a marginal record cannot count on appellate rescue.

- The decision is neutral on claim construction theory but practical in effect. The court rejected a waiver/surrender argument on the ’775 patent, showing some restraint in reading prosecution history as disavowal. Yet the court reached noninfringement on evidentiary insufficiency. Practitioners should not read Finesse as easing claim construction rules; rather, it heightens the evidentiary bar needed to support a verdict.

Collectively, those effects will likely increase the premium on careful pretrial preparation in tech‑heavy cases: clearer expert reports, thorough demonstratives, targeted direct examination, and predictable responses to likely cross‑examination points that could reveal an expert’s own uncertainty.

## Practical takeaways and appellate‑preservation tips

For patentees / plaintiffs:
- Lock down your mapping early. Your expert report should do more than describe a mapping in generalities; it should identify the exact signals, modules, or code paths and explain temporality (e.g., what exists before sampling, what is generated after).
- Anticipate cross‑examination pitfalls. If your authoritative doc uses shorthand (e.g., a legend that lumps two items together), prepare a crisp explanation and, if possible, a corroborating diagram that disambiguates the shorthand for the jury.
- Put the mapping in evidence multiple ways. Live testimony is critical, but also use demonstratives, admissions, deposition clips, and demonstrable lab reproductions. The appellate court will look to the entire record, and multiple forms of corroboration reduce the risk that a single equivocal answer will be dispositive.
- Avoid “on the fly” pivots. If an expert is forced into a new theory at trial (e.g., claiming x1/x2 are the claimed inputs only after cross‑examination), the lack of prior notice and supporting analysis will undermine the verdict on appeal.

For defendants / accused infringers:
- Target the linkage. Cross‑examination that exposes internal inconsistency or temporal impossibility (e.g., pointing out that the “modeled” signal is created after sampling) can be decisive at JMOL or on appeal.
- Preserve Rule 50(a) motions carefully. The Federal Circuit applied the Fifth Circuit’s standards and was willing to reverse where evidence was plainly insufficient; make sure arguments emphasizing evidentiary gaps are pressed at the Rule 50(a) stage so they can be revisited on appeal.
- Use focused Daubert/Kastigar‑style challenges. Where the patentee’s theory hinges on a complex model, a timely challenge to methodology or an expert’s qualifications to map software/hardware behavior can shorten the path to JMOL.

For district courts:
- Manage technical testimony closely. Gatekeeping is not only about admissibility; it includes ensuring that expert testimony is presented in a way a jury can reasonably rely on. Where testimony is internally inconsistent, consider curative instructions or limiting what the jury may treat as settled fact.
- Watch for late‑appearing theories. Parties should not be permitted to spring materially new mappings at trial without adequate notice and foundation.

## Conclusion

Finesse is not a surprise. It simply applies familiar appellate doctrines — substantial‑evidence review and the rule that an expert’s contradictions cannot sustain a verdict — in a contemporary, signal‑processing case. But its practical bite should be felt in every technical patent trial: juries cannot be asked to fill evidentiary gaps created by ambiguous diagrams, shorthand legends, or equivocal expert testimony. Litigants would do well to treat Finesse as a cautionary tale: if you cannot explain, clearly and consistently, how every claim element maps to the accused product before and during trial, you may have a verdict that looks good in the courtroom but will not survive appeal.



# Damages on a House of Cards: $166 Million Lump Sum Vacated After JMOL Reversal

## Why this case matters

When a jury awarded Finesse Wireless a $166.3 million lump-sum royalty for two patents covering methods to mitigate passive intermodulation (PIM) in radios, the award looked like a big win for a patent-holding plaintiff asserting complex wireless-technology claims. The Federal Circuit’s September 24, 2025 opinion in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC, however, shows how quickly a damages verdict built on an undifferentiated, portfolio-style or feature-based lump sum can collapse when liability fails. The court reversed JMOL-denials of noninfringement for all asserted claims of both patents and vacated the life-of-patent lump-sum damages award. The opinion underscores two interrelated lessons: (1) damages awards that rest on a single, large-number lump-sum tied to multiple patents or product features are especially vulnerable if the evidence of infringement is weak on essential claim elements; and (2) careful, claim-by-claim, feature-by-feature linkage between the asserted claim elements and the damages model is indispensable.

## What the Federal Circuit held

The Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for both the ’134 and ’775 patents and vacated the damages award. The court’s substantive rulings turned on the sufficiency of Finesse’s infringement evidence.

For the ’134 patent—which requires that a receiver “over-sampl[e] a passband of received signals” that includes both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals”—the court concluded that Finesse’s expert testimony was internally inconsistent and, crucially, failed to show the accused Nokia radios actually sampled the claimed “interference generating signals.” Finesse’s expert repeatedly identified a “modeled PIM path” in Nokia documents as the interference-generating signal, but the evidence showed that the modeled PIM path was generated after the receiver’s ADC sampling stage. The expert’s shifting testimony—later attempting to treat the two inputs x1 and x2 as the claimed signals—was ambiguous and contradicted earlier mappings. The panel found that where the patentee bears the burden of proof, contradictory expert testimony cannot supply substantial evidence of infringement.

For the ’775 patent—which claims generating intermodulation cancellation signals by digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven specified third-order combinations—the court concluded that the Nokia documents and the expert’s trial testimony established at most three distinct multiplications (and possibly ten listed in a separate Nokia document), but did not support the contention that the seven specific claimed multiplications were performed by the accused radios. The expert had not explained how the three (or ten) multiplications mapped onto the seven claimed products, and the documents described modulus and complex-conjugate operations that did not straightforwardly show the seven claimed multiplications. Thus, the court held the jury’s verdict of infringement was not supported by substantial evidence.

Because the court reversed noninfringement JMOL denials for all asserted claims, it vacated the damages award rather than remand on damages—there was no longer a valid infringement basis to sustain the life-of-patent lump sum.

## Why the court vacated rather than remanded

The panel did not simply remand the damages issue for recalculation; it vacated the award. That outcome follows directly from the appellate rulings: damages are remedial and depend on proven liability. If, as here, liability (infringement) has been reversed for all asserted claims, there is no legal basis on which to sustain an award of damages tied to those claims. The Federal Circuit therefore vacated the judgment as to damages and taxed costs to the defendants.

Put differently: remand on damages generally makes sense where some liability remains or where the appellate court alters only a portion of the liability findings. When the appellate court concludes that liability itself collapses across the asserted set of claims, nothing remains to support the jury’s damages calculation, so vacatur rather than recalculation is the predictable and doctrinally correct result.

## Doctrinal implications — proof, apportionment, and lump sums

Finesse reinforces several doctrinal points now familiar in modern patent litigation, and it sharpens certain practical boundaries.

First, the decision is a reminder that the Federal Circuit will demand coherent, substantive expert linkage between asserted claim elements and the accused product. Courts will not credit a damages or infringement story that depends on an expert’s shifting or internally inconsistent testimony. Johns Hopkins and other precedents make clear that where an expert’s opinion is the patentee’s proof, it must be consistent, well-founded, and tethered to the record.

Second, the case highlights the fragility of portfolio-wide lump-sum awards and feature-based lump sums when the plaintiff’s infringement proof does not survive careful scrutiny. A lump-sum, life-of-patent number can obscure the necessary mapping from individual claims (and specific claim limitations) to the value or value increment provided by the accused products or features. When that mapping fails because key claim elements are not proved, the whole structure can fall.

Third, the decision illustrates the continuing importance of apportionment. Even where infringement is established, a lump sum that aggregates across patents, features, or an undefined market must still satisfy basic apportionment principles: the royalty must be tied to the value that the patented invention contributes, not to the entire value of a multi-feature product. That principle cuts both ways—if an infringement verdict relies on only some claims or features, damages experts must show how the awarded sum corresponds to those claims/features, and cannot rely on an undifferentiated portfolio premium.

## Downstream effects for damages strategy

Practitioners—both plaintiffs and defendants—should absorb the practical lessons this opinion drives home.

For plaintiffs and their damages experts:
- Tie damages to proven claim elements. The damages model should include discrete pathways that connect specific asserted claim limitations to discrete product features and revenues. If a claim requires a particular internal signal path or a particular multiplication, the expert should show (with documents, code, or clear expert testing) that the accused product uses that exact path or operation and that the feature is economically meaningful.
- Prepare alternative damages scenarios. Present a primary model rooted in the strongest infringement theory, but also show fall-back models that apportion value by claim, feature, or market segment so that if some claims are lost, a robust residual model survives.
- Use clear, documentary mappings. Relying on a proprietary vendor document is fine, but experts must explain, in non-contradictory terms, how the document maps to claim language. If a document shows intermediate-model signals generated after sampling, the expert must reconcile that with patent requirements (or concede noninfringement for that theory).
- Avoid single, undifferentiated lump sums without a claim-by-claim and feature-by-feature bridge to revenue. Juries may award big numbers on the basis of persuasive narratives; the record must sustain those narratives on appeal.

For defendants:
- Challenge the linkage early and often. Motions in limine, Daubert challenges, and targeted cross-examination on the mapping between claimed elements and product operation can create the record for JMOL or JMOL alternatives if experts’ testimony is internally inconsistent.
- Attack apportionment and nexus directly. Even a sizable lump-sum award may be vulnerable if the plaintiff cannot show the patented technology drove the market or commanded a segregable portion of the product’s value.

For courts and juries:
- Be attentive to expert clarity. As the Federal Circuit said, when the patentee rests its case on expert testimony that is internally inconsistent, reasonable jurors cannot rely on it as substantial evidence. Trial judges should be willing to require clarity at the time of trial, both in testimony and in the underlying demonstratives and documents.

## How to structure damages evidence to survive a liability setback

If you represent a patentee who expects liability challenges, consider these concrete structural steps when building the damages case:

- Build modular damages models that can be recomputed at varying levels of liability. For example, produce damages spreadsheets keyed to (a) each asserted claim, (b) each accused product line, and (c) each accused radio feature (e.g., PIM cancellation on/off, inner vs. outer band). Make these independent so they survive whether the jury finds infringement of all, some, or none of the claims.

- Provide claim-feature charts that every damages witness can rely upon. Create explicit claim-to-feature charts that link each asserted limitation to a tangible product component, software module, or operational mode. Use the same exhibit across technical and damages experts to avoid inconsistent testimony.

- Quantify incremental value and nexus. Tie the patented feature to customer demand, willingness to pay, or cost savings. Use market evidence (marketing materials, product advisories, RFPs) and competitive comparisons to show the claimed feature had an observable effect on sales or pricing.

- Offer apportionment analyses grounded in economics and market reality. Avoid purely proportional or “because it’s a core function” arguments. Use discrete comparables, hypothetical-license baselines, or component-level profit/cost evidence where possible.

- Present alternate royalty structures that are defensible in the absence of full liability: running royalties by unit or per-feature, component-only lump sums for only those devices proved to infringe, or short-term lump sums for limited remaining life. Each alternative should be anchored in the Georgia-Pacific factors or equivalent negotiation-driven logic.

- Vet and harmonize expert testimony in advance. Ensure technical and damages experts use the same mappings and underlying documents. Cross-examine extensively in deposition to flush out any ambiguities before trial.

## Conclusion

Finesse Wireless is a cautionary tale for patentees who rely on an all-or-nothing lump-sum damages narrative without an ironclad, internally consistent evidentiary bridge from claim elements to product features and value. The Federal Circuit’s reversal and vacatur turned on failures of proof at the element level—confusing expert mappings and a lack of demonstrated performance of the claimed multiplications. The practical takeaway is straightforward: build damages models that are modular, tightly linked to proven claim elements, and capable of surviving partial or total reversals of liability. Lump sums can be persuasive to juries; they are perilous on appeal unless the record makes crystal clear why a single number equals the economic value of the patented contributions the jury has actually found.



# The Road Not Taken: Unreached Constructions of “Receiver” and “Co‑Located” in Interference‑Cancellation Claims

## Why this case matters

Finesse Wireless v. AT&T/Nokia ends with a tidy appellate reversal: the Federal Circuit concluded the record lacked substantial evidence that the accused radios performed the signal‑sampling and multiplication steps the asserted claims require, and thus reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL and vacated the damages award. But the opinion leaves open two doctrinally and practically important questions—how to construe “receiver” (in the context of sampling and what counts as the “passband of received signals”) in the ’134 patent, and what “co‑located” means for a transmitter and receiver in the ’775 patent—because the panel explicitly declined to reach them. Those unresolved constructions matter because they go to basic fault lines in radio architecture litigation: where in a base‑station pipeline does “receiving” occur, and how close must transmit and receive chains be to meet a “co‑located” limitation? The court’s disposition underscores a recurring lesson: even when a case collapses on the sufficiency of an expert’s mapping to claim language, claim construction battles remain vital and litigants should preserve them.

## What the court did — and did not — decide

The Federal Circuit reversed infringement findings for both patents on record‑specific sufficiency grounds. For the ’134 patent, which claims a method that begins by “over‑sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals,” the court focused on the jury evidence tying the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals” to elements in Nokia documents and to Dr. Wells’s testimony. The court found the expert’s testimony internally inconsistent—at times equating the “interference generating signals” to a “modeled PIM path” that the accused radios only generate downstream of the RF ADC (i.e., after sampling), and at other times invoking two inputs x1 and x2 that might be sampled. Because the asserted infringement theory depended on treating a signal generated after sampling as one of the “received signals” the RF ADC samples, the panel held that no reasonable jury could have found the receiver sampled the claimed passband that included both kinds of signals.

For the ’775 patent, the court rejected the jury’s verdict because the evidence did not show the accused radios performed the seven third‑order multiplications the claims require; Nokia’s documents listed three distinct multiplications (or, as argued at oral argument, perhaps ten different terms in a polynomial expansion), but Dr. Wells did not map those to the precise seven claimed multiplications in the way the jury would have needed.

Notably, the panel expressly declined to resolve two contested claim‑construction issues: what counts as a “receiver” for purposes of the ’134 claims, and what “co‑located” means for a transmitter and receiver in the ’775 claims. Those terms potentially would have been dispositive on different evidence.

## Why the withheld constructions matter

“Receiver.” The heart of the ’134 dispute is architectural: is the RF ADC (the antenna‑frontend analog‑to‑digital converter) the locus of the claimed “passband of received signals,” or can signals that are produced internally after digitization—e.g., modeled signals within an FPGA—be treated as “received” for the purposes of the claimed sampling step? A narrow construction that ties “receiver” to the physical front‑end and the point where electromagnetic signals become a sampled bit‑stream will foreclose theories that rely on modeled or internally generated signals as inputs to cancellation algorithms. Conversely, a broader construction that permits downstream internally generated representations to count as “received signals” could expand infringement theories to encompass systems that synthesize reference paths after sampling for adaptive cancellation.

The Federal Circuit’s reluctance to adopt either approach leaves future litigants with multiple plausible paths. A patentee confronted with similar architecture will want a construction that locates “receiving” squarely at the RF front‑end; an accused infringer will press for a construction that excludes post‑sampling digital artifacts. The court’s opinion hints at the importance of that line: part of the insufficiency problem was precisely the mismatch between a post‑sampling modeled path and the requirement that the passband include the interference‑generating signals. But the panel did not adopt a fixed doctrinal rule about when a signal is “received,” leaving lower courts to confront the issue anew.

“Co‑located.” The ’775 claim requires performing interference cancellation “in a receiver, with a transmitter and the receiver being co‑located with each other.” That clause is plausibly meant to capture common real‑world scenarios (cellular base stations where transmit and receive chains share antenna systems or site hardware) and to distinguish the passive‑IMP mitigation of the claims from solutions that assume distributed transmit/receive setups. How strictly should “co‑located” be read? Does it require a shared physical enclosure, immediate adjacency on the same RF chain, or merely geographical proximity (same tower or site)? The district court construed “three signals” as “separately identifiable” rather than “unique,” but the co‑located question was not litigated to the end because the panel reversed on the multiplication issue. A narrower construction of “co‑located” would be a defense boon in many telecom contexts (cloud radio or distributed MIMO systems), while a looser construction benefits patentees who want to sweep in a wider range of deployments.

## Doctrinal and practical implications

First, the case is a reminder that claim construction and proof at trial are complements, not alternatives. A robust, jury‑level infringement case often depends on a clear, tethered mapping from accused device elements to claim language. When that mapping is ambiguous or internally inconsistent—especially on architectural questions like where “receiving” happens—the patentee’s burden is at risk, regardless of the ultimate construction. Hence, litigants should work to secure clear claim constructions early and to ensure their experts’ demonstratives and testimony build an unambiguous bridge from accused products to the construction.

Second, appellate courts may be willing to reverse on sufficiency grounds without taking on technically difficult claim‑construction questions. That can be strategically good or bad depending on the side: an accused infringer gets a win now but the unsettled construction remains available for other suits; a patentee sees a setback without a controlling interpretation it might have used elsewhere. In crowded standards‑based markets, this “no‑opinion” on construction can leave follow‑on litigation unresolved and splinter doctrine across districts.

Third, prosecution history and amendment practice matter. The opinion reiterates familiar rules about prosecution disclaimer and claim amendments: amending to recite “three signals” did not in this record amount to a clear disavowal of systems using two unique inputs, but patentees should remain mindful that amendment language and the surrounding arguments can be used to pin down claim scope. For litigators, preserving alternate claim‑construction theories (and raising them at Rule 50(a) in appropriate form) is essential, because a sufficiency reversal may otherwise render those arguments unaddressed and available for subsequent suits.

## What litigants and patentees should take away

For patentees: make sure claim constructions that bear on architectural divides—what it means to “receive” a signal or to be “co‑located”—are preserved and pressed below. Prepare expert testimony that cleanly maps each claimed element to an accused product under whatever construction the court adopts; avoid contradictory live testimony that gives appellate courts an easy sufficiency hook.

For accused infringers: press early for narrow constructions that pin down where in the signal chain claim elements occur. If the case proceeds to trial, exploit any inconsistencies in the patentee’s mapping and preserve your Rule 50 arguments as to both construction and sufficiency.

The Federal Circuit’s reversal in Finesse Wireless resolves this dispute on the facts, but by leaving “receiver” and “co‑located” undecided it spotlights the continuing role that clear claim construction plays when modern patents meet layered, modular radio architectures. The road not taken in this opinion will likely be the scene of future doctrinal skirmishes—until a court picks one of these forks and says, definitively, where receiving ends and co‑location begins.



# Customer‑Suit Dynamics in the 5G Era: OEM Intervention, Evidence Control, and Strategic Risk

## Why this case matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in Finesse Wireless v. AT&T/Nokia is not just another routine patent reversal. At base it is a vivid reminder that as networks become more complex and OEMs increasingly embed sophisticated signal‑processing features in radios, the interplay among carrier defendants, equipment manufacturers, and the evidentiary record can decisively shape infringement outcomes. The court reversed the denial of JMOL on infringement for asserted claims of two patents directed to PIM (passive intermodulation) cancellation and vacated the damages award. The reversals were driven by the court’s view that the plaintiff’s infringement case rested on confused or insufficient expert testimony tied to OEM technical documents — documents that can be simultaneously indispensable and dangerous.

Practitioners in telecom litigation should take note: OEM technical materials and the expert narratives built around them can make or break a case. How parties handle OEM intervention, coordinate experts across defendants, and curate technical exhibits for trial increasingly determines both liability and damages exposure.

## What the court did — the analytical core

Two issues drove the Federal Circuit’s analysis.

First, for the ’134 patent, claim 1 required that the accused receiver “over-sample[] . . . a passband of received signals” that includes both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” Finesse’s infringement theory leaned on a Nokia diagram and its expert testimony mapping signals on that diagram to the claimed terms. But the expert’s testimony was internally inconsistent and, crucially, the diagram’s “modeled PIM path” appeared to be generated after the radio’s ADC sampling. If the “interference generating signals” are only generated after sampling, they are not “received” signals within the meaning of the claim. The Federal Circuit concluded that Finesse’s expert testimony was too equivocal and self‑contradictory to support the jury verdict — when the party bearing the burden of proof rests its case on such testimony, JMOL is appropriate.

Second, for the ’775 patent, the claim required generating seven distinct third‑order products from three separately identifiable signals (S1, S2, S3). The court accepted the district court’s construction that “separately identifiable” does not necessarily mean “unique,” but it nevertheless found that the Nokia documents and the plaintiff’s expert only showed three distinct multiplications; there was no evidentiary bridge showing the seven claimed multiplications were actually produced. The Federal Circuit therefore held the evidence was insufficient as a matter of law.

In short: (1) ambiguous expert testimony tied to ambiguous or time‑sequenced elements of OEM diagrams is legally dangerous; and (2) pointing to block‑diagram labels, modulus/complex‑conjugate operations, or lists of coefficients in OEM materials without a clear mapping to claimed mathematical operations will often fall short.

## Tactical lessons on OEM intervention and evidence control

This case surfaces a cluster of strategic issues that litigants in telecom and software‑defined hardware disputes should confront.

First, OEMs are likely to intervene when a carrier is sued over equipment features. Intervention gives the OEM the ability to control technical records, provide declarants, and present its own expert to rebut or refine the carrier’s case. That can help defendants — but it also introduces coordination challenges. Carrier counsel and OEM counsel must align on defensive themes (e.g., noninfringement mappings, sequence of operations relative to sampling) and on how to present technical diagrams without overreaching interpretive inferences that the plaintiff can attack at trial.

Second, technical documents are double‑edged. Diagrams and legends are indispensable to explain complex signal paths. But as the Federal Circuit shows, they can be fatal when an expert leans on a legend or block label that, upon closer inspection, does not support a literal claim element (e.g., a signal “modeled” post‑ADC cannot be one of the “received” signals sampled by the ADC). Practitioners should therefore vet OEM materials early for any sequence or causation issues that a plaintiff can exploit. If an OEM device produces intermediate, modeled, or calculated signals downstream of the alleged point of sampling, that temporal sequence must be anticipated and integrated into the defendants’ expert narrative.

Third, the coordination of expert testimony matters. The court faulted the plaintiff for relying on an expert whose testimony appeared contradictory — at different points mapping labels to different signals and not offering a clear, consistent mapping to claimed elements. Defendants should strive for crisp, reproducible expert explanations that connect operation diagrams to claim constructions with precision: identify where signals exist in time, where they are measured or sampled, and show (if true) how mathematical operations in code/algorithms correspond to claimed multiplications or filtering steps. Avoid relying on inferences pieced together from testimony “ninety pages apart,” as the court expressly discounted that approach.

Fourth, consider the evidentiary chain for mathematical claims. The Federal Circuit did not accept an appeal‑time mapping from three listed operations to the seven claimed products. If claims require a specific number or pattern of algebraic operations, defendants and plaintiffs alike need to present concrete traceable derivations: if an OEM document lists coefficients or ten possible multiplier terms, explain to the jury which subset maps to the claim and why any remaining terms are not cumulative or repeated versions of the claimed operations.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Doctrinally, the decision underscores two recurring appellate themes. One is the court’s willingness to reverse juries when the patentee fails to carry its burden with reliable, consistent expert testimony that ties the accused product to claim language. That reinforces Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope and related authorities: expert ambiguity can be fatal where the patentee has the burden. Second, the opinion illustrates the limits of relying on extrinsic OEM materials without a rigorous evidentiary bridge to the claims. Block diagrams, legends, and internal technical notes are not magic: labels alone do not substitute for proof that the device performs the claimed steps at the claimed times.

For defendants, the decision will encourage aggressive early focus on the temporal sequencing of signals (what exists before and after ADC sampling, what is modeled downstream) and on identifying gaps in a patentee’s mapping from technical exhibits to claim elements. For plaintiffs, the lesson is blunt: do not rest on convenient readings of legends or inconsistent expert testimony — be prepared to show an unambiguous, step‑by‑step mapping from OEM documentation (and from code/firmware where available) to each claim limitation.

## Practical takeaways

- When an OEM intervenes, use that opportunity to introduce declarants and engineering witnesses who can explain sequence and causation around signal generation and sampling.
- Coordinate expert testimony tightly: produce demonstratives that map specific signals and operations to claim limitations with a temporal narrative (before sampling/after sampling).
- Audit OEM documents for downstream “modeled” or simulated signals that could be mischaracterized as “received” or “sampled” inputs; prepare fallback theories supported by firmware, logs, or unit tests.
- For claims requiring specific arithmetic operations or counts (e.g., “seven multiplications”), build a clear evidentiary path — do not rely on labels, lists of coefficients, or post hoc mappings.

Finesse Wireless v. AT&T/Nokia is a cautionary tale for an era where radio functionality is implemented in firmware, FPGAs, and DSP chains. As devices get smarter and signal chains more abstract, litigation will hinge less on broad characterizations and more on fine‑grained proof about when a given signal exists, where it is sampled, and what arithmetic operations actually occur. The lawyers who appreciate that complexity and translate it into tightly controlled evidence and expert narratives will fare best.



## From PIM to JMOL: Proving Interference Cancellation in Complex Radios

Why this case matters
Finesse Wireless v. AT&T (Fed. Cir. Sept. 24, 2025) is a useful reminder that technical sophistication cannot substitute for clear, claim‑by‑claim proof at trial. The Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for two patents directed to passive intermodulation (PIM) cancellation in radios because the patentee’s infringement case rested on confusing expert testimony and inadequate mappings between the claimed elements and the accused products. For practitioners who litigate patents in fields that intermingle hardware, firmware, and digital signal processing (DSP), the decision crystallizes a practical rule: when you sue over signal‑processing algorithms and mixed analog/digital systems, precision in the record — not rhetorical flourish — is what carries the jury’s verdict through appellate review.

What the court did and why
The appeals turned on two separate claim packages: the ’134 patent, which claims a method that begins with sampling a “passband of received signals” containing both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” and the ’775 patent, which claims generating intermodulation cancellation signals by computing seven specific third‑order multiplications of three signals S1, S2, and S3. The Federal Circuit reviewed whether substantial evidence supported the jury’s finding of infringement and whether the denial of JMOL was proper under Fifth Circuit standards.

On the ’134 patent, the patentee’s expert repeatedly pointed to a Nokia technical slide in which a red path in the diagram was labeled “DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path,” and testified that the receiver’s RF ADC samples both the signal of interest and the interference‑generating signal. But cross‑examination showed the modeled PIM path in the document is generated downstream of the ADC — in the FPGA — and thus could not have been sampled by the ADC. The expert’s testimony wavered: at times he appeared to equate the two red‑path boxes with two inputs x1 and x2, but he never clearly mapped x1 and x2 to the claimed “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” The court held that this contradictory and confusing testimony could not support the jury’s verdict; where the patentee bears the burden of proof, an expert’s self‑contradiction can doom the case.

On the ’775 patent, claim scope required that seven particular third‑order multiplications be produced from three separately identifiable signals. The district court had construed “three signals” as separately identifiable but not necessarily unique. The patentee’s expert relied on Nokia materials that showed three distinct multiplications (involving complex conjugates and moduli) and argued — without a clear, trial‑record mapping — that those operations satisfied the seven claimed multiplications. The Federal Circuit held that the evidence showed only three distinct operations and no support that the seven claimed multiplications were actually produced; the patentee’s post‑hoc mappings were not presented to the jury and could not be supplied on appeal.

Analytical perspective: why precision mattered here
Finesse underlines two related evidentiary truths in DSP‑heavy patent litigation. First, when a claim turns on where in a signal chain a particular signal exists — pre‑ADC versus post‑ADC, inside an FPGA versus on an analog path, internal versus external PIM sources — the plaintiff must put on unambiguous evidence that ties the claimed signal to the accused device at the right physical or logical point. A diagram with a conflated legend (e.g., “A & B”) plus equivocal expert testimony is a brittle foundation once the defendant shows that a labeled element is actually produced downstream of the claimed sampling action.

Second, when claims recite algorithmic specifics (here, seven distinct third‑order multiplications of three signals), the patentee must walk the jury through each claimed computational step with demonstrative clarity. It is not enough to show the accused product performs “something like” the claimed math; the jury record must identify which signals correspond to S1, S2, S3, which multiplications are computed, and whether any listed operation is repeated or transformed (e.g., via modulus or conjugation) in a way that preserves the claimed structure. The Federal Circuit’s approach is not hostile to jury fact‑finding, but it requires that the technical mapping be presented coherently so the court can find substantial evidence on appeal.

Doctrinal implications
Finesse sits at the intersection of a few well‑established doctrines. The decision applies the familiar substantial‑evidence standard for jury findings of infringement, but it also reminds litigants of the Johns Hopkins line of cases in which inconsistent expert testimony can be fatal when the patentee bears the burden of proof. The court did not announce new claim‑construction rules or attempt to tighten inventorship or prosecution‑history estoppel doctrines; rather, it enforced the practical consequence that amorphous expert narrative cannot fill gaps in the evidentiary record.

A secondary doctrinal point concerns claim amendments during prosecution. The Federal Circuit declined to find clear and unambiguous disclaimer where the patentee amended claims to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3” as opposed to “two or three signals,” finding the prosecution history did not amount to surrender of coverage over two signals. But that was not enough to save the verdict because the patentee still failed to prove that the accused radios in fact produced the seven specific multiplications required by the claims. So the takeaways are twofold: claim amendments can be decisive if prosecution history clearly disavows scope; and even absent clear disavowal, the patentee still must present concrete trial proof that the accused device practices the claimed elements.

Practical downstream effects for litigators and experts
Finesse should prompt plaintiffs and their experts to make deliberate evidentiary choices in complex electronics cases. Two practical consequences stand out.

First, demonstratives and device‑level evidence must tie the claim language to the device’s real signal flow. Diagrams that conflate labels or rely on high‑level legends are dangerous unless the expert can point to hardware traces, software blocks, logs, firmware code, or oscilloscope captures that show where each claimed signal exists and when relative to sampling and processing events. If a claimed element depends on whether something exists pre‑ or post‑ADC, get the oscilloscope screenshots, block‑diagram exports, and firmware flow that show the timing and locus of those signals.

Second, for algorithmic claims, have a trial‑ready mapping that explains each claimed arithmetic operation. This means preparing exhibits that break the accused algorithm into discrete steps, show the actual data representations used (complex samples, conjugates, magnitudes), and demonstrate how each of the claimed multiplications is realized (including any repetition or permutation). If your theory relies on interpreting conjugates or magnitudes as satisfying multiplicative terms in the claim language, build before trial an admissible chain of evidence (expert linear algebraic explanation plus device evidence) showing that those transformations are functionally equivalent to the claim’s multiplications, and present that mapping to the jury rather than saving it for appellate briefing.

A practitioner’s checklist, derived from Finesse
Prove signal locus relative to sampling: If the claim requires sampling a passband that contains particular signals, show through device artifacts and timing evidence that those signals exist at the sampling point. Don’t rely on a slide legend or on downstream model‑signals; instead, demonstrate the physical or logical presence of the claimed “interference generating signals” at the ADC or other sampling block with data captures, firmware traces, or authenticated design documentation that the expert can point to confidently on direct examination.

Demonstrate closed‑loop adjustments concretely: For claims that require closed‑loop phase and amplitude adjustment (especially when the claim calls for sub‑sample phase shifts), put on cumulative evidence of the control loop: the sensing path, the feedback path, the digital implementation of phase‑shifting (e.g., fractional‑delay filters or interpolation used to effect sub‑sample shifts), and logs or test vectors showing the loop operating. Explain how the claimed sub‑sample shifts are implemented in the accused system (what algorithm, what interpolation order), and show any timing or convergence data that supports the “closed‑loop” characterization.

Map each algorithmic operation to an artifact: For claims that enumerate specific multiplications or other arithmetic operations, produce a trial exhibit that lists the claimed operations and shows, one by one, where each operation is performed in the accused device. If the accused product computes only a related set of operations (conjugates, moduli, repeated terms), show by admissible analysis whether and how those operations correspond to the claimed terms, and make that correspondence a part of the expert’s direct testimony. Don’t leave such mappings to appellate argument.

Avoid internally inconsistent expert narratives: Prepare experts to testify with a single, coherent mapping throughout direct and cross. If an expert must change a theory, refresh the record on the new mapping and make sure the definitive mapping is stated plainly and repeatedly in testimony and demonstratives. The Federal Circuit is likely to treat equivocation — particularly on core, dispositive issues — as fatal when the patentee carries the burden of proof.

Preserve critical issues at the right stages: If you plan to rely on a particular claim construction (for example, whether the “three signals” must be distinct or unique), raise and preserve that at the claim‑construction and 50(a) stages. But remember that preserving a construction is necessary, not sufficient: you must also build the evidentiary record that ties the accused product to that construction.

Conclusion: precision beats persuasion in DSP cases
Finesse is not a rejection of sophisticated technical theories; it is a reminder that the judicial system requires those theories to be translated into a clear, coherent record at trial. When your case turns on the existence, timing, or mathematical form of signals inside a radio, don’t trust a colorful slide or a persuasive sound bite. Prepare the artifacts, prepare the expert to map claim term to device element unambiguously, and walk the jury — and the appellate court — through each required step. In DSP‑heavy patent litigation, precision wins where persuasion alone may fail.
