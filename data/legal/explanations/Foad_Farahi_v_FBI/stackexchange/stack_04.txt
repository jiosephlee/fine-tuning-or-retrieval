### Why do ongoing criminal investigations qualify as 'enforcement proceedings' under 7(A)?
Question:
Exemption 7(A) protects records if disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with 'enforcement proceedings.' The court, citing CREW and Juarez, treats an ongoing criminal investigation as such a proceeding. From a statutory-interpretation standpoint, how did courts get from 'proceedings' (which sounds formal) to including pre-charge investigations, and what limits exist on that reading?
Answer:
Short answer
Courts treat an ongoing criminal investigation as an “enforcement proceeding” under Exemption 7(A) because the statutory word “proceedings” is read functionally, not formally: if an investigation is actively gathering evidence for a possible criminal prosecution, it is the kind of law‑enforcement activity that Exemption 7(A) protects. That understanding is limited, however: the proceeding must be “pending or reasonably anticipated,” the agency must carry its FOIA burden (with reasonably specific affidavits), and vague or stale assertions will not suffice.

Why “proceedings” covers pre‑charge investigations (the doctrinal move)
- Functional, not formal, reading: The court follows this Circuit’s precedent (CREW and Juarez) that “proceedings” in Exemption 7(A) need not mean only formal court or administrative adjudications. Instead, an investigation that is actively gathering evidence for a possible future criminal case is treated as a prospective enforcement proceeding. The opinion explicitly cites CREW and Juarez (¶ IV.B) and quotes Juarez: “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies.” J.A. cited in ¶ IV.B (Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59).
- Statutory text plus function: The court applies the statutory requirement that records be “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and then asks whether disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” That interference question is sensibly answered by asking whether disclosure would harm an ongoing investigatory effort that aims to produce prosecutable evidence (¶ IV.B).

What courts require before accepting an investigation as a “proceeding”
- Pendency or reasonable anticipation: Following CREW and Mapother, this Circuit insists the proceeding be “pending or reasonably anticipated” (¶ I, ¶ IV.B). A bare, speculative hope of a future prosecution will not do; there must be a concrete prospective enforcement effort.
- Agency burden and specificity: The government bears the FOIA burden to justify Exemption 7(A). Courts accept agency declarations, but they must establish a rational nexus between the records and law‑enforcement duties and provide enough detail to show the existence and scope of the investigation (¶ IV.A, citing Campbell and Pratt). The opinion notes that the government may show pendency by attesting to the investigation’s “existence” (CREW, ¶ IV.B), but the court also warns against purely “vague” assertions (CREW language quoted in ¶ IV.B).
- Context and corroboration: The panel accepted a combination of public and ex parte affidavits here—Hardy’s public declaration and a more detailed ex parte declaration—plus a later update by Bender confirming the investigation remained ongoing. The court explained that the terse update was sufficient only because it was read in context of the earlier, detailed submissions (¶ IV.B). So specificity may be satisfied by the record as a whole, including sealed material the court reviews in camera.

Practical limits on the expansive reading
- Not unlimited: The opinion and its precedents set real limits. The proceeding must be pending or reasonably anticipated; courts will not accept conclusory or stale statements. CREW’s caution about the “vague nature” of assertions was applied here: the court required updated, reasonably specific declarations because of the “passage of time” (¶ IV.B).
- Temporal and factual constraints: Passage of time can undermine an assertion that proceedings are pending; an agency must update its showing if circumstances change (the district court ordered exactly that in this case, ¶ II). The government’s showing must be credible in light of the record (the panel relied on the earlier detailed ex parte filing and public allegations about the subject’s alleged connections to terrorism to sustain the claim of ongoing investigation, ¶ IV.B).
- Segregability and scope: Even if Exemption 7(A) applies, the agency must still segregate and release nonexempt information, and categories used to justify withholding must be sufficiently distinct (¶ V and ¶ IV.C). The court will scrutinize whether claimed categories are overbroad (the opinion found Farahi forfeited that challenge here, but reiterates the standard from CREW and Gallant).

Bottom line
The Circuit reads “proceedings” to include ongoing investigations because the statute’s purpose is to prevent disclosure that would interfere with law‑enforcement functions—an active investigation that aims to produce evidence for prosecution is squarely within that purpose. But the government cannot rely on mere speculation or stale, conclusory statements; it must show (through reasonably specific declarations and, when necessary, timely updates) that an investigation is ongoing or reasonably anticipated and that disclosure would likely interfere. The Farahi opinion applies those limits: it accepted the FBI’s showing because of the combination of public and ex parte detail and a timely update confirming continued investigatory activity (¶¶ IV.A–B, V).
