Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation

### How demanding is the segregability obligation when sensitive information is interspersed throughout, and was ex parte proof alone enough?
Question:
The district court found, based on Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission, that no reasonably segregable non-exempt material remained, citing Mead’s 'interspersed line-by-line' principle. In 7(A) cases, do courts require in camera review of samples or the records themselves, or can detailed affidavits suffice? What counts as 'reasonable steps' to segregate under § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) in an investigation file of 10,000+ pages?
Answer:
Short answer
- Segregability under FOIA is context‑specific but not toothless: an agency must take “reasonable steps” to identify and produce nonexempt material and must explain with reasonable specificity why further segregation would not yield meaningful, nonexempt information. When nonexempt material is truly “interspersed line‑by‑line,” courts have accepted that little or nothing is “reasonably segregable.” (See Mead Data Cent., 566 F.2d at 261, cited at J.A. 139–40.)
- Courts do not always require in camera review of the full record in Exemption 7(A) cases; carefully supported agency affidavits (including ex parte/supplemental submissions when necessary) can suffice. This opinion accepted the combination of public and ex parte declarations here rather than ordering a complete in camera inspection. (See J.A. 138–40; Perioperative Servs., cited at J.A. 139–40.)
- What counts as “reasonable steps” depends on the file and the agency’s showing. The FBI’s approach here — updating its prior declarations, consulting the case agent, and providing a detailed ex parte explanation (supplementing public affidavits) — met the district court’s and this Court’s standard for reasonableness given that the nonexempt material was interspersed throughout a 10,000+ page file. (See J.A. 121–22, 138–40.)

Why the court reached this result (doctrine and the opinion’s specifics)
1) The legal standard. The agency must disclose “any reasonably segregable portion” of a record after deleting exempt material (5 U.S.C. § 552(b); § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)). The controlling line of D.C. Circuit precedent recognizes two relevant points cited in the opinion:
   - If nonexempt material is “inextricably intertwined” or “interspersed line‑by‑line” with exempt material, it may not be “reasonably segregable.” Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260–61 (1977) (quoted at J.A. 139–40).
   - Courts may “rely on government affidavits that show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld … cannot be further segregated.” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (cited at J.A. 139).

2) What the FBI did here (and why the court accepted it). The record shows a sequence the court found sufficient:
   - The FBI submitted an initial public declaration (Hardy) asserting the law‑enforcement nature of the files and that disclosure could interfere with ongoing investigations (J.A. 42, 49).
   - The district court, concerned about staleness and segregability, required updated information (J.A. 121–22).
   - The FBI provided updated public and ex parte declarations from Bender and an ex parte supplement to Hardy. Bender confirmed the investigation remained ongoing and the ex parte submission explained in detail why nonexempt material could not meaningfully be segregated (J.A. 138–40).
   - The district court credited that combination and concluded reasonable steps had been taken and that no meaningful segregable information remained (J.A. 139–40). The D.C. Circuit affirmed, stressing that the ex parte material was not improper and that the court did not have to make its reasoning public where disclosure would defeat the protective purpose (J.A. 139–40; Perioperative Servs. quote at J.A. 139).

3) When ex parte proof is enough — and when a court may want more. The opinion teaches three practical points:
   - Ex parte or sealed submissions are permissible when disclosing the basis for withholding would itself reveal sensitive information; the court accepted that dynamic here (J.A. 139–40).
   - Ex parte submissions need not stand alone if the government has already provided public affidavits showing a factual predicate. The Court treated Bender’s terse public update as an update to, not a replacement for, the more detailed Hardy declarations (J.A. 131–32, 138–39).
   - Nevertheless, courts have rejected conclusory, “vague” assertions of ongoing investigation in other cases (see CREW, 746 F.3d at 1098, discussed at J.A. 136–38). So ex parte evidence must supply the specificity the public declarations lack; the absence of challenge here (Farahi did not oppose ex parte filings) further weakened his ability to contest reliance on them (J.A. 139).

Practical guidance for a 10,000+ page investigative file
- The agency should (and here did) do more than assert a blanket claim. Reasonable steps typically include:
  - A document‑by‑document or sampled review sufficient to explain how nonexempt snippets are “interspersed” such that extraction would be meaningless or would itself reveal exempt content.
  - Consultation with the investigative case agent to confirm current status and identify which portions, if any, could be released without harm (the opinion notes RIDS contacted the agent — J.A. 138–39).
  - Detailed affidavits that identify categories of documents, explain why segregation is impractical or harmful, and, where appropriate, provide examples (the Court credited the detailed ex parte Hardy submission and Bender’s update — J.A. 138–40).
- The court did not require a blind order to produce segregable words or phrases; it evaluated whether meaningful information remained after any feasible redaction (Mead principle at J.A. 139–40). When remaining nonexempt parts convey no useful information independent of the exempt material, they need not be produced.

Bottom line
- Segregability is not a formalistic demand for line‑level redaction in every instance; it requires reasoned, document‑specific (or sufficiently sampled) explanations showing that further segregation would not yield meaningful, nonexempt information or would itself reveal exempt content.
- In Exemption 7(A) cases involving national security or ongoing investigations, well‑supported affidavits — including ex parte submissions when necessary and accompanied by public declarations — can satisfy the agency’s burden without full in camera review, provided they supply the necessary specificity. The FBI’s updated public and ex parte declarations met that standard here, and the district court’s reliance on them was upheld (J.A. 138–40).
