Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Parties and context: Farahi’s immigration history and FBI involvement in DHS’s removal case (alleged terrorism links)
Foad Farahi is an Iranian national who has lived in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) placed him in removal proceedings for overstaying his visa. During those immigration proceedings, DHS relied in part on material and live testimony from an FBI agent, asserting that Farahi had ties to high‑level terrorists and organizations the State Department has designated as foreign terrorist organizations. DHS’s filings in court and the Eleventh Circuit’s subsequent summary of the immigration record identify allegations that Farahi acted as a recruiter, had connections to Khaled Sheikh Mohammed and other al Qaeda associates, was linked to convicted or indicted terrorists (including Jose Padilla), and supported charities accused of financing terrorism. Those allegations provide the factual and investigative context for the FBI records at issue in this FOIA litigation.

## FOIA request scope and volume: approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs located
Farahi filed a Freedom of Information Act request seeking his FBI file. The FBI’s search located a very large body of potentially responsive material—about 10,750 pages and roughly 80 compact discs. That volume and the sensitive national‑security and counterterrorism context of the materials shaped how the FBI processed the request and how the courts handled the litigation.

## FBI’s initial processing, partial releases, and withholdings; missed statutory deadline leading to suit
The FBI reviewed the located materials over several years. It released some records to Farahi but withheld most of the pages and electronic records, invoking FOIA exemptions. The agency did not meet FOIA’s initial statutory response deadline, and Farahi sued to compel a timely response. After that suit was filed, the FBI continued its review, ultimately completing a substantive production and asserting exemptions for much of the material. The litigation became a typical FOIA summary‑judgment contest in which the agency relied on declarations to justify withholding while the requester contested the sufficiency of those justifications.

## The Hardy declarations: public and ex parte filings supporting Exemption 7(A) and categorical withholdings
When the FBI first moved for summary judgment (October 2019), it supported its motion with a public declaration from David Hardy, then Section Chief of the FBI’s Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Hardy’s public declaration asserted that the withheld records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings—principally investigations relating to national security and international terrorism. The FBI also sought leave to file a supplemental, sealed, ex parte declaration from Hardy that would provide additional factual detail the public declaration could not safely disclose. Farahi did not oppose that request, and the district court granted it in October 2020. Thus the agency’s initial withholding justification rested on both a public affidavit and a sealed ex parte submission that the court could review in camera.

## District court’s November 2022 order: apparent sufficiency on 7(A) but request for updated status and segregability details due to passage of time
In November 2022 the district court considered the FBI’s first summary‑judgment submission. The court found that the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and concluded the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden to show disclosure could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. But the court was concerned about the passage of time: declarations submitted in 2019/2020 might no longer reflect the current status of any investigation. The court therefore denied summary judgment without prejudice and ordered the FBI to update its assessment of whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or were reasonably anticipated. The court also said it needed additional information to decide segregability (whether any nonexempt portions could be released), and it explained that the reasons it needed more detail could not be placed on the public record—hence the expectation of further sealed filings.

## Renewed motion with Bender declarations (public and ex parte) confirming ongoing investigations and addressing segregability
The FBI renewed its summary‑judgment motion with updated supporting material: a public declaration and a sealed ex parte declaration from Joseph Bender, who by then was Acting Section Chief of RIDS. The public Bender declaration explained that RIDS had contacted the FBI agent responsible for overseeing the Farahi file, and that agent confirmed the relevant investigations remained ongoing. The Bender ex parte declaration supplied more detailed, classified or sensitive factual information about the investigation and explicitly addressed the district court’s segregability concerns—explaining why meaningful nonexempt information could not be segregated and released without compromising the investigation. In short, the Bender submissions were intended to be updates to Hardy’s earlier public and sealed declarations: confirming that the FBI’s earlier representations remained true and providing the additional sealed detail the district court had requested.

## District court’s grant of summary judgment: crediting ongoing proceedings and accepting segregability showing
Relying on the Bender submissions (including the sealed ex parte declaration), the district court granted the FBI’s renewed summary‑judgment motion. The court credited the FBI’s confirmation that the relevant law‑enforcement proceedings remained ongoing and accepted the agency’s explanation—provided in camera—that the exempt material was not reasonably segregable from the withheld records. The court therefore concluded Exemption 7(A) applied and that the agency had satisfied its obligation to disclose any reasonably segregable, nonexempt material (the court found there was none that could be meaningfully released).

## Appeal to the D.C. Circuit
Farahi appealed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit. On appeal the principal disputes included whether the FBI had adequately shown the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” whether disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that were “pending or reasonably anticipated” given the passage of time, whether the FBI’s document categories were sufficiently distinct, and whether the agency had carried its segregability burden. The D.C. Circuit’s opinion (Katsas, J., Sept. 30, 2025) affirms the district court: it accepts that the records were law‑enforcement records, concludes the agency adequately showed an ongoing investigation that would be jeopardized by disclosure, and upholds the district court’s finding that no meaningful nonexempt material was reasonably segregable.

# Governing FOIA Framework and Standards

This chapter explains the statutory framework and the doctrinal standards that the court applied in Farahi v. FBI, No. 23-5243. It focuses on the burdens the government must carry to invoke FOIA Exemption 7(A), the role and required content of agency declarations (including ex parte/in camera submissions), the segregability obligation, and the appellate standard of review. Each legal point is tied to how the district court and this Court treated the FBI’s submissions in Farahi.

## FOIA baseline: disclosure mandate and government’s burden

FOIA begins with a strong presumption of disclosure: agencies must release requested records unless a statutory exemption applies. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(3), (b). When a requester sues, the statutory scheme places the evidentiary burden on the government to justify any withholding. The court in Farahi repeatedly framed that allocation: the government had to show that a claimed exemption covered the withheld material, and it did so through sworn agency declarations. The opinion reminds students that courts do not presume exemptions; the agency must carry its burden under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B), and summary judgment in FOIA is an adjudication of whether that burden has been met on the administrative record.

## Exemption 7(A): law-enforcement purpose and interference with proceedings

Exemption 7(A) excepts from disclosure records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). That provision has two discrete elements the agency must prove: (1) the records were compiled for a law‑enforcement purpose, and (2) disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings. In Farahi the FBI satisfied the first element by showing, through public and sealed declarations, that the file was created in the course of national‑security and counterterrorism investigations. The opinion highlights that when the FBI investigates alleged ties to terrorism — as the agency alleged here — the required “nexus” to a law‑enforcement duty and the connection to a possible security risk are readily established.

## “Pending or reasonably anticipated” proceedings requirement

The statutory interference language has been judicially read to require that the enforcement proceedings be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” That standard, taken from CREW v. DOJ and earlier decisions, means the agency must show more than a speculative future interest: there must be a concrete prospective law‑enforcement proceeding or an ongoing investigation gathering evidence for a possible case. In Farahi the court credited the FBI’s public Hardy declaration and the more detailed ex parte Hardy declaration showing an ongoing counterterrorism investigation. The court treated the later Bender declaration as an update confirming that the previously described investigations remained ongoing rather than as an initial, standalone showing.

## Deference to law enforcement and national‑security considerations

The opinion reiterates the settled principle that courts afford agencies — and the FBI in particular — a measure of deference when they invoke law‑enforcement FOIA exemptions. Cases like Campbell and CNSS emphasize that this deference is especially appropriate where national‑security or counterterrorism interests are implicated. That deference does not relieve the agency of its evidentiary burden, but it means courts will credit agency explanations about law‑enforcement purposes, investigatory scope, and the risk of harm from disclosure, so long as the agency’s submissions are sufficiently specific and plausible. In Farahi, the nature of the allegations in related immigration proceedings (ties to designated organizations and convicted terrorists) provided contextual support for the FBI’s claim of ongoing national‑security investigations.

## Use of affidavits/declarations: sufficiency and specificity

Agencies ordinarily meet their FOIA evidentiary burden through sworn declarations (affidavits) by knowledgeable officials. Those declarations must describe with “reasonable specificity” the nature of the documents, the basis for withholding, and the reasons disclosure would cause harm, so a court can meaningfully assess the exemption claim without disclosing sensitive material. The Farahi litigation illustrates several practical points about this requirement. First, declarations can become stale: the district court denied the FBI’s first summary‑judgment motion without prejudice and ordered updated declarations because of the passage of time. Second, declarations need not be entirely public; the government may submit classified, sensitive, or detailed factual material ex parte or in camera to provide the court the necessary specificity without making the information public. The Court accepted that approach here: the public Hardy declaration established the existence of an investigation, the sealed Hardy declaration supplied detail, and the Bender declaration confirmed the continuing pendency of the proceedings.

## Segregability requirement and the “inextricably intertwined” doctrine

FOIA requires agencies to take “reasonable steps” to segregate and release nonexempt material and to provide any “reasonably segregable portion” of a record after deletion of exempt portions. 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); § 552(b). Courts will accept agency affidavits that, with reasonable specificity, explain why nonexempt material cannot be sensibly segregated. The “inextricably intertwined” doctrine allows withholding an entire record where nonexempt portions are so intermingled with exempt information that disclosure of the nonexempt material would itself reveal the exempt content or would be impracticable to separate. In Farahi the district court — after reviewing the FBI’s sealed ex parte submission — concluded the agency had adequately shown that meaningful nonexempt information was not reasonably segregable because relevant nonexempt phrases and words were interspersed throughout and disclosure would undermine the investigation. The opinion cites Mead and Juarez for the proposition that isolated words or phrases are not the FOIA’s focus and that line‑by‑line interspersion can make segregation unreasonable.

## Categorical withholdings and sufficiency of categories

When an agency withholds records on a category‑by‑category basis, the categories must be “sufficiently distinct” to permit judicial review of the asserted exemptions. See Gallant and CREW. That rule prevents overbroad, catch‑all labels that preclude meaningful assessment of whether particular exemptions fit particular material. In Farahi the Court noted that Farahi argued the FBI’s categories were overbroad but did not explain in what respect; because he failed to develop the point, the Court treated that challenge as forfeited. The lesson is twofold: agencies should define withholding categories clearly, and requesters must specifically contest any overbreadth to preserve their appellate arguments.

## Ex parte, in camera submissions in national‑security FOIA cases

FOIA litigation sometimes requires the court to examine sensitive material that cannot be publicly disclosed without defeating the very interests the government seeks to protect. In such circumstances courts accept ex parte, in camera submissions so the judge can evaluate the agency’s claims without enlarging the public record. The Farahi opinion acknowledges this practice as necessary and permissible in national‑security cases; it also notes a pragmatic consequence: reliance on sealed submissions handicaps the FOIA plaintiff, but that handicap is inherent when the only way to decide the dispute is to examine the withheld material privately. Practically, the opinion stresses that a plaintiff cannot complain about the court’s use of ex parte material when the plaintiff did not object to the agency’s filing of that material under seal.

## Appellate standard of review

Finally, the opinion restates the governing standard of review: courts review grants of summary judgment in FOIA cases de novo. The government retains the burden of proving the exemption. De novo review means the appellate court independently assesses whether the record (including agency declarations and any in camera material) demonstrates compliance with the statutory requirements discussed above. In Farahi the D.C. Circuit accepted the district court’s factual findings as supported by the record — including sealed declarations — and affirmed that those findings satisfied Exemption 7(A) and the segregability obligations.

Conclusion

Together these principles show how FOIA law balances the public’s right to information against legitimate law‑enforcement and national‑security interests. Farahi illustrates the mechanics: the government used public and sealed declarations to establish that records were law‑enforcement files connected to ongoing counterterrorism investigations, justified nondisclosure under Exemption 7(A), explained why nonexempt material was not reasonably segregable, and persuaded both the district court and this Court under de novo review that the statutory requirements were met.

# Applying 7(A) Prong One: Compiled for Law Enforcement Purposes

## The rational-nexus test — what the court looks for
Exemption 7(A) applies only to records “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” That phrase is not self-explanatory: courts ask whether there is a rational “nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s law enforcement duties” and whether the records have a “connection between an ‘individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law.’” Those formulations come from Pratt v. Webster and are repeated in Campbell and later precedent. In practice, then, an agency must do more than label a file “law enforcement”; its declarations must explain why the investigation served a law-enforcement function and how the subject or incident implicated a potential violation or security risk that the agency is charged to investigate. Because the FBI is a law-enforcement agency, courts give its factual judgments a “fair measure of deference,” but that deference does not eliminate the requirement that the agency articulate a rational link between its duties and the records at issue.

## What the public Hardy declaration said and why it mattered
In Farahi the FBI’s public Hardy declaration fulfilled the first part of that test by stating that the withheld records were compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism,” and by tying those investigations to “pending law enforcement proceedings and the subject’s association with individuals directly involved in such violations.” That language maps closely onto the Pratt/Campbell inquiry: it identifies the agency’s law-enforcement duty (investigating national-security and terrorism violations) and asserts that the records were created to advance that duty. A public declaration of this sort serves two functions: it provides the court (and the public) a baseline showing the agency’s claimed purpose for the records, and it anchors any sealed explanations that the agency relies on later.

## The role of the ex parte (sealed) Hardy declaration
The opinion makes clear that the public declaration did not stand alone. The Hardy ex parte declaration, filed under seal, supplied “considerable detail” about the nature and scope of the investigation. Although the appeals court properly refused to disclose the sealed content, it treated the sealed declaration as filling out the public statement: the sealed material showed how the records were used in specific investigative contexts and why they were part of law-enforcement work. Pedagogically, this illustrates a common FOIA pattern: a public affidavit states the general law-enforcement purpose while a sealed affidavit supplies factual specifics that would themselves be sensitive if revealed. The court accepted that combination as sufficient to show a rational nexus without requiring the agency to put operational details on the public record.

## Corroboration from the case record — Farahi’s own concessions
The court did not rely on FBI declarations in isolation. It emphasized contextual corroboration drawn from the record in the parallel immigration litigation: Farahi conceded before the district court that the FBI had investigated him for links to one or more State-Department-designated foreign terrorist organizations and for ties to an individual who had been detained as an enemy combatant and later convicted in federal court on terrorism-related charges. The immigration-court record described Farahi as having connections to high-level terrorists, alleged recruiting activity, and links to convicted or indicted terrorists (the opinion specifically references claims about connections to Khaled Sheikh Mohammed and to Jose Padilla, and alleged support for charities later tied to terrorism financing). Those concessions and record facts make plausible the FBI’s assertion that its records were compiled to pursue national-security and terrorism-related law-enforcement duties.

## How the court put the parts together — deference plus facts = satisfied prong one
Applying the law to these materials, the court concluded that the FBI carried its burden on the first prong of Exemption 7(A). The methodology matters: the court evaluated the public declaration’s general statement of purpose, the ex parte declaration’s sealed specifics, and the independent corroboration in the litigation record. Because the FBI’s role is inherently law-enforcement, the court afforded its declarations deference, but that deference was anchored to the evidentiary showing described above. The combined record established a rational nexus between the investigation that produced Farahi’s file and the FBI’s law-enforcement duties, and it showed a connection between Farahi (and the incidents described) and possible national-security violations. That is enough, under Pratt and Campbell, to satisfy the “compiled for law enforcement purposes” requirement of Exemption 7(A).

# Applying 7(A) Prong Two: Interference with Pending or Reasonably Anticipated Proceedings

This chapter explains how the D.C. Circuit evaluated the second prong of FOIA Exemption 7(A) — whether disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The court’s analysis relied on settled precedents about what counts as a “proceeding,” what the government must show, and the kinds of interference that justify withholding; it then applied those principles to the FBI’s declarations (both public and ex parte) and to Farahi’s staleness challenge.

## Proceedings requirement: ongoing investigations qualify; government attestations suffice

Exemption 7(A) protects records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” when release could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The court relied on CREW’s restatement of the “pending or reasonably anticipated” requirement and on earlier cases treating investigations as “proceedings” for this purpose. An ongoing criminal investigation counts as an enforcement “proceeding,” and the government may ordinarily satisfy its burden by attesting to the existence of such an investigation. The crucial point is that the investigation must be active in the sense that it is still gathering evidence for potential future charges; if disclosure would jeopardize that evidentiary work, Exemption 7(A) can apply.

The opinion cites Juarez for this principle: Exemption 7(A) applies “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence.” This standard frames the court’s inquiry: the government need not point to a filed indictment, but must show a concrete, continuing investigatory effort that disclosure could harm.

## Interference rationale: tipping off targets

A common and widely accepted rationale for withholding under 7(A) is that disclosure will “tip off” targets about the scope, direction, or subjects of an investigation. The court relied on CNSS and other precedents recognizing tipping-off as a legitimate basis for concluding that disclosure could interfere with enforcement proceedings. Tipping off can cause targets to destroy evidence, change behavior, flush out undercover sources, or otherwise frustrate law enforcement’s ability to collect proof — precisely the sort of prejudice Exemption 7(A) guards against.

The Hardy and Bender declarations asserted that revealing information from Farahi’s FBI file would risk tipping off targets and thereby interfere with the investigation. The court treated that statement as a standard, acceptable explanation of how disclosure could cause interference.

## Evidence of ongoing proceedings: Hardy’s public and ex parte attestations

On the record, the government’s initial showing came from David Hardy. His public declaration stated that the withheld records were part of investigations into national-security and terrorism matters and attested that the relevant investigation remained “ongoing.” Critically, the government also submitted an ex parte (sealed) Hardy declaration that the district court reviewed in camera; the sealed submission supplied considerable additional detail about the investigation’s nature and scope. Those two Hardy declarations together furnished the substantive factual basis — beyond a bare assertion — for concluding that the FBI was actively pursuing an investigation tied to Farahi.

Under the precedent the court cited, an agency affidavit that “show[s] with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated” and that explains the ongoing investigatory activity may be sufficient to satisfy the government’s burden. Here, the public Hardy declaration established the existence of the investigation, and the ex parte Hardy declaration provided the granular, sensitive details the government could not disclose publicly.

## Bender’s later update: confirming ongoing status as of March 2023

After the district court concluded that the Hardy submissions “seemingly” met the government’s burden but worried that the passage of time might have rendered them stale, the FBI supplied an updated submission from Joseph Bender. The Bender declaration was short in its public form and stated that the investigations “remain ongoing” as of March 2023. The government also supplied a supplemental ex parte submission from Bender that the district court reviewed.

The court treated Bender’s public declaration not as a standalone showing but as a temporal update to the earlier, more detailed Hardy submissions. Because the district court had already found the Hardy material to be at least sufficient in substance, the Bender declaration’s role was simply to confirm that the investigation continued while the case was pending. Read in that context, the terse public wording was adequate: it confirmed continuity rather than attempting to prove the initial predicate facts from scratch.

## Addressing staleness: why the short update sufficed in context

Farahi argued that the passage of years made the government’s assertion of ongoing proceedings dubious and that the Bender declaration was too conclusory. The court accepted the general point that when a significant amount of time has passed, a mere, vague assertion that an investigation is “ongoing” can be insufficient — a point drawn from CREW. But the key to the court’s ruling was context. The government’s showing was not limited to the brief Bender statement. It included (1) the public Hardy declaration attesting to the investigation’s existence, (2) a detailed ex parte Hardy declaration describing the investigation’s nature and risks of disclosure, (3) the district court’s earlier conclusion that the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden, and (4) Bender’s update confirming no change in status. That combination supplied the specificity CREW demands when staleness is a concern: the Bender declaration updated an earlier detailed record rather than attempting to substitute for it.

The court thus framed the staleness question as one of whether the government had shown a concrete, ongoing investigatory effort at the relevant time. Because the earlier submissions established that effort and Bender confirmed it continued, the court found no stale-gap problem.

## Breadth and sensitivity of the investigation make ongoing status plausible

Although the court could not reveal the contents of the sealed materials, it noted publicly available allegations from Farahi’s immigration proceedings that corroborated the FBI’s representation of a broad, serious investigation. Those public allegations—ties to terrorist figures and organizations, recruitment activity, and connections to convicted or indicted terrorists—made it plausible that the FBI’s inquiry was wide-ranging and sensitive. The court treated that factual context as reinforcing the reasonableness of the FBI’s claim that disclosure would jeopardize ongoing enforcement efforts. In other words, the more extensive and nationally sensitive the government’s investigative focus, the less likely a brief confirmation of ongoing activity would be viewed as inadequate.

## Court’s holding on prong two

Putting the legal rules and the factual record together, the court concluded that the FBI met its burden under Exemption 7(A): there was a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” — an ongoing criminal/terrorism investigation — and disclosure of the requested records could reasonably be expected to interfere with that proceeding (by tipping off targets and the like). The detailed ex parte Hardy declaration established the nature and danger of disclosure; the public Hardy declaration attested to the investigation’s existence; and the Bender declaration, properly read as a confirmatory update, showed that the investigation remained active when the district court made its ruling. Thus the court affirmed the district court’s conclusion that Exemption 7(A)’s interference prong was satisfied.

Takeaway for readers: when time has elapsed, courts require more than a bare, conclusory assertion that an investigation is ongoing; but a brief later attestation can suffice if it updates an earlier public/ex parte record that gave the necessary factual details. Where the government has particularized, sensitive facts (especially in national-security contexts) and the agency reasonably fears tipping off targets, Exemption 7(A) will often shield the records from disclosure.

# Segregability, Ex Parte Reliance, Remaining Arguments, and Disposition

## The segregability standard and how the court applies it
FOIA requires agencies to disclose any “reasonably segregable” nonexempt information from records that are otherwise withheld. The legal standard looks not to whether individual words or isolated phrases could theoretically be sanitized, but whether there is meaningful, releasable information that can be produced without revealing exempt material. The D.C. Circuit has long recognized a practical limit: when nonexempt material is “randomly interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” it is often not reasonably segregable because what FOIA protects is information, not merely discrete words on a page. That principle appears in Mead Data and was recently reiterated in Perioperative Services: trivial snippets that do not convey a meaningful, standalone informational value will not defeat a valid claim of exemption.

In Farahi, the district court concluded—and the court of appeals agreed—that the FBI satisfied its burden on segregability. The agency’s sealed ex parte submission (described below) set out why any nonexempt markings within the records were so intertwined with exempt material that they could not be released in a way that would not reveal sensitive investigative content. The court emphasized the point made in Mead and Perioperative: the mere theoretical ability to pick out a word or phrase does not obligate the agency to disclose items that, taken together, would either be meaningless to the requester or would risk disclosure of protected information. On that basis the courts found no “meaningful” segregable information remained and accepted the agency’s withholdings in full.

## Bender’s supplemental ex parte showing and the district court’s finding
After the district court expressed concern about the FBI’s initial, older declarations, the FBI submitted updated material, including a public declaration and a sealed ex parte supplemental declaration from RIDS leadership. The public declaration confirmed that the investigation remained ongoing; the ex parte filing supplied the factual detail the court said it needed but could not on the public record disclose.

When the district court reviewed the ex parte submission it concluded that the FBI had provided a “detailed justification” as to why the withheld material was not reasonably segregable. In other words, the sealed declaration explained with sufficient specificity how isolated nonexempt passages were embedded in a way that disclosure would either be trivial or would reveal exempt investigative content. On appeal the panel agreed that the district court’s reliance on the ex parte submission was appropriate and that the court did not err in finding no meaningful segregable information.

## Use of ex parte declarations in national-security and law-enforcement FOIA cases
Ex parte, sealed declarations are often the only practicable way for courts to evaluate certain FOIA claims without forcing public disclosure of the very information the agency contends is exempt. The Farahi opinion underscores two points about such submissions. First, they can be decisive: the district court explicitly relied on the sealed material to resolve both the pendency of investigations and the segregability question. Second, there is a strategic and procedural limitation for plaintiffs: Farahi did not oppose the FBI’s motion to file supplemental ex parte declarations under seal, and that procedural acquiescence foreclosed his later challenges to the district court’s reliance on them. The D.C. Circuit acknowledged that ex parte submissions handicap plaintiffs, but observed that this difficulty is inherent where secrecy must be preserved to adjudicate the dispute—an approach supported by precedent such as Perioperative Services.

The take-away is that courts will accept and act on sealed, ex parte submissions when national-security or sensitive-law-enforcement interests are at stake, particularly if the plaintiff does not object to the procedure. But because ex parte filings keep key factual detail off the public record, agencies commonly pair a public statement of the exemption claim with sealed elaboration for the court.

## The categorical-withholding challenge and forfeiture
When an agency withholds documents by category, the categories must be “sufficiently distinct” to allow the court to test whether the claimed exemptions properly apply to each group. That is the Gallant/CREW requirement: a court must be able to map claimed exemptions to specific sets of records. In Farahi, the FBI had organized its withholdings into categories, but the appellant contended those categories were overbroad.

The court of appeals rejected this argument on procedural grounds: Farahi failed to identify with specificity why particular categories were overbroad. Without particularized objections explaining how the categories were defective, the argument was forfeited under Khine. In short, a bare assertion that categories are too broad—without telling the court which categories, why they are overbroad, and with what evidence—will not preserve the issue on appeal.

## Appellate disposition and standard of review
The court reviewed the district court’s grant of summary judgment de novo, applying the settled allocation of burdens in FOIA: the government must show that an exemption applies. Giving appropriate deference to the FBI’s law-enforcement judgment—particularly in the national-security context—the panel agreed that the agency had shown the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings (Exemption 7(A)). It also upheld the district court’s segregability determination based on the sealed submission. Because the court found the FBI met both the exemption and segregability burdens, it affirmed the district court’s judgment in full.

## Practice notes for litigators and agency counsel
Several practical lessons follow directly from the court’s disposition:

- Update explanations when time has passed. If an agency’s initial declarations grow stale during litigation, a court may require updated declarations to confirm whether investigations remain ongoing and whether asserted harms still obtain.

- Pair a public justification with sealed detail in sensitive cases. Provide a public declaration that explains the legal basis for exemptions and a sealed ex parte declaration for the court that supplies the factual specifics the public filing cannot disclose without defeating the agency’s interest.

- Make categories concrete and defensible. If you withhold records on a category-by-category basis, define the categories clearly and be prepared to show why each category is subject to the claimed exemption; otherwise, categorical arguments can be forfeited for lack of specificity.

- Focus on meaningful segregability. Courts look for “meaningful” information that can be disclosed without revealing exempt material. Isolated words or trivial phrases that would be useless standing alone or would reveal exempt content when connected are not necessarily “reasonably segregable.”

- Anticipate the consequences of acquiescing to ex parte procedures. Objecting to sealed, ex parte filings preserves the right to challenge the court’s reliance on them; failing to object makes it difficult to later complain on appeal.

These practical points flow straight from how the court treated the FBI’s submissions and Farahi’s responses; they show how procedural choices and factual specificity can determine the outcome of FOIA litigation in national-security and law‑enforcement contexts.
