# Exemption 7(A) as a Perpetual Shield? The D.C. Circuit Blesses "Ongoing Investigations" a Decade On

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in Farahi v. FBI is a reminder that Exemption 7(A) remains a powerful tool for law‑enforcement secrecy — particularly when the FBI says an investigation is “ongoing.” At stake in a routine FOIA fight over an individual’s FBI file were larger questions about how courts should evaluate agency attestations of continuing investigations, how much probing is necessary when years pass, and whether an agency can effectively lock records away indefinitely by repeatedly calling an inquiry “ongoing.” The court affirmed the district court’s reliance on agency declarations — including sealed, ex parte submissions — and upheld a categorical withholding on the theory that meaningful nonexempt material was inextricably intertwined with exempt material. That combination of deference and procedural permissiveness has implications far beyond this one file.

## What the court did

The court affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the FBI under Exemption 7(A), which applies to law‑enforcement records whose disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” The opinion rests on three linked findings:

- The FBI’s records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes. That finding drew deference from the court, supported by the public Hardy declaration and the surrounding factual context (including the agency’s earlier use of material from the file in immigration proceedings alleging ties to terrorism).

- Disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. The court relied on a sequence of declarations. The initial Hardy declarations (one public, one ex parte) described an investigation into terrorism‑related activity and attested that enforcement proceedings existed. After the district court asked for an updated assessment because of the time lapse, the FBI submitted a short public declaration by Bender saying the investigation “remained ongoing,” plus an ex parte update. The panel treated Bender’s terse confirmation as an update to, not a replacement for, the earlier, fuller affidavits and concluded the government had carried its burden under CREW and Juarez.

- The withheld material was not reasonably segregable. The district court — on the strength of the agency’s sealed submission — concluded that nonexempt information was “randomly interspersed” and inextricably intertwined with exempt material, so disclosure of fragments would not yield meaningful information without harming the investigation.

The court also declined to entertain Farahi’s challenge to the FBI’s category designations for withholding because he failed to develop specific objections, deeming those arguments forfeited.

## The court’s reasoning in context

Two precedents do most of the heavy lifting here. CREW clarified that Exemption 7(A) covers proceedings that are “pending or reasonably anticipated” and that a government attestation of an ongoing investigation can, in many circumstances, be enough to justify withholding. Juarez likewise allowed an agency to invoke Exemption 7(A) where the investigation continued to gather evidence for a possible case and premature disclosure would jeopardize that process.

The panel emphasized the familiar principle of deference to the FBI in law‑enforcement and national‑security contexts. It also recognized the district court’s procedural precaution: because significant time had elapsed between the FBI’s initial declaration and the district court’s consideration of summary judgment, the court prudently required an update. But once the agency provided that update — even if terse in public form — the court treated the whole package (public declarations plus sealed, ex parte statements) as sufficient.

On segregability, the panel leaned on longstanding Mead principles: where nonexempt material is scattered line by line through sensitive records, disclosure of isolates may not meaningfully inform the public and may instead reveal the very information the exemption protects. The court accepted the district court’s assessment grounded in the ex parte submission that segregation was not “reasonable” in a practical sense.

## My take: reasonable procedure, but thin guardrails

There is something of a split personality to the opinion. On the one hand, the court did the right procedural thing by requiring an updated declaration when the case had been pending for years — that’s the correct response to concerns about staleness. Requiring a time‑stamped attestation recognizes that investigations can close, pivot, or peter out; it gives the agency an affirmative task to justify continued secrecy. That procedural posture preserves a role for the court to police stale claims.

On the other hand, the court ultimately treated a fairly terse public update as sufficient because it was tethered to prior, more detailed affidavits, some of them ex parte. The result underscores the practical effect of ex parte, sealed declarations: they can shore up otherwise thin public statements and insulate withholding decisions from public testing. For FOIA plaintiffs, that is a significant obstacle. The court candidly acknowledges the asymmetry: an ex parte declaration handicaps the requester, but the judge may need to rely on it to avoid disclosing sensitive material in open court. That is understandable, but it’s worth noting that the institutional pressure is asymmetric in the agency’s favor.

The opinion leaves open, but does not resolve, a critical question posed in the blog title: how long can an investigation reasonably be called “ongoing” before the term loses its force? The court hints at answers without articulating a strict limit: time matters; courts should demand fresh attestations; breadth and seriousness of the underlying investigation matter; and where initial affidavits supply a detailed factual foundation, later, narrow confirmations can suffice as updates. But those are guidelines rather than a bright‑line rule. The upshot is that an agency with a plausible law‑enforcement nexus and a history of detailed affidavits can, by periodically submitting short updates, sustain an Exemption 7(A) claim over long stretches of time.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Farahi reinforces several doctrinal tendencies that have long characterized FOIA litigation in the national‑security and law‑enforcement space:

- Enduring deference to law‑enforcement agencies. The opinion reiterates that courts give the FBI a “fair measure” of deference in assessing whether records are law‑enforcement records and whether disclosure would interfere with investigations. That will continue to make it difficult for FOIA requesters to overcome agency assertions in terrorism or counterintelligence contexts.

- The functional role of ex parte submissions. The decision tacitly endorses the pattern of submitting detailed, sealed declarations to justify withholding while relying on shorter public attestations for the public record. That practice preserves sensitive information but also means the public and the requester often cannot test the assertions that determine the outcome.

- A pragmatic approach to segregability. The court’s acceptance that fragmented nonexempt material scattered through many pages may not be “reasonably segregable” strengthens agencies’ ability to withhold voluminous documents in full when the sensitive and the non‑sensitive material are intermingled. That raises real concerns for transparency where files are lengthy but might contain isolated factual details of public interest.

- A shifting burden for freshness. The court required a time‑stamped update when the case lingered, and that is a meaningful requirement. But the decision suggests that courts will be satisfied with a short, verbal confirmation of ongoing investigation if it is linked to earlier, detailed attestations. That incremental standard could allow agencies to maintain secrecy for many years by filing periodic, minimal updates, so long as the original affidavits were substantial.

For FOIA practitioners and transparency advocates, Farahi will likely be read as both a caution and a roadmap. It cautions courts and requesters that time can erode a FOIA plaintiff’s claim unless the government proves the investigation has ceased. But it also illustrates a pathway whereby agencies can preserve confidentiality over long periods: provide a detailed initial declaration that establishes nexus and potential interference, then supply periodic confirmations to the court that those facts remain true.

## Open questions and potential avenues for limiting overreach

Farahi does not, and probably cannot, answer the normative worry that the title raises: whether Exemption 7(A) can become a perpetual shield. The opinion leaves several levers available for future courts to pull if they wish to impose more exacting limits:

- Demand more from updates. A district court skeptical of perpetual secrecy could require periodic updates to include a modest level of factual particularity (e.g., current investigative activities, whether grand jury subpoenas or arrests are contemplated, scope of ongoing evidence‑gathering) sufficient to show the investigation is actively continuing toward potential enforcement. The D.C. Circuit here accepted a short update because previous documents supplied the detail, but courts could require each update to be more than a conclusory “still ongoing.”

- Limit reliance on stale, sealed affidavits. A court could demand that the key factual predicate for a long‑standing withholding be demonstrably current in at least some unclassified or publicly summarized way so the requester and the public can assess the claim. That would preserve the utility of ex parte submissions for delicate specifics while diminishing their role as an untouchable black box.

- Tighten segregability scrutiny for large document sets. Where agencies produce tens of thousands of pages, courts could insist on sampling protocols or more granular justifications to ensure that meaningful, nonexempt information is not being hidden under a claim that it is merely “interspersed.” The Mead line is sensible, but it should not be a blanket justification for wholesale non‑disclosure when practicable redaction or partial release could yield significant public information.

- Reinvigorate CREW’s caution about vague “ongoing” assertions. CREW warned against accepting vague attestations of ongoing investigations. Farahi reads CREW as permitting attestations when grounded in earlier detailed filings, but courts might insist on contemporaneous specificity rather than treat historical detail as perpetually replenishing freshness.

None of these steps would prevent legitimate withholding when disclosure truly would jeopardize enforcement. They would, however, create clearer guardrails to prevent Exemption 7(A) from calcifying into indefinite secrecy rooted in initial investigations that have long since cooled.

## Conclusion

Farahi is not a surprise: the D.C. Circuit again deferred to the FBI on law‑enforcement and national‑security grounds and accepted a procedural pattern agencies have long used — detailed sealed affidavits plus periodic public confirmations. But the opinion also serves as a practical reminder of the tension at FOIA’s core: the need to protect ongoing investigations against legitimate harm to enforcement, on the one hand, and the public’s interest in access to information about government action, on the other.

The court required an update when the record grew stale, which is an important procedural protection. What it did not do — and perhaps could not do without transforming the law — was set a strict clock on how long an investigation can be characterized as “ongoing.” That leaves room for agencies to maintain secrecy for long stretches, provided they can point to a plausible nexus to law‑enforcement duties and periodically confirm the investigation’s existence. For those who press FOIA claims, the lesson is to challenge the sufficiency of updates and to push courts to require substance over form in attested “confirmations” of continuing investigations. For courts, the task will be to balance deference with a realistic check on the risk that Exemption 7(A) becomes a de facto perpetual shield.



# Behind Closed Doors: Ex Parte Declarations, FOIA, and the Limits of Adversarial Testing

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in Farahi v. FBI is routine in outcome but significant in what it confirms about FOIA litigation in national security and counterterrorism contexts: a court may resolve disputes over withholding and segregability on the strength of ex parte, sealed agency declarations without disclosing the underlying evidence to the requester. The practical consequence is stark. Where the government invokes Exemption 7(A) to protect records tied to ongoing investigations, a court — especially in national-security settings — will often accept sealed, non-public submissions as sufficient to show both that enforcement proceedings are pending or anticipated and that non-exempt material is not reasonably segregable. For requesters, particularly those whose records relate to sensitive counterterrorism inquiries, the decision underscores the steep uphill climb of proving the agency wrong when the agency furnishes classified or otherwise secret justifications under seal.

Farahi is therefore a useful lens on an enduring tension in FOIA law: the statutory presumption of disclosure versus the practical necessity of secrecy in certain law-enforcement or national-security investigations. The opinion reiterates settled doctrinal lines — deference to the FBI on law-enforcement purpose, the “pending or reasonably anticipated” standard for Exemption 7(A), and Mead-style deference to agency affidavits on segregability — and it gives those lines real bite by embracing ex parte declarations as an appropriate vehicle for making the agency’s case.

## What the court did and why

At bottom, the court affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment for the FBI. The agency had withheld the bulk of roughly 10,750 pages responsive to Farahi’s FOIA request and supported its decision with a public declaration and sealed ex parte declarations from senior RIDS officials. After the district court expressed concern that earlier declarations might have become stale, the FBI filed another public declaration and supplementary sealed material to confirm that the investigation remained ongoing and to address segregability. The D.C. Circuit accepted that combination of public and sealed submissions as satisfying the government’s burden under Exemption 7(A) and under the statutory segregability requirement.

The court’s reasoning rests on several familiar pillars:

- The FBI’s classification of records as “compiled for law enforcement purposes” is entitled to a fair measure of deference because the agency specializes in that work; here the public and sealed declarations supplied a rational nexus connecting the records to counterterrorism investigations.

- Exemption 7(A) requires that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The court treated the agency’s attestations that an investigation remained “ongoing,” together with the ex parte elaboration of its nature, as sufficient to show a concrete prospective enforcement proceeding.

- On segregability, the court accepted the sealed material as providing the “detailed justification” necessary to conclude that meaningful non-exempt information was not reasonably segregable, particularly where non-exempt content is “interspersed line-by-line” with exempt material.

- The court emphasized that Farahi did not oppose the FBI’s motion to file sealed, ex parte filings and therefore cannot now complain about the district court’s reliance on them. It also relied on Perioperative Services and its predecessors for the proposition that ex parte declarations are an unfortunate but sometimes unavoidable feature of FOIA litigation where the only way to adjudicate the dispute without revealing secret material is to receive some materials under seal.

Taken together, the panel framed the sealed declarations not as the preferred norm but as a pragmatic accommodation to the competing statutory directives: disclosure as a default, but protection where disclosure would itself undermine enforcement or national security.

## Analytical perspective: legal doctrine meets procedural reality

Doctrinally, nothing novel happened. The opinion adheres to the D.C. Circuit’s line of decisions that afford deference to agency affidavits in Exemption 7(A) cases (Campbell, CNSS, Juarez) and to Mead on segregability. But Farahi’s significance grows from the way those doctrines operate in practice when the agency submits ex parte, sealed declarations.

First, ex parte declarations — especially where they are the exclusive vehicle for explaining why records must be withheld in full — dramatically constrict the adversary testing that FOIA envisions. FOIA’s enforcement mechanism is adversarial litigation, in which the requester contests the agency’s claim. But sealed submissions curtail that contest: the requester cannot rebut factual assertions in a sealed declaration, and the public record cannot illuminate why particular lines or phrases were withheld. Farahi underscores how that dynamic produces near-insulation for the government when the subject matter touches on national security or ongoing investigations.

Second, the opinion highlights the importance of timing and procedural choices by requesters. The court repeatedly notes that Farahi did not object to the FBI’s motion to file sealed, ex parte declarations. The consequence is straightforward: by failing to contest the procedural route the government proposed, the requester effectively forfeited a key point of attack. The panel’s posture — treating the courthouse record as the operative record and the requester’s silence as conceding the propriety of ex parte material — signals that litigants must be strategically vigilant if they wish to prevent the case from being resolved primarily on sealed submissions.

Third, the decision reflects a jurisprudential trade-off. The court recognizes that relying on ex parte filings handicaps requesters, but it accepts that handicap as inherent to some FOIA disputes where the only way to decide the case without disclosing the protected material is to disclose a subset of evidence to the judge alone. That acceptance stems from pragmatic deference to the Executive’s role in national security and to the practical impossibility of litigating certain factual claims on a fully public record.

## Process questions the opinion leaves open

Farahi validates the use of ex parte, sealed declarations, but it does not drill down on what procedural structures courts should impose to ensure meaningful judicial scrutiny without revealing sensitive material. A few process-oriented questions follow from the opinion and merit attention from lower courts and practitioners.

First, how should a court evaluate the weight due to an ex parte declaration when the requester objects to in-camera or sealed treatment? Farahi involved no such objection, and the court emphasized that absence of opposition. Where a requester objects, courts must choose among imperfect options: require a more detailed public summary, conduct in-camera review, appoint a special master or security-cleared amicus, or order redacted disclosures. Farahi suggests that absent objection, courts will be comfortable resolving the case on sealed evidence; when objection exists, the opinion leaves to future panels how vigorously trial courts must press for alternatives.

Second, what should a court require in terms of substance when the government submits sealed factual material? The D.C. Circuit has previously demanded that agency submissions be sufficiently specific to allow meaningful judicial review (see CREW, Juarez, Mead). Farahi reiterates that standard but provides little procedural guidance: the sealed declaration in this case was credited after being considered together with public declarations and context from immigration proceedings. Lower courts struggling to balance confidentiality and adequate justification would benefit from more granular guidance — for example, what minimum factual predicate must be included in public declarations, and what depth of specificity is required in sealed material so as not to be merely boilerplate.

Third, what is the role of segregability when record volumes are immense? The court accepted that meaningful non-exempt information might be “interspersed line-by-line” such that segregation is not reasonably feasible. That is a defensible application of Mead, but it raises questions about proportionality and whether technological tools (searches, redactions, metadata analytics) should impose higher expectations on agencies before courts accept a “not reasonably segregable” conclusion. Farahi treats the FBI’s sealed explanation as sufficient; future litigants may press courts to require modern, document-by-document demonstrations where technology makes partial disclosure practicable.

## Downstream effects and practical takeaways

Practitioners and courts should take away several practical lessons from Farahi.

For requesters: procedural vigilance matters. If you expect the agency to rely on sealed submissions — and if the subject matter plausibly touches on ongoing investigations — consider objecting to the government’s motion to file ex parte material, press for a public Vaughn-like index or an unclassified summary, ask for in-camera review, or seek appointment of a cleared amicus or special master. Failure to do so risks forfeiting an avenue of attack on sufficiency and specificity.

For courts: Farahi models a posture of deference tempered by review, but it should not become a shortcut that forecloses creative procedures that allow meaningful adversarial testing without compromising classified information. Tools already exist — cleared amicus curiae, special masters, segregated in-camera review, carefully tailored public summaries — and the court system should use them more routinely when feasible.

For agencies: Farahi affirms that careful, contemporaneous public declarations, supplemented (when necessary) by sealed ex parte material, can suffice to carry the government’s burden under Exemption 7(A) and on segregability. But agencies should also recognize that relying on sealed submissions invites scrutiny about why less secret-sensitive information could not be released; clear explanations on those points help avoid perceptions of overreach.

For the law: Farahi will likely encourage agencies to continue submitting sealed material in national-security FOIA litigation and will likely make district courts comfortable resolving such disputes without placing sensitive details on the public record. That pattern will preserve confidentiality in many cases, but it will also shrink the publicly available doctrinal record about when the 7(A) interference standard is met and about when meaningful, non-exempt material can be released.

## Conclusion

Farahi is a reminder that FOIA litigation is not a pure transparency play when it collides with investigations into terrorism and national security. The decision reaffirms existing doctrine: deference to the FBI on law-enforcement purpose, a pragmatic reading of “pending or reasonably anticipated,” and acceptance of Mead’s limits on segregability. But its most consequential lesson is procedural: ex parte, sealed declarations remain a potent — and sometimes decisive — tool for agencies. That reality heightens the importance of strategic litigation choices by requesters and of careful, imaginative procedural steps by courts to permit adversarial testing without blowing open the secrets the government seeks to protect. The challenge for the lower courts going forward will be to calibrate those steps so that FOIA’s promise of public access is not hollowed out by secrecy, but secrecy is not allowed to swallow judicial review.



# Segregability in the Shadow of National Security: How 10,750 Pages Yielded “No Meaningful Information”

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in Farahi v. FBI is a quiet but doctrinally significant reaffirmation of two of FOIA law’s core, tension-filled principles: the government’s broad deference in national-security and law-enforcement contexts, and the statutory duty to release “any reasonably segregable portion of a record.” At stake is a familiar but still urgent question—when an agency invokes Exemption 7(A) to shield law‑enforcement files, how stringently must courts police the government’s segregability showing, especially where the withheld materials are voluminous and the declared harms implicate active counterterrorism investigations?

On the facts, the FBI located roughly 10,750 pages and 80 CDs responsive to a requester’s FOIA demand for his own file and withheld most of those records under Exemption 7(A). The district court initially required the FBI to update stale declarations; after supplemental public and ex parte filings, it concluded that (1) the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes; (2) release could be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings; and (3) meaningful, nonexempt material was not reasonably segregable. The D.C. Circuit affirmed.

The opinion matters because it illustrates how the Court of Appeals applies two doctrines—“inextricably intertwined”/non‑segregability and deference to national‑security‑adjacent agency declarations—in a high‑volume, counterterrorism context. The result is a practical, if unsurprising, outcome: thousands of pages can be withheld in bulk when the agency convinces a court that the benign-looking snippets within those pages are so enmeshed with sensitive law‑enforcement material that disclosure would itself reveal substantive intelligence or investigative contours.

## The court’s reasoning, in short

The court’s analysis proceeds along three tracks that track the statutory language of Exemption 7(A) and the segregability requirement.

First, the court accepted that the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” The FBI’s public declaration (Hardy) and an ex parte, sealed declaration provided sufficient detail about the investigation’s nature—ties to terrorism, named associations, and the connection to prosecutions—to establish the requisite “nexus” between the records and a law‑enforcement function. The majority underscored that courts traditionally afford the FBI “a fair measure of deference,” particularly where national‑security concerns are implicated.

Second, the court concluded that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that were “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The opinion is careful on timing: it recognizes that stale or conclusory assertions of ongoing investigations raise skepticism (citing CREW), but it treats the agency’s later Bender declaration as an update—not a standalone assertion—supplementing the earlier, more detailed Hardy affidavits. The court therefore accepted the agency’s showing that active investigations remained and that disclosure could tip off targets, jeopardizing evidence gathering.

Third—and the piece that will draw the most attention—the court upheld the district court’s segregability determination. The court relied on precedent (Mead, Juarez, Perioperative) permitting withholding where nonexempt material is “randomly interspersed line-by-line throughout the document” and therefore not “reasonably segregable.” The D.C. Circuit emphasized that the FOIA’s aim is disclosure of information, not of raw words, and that where the small chunks of nonexempt content cannot be extracted without revealing the substance of the exempt portions, the agency may withhold in bulk. The district court’s review of a sealed ex parte submission (describing why segregation would itself injure investigations) carried the day, and the appellate panel accepted that reliance.

## An analytical perspective: mosaics, granularity, and the danger of functional non‑segregability

There is force to the court’s reasoning. Where the subject of a FOIA request is an active national‑security investigation, releasing even apparently innocuous lines can reveal investigative techniques, priorities, timelines, or relationships among individuals that adversaries could exploit. The “mosaic” theory—fear that small pieces, when combined, yield significant intelligence—has intuitive appeal in counterterrorism contexts, and courts have long allowed agencies to make carefully supported showings along those lines.

But Farahi also demonstrates how the segregability obligation can be displaced by two structural features: volume and secrecy. Volume cuts both ways—large document sets increase the likelihood that some neutral facts are embedded somewhere, but they also make it practically difficult for courts to parse publicly what could be releasable without compromising sensitive details. Secrecy intensifies the problem: when an agency supplies ex parte, sealed declarations to make the segregability case, the requester cannot mount a public rebuttal and the court cannot explain, in an open opinion, why it accepted the agency’s view. The circuit recognizes this asymmetry and cites the necessity of sealed submissions in certain cases—but that necessity tends to entrench deference and narrows the practical reach of FOIA’s disclosure requirements.

Put bluntly, “line-by-line interspersion” and “inextricably intertwined” reasoning can functionally swallow segregability when combined with national‑security deference and sealed affidavits. A powerful agency can assert that disclosure of isolated phrases would enable targets to infer investigative connections or reveal methods; absent a robust public record, courts will often credit that assertion.

## Doctrinal implications and possible downstream effects

The Farahi decision reinforces precedents that tilt the balance toward nondisclosure in national‑security and terrorism-related FOIA disputes. Two doctrinal consequences are worth flagging.

First, the decision tightens the upper bound on what courts will consider “reasonably segregable” where disclosures are alleged to risk facilitating law‑enforcement harms. Courts will accept detailed—but sealed—justifications that explain how even minimal disclosures would be sensitive. That could mean fewer piecemeal disclosures from large law‑enforcement compilations going forward, especially in cases involving counterterrorism, organized crime, or intelligence‑adjacent investigations.

Second, the decision underscores an evidentiary path for agencies: combine a public affidavit that establishes the investigative nexus and a sealed ex parte submission that provides the granular rationale for non‑segregability and explains why public release of segregated snippets would itself cause the asserted harms. That combination was decisive here—Hardy’s public affidavit laid the groundwork; the sealed material made the segregability case.

Both implications are unsurprising; what is more consequential is the practical dynamic this creates. Requesters will find it harder to obtain meaningful disclosures from large FBI files; lower‑resource requesters and small nonprofits will be particularly disadvantaged because they lack the resources to litigate and to generate alternative evidence undermining sealed claims. Moreover, the use of ex parte declarations, while sometimes unavoidable, may increase, making FOIA litigation more opaque and judicial explanations less instructive for future parties.

## How courts and requesters can cabin overbroad non‑segregability claims

The opinion does not close off workable steps that courts and requesters can take to preserve FOIA’s disclosure function while respecting legitimate law‑enforcement concerns.

First, courts should insist on a high degree of specificity in public declarations before accepting that sealed submissions are needed. The D.C. Circuit here accepted the agency’s initial public declarations as sufficient, but courts can press agencies to provide categorical public descriptions that allow meaningful, adversarial testing of the asserted harms—without exposing operational details. For example, a public declaration could specify categories of information that are sensitive (e.g., “identities of confidential human sources,” “technical collection methods,” “timelines of ongoing operations”) and explain at a high level why those categories pervade the records. That kind of public, categorical specificity enables the requester to challenge overbreadth while limiting operational disclosure.

Second, courts should require agencies to produce a detailed Vaughn index (or its equivalent) in camera whenever practicable. The Vaughn index should be organized such that it allows the court to evaluate segregability line-by-line if necessary, but it may be submitted under seal with a clear public summary. The goal is to avoid relying solely on ex parte narrative affidavits; a court that has a detailed, itemized map of the withheld material is better positioned to separate the wheat from the chaff and to explain its rationale on the public record where possible.

Third, courts can adopt structured sampling techniques. When agencies claim that nonexempt material is randomly interspersed across thousands of pages, a court can order the agency to disclose representative samples of smaller subsets (e.g., particular file types, time periods, or document custodians), perhaps initially in camera and then redacted for public release if feasible. Sampling reduces the piecemeal‑catch‑all problem: agencies cannot claim that every line is sensitive if representative samples demonstrate otherwise. Sampling also conserves judicial resources relative to a page‑by‑page review of 10,750 pages.

Fourth, requesters can improve the quality of their challenges. A requester who can show, by way of publicly available parallel records, prior disclosures from similar cases, or declaratory evidence, that certain categories of information have been disclosed in other contexts undermines blanket claims of interspersion. Even if the requester cannot access the sealed affidavit, demonstrating that certain kinds of content have previously been disclosed may pressure the agency to narrow its claims or provide more public detail.

Finally, appellate courts should provide guidance on how to evaluate the necessity and sufficiency of ex parte submissions in FOIA cases. The circuits have recognized the legitimacy of sealed affidavits when disclosure of reasoning would itself reveal sensitive information; but appellate precedent can and should sketch guardrails—insisting on public categorical detail, requiring in camera indexes, encouraging sampling, and, where feasible, requiring courts to articulate the bases for non‑segregability in a manner that does not reveal operational specifics but does permit public understanding and review.

## Conclusion

Farahi is not a radical recalibration of FOIA law. It aligns with longstanding precedents affording deference to the FBI in national‑security contexts and recognizing that nonexempt material can be non‑segregable where disclosure of small bits would reveal the substance of the exempt material. But the case crystallizes an enduring problem: when the government marshals public and sealed statements and invokes the mosaic concern, segregability obligations can become functionally thin, especially in large files tied to terrorism investigations.

Preserving FOIA’s promise of transparency will require creative procedural care from courts and more robust, targeted advocacy from requesters. Courts should push agencies to provide as much public, categorical detail as possible, employ sampling and in‑camera indexes to test claims, and explain their conclusions in ways that preserve secrecy without insulating agency assertions from meaningful review. Without such guardrails, “inextricably intertwined” risks becoming a broad invitation to withhold entire troves of material under the banner of national security—precisely the result the FOIA’s segregability requirement was meant to prevent.



# What Counts as a "Proceeding" Under 7(A)? The Elastic Standard of "Pending or Reasonably Anticipated"

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit's decision in Farahi v. FBI (No. 23-5243, Sept. 30, 2025) is the latest installment in a long-running line of FOIA jurisprudence about Exemption 7(A): when may an agency withhold records because their disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings”? At stake is the tension between FOIA’s disclosure mandate and a law-enforcement interest in keeping ongoing or prospective investigations from being compromised. Farahi matters because the court reaffirms two important (and related) doctrines that shape that balance: (1) ongoing investigative activity counts as a “proceeding” for Exemption 7(A) purposes, and the government may show the existence of such proceedings largely by agency attestation; and (2) courts may rely on ex parte, sealed declarations to resolve both the pendency/anticipation inquiry and segregability questions when national-security or investigative sensitivities make public explanation infeasible.

Those holdings can make it significantly easier for agencies—especially the FBI—to resist disclosure of large volumes of records without revealing the very facts FOIA requesters seek. For requesters and courts committed to transparency, Farahi thus raises familiar (but acute) worries about how robust the government’s evidentiary showing must be, and how much public-facing factual particularity FOIA plaintiffs can realistically demand.

## What the court did

The facts are straightforward and stark: Farahi, long the subject of immigration proceedings, sought his FBI file—some 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. The FBI withheld most of the material under Exemption 7(A), supported by a public declaration (David Hardy) and an ex parte, sealed declaration elaborating on investigative details. After an initial denial without prejudice (prompted by the passage of time), the FBI submitted an update (a brief, public attestation from Joseph Bender plus additional ex parte material) confirming that relevant investigations remained “ongoing.” The district court, having reviewed the sealed submissions, concluded the FBI had shown the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes, that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings, and that little or nothing was meaningfully segregable. The D.C. Circuit affirmed.

In doing so, the court reiterated settled principles: Exemption 7(A) applies only to records “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” and the “proceedings” whose interference Exemption 7(A) protects must be “pending or reasonably anticipated,” a standard the court traces to CREW v. DOJ (746 F.3d 1082 (D.C. Cir. 2014)) and its predecessors. The court emphasized deference to the FBI’s judgments, particularly in national-security or counterterrorism contexts, and rested its conclusion on the combination of the public declarations (which attested to the existence of ongoing investigations) together with sealed, ex parte declarations that supplied the factual detail the public record could not.

On segregability, the court accepted the FBI’s explanation—again largely in sealed form—that any nonexempt material was “interspersed line-by-line” such that meaningful disclosure would still jeopardize the investigation or reveal the exempt material. The court invoked long-standing precedents (Mead Data; Juarez) that permit non-disclosure where nonexempt portions are inextricably intertwined with exempt material and where agency affidavits show with reasonable specificity why further segregation is impracticable.

## The “proceeding” question and the elasticity of “pending or reasonably anticipated”

The doctrinal core of Farahi is the court’s treatment of what counts as a “proceeding.” Two features of the opinion deserve careful attention.

First, the court reaffirms that ongoing criminal (or counterterrorism) investigations themselves qualify as Exemption 7(A) proceedings. That is not new—this Circuit has said so before—but the point matters because it means that agencies need not await an actual charging decision, grand jury, or other formal step before claiming that disclosure could interfere with a prospective enforcement action. The court cites Juarez and CREW for the proposition that “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies.” That is an expansive view of what it means to be “pending or reasonably anticipated”: a sufficiently active, evidentiary investigation is enough.

Second, and more consequentially, Farahi continues the D.C. Circuit’s practical deference to agency attestations about whether proceedings are pending or reasonably anticipated. CREW warned against “vague” and conclusory assertions of “ongoing” investigations that lack detail. Yet Farahi illustrates how that qualification is often met in practice: the court accepted that an initial, more detailed declaration plus a later, terse confirmatory attestation sufficed, because the later filing was an update, not the sole basis for the government’s claim. In short, a public declaration that describes the investigation’s nature and attests to ongoing enforcement activity, supplemented by sealed ex parte material giving the court necessary specifics, will usually pass muster.

That approach is defensible in many circumstances. The obvious pragmatic problem is that public specificity about investigative targets, methods, or evidence would itself risk the harm Exemption 7(A) seeks to prevent. The court’s solution—allow the agency to provide sensitive detail ex parte to the judge—is a long-standing accommodation balancing transparency and security. But it does vest considerable weight in agency submissions (and in judges’ secret review), and may leave requesters and the public without a robust way to test whether the agency truly faces a concrete prospect of enforcement or is simply invoking Exemption 7(A) to shield embarrassing or politically sensitive material.

The upshot is an elastic “pending or reasonably anticipated” standard: an agency that can show (publicly or in camera) that it is actively gathering evidence, and that a formal enforcement step might reasonably follow, will typically be able to claim Exemption 7(A). Absent egregious governmental overreach or a public record that contradicts the agency’s claims, courts are likely to defer.

## Foreseeable-harm, evidentiary burdens, and ex parte submissions

Farahi sits at the intersection of two related evidentiary questions: how strong must the government’s showing be that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere” (the foreseeable-harm standard), and how much of that showing must be in the public record? The opinion subtly lowers the practical bar for public-facing detail by validating reliance on sealed, ex parte declarations for the hard facts. The court explicitly recognizes that ex parte submissions “handicap” FOIA plaintiffs, but treats that handicap as “inherent” where disclosure of the very materials sought would undermine the investigation. The court does not create a new legal test allowing conclusory assertions in all cases, but its willingness to accept relatively brief public updates (so long as detailed ex parte material exists) tends to reduce the observable burden on the government.

From the requester’s vantage point, this is worrying. CREW’s admonition about vague assertions remains good law, but Farahi illustrates how agencies can meet the CREW standard while still keeping nearly all operative factual content off the public docket. That outcome is arguably consistent with FOIA’s statutory architecture—the Act contemplates in-camera review and authorizes exemptions that protect law enforcement—but it is nonetheless a practical constraint on transparency.

Judges thus remain the gatekeepers: their in-camera review must be sufficiently searching to ensure the agency’s withheld-material claim is not a pretext. Yet in many FOIA cases, the court’s review occurs behind seals and protective orders, which offers no external accountability. Farahi implicitly trusts district judges (and the D.C. Circuit on review) to perform that function, but the reputational and institutional pressures on judges to grant deference in national-security and counterterrorism contexts are real.

## Segregability and the “inextricably intertwined” problem

Farahi also underscores how segregability analysis can become practically moot where the agency shows that nonexempt material is diffused throughout records in a way that disclosure would itself disclose exempt information. The court relied on precedent permitting the withholding of documents in toto when meaningful segregability is impracticable—again pointing to the Mead Data “intertwined” concept and Juarez. That doctrine is doctrinally solid: FOIA requires disclosure of information, not isolated words; revealing snippets can itself reveal their context.

But the effect is that huge swaths of records may be kept secret as “not reasonably segregable” even when an imaginative or resource-intensive redaction project might yield some releasable material. The court’s reasoning reflects a practical balancing: the marginal public value of releasing stranded words or phrases is low relative to the incremental risk to a live investigation. For transparency advocates, the remedy is institutional: courts should continue to insist that agencies make a detailed, document-by-document showing that meaningful redaction cannot yield public information without harm, and should scrutinize any bare assertion that releasable content is “randomly interspersed” rather than systematically separable.

## Downstream effects

Farahi will be invoked in future FOIA fights—especially in national-security and counterterrorism contexts—to justify withholding of voluminous files where investigations are ongoing or plausibly prospective. Expect several practical consequences.

First, agencies will be encouraged to frontload a solid public declaration explaining the investigation’s nature and the grounds on which disclosure would interfere, then use sealed, detailed submissions to supply supporting facts. That approach reduces the public-facing showing required while preserving judicial review.

Second, FOIA plaintiffs will increasingly press procedural challenges: demanding in-court redaction demonstrations, contesting the sufficiency of categorical descriptions, or arguing that the passage of time defeats any realistic claim of an anticipated proceeding. Courts will face recurring methodological questions—how much time can elapse before an “ongoing” investigation becomes stale? Farahi suggests that a subsequent, even terse confirmation of continuity will be enough when initial declarations were detailed; plaintiffs will need to be ready to point to concrete indicia of investigative dormancy to succeed.

Third, Farahi reinforces a broader trend of deference in national-security-adjacent FOIA litigation, which may tilt the balance toward nondisclosure in cases implicating terrorism, espionage, and intelligence-gathering—even when the requested records relate to individuals like Farahi who are litigating immigration claims and may have professional or reputational interests in disclosure.

## Where the tension remains

Farahi does not and cannot erase the underlying tension of Exemption 7(A): the need to prevent interference with legitimate law-enforcement activity versus FOIA’s core purpose of public access. The D.C. Circuit has again articulated a framework that preserves judges’ ability to inspect sensitive materials in secret, while giving agencies a workable path to protect ongoing investigations. That approach is defensible on categorical and pragmatic grounds, but it presses courts to be careful, candid, and methodical in their in-camera reviews. The real question going forward is institutional: will courts assertively police agency assertions in sealed filings, or will the default of deference—especially in terrorism-related matters—harden into a near-permanent shield?

For FOIA practitioners, the lesson is pragmatic. To challenge 7(A) with any hope of success, a requester should: (1) force the agency to produce a detailed public declaration where possible; (2) challenge the scope and specificity of asserted categories and segregability on the public record; (3) marshal public evidence of investigative dormancy or lack of reasonable anticipation of enforcement; and (4) where appropriate, litigate the sufficiency of in-camera detail (asking courts to describe, at a minimum on a non-confidential basis, the nature of the sealed showing). Those steps are uphill but not futile; Farahi accepts sealed submissions but does not license carte blanche.

In the end Farahi is a reminder that Exemption 7(A) remains an elastic tool. It is elastic by necessity: law-enforcement secrecy sometimes cannot be reconciled with full public disclosure. But that elasticity is only legitimate if courts remain vigilant—examining sealed justifications closely, demanding particularized showings where the public record permits, and refusing to allow the exemption to become a reflexive blanket against accountability.



# Requester Neutrality Meets Real-World Context: Using Farahi’s Immigration Record to Prove Law‑Enforcement Purpose

Why this case matters: FOIA is famously “requester-neutral” — an agency’s decision to release or withhold records should not turn on who asks for them or why. Yet the D.C. Circuit’s opinion in Farahi v. FBI (Sept. 30, 2025) illustrates how, in practice, facts gleaned from parallel adjudications and even concessions by a requester can do substantial work in a FOIA fight. The court relied on public and sealed submissions from the FBI, together with allegations Farahi made in immigration proceedings, to satisfy the familiar Pratt/Campbell “rational nexus” requirement that withheld materials were “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” This raises an important doctrinal question: how far can courts recruit facts from other forums — sometimes including statements the requester has made against his own interest — to sustain Exemption 7(A) claims? The answer the panel gives is pragmatic and deferential, but it carries risks for FOIA’s transparency goals and for requesters who can’t effectively contest allegations rolled into the administrative record.

What the court held and how it reasoned: Farahi sought his FBI file and the bureau withheld most of the identified records under Exemption 7(A), which covers law‑enforcement records whose disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The government supported its withholdings with a public declaration and a more detailed ex parte declaration from FBI officials; after the district court asked for updated assurances given the passage of time, the FBI supplied a short public update and another sealed ex parte submission. The D.C. Circuit affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment for the FBI.

Two strands of authority drove the opinion. First, the court applied the Campbell/Pratt line of cases requiring a “rational nexus” between the records and the agency’s investigatory duties; because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency, its characterization of files as investigatory receives a “fair measure of deference.” Second, the court applied CREW and Juarez on the “pending or reasonably anticipated” enforcement‑proceeding requirement, holding that ongoing investigations — and the risk of tipping off targets about their scope — can sustain Exemption 7(A). Important to the court’s conclusion, however, was the content of prior immigration proceedings in which Farahi himself had conceded or allowed into the record allegations tying him to terrorism‑related activity and known terrorists. The panel treated those allegations, together with the FBI’s (public and sealed) attestations, as reinforcing the rational nexus and the agency’s continued investigative interest.

Why importing parallel‑proceedings facts is doctrinally plausible — and why it is not novel: The courts have long looked beyond plain labels to the factual circumstances surrounding records to determine whether they were “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” Pratt requires a “rational nexus” between the records and a law‑enforcement duty; that is a fact‑driven inquiry. In many FOIA cases, the only available facts will be agency declarations and whatever the requester has put in the public record. Where, as here, the requester has litigated related matters and generated a record containing damning allegations or admissions, it is not a leap for a court to consider those materials in evaluating the government’s position. The D.C. Circuit’s deference to FBI factual assessments — particularly in national‑security and counterterrorism contexts — is well‑established (see CNSS and Campbell), and the court’s reliance on the immigration record sits comfortably within that deference framework.

But the pragmatic fit does not eliminate normative concerns. FOIA’s requester neutrality is meant to ensure that an agency cannot use knowledge of a requester’s identity or motives as a pretext for secrecy. The Farahi opinion does not adopt the pernicious view that agencies can withhold documents simply because the requester is a suspected criminal; rather, it treats prior adjudicative facts as substantively probative of the law‑enforcement nexus. That distinction matters, but it is fine and easily blurred in practice.

Risks and downstream effects: Two related risks follow from this approach. First, it amplifies asymmetries in FOIA litigation. Requesters who are strangers to the records at issue start from a neutral position: the agency must show a nexus between the records and law enforcement. But when a requester has already been the subject of parallel proceedings that generated allegations or admissions, the government can lean on those materials — sometimes in sealed submissions that the requester cannot publicly rebut — to bolster its claim of investigatory purpose and ongoing enforcement jeopardy. That has the practical effect of placing requesters who have been litigants in other forums at a disadvantage in FOIA suits: facts they uttered while pursuing other remedies can be used against their interest in the FOIA case.

Second, there is the risk of circularity and strategic gamesmanship. Agencies could plausibly point to a requester’s adverse allegations in unrelated proceedings as the very evidence that an investigation is ongoing and that disclosure would interfere with enforcement. This is particularly troubling where the requester may be unable, as a matter of procedure or resource constraints, to vigorously contest those allegations in the FOIA case — for example, if critical evidence is presented ex parte. The court acknowledged the handicap of ex parte filings but justified them as sometimes necessary to resolve disputes without revealing the very information the government seeks to protect. That is a defensible pragmatic move, but it increases the pressure on courts to police misuse.

Possible guardrails and where courts might draw lines: Farahi does not announce a new rule that agencies can hide behind any collateral allegation. The panel relied on multiple pieces of evidence: a detailed (albeit sealed) FBI declaration describing the nature of the investigation, a public declaration attesting to its ongoing status, and the immigration record. A few modest doctrinal guardrails would help reduce abusive outcomes while respecting legitimate national‑security concerns.

First, courts should require that parallel‑proceedings facts used to support an Exemption 7(A) claim be directly probative of the records’ law‑enforcement nexus, not merely tangential allegations. A showing that a requester faced unrelated civil allegations should not suffice to transform an otherwise neutral file into an investigatory one.

Second, when agencies rely heavily on sealed ex parte submissions to fill evidentiary gaps, district courts should, where practicable, perform in camera review and explain on the public record — at a level that preserves secrecy — the factual basis for deference and why nonexempt portions are not reasonably segregable. That is what the district court did here (and the D.C. Circuit affirmed), but future courts should be explicit about the limits of such reliance.

Third, courts should be wary of untested admissions. If a requester’s statement in a separate proceeding is disputed and central to the Exemption 7(A) analysis, the FOIA record should reflect that dispute; otherwise, the court risks granting deference based on an uncontroverted claim that the requester did not meaningfully concede.

Conclusion: Farahi is a reminder that FOIA doctrine operates in messy factual settings. The D.C. Circuit’s decision is faithful to precedent: it applies Pratt/Campbell’s “rational nexus” test, respects agency expertise in law‑enforcement matters, and recognizes the legitimate use of ex parte submissions when secrecy is essential. But the opinion also highlights a structural tension: courts will often need to evaluate an agency’s withholding by parsing materials generated in other forums — materials the requester might have no practical way to contest within the FOIA case. Going forward, judges should preserve the pragmatic tools the government needs to protect ongoing investigations while policing against circular or strategic uses of collateral adjudicative facts that would chip away at FOIA’s transparency promise.



# Practice Pointers After Farahi: Building and Challenging Category-Based Withholdings

## Why Farahi matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in Farahi v. FBI is a useful, practical guidepost for FOIA practitioners litigating Exemption 7(A) cases against the FBI. On the one hand, the court reaffirmed well-established principles that give the FBI substantial deference where national-security or counterterrorism investigations are implicated: agency declarations that an investigation is “ongoing,” supplemented by classified or ex parte detail, can sustain a 7(A) withholding so long as the district court has independently reviewed whatever nonpublic material is necessary. On the other hand, the panel refused to entertain a challenge to the FBI’s category-based withholdings because the requester failed to press a specific, developed objection below — a procedural lesson that will sting litigators who assume generalized complaints about categories are enough.

For practitioners, Farahi crystallizes a set of tactical do’s and don’ts. It reinforces the judiciary’s willingness to accept (and rely on) a mix of public and ex parte declarations, to permit category-based Vaughn-style summaries, and to treat stale declarations with appropriate skepticism by requiring updates. It also underscores that a litigant who wants the court to scrutinize categories more granularly must preserve and articulate specific objections, and must use the tools available in FOIA practice — sampling, detailed Vaughn indices, requests for in camera review — to force the agency to justify each contested category.

## What the court did and why

The panel affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the FBI on Exemption 7(A). The court walked through the familiar two-step 7(A) inquiry: (1) whether the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” and (2) whether disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Because the FBI is a law-enforcement agency and because the underlying investigation touched on terrorism and national-security matters, the court applied ordinary deference to the agency’s determination that the records were law‑enforcement documents. It also accepted the FBI’s explanation that disclosure could tip off targets and thus interfere with ongoing investigative efforts — a well-trod 7(A) rationale.

Two procedural features of the record were dispositive here. First, when the district court initially denied summary judgment without prejudice because the FBI’s 2019 declarations were potentially stale, the FBI responded with updated declarations (one public and an ex parte sealed submission). The district court — after reviewing the ex parte material — found the investigation still “ongoing” and that the agency had adequately explained nonseparability. The D.C. Circuit treated the later declarations as proper updates that remedied any staleness concerns and validated the district court’s reliance on the ex parte exposition. Second, Farahi challenged the sufficiency of the FBI’s category definitions on appeal, but the panel held those arguments forfeited because he had failed to explain below why the categories were overbroad or insufficiently distinct. The court therefore declined to parse the category-by-category showing.

The bottom line: the combination of (i) public declarations attesting to an ongoing law‑enforcement investigation, (ii) more detailed ex parte declarations that the district judge reviewed, and (iii) a failure by the requester to press specific challenges to the agency’s categorical designations, produced a ruling affirming broad withholding.

## Tactical lessons for FOIA requesters

Farahi should sharpen the FOIA litigator’s playbook, especially in cases involving national security or criminal investigations.

First, preserve and particularize objections to categories. The D.C. Circuit refused to review Farahi’s attack on the FBI’s category labels because the challenge lacked specificity below. That means a requester who suspects overbreadth or excessive lumping must do more than say “categories are too broad.” Your district-court opposition should identify which categories seem problematic, explain why the agency’s descriptions fail to permit meaningful judicial review, and, to the extent possible, illustrate with examples or reasoned arguments about how the categories mask distinct kinds of records.

Second, force a robust Vaughn-style showing or demand a sampling protocol. When agencies rely on category‑based withholdings, courts expect enough detail “to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied.” If the agency’s Vaughn-type declarations are terse or high-level, press for a more granular Vaughn index or for sampling that requires the agency to produce representative documents from each challenged category for in camera review. Sampling can reveal whether the agency is lumping clearly nonexempt material into broader buckets, and it tends to be especially effective where a voluminous collection (10,000+ pages, as here) makes full document-by-document indexing burdensome for the agency.

Third, be proactive about seeking in camera review where permitted. Farahi confirms that district courts may — and sometimes must — examine ex parte submissions to evaluate both the pendency of investigations and segregability. You should therefore request in camera review when the agency relies on classified or sealed material to justify exemptions. Coupled with a motion for a protective order, in camera review is often the only practical way to test the agency’s assertions without jeopardizing underlying investigations.

Fourth, exploit the staleness doctrine by demanding updated declarations if the case lingers. The district court here required the FBI to refresh its declarations because of the time lapse between the agency’s first submission and the court’s consideration. That is a useful tactical lever. If a case stalls or discovery and summary judgment briefing stretches, file a motion asking the court to require the agency to update its declarations — and if the agency claims ongoing proceedings, insist that it identify the basis for that claim (even if only in an ex parte submission to the judge).

Fifth, litigate segregability aggressively but realistically. Farahi reiterates that when nonexempt material is “randomly interspersed line-by-line” throughout an otherwise sensitive document, a court may find that disclosure of individual words or phrases is not “reasonably segregable.” Still, don’t concede segregability; instead, press the agency to show how withheld passages are intertwined with exempt material and to produce a manageable list of candidate releases. If the agency produces only a conclusory statement that “nothing is segregable,” move for a more particularized explanation and, if justified, in camera review targeted at the segregation issue.

## Tactics for agencies — and the limits of deference

For agencies, Farahi is a reminder that category-based withholdings remain a viable, efficient means of handling massive FOIA productions — especially for national-security files. The decision endorses the use of updated public declarations and supplementary ex parte submissions to preserve sensitive explanatory detail. When agencies provide ex parte material to the judge and factual updates that confirm ongoing investigations, courts will give substantial weight to the agency’s assessments.

That said, deference has boundaries. The panel reiterated that courts must still be able to trace a “rational nexus” between the records and law-enforcement duties and that a district court must be able to assess segregability. Practically, then, agencies embracing category-based withholding should be prepared to: (i) provide a clear explanation tying categories to the government’s investigative duties, (ii) describe with specificity why disclosure would interfere with pending or anticipated enforcement proceedings, and (iii) explain why nonexempt material is not reasonably severable without sacrificing meaningful information. If the agency’s public declaration is thin, courts are likely to require a more detailed sealed proffer to sustain the exemption.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Farahi does not change the law; it reaffirms existing D.C. Circuit doctrine on Exemption 7(A), deference to the FBI, the usefulness of ex parte submissions, and the standards for segregability. But it clarifies how those doctrines operate in practice. Two downstream effects are worth watching.

First, expectation management: requesters should now expect more litigation to center on process. The decision incentivizes plaintiffs to litigate sharply over the sufficiency of Vaughn-like category descriptions and to demand sampling and updated declarations when litigation stretches out. Courts will likely see more motions to compel supplemental declarations and to order sampling or in camera review of discrete categories. Conversely, agencies will continue to prefer category-based submissions because they scale to large data sets, provided they can give the judge enough ex parte detail to shoulder the burden of evaluating the exemption.

Second, the decision may subtly raise the bar for appellate reversal of category-based withholdings. Because the D.C. Circuit treated the appellant’s category challenge as forfeited for lack of specificity, appellate courts may be more inclined to treat thin record-keeping or vague challenges as waiver, putting greater responsibility on lower-court advocates to build the record. That dynamic may push litigants earlier toward aggressive fact-gathering — more motions for sampling, tailored in camera review, and precise record-by-record challenges.

## Bottom line

Farahi is a pragmatic, practitioner-centered decision: it confirms that the FBI can carry its burden on Exemption 7(A) through a combination of public and sealed declarations and that district courts may examine sealed submissions to resolve pendency and segregability. But it also sends a blunt message to FOIA requesters — do not rely on generic objections to categories. If you want the court to scrutinize category-based withholdings, preserve and particularize your challenges in the district court, force the agency to produce a useful Vaughn-style explanation (or representative samples), and insist on updated declarations when the case grows stale. Those steps will maximize your chances of piercing broad 7(A) claims and obtaining meaningful disclosure.



# FOIA at the National-Security/Immigration Crossroads

Why this case matters: Farahi v. FBI is an instructive recent example of how FOIA’s law‑enforcement exemptions operate where national‑security investigations intersect with civil immigration proceedings. The case involved an Iranian national, long subject to removal proceedings in which DHS relied on FBI counterterrorism evidence, and a subsequent FOIA request for his FBI file. The D.C. Circuit affirmed withholding under Exemption 7(A), finding that the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that were pending or reasonably anticipated. The opinion underscores three practical realities: (1) FOIA is a limited tool when national‑security investigations are implicated; (2) Exemption 7(A) can bar access to material that has already been used adversarially against a noncitizen in civil enforcement; and (3) courts will give substantial weight to agency declarations — including ex parte submissions — in resolving the tension between public access and investigative secrecy. Those realities have implications both for FOIA practitioners and for due‑process concerns in immigration adjudications where the government’s case turns on hidden records.

What the court did: The D.C. Circuit’s analysis tracked familiar doctrinal contours. Exemption 7(A) requires that records be “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The FBI’s initial public and sealed declarations (from RIDS chiefs Hardy and, later, Bender) described an ongoing counterterrorism investigation and, in the court’s view, provided the factual substrate to find the required “nexus” to law enforcement and the existence of a continuing investigation. The district court had initially pressed the FBI to update potentially stale assertions — a common judicial check when years elapse between an agency’s declaration and a decision — and after that update the court concluded the agency met its burden. On segregability, the court accepted the FBI’s ex parte explanation that non‑exempt material was inextricably intertwined with exempt content, making disclosure of “meaningful information” impossible without compromising the investigation.

Why the result is doctrinally unremarkable — and practically significant: From a precedent perspective, this decision sits well within prior D.C. Circuit FOIA law. The court reiterated the deference owed to the FBI in Exemption 7(A) contexts (particularly where national security is implicated), applied the CREW standard that proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated,” and relied on Mead and Juarez principles on inseparability of exempt and nonexempt material. What makes Farahi noteworthy is not an expansion of Exemption 7(A)’s textual limits but the lesson it offers about FOIA’s limited capacity to function as discovery when federal law enforcement and national security are involved.

Immigration proceedings often depend on evidence the government will not disclose outside the case. FOIA has sometimes operated as a shadow discovery route for noncitizens and their counsel seeking the records used against them — a possibility courts and agencies have recognized and sometimes constrained. Farahi confirms that Exemption 7(A) can preclude disclosure even where material has played an evidentiary role in civil immigration litigation. The government’s ability to withhold under Exemption 7(A) does not vanish merely because related proceedings have used or cited the records; what matters is whether disclosure in the FOIA context would risk undermining ongoing or anticipated enforcement efforts. In practice, that can mean that immigration respondents — who confront information in government filings or testimony — may still be denied access to the underlying investigative materials through FOIA.

The role and limits of ex parte declarations: Farahi also illustrates the judiciary’s uneasy but pragmatic reliance on ex parte, in‑camera submissions in national‑security FOIA litigation. Courts recognize that some explanatory detail cannot be made public without itself disclosing the very harms the government seeks to prevent. The D.C. Circuit reiterated that a district court may properly review ex parte material off the public record to assess the legitimacy of exemption claims and segregability. At the same time, the opinion gestures toward constraints: the district court had properly required updated declarations when time had passed, and the appellate court treated the later Bender declaration as an update to, rather than a substitute for, the earlier detailed Hardy ex parte declaration. For FOIA challengers, this means that timing and layered agency submissions matter: a stale declaration can invite further judicial probing; a robust initial (even sealed) declaration can carry the day when updated by a brief public attestation.

Due‑process‑adjacent pressures the opinion sidesteps: The court stayed within FOIA doctrine and did not attempt to reconcile FOIA outcomes with immigration‑process fairness. But the tension is concrete. Noncitizens in removal proceedings face significant consequences based on evidence that may be withheld from them in other fora. FOIA’s statutory framework and Exemption 7(A) are not designed as a due process cure; the D.C. Circuit appropriately avoided conflating the two. Still, this siloing raises practical due‑process pressures that are likely to persist. Under current practice, remedying that tension typically rests on immigration courts and Article III courts in contexts where classified or sensitive material is at issue (e.g., special procedures adopted in the criminal context or limited discovery mechanisms in administrative proceedings). Absent structural reform — whether legislative (narrowing or conditioning exemptions when material is relied upon in civil administrative adjudication) or procedural (strengthening in‑camera review standards, appointing amicus counsel, or fashioning safer disclosure protocols) — FOIA will continue to be an imperfect and often unavailable route for those challenging the evidence used against them.

Downstream effects and practical takeaways: For practitioners, Farahi is a reminder to expect robust Exemption 7(A) assertions when FOIA requests touch counterterrorism files, and to litigate aggressively over timeliness and the freshness of agency declarations. When facing national‑security FOIA denials, litigants should plan for layered challenges: push for detailed public affidavits where possible, press the court to require timely updating of justifications, and preserve challenges to categorical descriptions of withheld material (the court here found a forfeiture on that front). For policymakers and advocates concerned about procedural fairness in immigration adjudications, Farahi underscores the policy choice embedded in FOIA: transparency yields to the perceived risks to ongoing investigations, even when the disclosed information has been employed against an individual in civil removal proceedings.

Conclusion: Farahi v. FBI is a predictable application of established FOIA principles, but its practical consequences are sharp. Where national‑security investigations and immigration enforcement overlap, FOIA will too often be a closed door. The case presses, without resolving, the harder question about whether — and how — our procedural systems should bridge the gap between national‑security secrecy and fairness in civil adjudication.



# Deference Endures: Farahi in the Post-9/11 FOIA Canon

## Why this case matters

Farahi v. FBI is the latest reaffirmation by the D.C. Circuit of a familiar, and for some critics frustrating, principle: when national-security or counterterrorism investigations are at stake, courts will give the FBI considerable deference in invoking FOIA Exemption 7(A). The opinion matters for three reasons. First, it reiterates the court’s willingness to accept agency attestations—sometimes sealed and ex parte—about ongoing investigations as sufficient to show that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Second, it clarifies how the court treats updates to earlier, more detailed declarations: a short, confirmatory filing need not stand alone if it supplements a robust public and ex parte record. Third, the decision sits at the intersection of two competing pressures in modern FOIA practice: doctrinally entrenched deference to the executive in national-security contexts and policy-level pushes—like DOJ pro-disclosure guidance—aimed at greater transparency. Reading Farahi against the court’s recent FOIA jurisprudence shows that, at least on Exemption 7(A), deference endures.

## The court’s reasoning, in plain terms

The appeal was straightforward. Farahi sought his FBI file; the Bureau located more than 10,000 pages and withheld most of them under Exemption 7(A). The district court initially found the FBI “seemingly” had met its burden but prudently required updated declarations given the passage of time and the public docket’s sensitivity. The FBI responded with a mix of public and ex parte declarations: an initial detailed set from David Hardy, an ex parte declaration that furnished specifics the government could not publicly disclose, and a short confirmatory declaration from Joseph Bender that the investigation remained “ongoing.”

The D.C. Circuit affirmed. It first accepted that the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” noting the substantial public record—including Farahi’s own concessions in immigration proceedings—linking him to terrorism-related investigations. On the critical issue of whether disclosure could “reasonably be expected to interfere” with proceedings that are “pending or reasonably anticipated,” the court followed its usual approach: an agency can show an ongoing investigative proceeding by attesting to its existence; sealed ex parte material can provide the necessary factual specificity; and a brief confirmatory update need not recreate that factual foundation so long as it supplements prior detailed submissions. The court also upheld the district court’s segregability conclusion: where nonexempt material is “interspersed line-by-line” throughout a document and disclosure would itself reveal sensitive context, such fragments are not “reasonably segregable.”

Put differently, the panel treated the combination of (1) a detailed public declaration, (2) an illustrative ex parte declaration, and (3) a short update as a sufficient record to sustain Exemption 7(A) deference—particularly given the national-security character of the investigation.

## A continuation of an established trend

Farahi should be read as consistent with, rather than a departure from, the D.C. Circuit’s post-9/11 FOIA jurisprudence. The court has long recognized that the FBI’s law-enforcement role grants its Exemption 7 invocations “a fair measure of deference,” see Campbell, and has repeatedly sustained withholding when disclosure would tip off ongoing investigations, see CNSS. CREW and Juarez supply the key contours: proceedings must be “pending or reasonably anticipated,” and agency attestations of existence will often suffice when tied to a plausible investigative nexus.

At the same time, Farahi sits alongside more recent decisions that emphasize process and detail—cases like Perioperative Services and Reps. Comm.—in which courts have pushed agencies to provide sufficient factual bases and to take segregability obligations seriously. Here, the district court’s insistence that the FBI refresh its declarations before grant of summary judgment was a procedural check on stale or conclusory submissions. The appellate panel endorsed that approach: updates are appropriate and, taken together with prior robust declarations, adequate.

The upshot: the D.C. Circuit continues a dual posture—requiring enough factual specificity to avoid blind deference, while still showing substantial tolerance for classified or sealed ex parte statements that cannot be made public without defeating their purpose.

## Where DOJ’s pro-disclosure guidance bumps against doctrine

In recent years DOJ has issued pro-disclosure guidance encouraging agencies to err on the side of release where possible. That policy impulse is real and has pushed cultural change inside some agencies. But Farahi illustrates the limits of guidance when it collides with entrenched doctrinal precedent and practical realities. Exemption 7(A) gives the government a statutory pathway to withhold records when disclosure risks law-enforcement harm; courts are understandably reluctant to second-guess an agency’s reasonable predictive judgments about interference, particularly in counterterrorism cases where operational details are genuinely sensitive and disclosure could have grave consequences.

That said, DOJ guidance can make a difference at the margins: it may reduce overbroad invocations, encourage better public explanations where feasible, and nudge agencies toward more rigorous segregability analyses. Farahi shows those nudges can be effective procedurally—the district court’s demand for updated declarations is a kind of institutional thermostat that forces periodic re-examination of secrecy claims. But doctrinally, absent new caselaw or legislation, guidance alone does not—and likely cannot—overturn the longstanding rule that deference is appropriate in this area.

## Doctrinal and policy consequences; any need for reform?

Farahi reinforces some predictable downstream effects. FOIA requesters will continue to face uphill battles when seeking records tied to terrorism or national security investigations; litigants will need to challenge not only the initial merits but also the sufficiency of ex parte submissions, the timeliness of agency updates, and the court’s segregability assessment. Courts, for their part, will keep relying on sealed submissions to assess those claims, which means FOIA litigation in this space will often be a somewhat asymmetric affair: plaintiffs must operate blind to material that is decisive for the court.

Is reform needed? There are three avenues to consider.

First, doctrinal refinement from the D.C. Circuit or the Supreme Court could tighten standards for when ex parte, sealed declarations suffice—e.g., by requiring a higher baseline of public specificity or setting stricter temporal limits on “ongoing” investigations in the FOIA context. That would preserve genuine secrecy needs while improving public accountability. But court-driven tightening encounters separation-of-powers friction and courts’ institutional caution in national-security matters.

Second, legislative reform could amend Exemption 7(A) to add a statutory temporal limit (for example, requiring a showing that an investigation has produced investigative steps within a specified recent period) or to require more explicit findings before sealed submissions can be determinative. That would be transparent and politically accountable but politically difficult.

Third, administrative measures—stronger agency policies, transparency reports, and independent FOIA ombuds oversight—could bridge the gap. These reforms are plausible and less disruptive: they would push agencies to document with greater public specificity why secrecy remains necessary, to revisit old cases systematically, and to produce more granular segregability analyses.

## Bottom line

Farahi does not surprise. It confirms that in the D.C. Circuit the balance between disclosure and security continues to tilt toward deference when counterterrorism and national-security law enforcement are implicated. At the same time, the opinion underscores procedural levers courts can use—periodic updates, demands for detail, skeptical review of segregability—to keep secrecy from calcifying into forever secrecy. For FOIA advocates, the lesson is tactical as well as doctrinal: win transparency not by hoping to abolish deference overnight, but by forcing agencies to justify secrecy with contemporaneous, detailed public record and by pressing courts to require meaningful, not merely formal, segregability showings.
