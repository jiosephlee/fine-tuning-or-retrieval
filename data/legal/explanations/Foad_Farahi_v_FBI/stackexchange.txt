Law Stack Exchange Q&A: Foad_Farahi_v_FBI

### How did the D.C. Circuit have appellate jurisdiction over this FOIA summary judgment?
Question:
The panel affirms a district court's grant of summary judgment to the FBI on a FOIA request. I'm assuming jurisdiction came from 28 U.S.C. § 1291 as a final decision, but does anything about the use of sealed, ex parte declarations or the ongoing-update requirement affect appellate jurisdiction? Is there any FOIA-specific appellate jurisdiction wrinkle I should be aware of, or is this just a straightforward final-judgment appeal?
Answer:
Short answer: This was a straightforward final-judgment appeal under the ordinary final-judgment jurisdictional rule. Nothing in the district court’s use of sealed or ex parte declarations, nor its earlier order requiring an “ongoing” update, changed the fact that the later grant of summary judgment was a final decision appealable to the court of appeals.

Why: the opinion itself treats the case as a routine appeal from a district-court final judgment. The caption and opening state this is an “Appeal from the United States District Court for the District of Columbia,” and the court affirms “the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the FBI.” That posture is exactly the one governed by 28 U.S.C. § 1291 (final decisions of district courts).

Two points from the opinion that explain the procedural posture and why jurisdiction was proper:

- Finality: The district court initially denied the government’s first summary-judgment motion without prejudice and ordered updated declarations; after the FBI submitted updated (public + ex parte) declarations the district court granted the renewed motion. The Court of Appeals reviewed that grant de novo: “In FOIA cases, as in all others, we review a grant of summary judgment de novo.” (Op. § III.) A grant of summary judgment disposing of the merits is a final decision that is appealable under § 1291.

- Sealed / ex parte material does not change finality: The opinion repeatedly relies on sealed and ex parte declarations as part of the district-court record (e.g., the Hardy and Bender ex parte declarations) and explains that the district court could and did rely on ex parte material without putting those details on the public record. The court treats those materials as evidence relevant to the merits (and to segregability/deference issues), not as creating a separate, interlocutory, or jurisdictional problem. The court also explains why ex parte submissions are sometimes necessary in FOIA cases but does not suggest they impair appellate jurisdiction: “we note what the FBI publicly alleged … Based on the FBI’s public and sealed declarations, we conclude…” (Op. §§ IV.B, V).

Practical import for jurisdictional analysis
- An appeal from a district court’s final adjudication of FOIA claims follows ordinary appellate rules. The presence of sealed/ex parte declarations affects the substance of the record and the standard of review (e.g., courts may give deference to agency affidavits and accept sealed detail in camera), but it does not convert a final merits disposition into a non-final or interlocutory order.
- The court’s earlier order asking for updated declarations likewise did not change that result: the first denial was without prejudice (not final), the later grant was the final appealable act, and the panel reviewed that grant on the merits.

So there is no FOIA-specific “jurisdiction wrinkle” here — this was a final-judgment appeal from the grant of summary judgment, with sealed/ex parte filings relevant to the merits but not to appellate jurisdiction.

### What establishes Article III standing for a FOIA requester here, and why didn’t the court discuss it?
Question:
The opinion doesn’t mention standing, yet FOIA plaintiffs still need injury-in-fact, traceability, and redressability. Is denial of access to records alone sufficient Article III injury for FOIA (so courts often don’t discuss it)? Does the fact that the records concern national security or an ongoing investigation change the standing analysis at all?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes—denial of access to requested records ordinarily supplies the Article III “informational” injury that a FOIA plaintiff needs, and that is why the court here did not analyze standing. The rest of the Article III package (traceability and redressability) is also satisfied because the FBI’s withholding is the cause of the injury and a court order requiring disclosure would redress it. The fact that the records concern national security or an ongoing investigation does not change the standing elements; it affects only whether a FOIA exemption permits withholding (the merits), not whether the plaintiff has Article III standing to litigate.

Why the court didn’t discuss standing (grounded in the opinion)
- The opinion records the core facts establishing an actual FOIA request and an agency refusal (or withholding): Farahi “submitted a FOIA request for his FBI file,” the FBI “located some 10,750 pages …” and “withheld most of them,” and Farahi “sued after the FBI missed the statutory deadline for responding.” (Opinion, Part II.) Those facts show a concrete denial of access.
- Courts routinely treat a plaintiff’s alleged entitlement to documents plus the government’s withholding as an injury-in-fact in FOIA suits; when standing is not in dispute, courts often proceed straight to the merits. The opinion moves directly to the statutory exemption analysis (Exemption 7(A)) and the agency’s burden to justify withholding, which is the ordinary sequence when standing is uncontested.
- The panel’s discussion of procedure and burdens (Part III: “The government bears the burden of showing that a FOIA exemption applies,” and the substantive Exemption 7(A) analysis in Parts IV–V) assumes the case is properly before the court; that implicit assumption is consistent with the usual practice of not separately discussing standing where the plaintiff plainly requested records and the agency plainly withheld them.

How the Article III elements are satisfied here
- Injury-in-fact: The court’s recitation of events in Part II—an express FOIA request and agency withholding of thousands of pages—identifies the classic FOIA informational injury: denial of access to requested records.
- Traceability (causation): The withheld records are withheld by the FBI; the opinion treats the FBI’s withholdings as the operative act that gave rise to the litigation (Part II). That establishes the causal link between the defendant’s conduct and the plaintiff’s injury.
- Redressability: The relief FOIA authorizes (release of records) would remedy the injury. The court’s entire remedial inquiry—whether the records must be released or may be withheld under Exemption 7(A), and whether any nonexempt portions are segregable (Part V)—presupposes that a favorable ruling would redress Farahi’s informational injury.

Why national-security or ongoing-investigation concerns don’t change standing
- The opinion repeatedly treats national-security and ongoing-investigation concerns as relevant to the merits (whether an exemption applies and whether deference to the FBI is appropriate), not to standing. See Part III (deference to FBI “particularly appropriate for records implicating national security”) and the Exemption 7(A) analysis in Part IV (whether disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings”).
- Those concerns can support withholding under an exemption (and the court here found Exemption 7(A) satisfied), but they do not negate the basic fact that the requester has been denied access or alter the causation/redressability analysis. In short: national-security sensitivity bears on whether the plaintiff wins, not on whether the plaintiff has the right to sue.

Practical note for future FOIA litigants
- Courts will often assume standing in cases like this where the requester made an identifiable request and the agency withheld records. If a defendant contests standing, plaintiffs should be prepared to show the same facts the D.C. Circuit recited here (request, identification of withheld records, agency action or failure to respond), and explain that judicial ordering of disclosure would redress the injury.

Conclusion
The opinion’s silence on Article III standing reflects that the basic FOIA facts are present and uncontested: Farahi requested his FBI file and the FBI withheld most of it. That denial of access supplies the informational injury-in-fact, and the agency’s withholding is traceable and redressable by a court order. National-security or investigative sensitivity affects Exemption 7(A) on the merits (which the court addressed at length) but does not change the standing inquiry.

### How can the court review de novo but still defer to the FBI under Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
The panel says FOIA summary-judgment rulings are reviewed de novo, but also that the FBI’s decision to invoke Exemption 7 is entitled to deference, especially in national-security contexts (citing Campbell and CNSS). What exactly is reviewed de novo versus what receives deference? Is the deference about the weight accorded to agency affidavits (presumption of good faith), while legal conclusions remain de novo?
Answer:
Short answer
The court reviews the district court’s grant of summary judgment de novo, but it gives deference to the FBI’s factual and predictive law‑enforcement judgments (as embodied in its declarations) because the FBI is the specialized law‑enforcement agency. That means: (1) the appellate court independently decides whether the exemption, as a matter of law, was properly applied; (2) but the court will accept the agency’s factual showings and predictive judgments (e.g., that an investigation is “ongoing” or that disclosure would “tip off” targets) so long as the agency’s declarations are reasonably specific and plausible. In short: legal conclusions are reviewed de novo; the agency’s factual/predictive affidavits and expertise receive deference and persuasive weight.

How the opinion shows that
- De novo review of summary judgment: the panel begins by stating the usual rule—“we review a grant of summary judgment de novo.” (III, opening paragraph; citing Reps. Comm. for Freedom of the Press v. FBI.) That establishes the procedural posture: the court independently reviews whether the district court properly entered judgment for the FBI.

- Where deference fits in: immediately after noting de novo review, the opinion explains that “Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference,” and that deference is “particularly appropriate for records implicating national security.” (III, ¶2 (citing Campbell and CNSS).) So the court is not surrendering its review; it is describing the evidentiary and substantive weight given to agency submissions in the FOIA context.

- Factual and predictive judgments get the deference: the opinion repeatedly relies on the FBI’s declarations (Hardy and the ex parte Hardy and Bender declarations) to establish factual predicates—e.g., that the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and that the investigation remained “ongoing,” so disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere.” The court says the government “may show such proceedings simply by attesting to their ‘existence’” (IV.B) and that a court “may rely on government affidavits that show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated.” (III, last ¶; IV.B & V.) These are classic examples of deference to agency factual/predictive statements.

- Courts still police adequacy and specificity: deference is not carte blanche. The opinion cites CREW and Juarez to show that conclusory assertions may be inadequate (IV.B). The court examined the record as a whole and treated Bender’s terse update in context of the earlier, more detailed Hardy declarations and the district court’s prior finding that the FBI “seemingly” met its burden. (IV.B.) Also, the court required updated declarations because “the significant passage of time” could defeat the factual premise that proceedings remained pending. (II, district court action summarized.) That illustrates that courts will independently test whether the agency has carried its burden, even while giving weight to agency expertise.

Putting it together for practice
- When reading this opinion: think of two separate things the appellate court does.
  1. Legal review (de novo): Ask whether, as a matter of law, Exemption 7(A)’s elements were satisfied and whether the district court applied the correct legal standards (statutory text, case law). The court decides these questions independently.
  2. Factual/predictive evaluation (deferential weight): Ask whether the agency has produced sufficiently detailed, plausible factual declarations showing an investigation is “pending or reasonably anticipated,” that disclosure would interfere, or that non‑exempt material is not “reasonably segregable.” The court will generally accept the agency’s specialized judgments on these matters unless they are conclusory or implausible.

So your intuition is correct: the “deference” principally concerns the evidentiary weight accorded to agency affidavits (their factual and predictive assertions rooted in agency expertise), whereas the court retains de novo oversight over legal conclusions and the application of FOIA law. The Farahi opinion exemplifies that approach: de novo review of the summary‑judgment ruling combined with respectful deference to the FBI’s detailed declarations about ongoing national‑security investigations.

### Why do ongoing criminal investigations qualify as 'enforcement proceedings' under 7(A)?
Question:
Exemption 7(A) protects records if disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with 'enforcement proceedings.' The court, citing CREW and Juarez, treats an ongoing criminal investigation as such a proceeding. From a statutory-interpretation standpoint, how did courts get from 'proceedings' (which sounds formal) to including pre-charge investigations, and what limits exist on that reading?
Answer:
Short answer
Courts treat an ongoing criminal investigation as an “enforcement proceeding” under Exemption 7(A) because the statutory word “proceedings” is read functionally, not formally: if an investigation is actively gathering evidence for a possible criminal prosecution, it is the kind of law‑enforcement activity that Exemption 7(A) protects. That understanding is limited, however: the proceeding must be “pending or reasonably anticipated,” the agency must carry its FOIA burden (with reasonably specific affidavits), and vague or stale assertions will not suffice.

Why “proceedings” covers pre‑charge investigations (the doctrinal move)
- Functional, not formal, reading: The court follows this Circuit’s precedent (CREW and Juarez) that “proceedings” in Exemption 7(A) need not mean only formal court or administrative adjudications. Instead, an investigation that is actively gathering evidence for a possible future criminal case is treated as a prospective enforcement proceeding. The opinion explicitly cites CREW and Juarez (¶ IV.B) and quotes Juarez: “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies.” J.A. cited in ¶ IV.B (Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59).
- Statutory text plus function: The court applies the statutory requirement that records be “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and then asks whether disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” That interference question is sensibly answered by asking whether disclosure would harm an ongoing investigatory effort that aims to produce prosecutable evidence (¶ IV.B).

What courts require before accepting an investigation as a “proceeding”
- Pendency or reasonable anticipation: Following CREW and Mapother, this Circuit insists the proceeding be “pending or reasonably anticipated” (¶ I, ¶ IV.B). A bare, speculative hope of a future prosecution will not do; there must be a concrete prospective enforcement effort.
- Agency burden and specificity: The government bears the FOIA burden to justify Exemption 7(A). Courts accept agency declarations, but they must establish a rational nexus between the records and law‑enforcement duties and provide enough detail to show the existence and scope of the investigation (¶ IV.A, citing Campbell and Pratt). The opinion notes that the government may show pendency by attesting to the investigation’s “existence” (CREW, ¶ IV.B), but the court also warns against purely “vague” assertions (CREW language quoted in ¶ IV.B).
- Context and corroboration: The panel accepted a combination of public and ex parte affidavits here—Hardy’s public declaration and a more detailed ex parte declaration—plus a later update by Bender confirming the investigation remained ongoing. The court explained that the terse update was sufficient only because it was read in context of the earlier, detailed submissions (¶ IV.B). So specificity may be satisfied by the record as a whole, including sealed material the court reviews in camera.

Practical limits on the expansive reading
- Not unlimited: The opinion and its precedents set real limits. The proceeding must be pending or reasonably anticipated; courts will not accept conclusory or stale statements. CREW’s caution about the “vague nature” of assertions was applied here: the court required updated, reasonably specific declarations because of the “passage of time” (¶ IV.B).
- Temporal and factual constraints: Passage of time can undermine an assertion that proceedings are pending; an agency must update its showing if circumstances change (the district court ordered exactly that in this case, ¶ II). The government’s showing must be credible in light of the record (the panel relied on the earlier detailed ex parte filing and public allegations about the subject’s alleged connections to terrorism to sustain the claim of ongoing investigation, ¶ IV.B).
- Segregability and scope: Even if Exemption 7(A) applies, the agency must still segregate and release nonexempt information, and categories used to justify withholding must be sufficiently distinct (¶ V and ¶ IV.C). The court will scrutinize whether claimed categories are overbroad (the opinion found Farahi forfeited that challenge here, but reiterates the standard from CREW and Gallant).

Bottom line
The Circuit reads “proceedings” to include ongoing investigations because the statute’s purpose is to prevent disclosure that would interfere with law‑enforcement functions—an active investigation that aims to produce evidence for prosecution is squarely within that purpose. But the government cannot rely on mere speculation or stale, conclusory statements; it must show (through reasonably specific declarations and, when necessary, timely updates) that an investigation is ongoing or reasonably anticipated and that disclosure would likely interfere. The Farahi opinion applies those limits: it accepted the FBI’s showing because of the combination of public and ex parte detail and a timely update confirming continued investigatory activity (¶¶ IV.A–B, V).

### Did the court miscite § 552(b)(7)(B) when discussing 7(A), and does that affect the analysis?
Question:
In Part IV.B the opinion quotes the 'could reasonably be expected to interfere' language but cites § 552(b)(7)(B) instead of 7(A). Is that just a scrivener’s error? If so, does it have any substantive consequence, considering 7(B) deals with fair trial rights rather than interference with enforcement proceedings?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — the citation in Part IV.B to § 552(b)(7)(B) is a scrivener’s error, and it is harmless. The opinion’s reasoning plainly invokes and applies Exemption 7(A), not 7(B), so the miscitation does not alter the legal analysis or the result.

Why: 
- The disputed language — that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” — is the statutory text of Exemption 7(A), 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). The court itself elsewhere correctly states the Exemption 7(A) standard (see Part I and Part IV.A: “Exemption 7(A) covers … could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings,” and the opinion repeatedly cites § 552(b)(7)(A)).  
- The single miscite appears in the opening paragraph of Part IV.B, where the opinion says “Exemption 7(A) also requires that release …” and then cites § 552(b)(7)(B). That is a typographical/citation mistake: the quoted standard and the surrounding discussion are about interference with enforcement proceedings (the subject matter of 7(A)), not the fair-trial/witness-protection concerns addressed by 7(B).  
- The court’s doctrinal sources and analytical framework confirm it is applying 7(A): it repeatedly relies on CREW, Juarez, and CNSS (all 7(A) authority dealing with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings and the “could reasonably be expected to interfere” formulation), and it frames the inquiry in the terms that 7(A) requires (pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings, ongoing investigations, danger of tipping off targets). Those citations and reasoning are in Part IV.B (e.g., CREW at 1096–98; Juarez at 59–60), and the opinion twice expressly identifies the exemption at issue as 7(A).  
- Substantive consequences: None follow from the miscitation. If the court had intended to apply Exemption 7(B) (which concerns interference with a fair trial), the analysis and authorities would look very different. But the opinion’s factual findings (existence and ongoing nature of investigations, risk of tipping off targets) and legal discussion track 7(A) doctrine throughout. The miscitation therefore does not create a conflict between the text and the applied law, nor does it change the legal standard the court used to resolve the case.

Conclusion: Treat the citation to § 552(b)(7)(B) in Part IV.B as a harmless scrivener’s error. The opinion consistently applies Exemption 7(A) and the governing precedents for the interference-with-enforcement-proceedings inquiry, so the mistake does not affect the outcome.

### What made the Hardy and Bender declarations sufficiently specific despite CREW’s warning about vague assertions?
Question:
CREW says vague claims that an investigation is 'ongoing' can be insufficient for 7(A). Here, the court acknowledges Bender’s public declaration was 'surprisingly terse,' but deems it adequate in light of the earlier Hardy declarations (including ex parte detail) and the district court’s prior 'seemingly' satisfied finding. Do courts accept a short 'update' affidavit if earlier public and sealed submissions provided the specificity? What level of public specificity is required post-CREW?
Answer:
Short answer
The court upheld the FBI’s withholding because the Bender “terse” public declaration was not considered in isolation — it was an update to the earlier Hardy submissions (one public declaration plus a more detailed ex parte declaration) and the district court had already found that the FBI “seemingly” met its burden. Under CREW and this opinion, a brief public “update” can suffice so long as (1) earlier public and/or sealed filings already supplied the required factual nexus and detail showing investigations were “pending or reasonably anticipated,” and (2) the update genuinely confirms that nothing material has changed. If the agency’s only public statement is conclusory (no prior detail or sealed support), CREW warns that that will likely be insufficient.

Why the declarations were adequate here (with citation to the opinion)
- CREW’s baseline rule: Exemption 7(A) requires proceedings that are “pending or reasonably anticipated,” and the government may show such proceedings by attesting to their “existence.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096–99 (quoted in Opinion § IV.B). The court reiterates that a showing of existence can be made by an agency attestation. Op. § IV.B (pp. 10–11 of the excerpt).
- The Hardy declarations provided the necessary foundation: the public Hardy declaration attested the records were compiled for national-security and terrorism-related investigations (J.A. 42, Op. § IV.A), and the sealed ex parte Hardy declaration “supplied considerable detail about the nature of that investigation.” Op. § IV.A.
- Bender was an update, not a freestanding assertion: the court accepted that Bender’s public line — that the investigations “remain ongoing” — was “surprisingly terse” if viewed alone, but it was adequate because it simply confirmed that nothing changed since Hardy’s submissions and the district court had already found the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden earlier. Op. § IV.B (J.A. 122–23, 131).
- Practical deference/context: the court gave deference to the FBI on law-enforcement and national-security matters and relied on the combination of public filings, the sealed ex parte material, and the district court’s prior assessment to conclude there remained a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding.” Op. § IV (citing Campbell and CNSS).

What this means for later FOIA practice (what level of public specificity is required post-CREW)
- Minimum public showing: At least an attestation that an investigation or enforcement proceeding exists and that disclosure could interfere with it (CREW’s “existence” test). Op. § IV.B; CREW, 746 F.3d at 1099.
- When an agency can rely on a short update: If the agency previously filed public declarations and, where necessary, a sealed/ex parte declaration that provide substantive factual detail (nexus to law-enforcement purpose, scope, nature of investigation, and why disclosure would interfere), a brief subsequent public statement confirming continuity (e.g., “investigation remains ongoing”) will usually be accepted. Op. § IV.B.
- When a short public statement will fail: If there are no prior public or sealed factual submissions establishing the character and pendency of the investigation, a lone conclusory public declaration that an investigation is “ongoing” risks being “vague” under CREW and may not carry the agency’s burden. Op. § IV.B (discussing CREW).
- Role of sealed/ex parte material and segregability: Courts may (and often will) rely on ex parte declarations for detail the agency cannot publicly disclose; the availability of such sealed detail strengthens the validity of a short public update. The court also accepted the FBI’s sealed justificaton for non-segregability. Op. § V.

Bottom line rule of thumb
- Agencies should provide a substantive public declaration (showing nexus to law enforcement and attesting that disclosure would interfere) and may supplement it with sealed/ex parte detail when necessary. If time passes, a concise public update that confirms the original facts still hold will ordinarily be adequate. But where the public record lacks factual substance, CREW warns that conclusory “investigation ongoing” statements alone will likely fail. The Farahi opinion applies exactly that combined-record approach in sustaining exemption 7(A).

### When are FOIA affidavits considered 'stale,' and what triggers a duty to update them?
Question:
The district court denied summary judgment without prejudice because the initial declarations might have become stale over time and ordered an update. Are there established benchmarks or factors for when staleness requires updated declarations (e.g., elapsed time, changed circumstances, nature of the investigation)? Or is this a case-by-case, discretionary call?
Answer:
Short answer
There is no fixed time‑limit after which FOIA affidavits automatically go “stale.” Courts decide whether an affidavit needs updating case‑by‑case, but the decision is guided by familiar, objective considerations: the elapsed time since the affidavit, whether the underlying facts or investigative posture could have changed, the specificity of the original affidavit, and the nature/breadth of the investigation. An agency must update when those considerations make the original attestations untrustworthy to show that enforcement proceedings are still “pending or reasonably anticipated.”

How the opinion applies those principles
- The district court in this case denied the FBI’s initial summary‑judgment motion without prejudice because of “the significant passage of time” since the FBI’s 2019 Hardy declaration and ordered an update to confirm whether proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated. J.A. 121–22. That is the court exercising the case‑by‑case discretion the law contemplates.  
- The Court of Appeals approved treating the subsequent Bender declaration as an “update” to the Hardy declarations, not as a standalone showing, because the Hardy submissions (including a sealed ex parte declaration) had already supplied the detailed factual predicate and the Bender filing was intended only to confirm nothing had changed while the initial motion was pending. J.A. 131; J.A. 138–39.  
- The opinion relied on CREW v. DOJ for the underlying rule that Exemption 7(A) requires proceedings that are “pending or reasonably anticipated” and that vague assertions that an investigation is “ongoing” may be insufficient by themselves. 746 F.3d at 1096–98; see also J.A. 122–23 (discussing CREW).

Practical factors courts consider (drawn from the opinion and governing precedents)
- Elapsed time: A long delay between the affidavit and the court’s decision is a primary reason to require an update (the district court ordered an update here for that reason, J.A. 121–22).  
- Changed circumstances: If events (e.g., indictments, case closures, transfers, institutional reorganizations) could have altered the pendency or anticipated nature of proceedings, courts will expect an updated attestation.  
- Specificity of the original affidavit: General or “vague” statements that an investigation is “ongoing” are weaker than detailed affidavits describing the investigation’s scope and the steps being taken; CREW warns that a conclusory assertion may not suffice. See CREW, 746 F.3d at 1098; J.A. 122–23.  
- Nature and breadth of the investigation: Large, continuing counterterrorism or national‑security investigations (as described in the sealed Hardy declaration and the public filings here) make it more plausible that proceedings remain ongoing and thus may justify less frequent updates. The court emphasized the breadth of the FBI’s investigation in assessing the adequacy of the government’s showing. J.A. 136–38.  
- Prior court findings: If a court has already found the agency “seemingly” met its burden (as the district court did here), a short confirming update may suffice rather than a full restart of the agency’s showing. J.A. 122; J.A. 138–39.  
- Whether the agency can supply sensitive detail ex parte: Courts may accept sealed, ex parte declarations that reveal more of the underlying investigative facts without public disclosure; the opinion approved reliance on such filings when necessary and when the plaintiff did not oppose their use. J.A. 42, 49; J.A. 139–40.

What triggers the duty to update (summary)
- An agency must update when the facts material to its exemption claim may have changed since its last affidavit, or when the passage of time undermines the probative value of the prior attestations. The district court’s order in this case—prompted by “significant passage of time”—illustrates the trigger. J.A. 121–22. The updated declaration need only fill the gap identified by the court (here, confirming whether the investigation remained ongoing), and can be considered together with the original, more detailed declarations. J.A. 131; J.A. 138–39.

Bottom line for practitioners
- Defense: If you represent an agency, anticipate that a court may require an update whenever significant time passes or circumstances change; be prepared to supply a short confirming declaration plus, if necessary, sealed/ex parte detail.  
- Requesters: If you represent a FOIA requester, challenge stale or conclusory affidavits and ask the court to require an updated showing as to pendency/anticipation. Cite CREW and press for specifics if the original affidavit is vague.  
- Courts will balance the agency’s need for confidentiality and deference (especially in national‑security matters) against the requester’s right to a verifiable showing that Exemption 7(A) applies, on the facts of the case.

References in the opinion: district court’s order citing “significant passage of time” and requiring an update (J.A. 121–22); CREW on the insufficiency of vague “ongoing” assertions (746 F.3d at 1096–98, cited at J.A. 122–23); appellate discussion treating Bender as an update to Hardy and crediting the updated confirmation that proceedings “remain ongoing” (J.A. 131; J.A. 138–39).

### How did the FBI satisfy the 'compiled for law enforcement purposes' requirement under Pratt/Campbell here?
Question:
The court requires a rational nexus to a law-enforcement duty and a connection to a security risk or violation of federal law. Publicly, Hardy said the records related to national security/terrorism investigations and ties to persons involved in such violations; the ex parte declaration added detail. Is that combination (general public assertions plus sealed specifics) typically enough to meet Pratt’s nexus test? Does the use of the file in immigration proceedings affect the 'law enforcement purposes' analysis?
Answer:
Short answer
The FBI satisfied the Pratt/Campbell “compiled for law enforcement purposes” requirement by (1) invoking the FBI’s institutional law‑enforcement role (which gets deference), (2) offering a public declaration tying the records to investigations of national security/terrorism, and (3) supplementing that public assertion with an ex parte declaration supplying “considerable detail” about the nature and scope of the investigation. The court treated the public Hardy declaration and the sealed Hardy/Bender declarations together as establishing the required rational nexus and connection to a possible violation of federal law. Farahi’s use of the file in immigration proceedings did not defeat the analysis; the opinion relies on the FBI’s demonstrated ties to terrorism‑related investigations (including facts already in the public immigration record) to show a law‑enforcement purpose.

Why that satisfied Pratt/Campbell (the doctrinal test)
- The controlling test (Pratt, Campbell): Exemption 7(A) applies only to records “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” The agency must establish a rational nexus between the records and one of its law‑enforcement duties and a nexus between the individual/incident and a possible security risk or federal‑law violation. Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (quoting Pratt). (Op. § IV.A.)
- Deference to the FBI: The court reiterates that the FBI’s judgment is entitled to a “fair measure of deference” because it is a law‑enforcement agency, and that deference is heightened where national‑security subjects are implicated. (Op. § IV.A; citing Campbell and CNSS.) That means the court asks whether the FBI’s declarations reasonably establish the required nexus, not whether the court independently proves the underlying criminality.
- How the FBI met the test here: The public Hardy declaration explicitly stated the records were “compiled in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism” and tied the subject’s associations to persons “directly involved in such violations.” J.A. 42 (quoted in Op. § IV.A). The court emphasized that the sealed (ex parte) Hardy declaration supplied “considerable detail” about the investigation’s nature and scope, and that Bender’s later declarations confirmed the investigation remained ongoing. The court relied on the combination of the public assertion plus the ex parte details to find the rational nexus and connection to terrorism/federal offenses satisfied. (Op. § IV.A–B.)
- Corroborating public context: The court also pointed to facts already in the public immigration record (e.g., alleged ties to designated foreign terrorist organizations, connections to an individual later detained as an enemy combatant, links to convicted/indicted terrorists, and alleged support for charities that financed terrorism) as reinforcing that the file was compiled for law‑enforcement purposes. Op. § IV.A (citing Farahi’s immigration litigation). The court explicitly declined to disclose the sealed declaration but treated it as supplying substantive detail the public declaration did not. Op. § IV.A–B.

Is a combination of general public assertions plus sealed specifics typically enough?
Yes, within limits. The opinion treats the pattern used here as sufficient: a public declaration that asserts existence and general scope of law‑enforcement investigations coupled with an ex parte sealed declaration that provides the particularized factual detail courts cannot safely put on the public record. The court stressed that the Bender declaration need not stand alone; it was an update confirming nothing changed since the detailed (but partially sealed) Hardy submissions. Op. § IV.B. The court also reiterated precedent that the government may show an ongoing investigation by attesting to its existence and that ex parte declarations are permissible where necessary to avoid disclosing the very materials sought. Op. § IV.B, V. So, a public-plus-sealed presentation is a frequently accepted method so long as the public declaration and (on in camera/ex parte review) the sealed material together meet the Campbell/Pratt standards and the district court is satisfied the detail supports the claim.

Does the file’s use in immigration proceedings affect the “law enforcement purposes” analysis?
Not in a way that defeats Exemption 7(A) here. The opinion explicitly relies on facts revealed in immigration proceedings (publicly available pleadings and testimony) to corroborate the FBI’s claim that the file relates to terrorism investigations; those facts helped the court conclude the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes. Op. § II (background) & § IV.A (noting Farahi’s concession in the district court about the FBI’s investigations) — the court treated the immigration record as confirmatory evidence of a law‑enforcement nexus. The opinion does not treat the immigration use as converting the file into a non‑law‑enforcement record; rather, the existence of parallel immigration usage did not undermine the FBI’s showing that the files were created and maintained in connection with law‑enforcement investigations. Op. § IV.A–B.

Practical takeaway for future FOIA disputes
- Public declarations that assert a terrorism/national‑security nexus, when paired with more detailed ex parte/in‑camera submissions, can satisfy Pratt/Campbell if the sealed material actually supplies the factual glue the public statement lacks. (Op. § IV.A–B.)
- Courts will credit an agency’s showings where (i) the agency is the FBI (entitled to deference), (ii) the public statements at least identify the law‑enforcement subject matter (e.g., terrorism/national security), and (iii) the sealed materials—reviewed by the court—supply concrete detail, especially if corroborated by other public records. (Op. § IV.A–B.)
- Parallel civil/administrative uses (like immigration proceedings) do not automatically defeat a claim that records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes; they may even corroborate it. (Op. § IV.A.)

Caveat
The court did note that an isolated, conclusory update (the Bender declaration taken alone) might have been thin; the government succeeded here only because the update was linked to the earlier, more detailed (including ex parte) submissions and prior district‑court findings. Op. § IV.B.

### Do immigration removal proceedings count as 'enforcement proceedings' for 7(A), or was the court relying only on criminal investigations?
Question:
The opinion repeatedly frames the relevant 'proceedings' as ongoing criminal investigations (citing Juarez). But Farahi’s file was used in immigration removal litigation. Can civil administrative proceedings like removal independently satisfy 7(A)? If so, why did the court emphasize criminal investigations instead of DHS removal proceedings?

Short answer: The court treated the relevant “proceedings” as law‑enforcement (criminal) investigations, not as immigration removal proceedings standing alone. The opinion does not rest its Exemption 7(A) holding on the DHS removal case; it rests on the FBI’s ongoing criminal investigation into terrorism‑related activity.

Why — doctrine and how the opinion applies it
- Exemption 7(A) applies to records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” where disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The opinion repeatedly cites CREW and Juarez for that standard (Op. §§ I–III, and IV.B).  
- Juarez and CREW make clear that ongoing criminal investigations count as “proceedings” and that an agency can show pendency by attesting to the investigation’s existence (Op. § IV.B, citing Juarez and CREW). The court reiterates the point: “Ongoing criminal investigations count as ‘proceedings’ for these purposes” and emphasizes that the government may show such proceedings by attestation (Op. § IV.B).
- The court credited the FBI’s public and ex parte declarations (Hardy and Bender) describing an active FBI investigation into terrorism‑related activity and relied on those declarations to conclude disclosure would tip off targets and interfere with the investigation (Op. § IV.B; J.A. 49, 131–39). The court also relied on the sealed ex parte declaration for detailed factual support (Op. § IV.B).

How the immigration removal proceedings feature in the opinion
- The opinion acknowledges that DHS used FBI materials in Farahi’s removal proceedings and quotes public factual allegations from those proceedings (Op. § II and § IV.B). That factual background supports the conclusion that the FBI had law‑enforcement reasons to investigate (nexus to terrorism, ties to convicted terrorists, etc.).  
- But the court does not say the civil immigration removal proceedings themselves are the “enforcement proceedings” that justify Exemption 7(A). Instead, the immigration litigation is cited as corroborating context for the FBI’s continued investigation; the legal analysis invokes criminal investigation authorities (Juarez, CREW) and focuses on the FBI’s attestations about an ongoing criminal inquiry (Op. §§ IV.A–B).

Practical conclusion for this case (and generally)
- In this case, Exemption 7(A) was upheld because the FBI showed an ongoing criminal investigation and that disclosure would interfere with enforcement (Op. § IV.B). The opinion’s rationale is predicated on criminal investigative activity, not on the civil removal litigation standing alone.  
- That said, nothing in the opinion doctrinally forbids treating civil administrative enforcement (including removal proceedings) as “enforcement proceedings” under 7(A) where the record shows a nexus to the agency’s law‑enforcement duties and that disclosure would interfere with the proceeding. But the court here did not rely on that theory — it relied on the FBI’s criminal investigation as the operative enforcement proceeding.

So: civil removal proceedings appear only as background corroboration of an FBI criminal investigation; the court’s Exemption 7(A) holding depends on the existence of ongoing criminal law‑enforcement proceedings and the FBI’s attestations about them.

### Was it appropriate for the court to rely on allegations from an Eleventh Circuit immigration decision to support an 'ongoing' investigation?
Question:
Because it couldn’t disclose sealed details, the panel cited public allegations about Farahi (e.g., ties to Al Qaeda figures) from an Eleventh Circuit opinion. Are courts permitted to use such allegations as contextual support for the plausibility that an investigation is ongoing? Do those allegations have to be proven facts, or is their existence in the public record enough to bolster the inference?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — the court permissibly relied on publicly available allegations from the Eleventh Circuit immigration opinion as contextual support for the FBI’s claim that an investigation remained “ongoing.” The opinion treats those published allegations as part of the public record that makes the FBI’s attestations plausible, but it does not treat those allegations as adjudicated facts standing in for the government’s burdens; the government still had to and did satisfy Exemption 7(A) through its declarations (including ex parte material).

Why — legal doctrine and how the opinion applies it

- Exemption 7(A) requires the government to show (1) the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and (2) disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096; J.A. 36–37 (Op. III–IV).

- For the “pending or reasonably anticipated” element, this court has held that the government may show an investigation’s existence by attestation — an affidavit that an investigation is ongoing can suffice. CREW, 746 F.3d at 1099; Juarez, 518 F.3d at 59. The opinion repeats that principle and applies it here: the Hardy and Bender declarations (public and ex parte) attested that the investigation remained ongoing, and the court found those attestations probative. See J.A. 48–51, 131–39 (Op. IV.B).

- The court explicitly relied on broader public materials (the Eleventh Circuit immigration opinion) as “broader context” that makes the FBI’s representations plausible. The opinion cites the Eleventh Circuit’s publicly reported allegations about Farahi — e.g., that he was “a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood,” “connected to Khaled Sheikh Mohamed and other known Al Qaeda members,” “linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists,” and that he supported charities alleged to have financed terrorism — and says those public allegations, together with the (sealed) government declarations, support the conclusion that a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” continues to exist. J.A. 51–52 (Op. IV.B).

What the court did — and did not — do with those allegations

- Used as corroborative context: The court used the Eleventh Circuit’s public statements to show that the FBI’s declared investigative subject and the alleged severity/breadth of the matters under investigation make it plausible the FBI still has active investigations worth protecting. The opinion treats the published immigration allegations as part of the public matrix that supports the government’s affidavits (J.A. 51–52).

- Did not treat allegations as proven law‑of‑the‑case: The opinion does not purport to convert those allegations into conclusive factual findings for purposes of FOIA. The court’s legal holding rests on the government’s declarations (including ex parte material) and on the law that an attestation of an ongoing investigation can suffice. The Eleventh Circuit’s published allegations are cited as public corroboration, not as substitute proof that the FBI satisfied its burden in place of its own attestations (see J.A. 48–52).

Practical limits and cautions the opinion signals

- Allegations in the public record can bolster plausibility, but they are not a free pass. The court emphasized that it was the combination of the government’s public and ex parte declarations plus public context that met the Exemption 7(A) showing. It expressly rejects treating the terse Bender declaration “in isolation”; context (including the earlier Hardy declarations and the public materials) made the update persuasive (J.A. 50–52).

- If the public allegations were stale, contradicted, or insufficiently connected to the asserted law‑enforcement interest, they might not carry the same weight. The opinion recognizes that the government’s attestations must be sufficiently particular and that the district court required updated declarations because of the passage of time (J.A. 44–46, 48–50). The court also cites CREW’s warning that vague assertions of ongoing investigations can fail (J.A. 48–49).

Bottom line

Courts may rely on publicly available allegations in other proceedings as contextual support for the plausibility of an ongoing investigation, and the existence of those allegations in the public record can help bolster the inference. But those allegations do not replace the government’s obligation to make a factual showing (through affidavits/declarations, including ex parte submissions where necessary) that disclosure would interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. The Farahi opinion applied that approach: it treated the Eleventh Circuit’s published allegations as corroborative context, while resting the Exemption 7(A) holding on the government’s sworn (public and sealed) attestations.

### What is required to justify categorical withholdings under 7(A), and how did Farahi forfeit his challenge?
Question:
The opinion says that when an agency withholds by category, the categories must be 'sufficiently distinct' (citing Gallant and CREW), but concludes Farahi forfeited any challenge for not explaining why the FBI’s categories were overbroad. What does an adequate challenge to categories look like in practice (e.g., specific Vaughn-index critiques)? How specific must the agency’s category descriptions be to allow meaningful adversarial testing?
Answer:
Short answer
- To justify categorical withholdings under Exemption 7(A) an agency must (1) describe categories that are “sufficiently distinct” so a court can test whether the claimed exemption fits each category, and (2) provide enough factual detail tying each category to the asserted harm (here, interference with pending or anticipated enforcement proceedings). (The opinion cites CREW and Gallant for the “sufficiently distinct” rule and applies it in Part IV.C.)
- Farahi forfeited his challenge because he never explained, with specific argument or evidence, why the FBI’s categories were overbroad—he made only a general objection. The court therefore declined to address the merits of the category challenge (citing Khine), see Part IV.C.

What the court required (doctrine and standard)
- The governing principle: when an agency withholds by category rather than item-by-item, the categories must be “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each category (CREW, quoted in the opinion at IV.C). The agency must also show the Exemption 7(A) harm (disclosure would “reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings”) with enough factual support to be credited (see Parts IV.A–B).
- The court will look to the public declarations and, where necessary, sealed/ex parte submissions for the factual showing. But even if some material is submitted ex parte, the categories themselves must be framed so the adversary (and the court) can meaningfully test them (IV.C and discussion of ex parte material in V).

What an adequate challenge looks like in practice (concrete examples)
A FOIA challenger attacking category-based withholding should do more than say “categories are too broad.” Practical, concrete criticisms that a court expects include some combination of the following (all grounded in the opinion’s prescriptions and precedents):

1. Ask for a Vaughn-style itemization (or more detailed index) tied to the categories
   - Demand a Vaughn index or a supplemental index that lists (for at least a representative sample) each withheld document or subpart, the category label the agency assigned, the exemption invoked for that item, and a short factual justification linking the item to the claimed harm.
   - Why: the court needs to see how the agency applied the category labels across actual records to test whether the category is “sufficiently distinct” (CREW/Gallant, cited in IV.C).

2. Identify specific categories and explain why they are not distinct
   - Point to two or more of the agency’s category labels and explain with examples why they overlap (e.g., “Category A = ‘investigative leads’” and “Category B = ‘sources and methods’” effectively cover the same content).
   - Show that the category description is so vague that the agency could hide non-exempt material within it.

3. Use document-level examples (even from disclosed pages) to show mismatch
   - Reference particular pages or redactions already released (or unredacted metadata) and explain why those examples show the category claim is overbroad (e.g., the released material shows the same diary entry labeled in the index as non-exempt, but other diary entries were lumped into a broad category).
   - Why: courts (and the opinion here) look to concrete factual comparisons rather than abstract objections.

4. Challenge the linkage between category and claimed harm
   - For each category, explain why disclosure of documents in that category would not “reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” For example, argue that a subcategory consists of purely historical or publicly known facts, or that disclosure would not reveal investigative scope, sources, or techniques.
   - Tie the argument to the government’s factual claim (e.g., the Hardy/Bender declarations): show inconsistencies, stale facts, or insufficient specificity.

5. Press for more specificity where the agency’s public justification is terse
   - If the agency’s public declaration is conclusory (as the opinion notes Bender’s looked “surprisingly terse” when considered in isolation, IV.B), press for supplementation: more detailed public descriptions or a narrowly tailored Vaughn index (not just ex parte material).
   - If the agency claims ongoing investigations, ask for dates, nature of the proceedings, names or categories of individuals involved (to the extent not protected), or other contextual facts that bear on whether the investigation remains “pending or reasonably anticipated” (the CREW test the court applied in IV.B).

How specific must the agency’s category descriptions be?
- Sufficiently specific to allow the court to determine whether the exemption properly applies to each category—i.e., the descriptions should delineate the type of information included, the reason each type would cause the asserted harm if released, and the connection to the investigation or enforcement proceeding.
- The agency need not disclose sensitive operational details on the public record; but it must provide enough of a factual framework in public declarations and, where necessary, in ex parte declarations for the court to evaluate the claim (see the court’s reliance on public Hardy declaration plus ex parte material in IV.B and the treatment of ex parte material in V).
- Where the public descriptions are necessarily limited for security reasons, the agency should still: (a) provide clear labels (e.g., “interview summaries concerning X,” “intelligence reports regarding Y,” “source identifying information”), (b) state why disclosure of that label/category would lead to the claimed interference (e.g., reveal scope or targets, compromise sources), and (c) supply a Vaughn-style mapping for at least representative samples so the court (and requester) can test the fit.

Why Farahi forfeited
- The court held that Farahi “provides no specific explanation for why the various categories identified by the FBI were overbroad” and therefore “forfeited his arguments on this score” (IV.C), citing Khine. In other words, he made a general objection but did not do the concrete work listed above—no targeted attacks on labels, no sample-by-sample critique, no demonstration of overlap or mismatch—so the court refused to reach the point.

Practical checklist for a challenger (short)
- Demand a Vaughn index or detailed category-to-document mapping (at least for samples).
- For each category you attack, explain specifically: (a) how the category is defined, (b) why that definition overlaps with others or is vague, and (c) why the category label does not justify the claimed 7(A) harm with examples.
- Use disclosed documents or metadata as demonstrative evidence.
- If the agency relies on ex parte declarations, press for a public proffer that gives enough factual context to test the claim without revealing sensitive details.

Conclusion
The court requires that category-based withholdings be susceptible to meaningful adversarial testing: categories must be distinct and tethered to a factual showing of the asserted harm. A successful challenge will be specific—pointing to category definitions, representative entries, and concrete mismatches between the label and the asserted exemption—rather than a general claim that categories are “too broad.” Farahi lost that fight because he never mounted that kind of particularized attack (IV.C; Khine).

### How demanding is the segregability obligation when sensitive information is interspersed throughout, and was ex parte proof alone enough?
Question:
The district court found, based on Bender’s supplemental ex parte submission, that no reasonably segregable non-exempt material remained, citing Mead’s 'interspersed line-by-line' principle. In 7(A) cases, do courts require in camera review of samples or the records themselves, or can detailed affidavits suffice? What counts as 'reasonable steps' to segregate under § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) in an investigation file of 10,000+ pages?
Answer:
Short answer
- Segregability under FOIA is context‑specific but not toothless: an agency must take “reasonable steps” to identify and produce nonexempt material and must explain with reasonable specificity why further segregation would not yield meaningful, nonexempt information. When nonexempt material is truly “interspersed line‑by‑line,” courts have accepted that little or nothing is “reasonably segregable.” (See Mead Data Cent., 566 F.2d at 261, cited at J.A. 139–40.)
- Courts do not always require in camera review of the full record in Exemption 7(A) cases; carefully supported agency affidavits (including ex parte/supplemental submissions when necessary) can suffice. This opinion accepted the combination of public and ex parte declarations here rather than ordering a complete in camera inspection. (See J.A. 138–40; Perioperative Servs., cited at J.A. 139–40.)
- What counts as “reasonable steps” depends on the file and the agency’s showing. The FBI’s approach here — updating its prior declarations, consulting the case agent, and providing a detailed ex parte explanation (supplementing public affidavits) — met the district court’s and this Court’s standard for reasonableness given that the nonexempt material was interspersed throughout a 10,000+ page file. (See J.A. 121–22, 138–40.)

Why the court reached this result (doctrine and the opinion’s specifics)
1) The legal standard. The agency must disclose “any reasonably segregable portion” of a record after deleting exempt material (5 U.S.C. § 552(b); § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)). The controlling line of D.C. Circuit precedent recognizes two relevant points cited in the opinion:
   - If nonexempt material is “inextricably intertwined” or “interspersed line‑by‑line” with exempt material, it may not be “reasonably segregable.” Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260–61 (1977) (quoted at J.A. 139–40).
   - Courts may “rely on government affidavits that show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld … cannot be further segregated.” Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008) (cited at J.A. 139).

2) What the FBI did here (and why the court accepted it). The record shows a sequence the court found sufficient:
   - The FBI submitted an initial public declaration (Hardy) asserting the law‑enforcement nature of the files and that disclosure could interfere with ongoing investigations (J.A. 42, 49).
   - The district court, concerned about staleness and segregability, required updated information (J.A. 121–22).
   - The FBI provided updated public and ex parte declarations from Bender and an ex parte supplement to Hardy. Bender confirmed the investigation remained ongoing and the ex parte submission explained in detail why nonexempt material could not meaningfully be segregated (J.A. 138–40).
   - The district court credited that combination and concluded reasonable steps had been taken and that no meaningful segregable information remained (J.A. 139–40). The D.C. Circuit affirmed, stressing that the ex parte material was not improper and that the court did not have to make its reasoning public where disclosure would defeat the protective purpose (J.A. 139–40; Perioperative Servs. quote at J.A. 139).

3) When ex parte proof is enough — and when a court may want more. The opinion teaches three practical points:
   - Ex parte or sealed submissions are permissible when disclosing the basis for withholding would itself reveal sensitive information; the court accepted that dynamic here (J.A. 139–40).
   - Ex parte submissions need not stand alone if the government has already provided public affidavits showing a factual predicate. The Court treated Bender’s terse public update as an update to, not a replacement for, the more detailed Hardy declarations (J.A. 131–32, 138–39).
   - Nevertheless, courts have rejected conclusory, “vague” assertions of ongoing investigation in other cases (see CREW, 746 F.3d at 1098, discussed at J.A. 136–38). So ex parte evidence must supply the specificity the public declarations lack; the absence of challenge here (Farahi did not oppose ex parte filings) further weakened his ability to contest reliance on them (J.A. 139).

Practical guidance for a 10,000+ page investigative file
- The agency should (and here did) do more than assert a blanket claim. Reasonable steps typically include:
  - A document‑by‑document or sampled review sufficient to explain how nonexempt snippets are “interspersed” such that extraction would be meaningless or would itself reveal exempt content.
  - Consultation with the investigative case agent to confirm current status and identify which portions, if any, could be released without harm (the opinion notes RIDS contacted the agent — J.A. 138–39).
  - Detailed affidavits that identify categories of documents, explain why segregation is impractical or harmful, and, where appropriate, provide examples (the Court credited the detailed ex parte Hardy submission and Bender’s update — J.A. 138–40).
- The court did not require a blind order to produce segregable words or phrases; it evaluated whether meaningful information remained after any feasible redaction (Mead principle at J.A. 139–40). When remaining nonexempt parts convey no useful information independent of the exempt material, they need not be produced.

Bottom line
- Segregability is not a formalistic demand for line‑level redaction in every instance; it requires reasoned, document‑specific (or sufficiently sampled) explanations showing that further segregation would not yield meaningful, nonexempt information or would itself reveal exempt content.
- In Exemption 7(A) cases involving national security or ongoing investigations, well‑supported affidavits — including ex parte submissions when necessary and accompanied by public declarations — can satisfy the agency’s burden without full in camera review, provided they supply the necessary specificity. The FBI’s updated public and ex parte declarations met that standard here, and the district court’s reliance on them was upheld (J.A. 138–40).

### Do sealed, ex parte declarations in FOIA raise due process concerns, and how are they reconciled with FOIA’s in camera review provision?
Question:
The court notes ex parte submissions 'handicap FOIA plaintiffs' but are sometimes necessary to avoid disclosing the very information at issue (citing Perioperative). Are there constitutional constraints (e.g., due process) on relying heavily on ex parte declarations? How does § 552(a)(4)(B)’s in camera review authority interact with or limit the use of ex parte affidavits?
Answer:
Short answer
Sealed ex parte declarations in FOIA litigation do not automatically violate the Constitution. The D.C. Circuit’s opinion treats them as a permissible — though burdensome to a requester — tool so long as the court itself performs meaningful (but not necessarily public) judicial scrutiny of the agency’s claims. The court reconciles that practice with FOIA’s judicial-review authority (including in camera review under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B)) by requiring agency declarations that satisfy FOIA’s statutory and precedential standards (specificity about law‑enforcement purpose and interference) and by permitting the court to examine sensitive material ex parte or in camera when necessary to avoid defeating the protection claimed.

What the opinion says about due process and ex parte submissions
- The opinion recognizes that ex parte declarations “handicap FOIA plaintiffs,” but endorses their use when they are “the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” (See discussion in Part V; citing Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067; J.A. 139.)  
- The court notes that nothing required the district court to put its analysis of the ex parte declaration on the public record, and that the plaintiff here did not oppose the district court’s acceptance of ex parte declarations — a fact that undercuts any challenge to reliance on those materials. (Part V, J.A. 139–40.)

What legal standard courts must apply (and what protects requesters)
- The agency bears the statutory burden to show the exemption applies; affidavits must “show with reasonable specificity” why material is exempt and why nonexempt portions are not reasonably segregable (see Juarez, Mead Data cited in Part III and IV). The court signals that ex parte submissions do not excuse that obligation — they must still supply the detail the court needs to evaluate the exemption. (Part III, IV.A–B, V.)  
- The court used a layered approach here: public declarations establishing the broad predicates (e.g., records compiled for law enforcement; investigations “exist”) plus sealed/ex parte declarations providing the concrete details the public filings could not disclose. The district court also required updated declarations because of the passage of time before granting judgment — demonstrating active judicial oversight. (See Part II; IV.B; discussion of district court ordering updated assessment.)

How § 552(a)(4)(B) (judicial review/in camera authority) fits in
- Section 552(a)(4)(B) gives the district court the authority to determine whether a FOIA exemption applies and to inspect materials in camera if needed. The opinion treats ex parte sealed filings and in camera inspection as part of the same judicial toolbox: where public disclosure of facts would defeat the claimed secrecy, the court may review sensitive materials without putting those materials in the public record. (See Part V: “nothing required the court to put on the public record its analysis of that declaration.”)  
- In practice, the court requires that the agency’s ex parte submissions supply the factual specificity FOIA and precedents (e.g., CREW, Juarez, Mead) demand. If the public record alone is insufficient, the court may examine sealed material — but the key is that the court itself must be satisfied that the agency met its burden. (Parts III–V.)

Practical implications for due process challenges
- The opinion implies two limiting principles relevant to any constitutional challenge: (1) the court must independently satisfy itself that the agency met its FOIA burden; and (2) a requester must have an opportunity to contest the agency’s claims (and challenging the court’s reliance on ex parte material is weaker where the requester did not oppose use of sealed submissions). (Parts IV–V.)  
- The opinion does not hold that ex parte process is constitutionally prohibited; instead it treats the practice as compatible with FOIA’s judicial-review framework so long as the court performs the required scrutiny and the agency provides sufficient detail (even if some of that detail is supplied ex parte/in camera).

Bottom line for a FOIA practitioner or student
- Sealed/ex parte declarations raise procedural and fairness concerns, but they are not per se unconstitutional. Courts must still enforce FOIA’s statutory standards (agency burden of proof; “reasonable specificity”; segregability) and may use in camera/ex parte review when public disclosure would destroy the very secrecy the agency asserts. The opinion illustrates that courts will accept sealed submissions when they meaningfully support exemption claims and when the court (not the agency) performs the necessary review — even if the court’s detailed reasoning about the sealed material does not appear on the public docket. (See Parts IV–V, and the court’s reliance on Perioperative and CREW.)

### Why didn’t the court reach other exemptions (like 7(C), 7(D), or 7(E)) once 7(A) applied?
Question:
Given the nature of the records (counterterrorism files), other 7-series exemptions might have applied. Is it standard practice for courts to stop at 7(A) if it fully resolves the case? If the agency asserted multiple exemptions below, must the court address them, or can it affirm solely on 7(A)?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — the court was entitled to stop at Exemption 7(A). Because the FBI met its burden to show the records were law‑enforcement files whose disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings, and because the court concluded the non‑exempt material was not reasonably segregable, the district court’s judgment could be affirmed on that ground alone. The opinion explains both the doctrinal basis for that approach and why the particular record‑management and segregability showings made further analysis unnecessary.

Why that is correct (doctrine + how the opinion applies it)

- Burden and deference. Under FOIA the government bears the burden to show an exemption applies, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). The panel reiterates that the FBI’s showing that records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” satisfies Exemption 7(A) (Opinion § IV.A–B). Because the FBI is a law‑enforcement agency and this involves national‑security/counterterrorism material, the court gives the agency “a fair measure of deference” (citing Campbell and CNSS) — a point the panel relies on in affirming the 7(A) finding (Op. § IV.A).

- No need to reach alternative exemptions. Once an exemption asserted by the agency fully justifies withholding, a court may affirm on that ground without addressing every alternative exemption the agency also invoked. The panel here explains that Exemption 7(A) applies and that the FBI’s submissions (public and ex parte declarations) adequately showed both pendency/anticipation of proceedings and why disclosure would interfere. Because those showings were sufficient, the court affirmed on 7(A) (see Op. § IV intro and §§ IV.A–B).

- Segregability and “inextricably intertwined.” FOIA requires release of “any reasonably segregable portion” of a record. The opinion applies the Mead/Juarez line: the government may withhold in whole when nonexempt portions are “inextricably intertwined with exempt portions,” and courts may rely on agency affidavits showing with reasonable specificity why documents are not further segregable (Op. § IV, V). The district court — and the panel — accepted the FBI’s ex parte submissions showing that any non‑exempt material was not reasonably segregable (Op. § V). That segregability conclusion removes a hook that might otherwise require parsing other exemptions line‑by‑line.

How the opinion applies these principles to this case

- The panel found the FBI carried its burden under 7(A): the Hardy public and sealed declarations and the later Bender update showed (1) the file was compiled for law enforcement and (2) the investigation remained “ongoing,” so disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere (Op. §§ IV.A–B). Because that showing was sufficient, the court had a complete basis to uphold withholding.

- The panel also accepted the district court’s segregability determination based on ex parte material explaining why nonexempt bits were not meaningfully separable (Op. § V). Given that, even if other 7‑series exemptions might also fit, there was no need to reach them: the records were properly withheld in full under the 7(A) finding plus the inextricability/segregability rationale.

Practical point for litigation strategy
- If a FOIA defendant advances multiple exemptions, a plaintiff can still press alternative exemptions and segregability. But courts commonly stop after resolving the threshold exemption that fully disposes of the dispute. The opinion follows that practice: once 7(A) and non‑segregability were established (with appropriate deference and ex parte support given national‑security concerns), additional exemptions (7(C), 7(D), 7(E), etc.) were unnecessary to decide.

Conclusion
The court did what courts routinely do: it resolved the case on the narrow ground that sufficed (Exemption 7(A) plus non‑segregability), applying deference to agency law‑enforcement/national‑security declarations and Mead/Juarez segregability principles, and so did not address other 7‑series exemptions.

### What evidence must the government provide to show disclosure 'could reasonably be expected to interfere' beyond generic 'tipping off' rationales?
Question:
The court accepts the FBI’s explanation that release would tip off targets to the scope of the investigation, a common 7(A) rationale (citing CNSS). When, if ever, do courts require more document-specific or category-specific showings of interference? How does CREW’s skepticism toward boilerplate assertions play into the required level of detail?
Answer:
Short answer

Exemption 7(A) requires the government to show that (1) the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and (2) disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” A bare, boilerplate “tipping‑off” statement may be enough when the agency’s public and (if necessary) ex parte declarations together provide documentary and contextual detail showing an ongoing investigation and why disclosure would jeopardize it. But where the agency’s assertions are vague or conclusory, CREW and precedents require more document- or category‑specific factual detail so a court can assess the risk of interference and whether exemptions are properly applied.

What the courts require (doctrine and standards)

- The statutory text and controlling tests:
  - Exemption 7(A) covers records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A).
  - Those “proceedings” must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (quoted in the opinion at ¶ III and IV.B).

- Deference, but not carte blanche:
  - Courts give the FBI “a fair measure of deference” because it specializes in law enforcement, and especially so where national security is implicated (see CNSS). (Opinion ¶ III, ¶ IV.B.)
  - Deference, however, does not permit conclusory or stale assertions to stand where they do not enable judicial review. CREW cautions against the “vague nature” of asserting merely that an investigation is “ongoing.” (Opinion ¶ IV.B, citing CREW.)

- What the agency must supply:
  - At a minimum, the government must attest to the existence of pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings; an attestation of existence can suffice (CREW, cited in the opinion at IV.B).
  - If the public record is thin or the passage of time raises doubt, courts expect updated factual detail — either publicly or in camera/ex parte — explaining (a) the nature/scope of the investigation, (b) that it is still operative, and (c) how disclosure of the particular files or categories would interfere (e.g., by enabling targets to evade, destroy evidence, or learn investigative methods). The Farahi opinion treats the public Hardy declaration plus the ex parte Hardy declaration as supplying that necessary detail (Opinion ¶ IV.A–B).

When courts demand more document‑ or category‑specific showings

- Vague/boilerplate assertions fail when they do not permit the court to evaluate the asserted risk:
  - CREW flagged that a conclusory assertion that investigations are “ongoing” may be insufficient; courts will require more particularized factual grounding when the assertion is otherwise vague or stale (Opinion ¶ IV.B, discussing CREW).

- Document/category specificity:
  - When an agency withholds by category, the categories must be “sufficiently distinct” to allow the court to determine whether exemptions apply to each category (citing CREW and Gallant). If the agency lumps broadly or makes no showing tying particular categories or documents to the asserted harms, a court may reject the claim. The court here noted that requirement (Opinion ¶ IV.C) but found Farahi forfeited his challenge and that the FBI’s categories were not shown to be overbroad.

How CREW’s skepticism operates in practice (applied to Farahi)

- CREW imposes a reality check: the court will look beyond shorthand “tipping‑off” language when necessary.
  - In Farahi the D.C. Circuit accepted the “tipping off” rationale as a “widely accepted justification” (Opinion ¶ IV.B), but it did not treat that justification alone as decisive. The court stressed context: the public Hardy declaration attested to the investigation’s character and existence, the ex parte Hardy declaration supplied considerable nonpublic detail, and the Bender declaration served as an update confirming nothing had changed (Opinion ¶ IV.B).
  - The opinion illustrates the typical pattern: if public declarations are thin, ex parte submissions that provide additional concrete facts (kept sealed to avoid the very harm claimed) can cure the deficiency and satisfy the court under CREW so long as the court can, either on the public record or in camera, assess the risk (Opinion ¶¶ II, IV.B, V).

Practical takeaways for challengers and agencies

- For the government:
  - Provide an initial detailed public declaration about the investigation’s nature and the types of risks disclosure would bring.
  - If necessary, supplement with sealed/ex parte declarations that give document‑ or category‑level detail about why disclosure of the requested records would interfere, and update those declarations if circumstances change (as the FBI did with Bender/Hardy here).

- For requesters/challengers:
  - Point to specific shortcomings: identify categories you think are overbroad or documents that the agency has not tied to the asserted harms. Merely asserting that the volume suggests segregable material is often forfeited absent particularized argument (the court found Farahi forfeited his category-overbreadth claim in ¶ IV.C).
  - When the agency relies on ex parte submissions, argue that the public record is insufficient and press the court to review the sealed material in camera (the opinion recognizes the problem but accepts ex parte material when necessary to decide without producing the harmful material, ¶ V).

Key citations from the opinion
- Exemption standard and CREW requirement for “pending or reasonably anticipated” proceedings: Opinion ¶ III and ¶ IV.B (quoting CREW).
- Deference to FBI / CNSS on national security: Opinion ¶ III and ¶ IV.B (citing CNSS).
- CREW’s warning about “vague” assertions and the need for more when declarations are conclusory: Opinion ¶ IV.B (discussing CREW).
- Requirement that categories be “sufficiently distinct” (CREW/Gallant) and the court’s finding of forfeiture here: Opinion ¶ IV.C.
- Acceptance of ex parte declarations to provide necessary detail and justification for nondisclosure/segregability: Opinion ¶ V.

Bottom line

A generic “tipping‑off” rationale can carry Exemption 7(A) if the agency’s public and, when appropriate, ex parte submissions give concrete facts showing an ongoing investigation and explaining how disclosure of the requested records would jeopardize it. But CREW limits the government’s leeway: courts will require more specific, document‑ or category‑linked facts when the agency’s assertions are conclusory, stale, or otherwise inadequate for meaningful judicial review. The Farahi opinion shows that courts will accept sealed, detailed declarations to supply the needed specificity so long as the overall record (public plus sealed) permits the court to evaluate the claimed risk.

### What is the role of in camera inspection of the records versus reliance on agency affidavits in Exemption 7(A) cases?
Question:
The opinion relies on agency declarations (some ex parte) and doesn’t mention the district court conducting in camera review of the actual records. Under § 552(a)(4)(B), when is in camera inspection expected or required, particularly for segregability or to test 'ongoing investigation' claims? Do courts avoid in camera inspection in 7(A) cases as a matter of practice?
Answer:
Short answer
- There is no categorical rule requiring courts to conduct in camera inspection in Exemption 7(A) cases. The statute and D.C. Circuit precedent require the government to carry the burden of showing an exemption applies and to show with “reasonable specificity” why material is withheld or non‑segregable, but they do not mandate that the court itself inspect the records in every case. Courts routinely decide 7(A) claims on the basis of agency affidavits/declarations — including sealed ex parte submissions — so long as those submissions supply sufficient detail to carry the government’s burden. When an affidavit is conclusory or the record facts are genuinely in dispute, the court may require more (a more detailed affidavit, supplementation, or in camera review), but the choice is discretionary and fact‑dependent.

Why (doctrinal) — statutes and leading precedents
- Burden and review standard: The government bears the burden of proving an exemption under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). Courts review FOIA summary judgment de novo (opinion § III).
- Segregability rule: Agencies must take “reasonable steps” to segregate and release nonexempt information (5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii); opinion § I and V).
- Affidavits may suffice: The D.C. Circuit has repeatedly held that a court may rely on agency affidavits that “show with reasonable specificity” why withheld material is exempt and cannot be segregated (Juarez v. DOJ; Mead Data; quoted in the opinion § III and IV). For sensitive national‑security and law‑enforcement records, courts give the agency’s factual showing a measure of deference (Campbell; CNSS) and will accept ex parte/sealed declarations where necessary to avoid revealing the very secrets at issue (opinion §§ III, IV, V).
- When in camera review or supplementation is appropriate: The court has the responsibility to ensure the government has met its burden. If the government’s public affidavit is vague or stale, the court may require supplementation (CREW) or, in close cases, perform in camera review of the underlying records to test the adequacy of the agency’s justifications and segregability analysis. The opinion cites exactly that dynamic: the district court originally denied summary judgment and ordered updated declarations because of the passage of time and concerns the court could not state on the public record (opinion § II). CREW is cited for the proposition that a conclusory claim that an investigation is “ongoing” may not be enough (opinion § IV.B).

How the opinion applied these principles
- The district court did not conduct an in camera inspection of the actual records on the public docket. Instead it required the FBI to update its declarations and ordered supplemental ex parte submissions addressing both whether proceedings remained “pending or reasonably anticipated” and the segregability issue (opinion § II).
- The court credited the public and sealed declarations (Hardy and Bender). The public Hardy declaration initially established the existence and law‑enforcement nexus of the investigation; the sealed Hardy declaration supplied details; the later Bender affidavit was treated as an update confirming the investigation remained ongoing (opinion §§ III–IV). The district court reviewed the ex parte submission and found the agency’s segregability explanation adequate; the D.C. Circuit affirmed (opinion §§ IV–V).
- The panel explained why this procedure was permissible: the government had provided non‑conclusory public and sealed declarations, the district court had already found the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden before ordering updates, and reliance on ex parte material is sometimes the only practicable way to resolve the dispute without disclosing protected information (opinion § V).

Practical rule of thumb for 7(A) cases (from the opinion)
- If the government’s public affidavit is detailed, and sealed/ex parte material adds non‑public detail, courts will often accept those submissions without in camera inspection, particularly in national security or counterterrorism matters (opinion §§ III–IV).
- If the public affidavit is conclusory, stale, or fails to address segregability with reasonable specificity, the court should (and here did) order supplementation. If supplementation still leaves reasonable doubt, in camera review is one tool the court can use to test the agency’s claims — but it remains discretionary, not automatic (CREW; opinion § IV.B; § V).
- On segregability, the court may accept an agency’s showing that non‑exempt material is “inextricably intertwined” or “randomly interspersed” such that meaningful disclosure is not “reasonable” (Mead; opinion § V).

Conclusion
The opinion illustrates the D.C. Circuit’s usual approach: courts do not reflexively conduct in camera inspection in Exemption 7(A) cases. Instead they require the agency to make a sufficiently detailed showing (public plus, if necessary, sealed/ex parte declarations), may order supplementation when declarations are stale or vague, and will use in camera review only where the court needs to test the adequacy of the government’s sworn justification and segregability claim. This is a discretionary, context‑specific choice driven by the sufficiency of the agency’s affidavits and the sensitivity of the material.

### How does the court reconcile FOIA’s pro-disclosure presumption with strong deference in national-security contexts?
Question:
FOIA is generally pro-disclosure, but the panel emphasizes deference to the executive on national security and counterterrorism investigations (citing CNSS). Is there a doctrinal framework explaining how the pro-disclosure presumption operates where national-security justifications are invoked under 7(A)? Does the burden meaningfully shift in practice, or does 'deference' just affect how much detail is demanded in the affidavits?
Answer:
Short answer: The court treats the FOIA pro‑disclosure presumption as the starting point but applies a settled, two-part framework in which deference to the Executive narrows how courts evaluate the government’s evidence under Exemption 7(A). The government still bears the burden of proof (the presumption does not disappear), but deference makes agency attestations—including ex parte, classified affidavits—more likely to satisfy that burden without the kind of public, document‑by‑document proof FOIA plaintiffs would prefer.

Why and how the court reconciles the two (doctrinal framework)
- The baseline rules. FOIA is presumptively pro‑disclosure, and the government “bears the burden of showing that a FOIA exemption applies.” Opinion ¶III (citing 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B)). The agency must show (1) the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and (2) disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Opinion ¶III (citing Exemption 7(A), CREW, Mapother).
- Where national security is implicated, courts give the FBI (and the Executive) a “fair measure of deference.” Opinion ¶III (citing Campbell), and that deference is “particularly appropriate” for national‑security or counterterrorism records (citing CNSS). In short: courts remain neutral about the legal standard but afford more weight to the government’s factual attestations in those contexts.
- What deference does, doctrinally. Deference affects the evidentiary posture, not the legal allocation of the burden. The opinion repeats that the government still must carry its burden (¶III, ¶IV), but it may do so through agency declarations that courts may credit if they “show with reasonable specificity” why withholding is appropriate and segregability is not feasible (Juarez; Mead quoted in ¶III and ¶V). Deference means a court will accept agency attestations about ongoing investigations and harm to investigations—especially when the agency provides classified or ex parte detail that cannot safely be placed on the public record. See opinion ¶IV.B (government may show proceedings by attesting to their “existence,” citing CREW; court credits Hardy’s public and ex parte declarations and the Bender update).
- Limits on deference. Deference is not carte blanche. The court still reviews de novo, requires that declarations establish a rational “nexus” to law‑enforcement duties (¶IV.A, citing Campbell and Pratt), and will reject conclusory or “vague” attestations (the court pointed to CREW’s warning about the “vague nature” of assertions that an investigation remains ongoing, ¶IV.B). The opinion shows the court will ask for updated or more detailed declarations when time has elapsed or the public record is inadequate (the district court required updated declarations here because of the passage of time, ¶I–II; the panel accepted the update only because there was public Hardy plus ex parte detail and a confirming Bender update, ¶IV.B).
- Segregability interplay. Deference also extends to segregability analysis: courts may accept ex parte explanations why non‑exempt material is “inextricably intertwined” or not “reasonably segregable” (¶V, citing Juarez, Mead, Perioperative). But the agency still must provide the court a “detailed justification” (¶V—district court credited Bender’s ex parte submission).

How that plays out in practice
- Burden: unchanged in theory. The government still “bears the burden” and must satisfy the statutory elements (¶III, ¶IV).
- Burden: softened in practice. Deference reduces the degree of public proof required. An agency may satisfy its burden through credible, reasonably specific declarations—including sealed/ex parte submissions—especially in national‑security investigations where disclosure of the details of the justification would itself be harmful. The opinion exemplifies this: the panel accepted the combination of a public Hardy declaration, an ex parte Hardy declaration with “considerable detail,” and a short Bender update as sufficient (¶IV.B).
- What plaintiffs can do: challenge conclusory assertions, insist on updated evidence when facts have changed or much time has passed, and press on category definitions and segregability when the government’s explanations are overbroad or unclear. The opinion rejects a challenge based on overbreadth only because the plaintiff failed to identify specific problems (¶IV.C).

Key passages from the opinion supporting this account
- “The government bears the burden of showing that a FOIA exemption applies.” ¶III.
- “Because the FBI specializes in law enforcement, its decision to invoke exemption 7 is entitled to deference.” ¶III (citing Campbell); “Such deference is particularly appropriate for records implicating national security.” ¶III (citing CNSS).
- The government may show pendency by attesting to the “existence” of an investigation; the court will credit such attestations where they are supported by declarations showing reasonable specificity and, where necessary, ex parte detail. ¶IV.B (citing CREW, Juarez).
- The court will require updated or more detailed declarations when earlier submissions are stale or “vague.” The district court required updated declarations here because of delay; the panel accepted the update as an “update to the Hardy declarations, not as a standalone document.” ¶II, ¶IV.B.
- On segregability: agency may show non‑segregability with “reasonable specificity,” and ex parte submissions are permissible where public disclosure would defeat the protective purpose. ¶V (citing Mead, Perioperative).

Bottom line
FOIA’s pro‑disclosure presumption remains the legal starting point and the government still bears the burden of proving an exemption. But in national‑security and counterterrorism contexts courts give the Executive “a fair measure of deference,” which means courts are willing to credit agency declarations—including classified/ex parte affidavits—so long as they provide reasonable specificity and a rational nexus to law‑enforcement duties. Deference thus changes how the burden is satisfied in practice (less public, more reliance on sworn agency attestations), but it does not eliminate judicial review or the statutory burdens on the government.

### If the investigation ended during the appeal, would that moot the 7(A) justification, and what happens then?
Question:
Exemption 7(A) depends on pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings. If those ended while the case was on appeal, would the exemption evaporate? Would the proper remedy be a remand for reprocessing with updated declarations, or could the court consider post-judgment facts to affirm/deny?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — Exemption 7(A) rests on the existence of “pending or reasonably anticipated” enforcement proceedings, so if those proceedings actually ended while the case was on appeal the factual predicate for 7(A) would disappear and the government could no longer carry its burden on that exemption. Practically, the correct course is to update the factual record (declarations and any sealed material) and remand to the district court to decide disclosure/segregability in light of the changed facts; an appellate court will not simply affirm a 7(A) withholding based on stale assertions.

Why (doctrine and how the opinion handles it)
- The statute and our cases: Exemption 7(A) applies only where disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A); CREW, 746 F.3d at 1096 (quoted at J.A. __; see Opinion § II–III). That is a factual showing the agency must make when seeking to withhold under 7(A).
- The agency’s burden is time‑sensitive: the government may show an ongoing investigation simply by attesting to its existence, but courts must be satisfied that the investigation remains “ongoing” or reasonably anticipated at the time of the court’s decision. See CREW discussion quoted at J.A. (Opinion § IV.B) and the court’s emphasis on the need for fresh facts given the “significant passage of time” (district court’s order requiring updated assessment, J.A. 121–22).
- This opinion illustrates the point: the district court denied the FBI’s first summary‑judgment motion without prejudice because of the passage of time and ordered updated declarations (J.A. 121–22). The FBI supplied updated public and ex parte declarations (Bender) and the district court relied on those updates in granting summary judgment (J.A. 138–40). On appeal the panel treated the Bender declaration as an update confirming nothing had changed and affirmed because the government had provided current attestations (Opinion § IV.B, J.A. 131, 138–39).

What follows if proceedings actually end on appeal
- If the investigation or enforcement proceeding ends while the case is pending (including on appeal), the factual basis for 7(A) would be gone — the exemption “could reasonably be expected to interfere” only so long as proceedings are pending or reasonably anticipated. The Opinion’s mechanics show the district court’s correct practice: require updated, contemporaneous declarations before deciding 7(A) (J.A. 121–22), and deny or remand if the updated record undermines the exemption.
- The appropriate remedy is to update the administrative record and remand for the district court to reexamine the exemption and segregability in light of the changed facts. That is what the district court did in this case when it asked for an updated assessment; the court relied on the updated declarations in granting the FBI’s renewed motion (J.A. 138–40). If the updated declarations showed the investigation had ended, the district court would then have to determine whether any 7(A) justification remained and whether any nonexempt material must be released (see the segregability discussion, Opinion § V, J.A. 139–40).

Can an appellate court consider post‑judgment facts and affirm/deny on that basis?
- The panel here evaluated the government’s updated submissions (hardy/bender declarations) as part of the record before the district court and on appeal (Opinion § IV.B). But the lesson of the opinion is procedural: courts need current factual support for 7(A), and if the government’s earlier showing becomes stale the district court should obtain new evidence before resolving the claim (see J.A. 121–22). Where the record on appeal already contains updated, contemporaneous declarations (as it did here), the appellate court can rely on them. Where the record lacks such up‑to‑date evidence because circumstances changed, the proper step is remand so the district court can take evidence and make findings — not that the appellate court should affirm a withholding based on an outdated record.

Bottom line
Exemption 7(A) is fact‑dependent and time‑sensitive. If the investigation ended while the case was on appeal, the exemption would likely “evaporate” unless the government could identify another pending or reasonably anticipated proceeding. The correct process — as the district court employed here — is to obtain updated declarations and, if necessary, remand to the district court for reconsideration and any required reprocessing / segregability analysis in light of the new facts (see J.A. 121–22, 131, 138–40).

### Did Farahi waive challenges to the use of ex parte submissions by not objecting below?
Question:
The panel notes Farahi did not oppose the district court’s acceptance of ex parte declarations and says he therefore can’t challenge the court’s reliance on them. Is failure to object treated as waiver/forfeiture in FOIA cases? Are there any exceptions (e.g., plain error or structural concerns) that would allow a later challenge to ex parte reliance?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — the panel treated Farahi’s failure to object in the district court as a forfeiture that bars his belated challenge on appeal. The opinion treats the point as a classic waiver/forfeiture doctrine in FOIA litigation and does not recognize any carve‑out or plain‑error remedy in this case.

Why (doctrine and how the court applied it)

- Forfeiture/waiver: The court relied on the familiar principle that a litigant who does not oppose a procedure in the district court ordinarily may not complain about it for the first time on appeal. That principle is applied twice in the opinion. First, the court says flatly: “Farahi did not oppose the district court’s acceptance of ex parte declarations, so he cannot challenge the district court’s reliance on them.” J.A. 139 (quoted and discussed in Part V). Second, the court treated Farahi’s failure to develop a more specific objection to the FBI’s categorical withholding as forfeiture: “Farahi accordingly forfeited his arguments on this score.” (citing Khine v. DHS). Those statements show the court is enforcing ordinary forfeiture rules in the FOIA context.

- FOIA context does not blunt the rule here: The opinion acknowledges that ex parte declarations impose a particular burden on FOIA plaintiffs, but it simultaneously recognizes that ex parte filings may be “the only way to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret.” The court therefore accepts that ex parte submissions are sometimes necessary (citing Perioperative Servs., 57 F.4th at 1067) and will enforce forfeiture where a plaintiff failed to object to the district court’s use of those submissions. J.A. 139–40.

Did the court leave room for exceptions (e.g., plain error or structural concerns)?

- The opinion does not invoke or apply a plain‑error or structural‑rights exception to forfeiture here. It gives two reasons for permitting reliance on the ex parte declarations: (1) Farahi never opposed the district court’s acceptance of them, and (2) ex parte submissions are sometimes indispensible in FOIA cases because the only way to adjudicate claims about classified or secret material is to review that material off the public record. J.A. 139–40. The opinion does not say that an appellate court may nevertheless reach the merits despite forfeiture based on plain error, a structural‑rights theory, or similar exceptions.

- Put differently, the panel did not identify any extraordinary exception to forfeiture for FOIA plaintiffs; instead it applied ordinary forfeiture doctrine and upheld the district court’s reliance on sealed/ex parte material when the plaintiff did not object below.

Practical takeaway for a FOIA plaintiff

- If you want to preserve a challenge to a district court’s use of ex parte or sealed submissions, you must object and press that objection below. The D.C. Circuit here enforces forfeiture: failure to object forecloses appellate review of the district court’s reliance on ex parte declarations. The court’s reasoning rests on normal forfeiture principles and on the recognition (from Perioperative and the court’s own practice) that sealed/ex parte submissions are sometimes unavoidable in FOIA cases; it does not announce or apply a plain‑error or structural exception that would rescue an unpreserved challenge in this setting.

### Can a FOIA plaintiff obtain discovery to test the agency’s declarations in a 7(A) case like this?
Question:
Given the heavy reliance on agency affidavits (some sealed), is civil discovery available to challenge the FBI’s assertions about pending proceedings or segregability? What is the standard for allowing discovery in FOIA cases (e.g., a showing of bad faith or contradictory evidence), and how does the national-security context affect that?
Answer:
Short answer: Almost never — not without some concrete basis to doubt the agency’s sworn explanations. Courts in this Circuit ordinarily decide FOIA Exemption 7(A) questions on agency declarations (including sealed, ex parte declarations or in‑camera submissions), will accept agency attestations that investigations are “ongoing,” and will permit minimal procedures (updated declarations, in‑camera review) rather than broad discovery. To get discovery a FOIA plaintiff must do more than point to the volume of withheld records or mere passage of time; the plaintiff must identify concrete, non‑conclusory reasons to suspect the agency’s declarations are false, in bad faith, or otherwise inadequate.

How the opinion applies these principles
- Reliance on affidavits and deference: The court emphasizes that the FBI’s decision to invoke Exemption 7(A) is entitled to deference (citing Campbell and CNSS). It says an agency may show an ongoing investigation simply by attesting to its “existence” (citing CREW and Juarez). The panel accepted the public Hardy declaration and the sealed Hardy/Bender ex parte materials as sufficient to show the records were compiled for law enforcement and that disclosure would “reasonably be expected to interfere” with pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings (Op. §§ III, IV.A–B).
- Time and supplementation, not discovery: Because the district court was concerned about stale declarations, it ordered updated declarations before ruling (Op. ¶¶ II–III; district court “ordered the FBI to update its assessment”). The court affirmed that procedure: the Bender declaration functioned as an update to Hardy, and the combination of public and sealed declarations cured any terseness in the later filing (Op. IV.B). The court did not allow civil discovery; it required refreshed and, where necessary, sealed explanations.
- Sealed/ex parte material and segregability: The court accepted the FBI’s ex parte submission as the means of explaining why material was not reasonably segregable and expressly recognized that ex parte declarations handicap plaintiffs but may be necessary when disclosure of the explanation itself would reveal the very secrets sought (Op. V, last paragraph). Once the agency provides a “detailed justification” (here, in the sealed submission), a court may rely on it to find non‑segregability and deny disclosure.
- What the plaintiff failed to show here: Farahi argued delay and the sheer volume of records made the FBI’s claims doubtful, but the court found these arguments insufficient. The panel rejected a claim that passage of time alone created a dispute because the agency supplied updated declarations and had already, earlier, “seemingly” carried its burden (Op. IV.B). Farahi’s failure to identify particular overbroad categories also was fatal to his segregability challenge (Op. IV.C, V).

Practical rule for when discovery may be allowed
- Discovery is not the default remedy to test agency declarations in Exemption 7(A)/national‑security contexts. Instead courts will:
  - Demand more detailed or updated agency declarations; and/or
  - Perform in‑camera review or accept sealed ex parte submissions to evaluate sensitive assertions.
- A court will consider permitting discovery only if the plaintiff points to specific, plausible evidence that the agency’s declarations are materially false, fabricated, or made in bad faith (or that the agency has categorically failed to identify segregable information). Vague objections, delay, or the volume of withheld records, without more, do not justify discovery under the governing caselaw reflected in this opinion (Op. IV.B–C; V).

Conclusion
In the Farahi litigation the court followed the established D.C. Circuit approach: require updated and detailed agency declarations and, where necessary, sealed/ex parte submissions or in‑camera review; give deference to agency attestations about ongoing investigations; and deny discovery unless the plaintiff can present concrete, non‑conclusory reasons to doubt the agency’s sworn representations. The decision thus forecloses broad discovery as a remedy to challenge 7(A) claims in national‑security or counterterrorism contexts unless the FOIA plaintiff can make a specific showing that the declarations are unreliable.
