Chapter 2: # The Law Enforcement Exemptions: Exemption 7 in Context

Exemption 7 of the Freedom of Information Act is the principal set of provisions that insulate law‑enforcement-related records from public disclosure when disclosure would harm specific law‑enforcement interests. Because law‑enforcement records often implicate national security, ongoing investigations, and protection of confidential sources, courts give the executive a measure of deference in applying Exemption 7. At the same time, the statute requires agencies to justify their withholdings and to disclose any reasonably segregable, nonexempt portions of records. This chapter uses Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation, No. 23‑5243 (D.C. Cir. Sept. 30, 2025), together with the influential D.C. Circuit precedents that Farahi cites, to explain the doctrinal architecture of Exemption 7: its threshold, the separate harms protected by 7(A) through 7(F), the relationship between Exemption 7 and other FOIA exemptions in national‑security contexts, and when courts will accept categorical agency showings instead of line‑by‑line, document‑by‑document disclosures.

## Threshold requirement: records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes

Exemption 7 applies only to records “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7). That threshold is not a mere formality: it imposes a two‑part factual connection that the agency must establish as a predicate to invoking any of the six subparts. The D.C. Circuit has distilled the proof into a requirement that the agency show (1) a rational nexus between the records and one of the agency’s law‑enforcement duties and (2) a connection between the individual or incident described in the records and a possible security risk or violation of federal law. See Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982); Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998).

Farahi illustrates how those showings operate in practice. The FBI’s declarations in Farahi described an investigation into alleged ties between the requester and individuals and organizations associated with terrorism. The D.C. Circuit treated the FBI’s representations with the “fair measure of deference” typically accorded to a law‑enforcement agency and held that the declaration established the requisite nexus to the FBI’s investigative duties and the connection to national‑security and criminal concerns. That deference is not boundless: declarations that are conclusory, inconsistent, or lacking basic factual indicia may not suffice. But where, as in Farahi, the agency supplies factual context (even if some of that context is provided in ex parte filings) showing that records were compiled in the course of an investigation into violations of federal criminal statutes or threats to national security, the threshold is typically satisfied.

A student should therefore think of the threshold as a fact‑intensive inquiry. The court will ask whether the agency’s activity that produced the records is recognizably part of law enforcement (investigations, witness interviews, tips, evidence gathering, threat assessments), and whether the subject of the records is linked to potential violations or security threats. Merely being an agency document is not enough; conversely, for specialized law‑enforcement agencies (the FBI, DEA, ATF), courts will give substantial weight to the agency’s invocation of law‑enforcement purpose because those agencies’ core missions are enforcement‑oriented.

## Subparts overview: 7(A) through 7(F) and the distinct harms each targets

Once the records are shown to have been compiled for law‑enforcement purposes, Exemption 7 subdivides into six separate harms, each protecting a different interest:

- 7(A): records may be withheld if disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” The interest protected is the ability of investigators and prosecutors to build a case—evidence gathering, witness cooperation, strategy—without tipping off targets or undermining a prospective or pending proceeding. In Farahi the FBI invoked 7(A) to protect an ongoing counterterrorism investigation; the court accepted agency attestations that public disclosure could “tip off” targets and interfere with investigative strategy.

- 7(B): protects records that “would deprive a person of a right to a fair trial or an impartial adjudication.” This targets disclosure that would prejudice jurors, witnesses, or the tribunal.

- 7(C): protects “personal privacy” interests of third parties appearing in law‑enforcement records. This is often invoked to shield identifying information about confidential sources, witnesses, or persons mentioned in reports.

- 7(D): protects “confidential sources” and “informant” identities or information that would reveal a source. This subpart is commonly invoked where a source’s security or safety would be threatened by disclosure.

- 7(E): protects the “techniques and procedures” for law enforcement investigations and prosecutions—criminal‑investigative methods that, if revealed, would allow subjects to avoid detection. This is often used to withhold material about surveillance methods, forensic techniques, or covert tactics.

- 7(F): protects records that would “endanger the life or physical safety” of law‑enforcement personnel or other individuals. This is the most plainly protective of personal safety.

Understanding the distinct rationale behind each subpart is essential because the government must tie its withholding claim to the specific harm an identified subpart protects. 7(A) is functionally concerned with the prospective or ongoing character of proceedings and the real risk that disclosure would frustrate those proceedings. The D.C. Circuit’s recent decisions—Juarez, CREW, and Farahi—emphasize that the government must show that proceedings are pending or that they are reasonably anticipated; a stale investigation that has terminated and will not yield enforcement proceedings cannot support a 7(A) withholding. Conversely, 7(E) or 7(D) may justify withholding even where no prosecution is pending if revealing methods or sources would produce the protected harm.

Farahi is principally a 7(A) case. It shows how courts evaluate an agency’s evidentiary submissions to determine whether disclosure would “reasonably be expected” to interfere with enforcement. The court accepted the FBI’s explanation that disclosure might tip off targets as a conventional and legitimate basis for 7(A). But the opinion also demonstrates that, when significant time has elapsed, courts may demand updated or augmented declarations to ensure that the asserted enforcement proceedings are still pending or reasonably anticipated.

## Relationship between Exemption 7 and other exemptions (e.g., 1 and 3) in national security cases

National‑security cases frequently implicate multiple FOIA exemptions. Exemption 1 protects properly classified national security information (5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1) and implementing Executive Orders); Exemption 3 covers information exempted by statute other than FOIA; and Exemption 7 protects law‑enforcement‑compiled records that implicate the six harms discussed above. The interactions among these exemptions are important to practical FOIA work and to judicial review.

First, the exemptions are cumulative. An agency may invoke multiple exemptions for the same document: for example, a classified investigative memorandum could be withheld under Exemption 1 (classified material) and under Exemption 7(E) (exposure of investigative techniques). Courts will assess each claimed exemption on its own terms, but they commonly afford deference to agency designations of classified status (Exemption 1) and to agency assertions about law‑enforcement harms (Exemption 7), particularly when national security interests are implicated. The D.C. Circuit has repeatedly emphasized deference in national‑security contexts; in Farahi the court reiterated that “[s]uch deference is particularly appropriate for records implicating national security” and cited Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918 (D.C. Cir. 2003), among other authorities.

Second, the scope and provenance of Exemption 3 are different. Exemption 3 applies when Congress or another statute “requires” or “specifically exempts” certain information from disclosure. Where a statute applies, courts generally do not probe the specific harms that Exemption 7 asks the government to show; instead, the question is whether the statute operates to withhold certain categories of information. In national‑security investigations, Exemption 3 may apply through statutes that protect intelligence‑gathering materials, for example. When both Exemption 3 and 7 are invoked, a court may accept the statutory prohibition while still assessing whether the particular records fall within the statutory category.

Third, the practical consequence of overlapping exemptions is that courts will often review the agency’s submissions under a tiered standard of deference: Exemption 1 claims receive deference to the Executive’s classification determinations unless there is evidence of bad faith; Exemption 3 claims turn on statutory language and congressional intent; and Exemption 7 claims require that the agency demonstrate the particularized harm enumerated in the subpart invoked. Farahi underscores that even where national‑security concerns are at stake, courts expect agencies to offer declarations that, while often ex parte and in camera, provide enough factual specificity for a court to determine whether the exemption’s elements are satisfied.

Finally, the overlap also affects segregability arguments. A document that contains classified material (Exemption 1) may still contain reasonably segregable nonclassified information unless that information is so intertwined with classified material that segregable disclosure would itself harm the protected interests. The D.C. Circuit’s segregability jurisprudence (Mead Data, Juarez, Perioperative) and the Farahi decision together demonstrate that agencies can withhold entire documents when the nonexempt material is “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material or when nonexempt bits are dispersed line‑by‑line such that a meaningful disclosure is not possible without compromising protected information.

## Supreme Court validation of categorical approaches under 7(A): Robbins Tire & Rubber Co. v. NLRB

Courts frequently confront the question whether an agency may lawfully withhold records on a category‑by‑category basis rather than litigating exemption applicability document by document. The Supreme Court has recognized in other administrative contexts that agencies may adopt categorical approaches to administrative decisionmaking and remedies where the subject matter or remedial scheme justifies a rule‑like treatment. Robbins Tire & Rubber Co. v. NLRB, 437 U.S. 214 (1978), is one exemplar of the Court’s willingness to tolerate categorical agency action in the administrative sphere. Robbins Tire addressed the National Labor Relations Board’s remedial order and accepted that categorical relief, though broad, could be appropriate if authorized by statute and supported by the Board’s reasoning.

Although Robbins Tire is not a FOIA case, its doctrinal posture is useful for understanding FOIA practice because it communicates a general principle: where an agency’s mission and the need for administrable, predictable decisionmaking support a categorical approach, courts will not automatically insist on atomized, document‑by‑document inquiry. D.C. Circuit FOIA jurisprudence has imported this administrative sensibility into the FOIA context: the agency need not always produce page‑by‑page justification if it reasonably explains why groups of records share a common exempt quality and why disclosure of the group would cause the same statutory harm. The caveat—repeated in Gallant, CREW, and Farahi—is that such categories must be meaningfully distinct and the agency’s explanation for each category must supply sufficient factual detail for the court to assess whether the claimed exemption applies to the group as a whole.

Robbins Tire thus functions as an analogical endorsement: courts will permit categorical showings where they make sense, but FOIA’s transparency mandate controls. The government’s category‑based approach must still allow a court to determine whether the exemption is properly applied. Robbins Tire’s broader lesson—administrative agencies may adopt categorical treatments when justified—helps explain why the D.C. Circuit will accept category‑based withholdings when the agency’s declarations offer adequate specificity and the categories are not overbroad.

## When agencies may rely on categorical rather than document‑by‑document showings

The central question is practical: when is a category‑level justification adequate under FOIA? The D.C. Circuit has articulated guiding principles that the Farahi opinion applied.

First, the agency must make the categories “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each category. Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994); see also CREW v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1088 (D.C. Cir. 2014). A category like “all documents” or “all investigative files” is too amorphous. Instead, the agency’s labels should identify the document types or subject matter that justify the particular exemption (e.g., “records discussing active sources or confidential informant identities,” or “records reflecting investigative techniques under 7(E),” or “reports of witness interviews concerning named suspects”).

Second, the agency must provide enough factual description—usually in an affidavit or declaration—so that the court can independently assess the claimed exemption for the category. In national‑security or sensitive law‑enforcement contexts, that factual description can occur in part ex parte. As Farahi emphasizes, courts can accept ex parte declarations that provide the necessary specificity when public disclosure of the details would itself defeat the purpose of the exemption. But there are limits: the agency cannot merely submit an unilluminating blanket statement; the declarations must explain why the documents fall into the category and why disclosure would cause the particular enumerated harm.

Third, the feasibility and efficiency considerations matter. Where an agency must process tens of thousands of pages (as in Farahi, where the FBI located more than 10,000 pages and numerous CDs), a document‑by‑document public justification would often force the agency to disclose sensitive information merely to explain why that information must remain secret. Courts recognize this dilemma and allow category‑level showings in appropriate circumstances. That allowance is most apparent when the withheld material is part of an ongoing investigation, when disclosure of even the fact of existence of certain subparts could harm the investigation, or when the documents are so heavily interspersed with exempt material that segregability is impracticable.

Fourth, after-the-fact vigilance: courts will be alert to stale or conclusory representations. CREW and Farahi both demonstrate that courts may require updated affidavits when significant time has elapsed between an agency’s initial declaration and the court’s decision. In Farahi the district court denied summary judgment without prejudice and required the FBI to update potentially stale declarations; the D.C. Circuit sustained that requirement. An agency relying on categorical assertions should therefore anticipate the need to update its explanations to show that the asserted harms remain real and imminent.

Fifth, segregability and “inextricably intertwined” material. Even where categories are accepted, the agency must still show that no meaningful, nonexempt information is reasonably segregable. Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. Department of the Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260–61 (D.C. Cir. 1977), established that when nonexempt information is “interspersed line‑by‑line” with exempt material, segregation may not be feasible and the agency may withhold the whole record. The D.C. Circuit in Farahi accepted the FBI’s showing—primarily in ex parte submissions—that the nonexempt snippets were inextricably intertwined with the exempt substance and that any piecemeal release would either reveal or compromise the exempt material. Students should note that courts will scrutinize these claims: blanket or conclusory segregability assertions will fail, but detailed ex parte justifications that explain the intermixing of exempt and nonexempt material often succeed.

Finally, the plaintiff’s response matters. Farahi illustrates procedural dynamics: the appellant did not oppose the FBI’s motion to file ex parte submissions, and the D.C. Circuit observed that a failure to object to ex parte treatment undercuts later attacks to some degree. More generally, when an agency relies on categorical treatment, the challenger should identify with specificity why a category is overbroad or why segregable material exists; absent a concrete showing, courts may treat generalized objections as forfeited.

Conclusion

Exemption 7 is a nuanced set of protections tailored to multiple law‑enforcement interests. Its threshold—records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes—requires a factual nexus between the records and the agency’s investigative duties. Once the threshold is met, the six subparts point to distinct harms that the agency must justify. In practice, courts give agencies a fair degree of deference—particularly in national‑security investigations—but require adequate factual grounding for categorical showings, careful treatment of segregability, and timely updates where investigations span many years. Farahi v. FBI is a contemporary exemplar: it demonstrates both the court’s willingness to credit the FBI’s attestations about ongoing counterterrorism investigations and the court’s insistence that the agency refresh and detail its submissions so the litigated balance between secrecy and disclosure remains properly calibrated.
