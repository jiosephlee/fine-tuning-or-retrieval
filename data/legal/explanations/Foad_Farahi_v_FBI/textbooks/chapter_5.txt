Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation

Chapter 5: # Segregability, Ex Parte Reliance, Remaining Arguments, and Disposition

## The segregability standard and how the court applies it
FOIA requires agencies to disclose any “reasonably segregable” nonexempt information from records that are otherwise withheld. The legal standard looks not to whether individual words or isolated phrases could theoretically be sanitized, but whether there is meaningful, releasable information that can be produced without revealing exempt material. The D.C. Circuit has long recognized a practical limit: when nonexempt material is “randomly interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” it is often not reasonably segregable because what FOIA protects is information, not merely discrete words on a page. That principle appears in Mead Data and was recently reiterated in Perioperative Services: trivial snippets that do not convey a meaningful, standalone informational value will not defeat a valid claim of exemption.

In Farahi, the district court concluded—and the court of appeals agreed—that the FBI satisfied its burden on segregability. The agency’s sealed ex parte submission (described below) set out why any nonexempt markings within the records were so intertwined with exempt material that they could not be released in a way that would not reveal sensitive investigative content. The court emphasized the point made in Mead and Perioperative: the mere theoretical ability to pick out a word or phrase does not obligate the agency to disclose items that, taken together, would either be meaningless to the requester or would risk disclosure of protected information. On that basis the courts found no “meaningful” segregable information remained and accepted the agency’s withholdings in full.

## Bender’s supplemental ex parte showing and the district court’s finding
After the district court expressed concern about the FBI’s initial, older declarations, the FBI submitted updated material, including a public declaration and a sealed ex parte supplemental declaration from RIDS leadership. The public declaration confirmed that the investigation remained ongoing; the ex parte filing supplied the factual detail the court said it needed but could not on the public record disclose.

When the district court reviewed the ex parte submission it concluded that the FBI had provided a “detailed justification” as to why the withheld material was not reasonably segregable. In other words, the sealed declaration explained with sufficient specificity how isolated nonexempt passages were embedded in a way that disclosure would either be trivial or would reveal exempt investigative content. On appeal the panel agreed that the district court’s reliance on the ex parte submission was appropriate and that the court did not err in finding no meaningful segregable information.

## Use of ex parte declarations in national-security and law-enforcement FOIA cases
Ex parte, sealed declarations are often the only practicable way for courts to evaluate certain FOIA claims without forcing public disclosure of the very information the agency contends is exempt. The Farahi opinion underscores two points about such submissions. First, they can be decisive: the district court explicitly relied on the sealed material to resolve both the pendency of investigations and the segregability question. Second, there is a strategic and procedural limitation for plaintiffs: Farahi did not oppose the FBI’s motion to file supplemental ex parte declarations under seal, and that procedural acquiescence foreclosed his later challenges to the district court’s reliance on them. The D.C. Circuit acknowledged that ex parte submissions handicap plaintiffs, but observed that this difficulty is inherent where secrecy must be preserved to adjudicate the dispute—an approach supported by precedent such as Perioperative Services.

The take-away is that courts will accept and act on sealed, ex parte submissions when national-security or sensitive-law-enforcement interests are at stake, particularly if the plaintiff does not object to the procedure. But because ex parte filings keep key factual detail off the public record, agencies commonly pair a public statement of the exemption claim with sealed elaboration for the court.

## The categorical-withholding challenge and forfeiture
When an agency withholds documents by category, the categories must be “sufficiently distinct” to allow the court to test whether the claimed exemptions properly apply to each group. That is the Gallant/CREW requirement: a court must be able to map claimed exemptions to specific sets of records. In Farahi, the FBI had organized its withholdings into categories, but the appellant contended those categories were overbroad.

The court of appeals rejected this argument on procedural grounds: Farahi failed to identify with specificity why particular categories were overbroad. Without particularized objections explaining how the categories were defective, the argument was forfeited under Khine. In short, a bare assertion that categories are too broad—without telling the court which categories, why they are overbroad, and with what evidence—will not preserve the issue on appeal.

## Appellate disposition and standard of review
The court reviewed the district court’s grant of summary judgment de novo, applying the settled allocation of burdens in FOIA: the government must show that an exemption applies. Giving appropriate deference to the FBI’s law-enforcement judgment—particularly in the national-security context—the panel agreed that the agency had shown the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings (Exemption 7(A)). It also upheld the district court’s segregability determination based on the sealed submission. Because the court found the FBI met both the exemption and segregability burdens, it affirmed the district court’s judgment in full.

## Practice notes for litigators and agency counsel
Several practical lessons follow directly from the court’s disposition:

- Update explanations when time has passed. If an agency’s initial declarations grow stale during litigation, a court may require updated declarations to confirm whether investigations remain ongoing and whether asserted harms still obtain.

- Pair a public justification with sealed detail in sensitive cases. Provide a public declaration that explains the legal basis for exemptions and a sealed ex parte declaration for the court that supplies the factual specifics the public filing cannot disclose without defeating the agency’s interest.

- Make categories concrete and defensible. If you withhold records on a category-by-category basis, define the categories clearly and be prepared to show why each category is subject to the claimed exemption; otherwise, categorical arguments can be forfeited for lack of specificity.

- Focus on meaningful segregability. Courts look for “meaningful” information that can be disclosed without revealing exempt material. Isolated words or trivial phrases that would be useless standing alone or would reveal exempt content when connected are not necessarily “reasonably segregable.”

- Anticipate the consequences of acquiescing to ex parte procedures. Objecting to sealed, ex parte filings preserves the right to challenge the court’s reliance on them; failing to object makes it difficult to later complain on appeal.

These practical points flow straight from how the court treated the FBI’s submissions and Farahi’s responses; they show how procedural choices and factual specificity can determine the outcome of FOIA litigation in national-security and law‑enforcement contexts.
