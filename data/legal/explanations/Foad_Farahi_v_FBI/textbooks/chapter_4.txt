Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation

Chapter 4: # Applying 7(A) Prong Two: Interference with Pending or Reasonably Anticipated Proceedings

This chapter explains how the D.C. Circuit evaluated the second prong of FOIA Exemption 7(A) — whether disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The court’s analysis relied on settled precedents about what counts as a “proceeding,” what the government must show, and the kinds of interference that justify withholding; it then applied those principles to the FBI’s declarations (both public and ex parte) and to Farahi’s staleness challenge.

## Proceedings requirement: ongoing investigations qualify; government attestations suffice

Exemption 7(A) protects records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” when release could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The court relied on CREW’s restatement of the “pending or reasonably anticipated” requirement and on earlier cases treating investigations as “proceedings” for this purpose. An ongoing criminal investigation counts as an enforcement “proceeding,” and the government may ordinarily satisfy its burden by attesting to the existence of such an investigation. The crucial point is that the investigation must be active in the sense that it is still gathering evidence for potential future charges; if disclosure would jeopardize that evidentiary work, Exemption 7(A) can apply.

The opinion cites Juarez for this principle: Exemption 7(A) applies “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence.” This standard frames the court’s inquiry: the government need not point to a filed indictment, but must show a concrete, continuing investigatory effort that disclosure could harm.

## Interference rationale: tipping off targets

A common and widely accepted rationale for withholding under 7(A) is that disclosure will “tip off” targets about the scope, direction, or subjects of an investigation. The court relied on CNSS and other precedents recognizing tipping-off as a legitimate basis for concluding that disclosure could interfere with enforcement proceedings. Tipping off can cause targets to destroy evidence, change behavior, flush out undercover sources, or otherwise frustrate law enforcement’s ability to collect proof — precisely the sort of prejudice Exemption 7(A) guards against.

The Hardy and Bender declarations asserted that revealing information from Farahi’s FBI file would risk tipping off targets and thereby interfere with the investigation. The court treated that statement as a standard, acceptable explanation of how disclosure could cause interference.

## Evidence of ongoing proceedings: Hardy’s public and ex parte attestations

On the record, the government’s initial showing came from David Hardy. His public declaration stated that the withheld records were part of investigations into national-security and terrorism matters and attested that the relevant investigation remained “ongoing.” Critically, the government also submitted an ex parte (sealed) Hardy declaration that the district court reviewed in camera; the sealed submission supplied considerable additional detail about the investigation’s nature and scope. Those two Hardy declarations together furnished the substantive factual basis — beyond a bare assertion — for concluding that the FBI was actively pursuing an investigation tied to Farahi.

Under the precedent the court cited, an agency affidavit that “show[s] with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated” and that explains the ongoing investigatory activity may be sufficient to satisfy the government’s burden. Here, the public Hardy declaration established the existence of the investigation, and the ex parte Hardy declaration provided the granular, sensitive details the government could not disclose publicly.

## Bender’s later update: confirming ongoing status as of March 2023

After the district court concluded that the Hardy submissions “seemingly” met the government’s burden but worried that the passage of time might have rendered them stale, the FBI supplied an updated submission from Joseph Bender. The Bender declaration was short in its public form and stated that the investigations “remain ongoing” as of March 2023. The government also supplied a supplemental ex parte submission from Bender that the district court reviewed.

The court treated Bender’s public declaration not as a standalone showing but as a temporal update to the earlier, more detailed Hardy submissions. Because the district court had already found the Hardy material to be at least sufficient in substance, the Bender declaration’s role was simply to confirm that the investigation continued while the case was pending. Read in that context, the terse public wording was adequate: it confirmed continuity rather than attempting to prove the initial predicate facts from scratch.

## Addressing staleness: why the short update sufficed in context

Farahi argued that the passage of years made the government’s assertion of ongoing proceedings dubious and that the Bender declaration was too conclusory. The court accepted the general point that when a significant amount of time has passed, a mere, vague assertion that an investigation is “ongoing” can be insufficient — a point drawn from CREW. But the key to the court’s ruling was context. The government’s showing was not limited to the brief Bender statement. It included (1) the public Hardy declaration attesting to the investigation’s existence, (2) a detailed ex parte Hardy declaration describing the investigation’s nature and risks of disclosure, (3) the district court’s earlier conclusion that the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden, and (4) Bender’s update confirming no change in status. That combination supplied the specificity CREW demands when staleness is a concern: the Bender declaration updated an earlier detailed record rather than attempting to substitute for it.

The court thus framed the staleness question as one of whether the government had shown a concrete, ongoing investigatory effort at the relevant time. Because the earlier submissions established that effort and Bender confirmed it continued, the court found no stale-gap problem.

## Breadth and sensitivity of the investigation make ongoing status plausible

Although the court could not reveal the contents of the sealed materials, it noted publicly available allegations from Farahi’s immigration proceedings that corroborated the FBI’s representation of a broad, serious investigation. Those public allegations—ties to terrorist figures and organizations, recruitment activity, and connections to convicted or indicted terrorists—made it plausible that the FBI’s inquiry was wide-ranging and sensitive. The court treated that factual context as reinforcing the reasonableness of the FBI’s claim that disclosure would jeopardize ongoing enforcement efforts. In other words, the more extensive and nationally sensitive the government’s investigative focus, the less likely a brief confirmation of ongoing activity would be viewed as inadequate.

## Court’s holding on prong two

Putting the legal rules and the factual record together, the court concluded that the FBI met its burden under Exemption 7(A): there was a “concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding” — an ongoing criminal/terrorism investigation — and disclosure of the requested records could reasonably be expected to interfere with that proceeding (by tipping off targets and the like). The detailed ex parte Hardy declaration established the nature and danger of disclosure; the public Hardy declaration attested to the investigation’s existence; and the Bender declaration, properly read as a confirmatory update, showed that the investigation remained active when the district court made its ruling. Thus the court affirmed the district court’s conclusion that Exemption 7(A)’s interference prong was satisfied.

Takeaway for readers: when time has elapsed, courts require more than a bare, conclusory assertion that an investigation is ongoing; but a brief later attestation can suffice if it updates an earlier public/ex parte record that gave the necessary factual details. Where the government has particularized, sensitive facts (especially in national-security contexts) and the agency reasonably fears tipping off targets, Exemption 7(A) will often shield the records from disclosure.
