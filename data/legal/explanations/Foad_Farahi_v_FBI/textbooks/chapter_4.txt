Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation

Chapter 4: # Exemption 7(A): Interference with Pending or Reasonably Anticipated Enforcement Proceedings

Exemption 7(A) of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) is a workhorse provision for law-enforcement agencies seeking to withhold records without disclosing details that could impede investigations or prosecutions. Its text is short—covering records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” whose disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings”—but the doctrine built around it is rich and factbound. This chapter develops the doctrinal framework courts use to determine when Exemption 7(A) shields records, explains the sorts of interference that count, and shows how courts treat the special problems of time (staleness), segregability, categorical withholding, and use of classified or ex parte submissions. The recent D.C. Circuit decision in Farahi v. FBI (2025) supplies a helpful modern example: the court sustained Exemption 7(A) withholding of an extensive FBI file after the agency refreshed and supplemented its declarations and explained why nonexempt material was not reasonably segregable.

## Text and burden: “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings”

Exemption 7(A) protects only records (1) compiled for law-enforcement purposes and (2) whose disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” The statutory structure places the initial burden on the agency opposing disclosure. In litigation the government must show both elements by affidavit or declaration and, where possible, with appropriate supporting materials.

The phrase “could reasonably be expected” sets a probabilistic, predictive standard. The government need not prove that disclosure will in fact cause interference; it must show a reasonable expectation of interference based on the facts known to, and the expertise of, the agency. Courts give agencies, and particularly the FBI, “a fair measure of deference” in making that predictive judgment. That deference is especially pronounced when the records touch national-security or counterterrorism investigations; courts recognize that specialized expertise informs a reasonable prediction about investigative harm.

But deference is not carte blanche. The agency’s affidavits must be sufficiently detailed and contemporaneous to allow a court to evaluate whether the claimed harms are plausible and connected to the records at issue. Broad, conclusory assertions that release “could” interfere are insufficient if unsupported by facts or context. Once the agency carries its burden, the court resolves the FOIA claim on summary judgment; the court’s review is de novo, but the government’s factual showings, when reasonable and specific, are entitled to weight.

## Defining “enforcement proceedings”: criminal, civil, administrative; ongoing investigations as proceedings

A threshold interpretive question is what counts as an “enforcement proceeding.” The D.C. Circuit has treated that term expansively. Exemption 7(A) covers federal criminal prosecutions, and it also reaches civil and administrative enforcement actions where the government is pursuing regulatory or statutory remedies. More important for many FOIA disputes is that an “enforcement proceeding” can include an ongoing law-enforcement investigation that is gathering evidence for a possible criminal (or civil/administrative) case.

The practical consequence is significant: an investigation does not need to have ripened into an indictment, complaint, or formal administrative charge before the government may invoke 7(A). So long as the investigation is “pending or reasonably anticipated”—that is, the investigative activity is ongoing and concrete steps could lead to enforcement—the government can assert that premature disclosure would interfere. This principle reflects the reality that disclosing evidence, sources, targets, methods, timelines, or investigative scope at the investigative stage can thwart the very possibility of later enforcement.

Farahi v. FBI illustrates this principle. The FBI’s withheld material arose from counterterrorism-related work and investigations that had not necessarily produced charges, but the court upheld withholding because the agency demonstrated the investigations remained ongoing and were of the kind that could mature into enforcement proceedings. The case thus reinforces that investigations, when concretely tied to law-enforcement functions, are “proceedings” for purposes of Exemption 7(A).

## Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Washington and Juarez: proving “pending or reasonably anticipated” proceedings

Two D.C. Circuit precedents provide the bookends for how to demonstrate that an enforcement proceeding is “pending or reasonably anticipated.” In Juarez v. DOJ, the court held that an ongoing investigation is a “proceeding” when the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case and that premature release of evidence would jeopardize that future case. Juarez made clear that as long as the government is actively investigating and collecting evidence for potential prosecution, exemption 7(A) can apply.

CREW (Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Washington v. DOJ) refined the showing required to establish that a proceeding is pending or reasonably anticipated. CREW stressed that the government must do more than make a vague assertion that investigations are ongoing; the agency should identify a “concrete prospective law-enforcement proceeding” or provide specific factual bases for anticipating enforcement. In practice CREW requires more than an offhand or blanket statement: courts scrutinize the agency’s affidavits for adequate factual detail, a rational nexus between the records and the asserted enforcement interest, and contemporaneity.

From these two precedents derive a working rule. The government can show a “pending or reasonably anticipated” proceeding either by attesting to the existence of an ongoing investigation (as Juarez allows) or by pointing to concrete facts that make enforcement reasonably foreseeable (as CREW demands if the initial showing is thin or stale). The government’s affidavit need not provide trial-level detail, but it must explain why releasing the requested records would meaningfully risk interference with enforcement.

Farahi applies and reconciles these authorities. There the FBI initially filed a detailed declaration explaining the nature of investigations tied to the requested records, satisfying the Juarez/CREW principles. Because time had elapsed, the district court required an updated assessment. The FBI supplied a terse confirming declaration plus an ex parte submission with greater detail; the court credited the whole record and concluded the “pending or reasonably anticipated” requirement was met. The lesson is twofold: initial specificity matters; and when time passes the agency should refresh its factual assertions.

## Types of cognizable interference: tipping off targets, revealing scope and strategy, chilling witnesses

What does “interfere with enforcement proceedings” mean in practice? Courts have recognized several cognizable categories of interference, grounded in how information disclosure can affect investigative work:

- Tipping off targets: Public release that identifies targets, reveals the existence of surveillance or investigative focus, or discloses queries or markers used by investigators can enable targets to flee, destroy evidence, alter behavior, or take countermeasures. “Tipping off” is a classic and frequently cited justification for 7(A) withholding. If disclosure would alert suspects to the nature, timing, or focus of an investigation, courts often accept a reasonable expectation of interference.

- Revealing scope and strategy: Disclosure of who has been interviewed, what lines of inquiry have been pursued, what investigative techniques are used (e.g., electronic surveillance, source development), or what theories the investigators are pursuing can enable adversaries to predict and evade future steps. Revealing investigative priorities or legal theories can also impair the government’s tactical advantage in negotiations, searches, or plea discussions.

- Chilling witnesses and cooperation: Release of materials that identify or disclose the involvement of confidential sources, informants, or witnesses can chill cooperation and jeopardize ongoing witness development. Even if a particular individual’s name is not harmful, disclosure of the pattern of cooperation or the existence of relationships can deter future witnesses from assisting investigators.

- Compromising evidence: Disclosing raw intelligence, uncorroborated tips, or the agents’ analysis may expose the evidentiary fabric of an investigation and enable targets to tailor defenses or manipulate evidence before charges are brought.

Courts assess these categories in light of the specific record and the agency’s expertise. A broad, unexplained assertion that disclosure “might” tip off targets is weaker than a declaration that the requested records would reveal identifiable investigative techniques, the presence of ongoing surveillance, or the identity of cooperative sources. The D.C. Circuit has emphasized that the government need not prove which exact harm will occur, only that a reasonable expectation of interference exists; nevertheless, it must provide a plausible explanation that links the nature of the records to the kind of interference feared. Farahi relied principally on the tipping-off rationale, buttressed by detailed albeit ex parte descriptions of the investigation’s scope and subject matter.

## Time and staleness: how passage of time affects the showing; when updates are needed

Time matters in Exemption 7(A) cases. An affidavit that persuasively shows an investigation is ongoing at the time of filing can become stale as months or years pass. Courts therefore scrutinize whether the agency’s factual assertions remain true at the time of judicial decision. CREW and subsequent cases make clear that courts may order agencies to update declarations if a significant interval has elapsed between the original declaration and the court’s decision.

There are two related concerns about stale declarations. First, the factual predicate for the “pending or reasonably anticipated” showing may no longer exist: an investigation may have closed, enforcement decisions may have been made, or the passage of time may have rendered disclosed information innocuous. Second, even if an investigation remains technically open, its posture may have shifted in ways that undercut the predicted harms of disclosure.

What procedural steps do courts take? If a declaration is old, courts often allow the government to file a supplemental declaration that confirms or revises earlier factual representations. That supplemental showing can be public or ex parte (sealed) depending on whether disclosure of additional specifics would itself reveal sensitive information. The district court in Farahi exemplified the common approach: the court denied summary judgment without prejudice to give the FBI an opportunity to update its assessment; the FBI then filed both public and sealed supplemental declarations. The court accepted the update, credited the agency’s expertise, and granted summary judgment.

Two practical rules flow from these doctrines. Agencies should monitor older FOIA litigations and refresh declarations when material time has passed. Requesters should press for updated agency statements if the government relies on old affidavits. Courts weigh these competing interests against the reality that certain information—especially in counterterror investigations—cannot be put on the public record without defeating the agency’s asserted harms.

## Categorical withholding authorized and how to define defensible categories

Agencies facing large volumes of responsive records commonly withhold by category rather than litigate document-by-document. The practice is permissible so long as the categories are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied.” Courts have long recognized that categorical descriptions can be necessary and efficient when an agency must process thousands of pages and when much of the record is similar in nature.

Several features make categorical withholding defensible:

- Logical, document-based grouping: Categories should reflect objective similarities—e.g., investigative memoranda, witness interview summaries, intelligence reports, referral material from other agencies—rather than undifferentiated lumps. Grouping by function or form helps a court assess whether the exemption logically applies.

- Connection to asserted exemptions: The agency must explain why each category is subject to the claimed exemption. For Exemption 7(A), that explanation should show how disclosure of that class of material would produce the kinds of interference discussed above (tipping off, revealing methods, chilling witnesses).

- Sufficient specificity: The descriptions must be detailed enough for a court to evaluate the claim. Mere labels (“operational files,” “investigative reports”) are weak; an affidavit should describe what those things contain and why their contents create a reasonable expectation of interference.

- Opportunity for challenge: The requester should be given enough information to meaningfully challenge the categorical descriptions. A court will be skeptical if a category is so broadly defined as to foreclose any useful review.

Farahi demonstrates both the use and limits of categorical withholding. The FBI identified categories to explain its withholding decisions and the district court’s opinion notes that Farahi failed to explain why the categories were overbroad—an argument the court treated as forfeited because it was not developed. But the D.C. Circuit has repeatedly stated that when an agency uses categories, a challenger must make specific showings about why a category is not distinct or why its application is improper. Absent such developed argumentation, courts will defer to the agency if its declarations otherwise provide a reasoned justification.

You will also see the doctrine described in older precedents sometimes associated with “categorical” allowances (sometimes referred to in practice by case names in different contexts). Whatever the label, the essential inquiry remains the same: can the court, based on the agency’s description and any supporting material, determine whether the exemption was properly applied to the category in question?

## Segregability, inextricable intertwinement, and ex parte submissions

Even when a record is covered by an exemption, FOIA requires agencies to disclose “any reasonably segregable” nonexempt portions. Courts apply a practical standard: if the nonexempt material is merely isolated words or phrases scattered line-by-line through a document, and if separating them would itself disclose the exempt context or provide no meaningful information, they may be treated as not reasonably segregable. By contrast, where identifiable chunks of nonexempt substantive content can be released without disclosing exempt information, the agency must produce them.

Mead Data established that courts should focus on information, not on purely mechanical word counts; Perioperative Services and subsequent cases emphasize that courts may rely on government affidavits that explain, with reasonable specificity, why segregability is not possible. Where segregable information is intertwined line-by-line with exempt material—so that excising the exempt parts would render remaining fragments unintelligible or of no informational value—courts have permitted withholding in whole.

Ex parte and in camera submissions complicate segregability review. Agencies sometimes provide classified or sensitive affidavits under seal so that they can explain why disclosure would harm ongoing investigations. While such filings handicap the requester’s ability to rebut, courts have permitted their use when necessary to protect secrets; the alternative would be to force disclosure of the very information the government seeks to keep secret. Courts must balance these competing interests, and when a requester fails to object to ex parte filings or fails to press for a public accounting, courts are less likely to fault the government.

Farahi typifies these issues. After an initial public declaration, the FBI filed sealed ex parte declarations that provided the district court with the particulars needed to assess interference and segregability. The district court found those sealed submissions persuasive and concluded that meaningful segregable material did not exist. The D.C. Circuit upheld that conclusion, explaining that the burden of showing segregability was satisfied by the government’s detailed sealed submission and that the presence of ex parte material—while prejudicial to the requester to some degree—is sometimes unavoidable in cases involving sensitive investigations.

## Practical implications for litigants and for courts

For agencies:
- Prepare declarations that tie the requested records to concrete investigative functions and explain the precise harms that would reasonably follow from disclosure.
- If your original affidavit is many months old, file an updated declaration to address staleness; be prepared to provide classified or sealed ex parte detail when necessary to explain the asserted harms and segregability.
- When withholding by category, define categories by document type and content, give reasons why each category is covered by the asserted exemption, and describe what, if anything, is nonexempt within each category.

For requesters:
- Attack categorical descriptions specifically and develop arguments showing why the categories are overbroad or why particular subcategories should be released.
- Monitor time: seek updated declarations if the government relies on old affidavits; ask for specific information about the status and posture of investigations when appropriate.
- When possible, seek in camera review or targeted public disclosures to test the government’s claims without forcing full public release of sensitive information.

For courts:
- Insist on adequate factual specificity in government declarations, particularly where the claimed harm is speculative or where significant time has elapsed.
- Give appropriate deference on predictive judgments about interference, especially from specialized agencies, but require a rational nexus between the records and a plausible interference scenario.
- Use sealed ex parte submissions when necessary to preserve national-security or law-enforcement secrets, but make clear on the public record the bases on which the in camera material supports withholding to the extent possible without revealing protected information.

## Conclusion

Exemption 7(A) balances competing public interests: the public’s right to know and the public interest in effective enforcement of the law. The jurisprudence developed in Juarez, CREW, and subsequent cases—illustrated by Farahi v. FBI—reflects a pragmatic approach. Agencies are permitted substantial latitude to predict harms and to withhold investigatory records where release would reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or anticipated proceedings, but that latitude is bounded by the need for sufficiently detailed, timely, and document-linked factual showings; sensible categorical descriptions; and honest engagement with the segregability requirement. When those procedural and substantive requirements are met, courts will often defer to the agency’s predictive judgments; when they are not, courts will push agencies to justify or revise their positions before ordering broad disclosure.
