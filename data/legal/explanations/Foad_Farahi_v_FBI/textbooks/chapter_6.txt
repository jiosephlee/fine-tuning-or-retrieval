Chapter 6: # Segregability: The Statutory Duty to Release Nonexempt Material

The Freedom of Information Act imposes a concrete duty on agencies not only to identify legitimately exempt records, but also to take affirmative and reasonable steps to separate and disclose whatever nonexempt information those records contain. That duty—often called the segregability requirement—is an indispensable part of FOIA’s design: the statute presumes disclosure as the norm and treats exemptions as limited exceptions. This chapter explains the statutory text and the D.C. Circuit’s doctrinal architecture for evaluating segregability claims. It uses the recent decision in Farahi v. FBI to illustrate how courts apply those principles in the law‑enforcement and national‑security context, and it surveys the principal doctrinal tools courts use—Vaughn indices, in camera review, sampling, category analysis, and ex parte submissions—to test whether an agency has satisfied its duty.

## Statutory language: 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) and § 552(b) mandates

FOIA’s segregability obligation appears in two complementary places in the statute as cited by the D.C. Circuit. The statute’s exemptions clause closes with an unequivocal command: “Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.” That sentence—found in 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)—expresses the fundamental rule: exemption is not a license to withhold whole documents when portions could lawfully be disclosed. Related provisions (as cited in current practice) require agencies to take “reasonable steps” to segregate and release nonexempt information. These parallel provisions together place the burden on the agency to show both that a claimed exemption applies and that it has complied with the duty to separate and disclose nonexempt content.

The text is categorical in form but requires judgment in operation. What counts as “reasonably segregable”? When must an agency redact rather than withhold an entire document? How precise must the agency’s justification be? The courts have developed a body of doctrine to answer these questions, recognizing two competing principles embedded in the statute: a strong presumption of disclosure, on the one hand, and practical deference to agency expertise and operational constraints, especially in law‑enforcement and national‑security contexts, on the other.

## Meaningful information versus isolated words: Mead Data Center’s “inextricably intertwined” concept

The seminal D.C. Circuit decision that framed the modern segregability inquiry is Mead Data Central, Inc. v. U.S. Department of the Air Force. Mead confronted the practical problem that some documents contain a mixture of exempt and nonexempt information so tightly interwoven that any attempt at disclosure would reveal exempt material or would leave only a handful of meaningless words. Mead articulated two key propositions that continue to govern courts’ analysis.

First, agencies are required to release any “meaningful” nonexempt information. The aim of FOIA is the dissemination of information, not the mere disclosure of words without informative content. Mead therefore rejects a mechanical rule that would treat the presence of any nonexempt token as requiring disclosure regardless of whether disclosure yields useful information. Conversely, the court explained that isolated words, or fragments whose separable disclosure would reveal no substantive information, are not the kind of “portion” that the statute requires agencies to reveal.

Second, Mead stated that an agency may withhold an entire document where the nonexempt and exempt information are “inextricably intertwined.” If separating the nonexempt content would itself expose exempt material, or if redaction would destroy the informative context making the remaining words meaningless, then the agency may withhold the whole record. Mead thus recognizes that segregability is a functional inquiry into whether redaction can preserve the utility of the released content without compromising the exemption interests.

The Mead standard requires courts to go beyond a literal word‑count and to consider the informational value of any portion that might be released. That is why courts insist on a showing—usually by detailed affidavit or index—explaining not simply that an exemption applies, but also why redaction would either fail to protect the exempt material or would reduce the remainder to trivia.

## When categorical withholdings defeat segregability and when they don’t

Agencies often confront large document sets and respond by withholding records in categories rather than itemizing every page. The D.C. Circuit permits categorical withholdings, but it imposes limits to ensure that categories are sufficiently concrete to permit judicial review. The controlling idea is procedural: a court must be able to determine whether the claimed exemptions properly apply to each category. Two lines of authority illuminate the permissible bounds.

First, categorical withholdings are acceptable where the agency defines categories that are “sufficiently distinct” and provides a logical explanation—often in a declaration or Vaughn index—showing why each category is exempt and why the records in that category are or are not reasonably segregable. When an agency files a declaration that describes the category, the nature of the withheld records, and the harm that disclosure would cause, a court can test the claim without door‑by‑door review. This approach recognizes practical realities: with thousands of pages, itemized indexing may be disproportionate or impossible.

Second, categorical withholding is defective when categories are overbroad, conclusory, or lack the factual detail necessary for a court to assess the exemption claim. In Gallant and subsequent cases, the court required categories to be described with enough specificity to permit meaningful judicial review. The plaintiff must be given sufficient information to challenge the agency’s assertions; the court must be able to determine whether the exemption fits the record and whether segregable information exists. A category label without explanation—merely saying “investigatory records” or “internal deliberations”—is often insufficient.

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in Farahi provides a concrete example of how courts balance these concerns. The FBI initially presented a large, complex corpus and relied on declarations from officials to describe the nature and risks of disclosure. The district court required updated information because of the passage of time; after the agency supplemented its showing with additional public and ex parte declarations, the court accepted the categorical descriptions and concluded that the agency had justified nondisclosure and nonsegregability. The Farahi court stressed that a categorical withholding can be adequate when declarations explain the categories with “reasonable specificity” and when the agency shows why nonexempt material is not meaningfully separable.

## Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. VA: reliance on ex parte submissions to decide segregability

Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. Department of Veterans Affairs is an important recent decision that clarifies the role of sealed or ex parte submissions in the segregability inquiry. The D.C. Circuit recognized that in some FOIA cases—especially those touching on law enforcement or national security—an agency’s only practicable means of explaining the full nature of its harm assessment may be a sealed, ex parte declaration. Revealing the content of that declaration on the public docket could itself disclose sensitive information and defeat the agency’s asserted exemption interests.

Perioperative held that courts may consider ex parte submissions so long as doing so is necessary to resolve the dispute without publicly exposing the exempt material, and so long as the agency has otherwise borne its burden of showing that the exemption applies. The decision acknowledged that ex parte evidence handicaps the requester, but concluded that such handicaps are sometimes inherent in FOIA litigation concerning classified or sensitive investigations. Perioperative therefore accepts sealed submissions as a permissible evidentiary device, while also reminding courts that agency affidavits—even when ex parte—must provide “reasonable specificity” and factual grounding sufficient to allow a reviewing court to evaluate the claimed exemptions.

Farahi illustrates this principle in practice. The FBI supplemented public declarations with a sealed, ex parte Hardy declaration describing investigatory details and explaining why meaningful segregable information could not be released. The district court relied on the sealed material to test the FBI’s segregability claim, and the D.C. Circuit affirmed that reliance. The opinion emphasized that Farahi did not oppose the use of ex parte declarations and that the sealed submissions were the only practical way to decide the dispute without revealing the very material the government sought to protect.

Even under Perioperative and Farahi, sealed submissions are not an automatic win for agencies. Courts still insist that the agency’s sealed affidavit provide enough information-even if only to the judge‑to satisfy the statutory command. Where sealed affidavits are conclusory or evasive, courts have required further public detail, in camera testing, or other remedial measures.

## Practical indicators of non-segregability in law enforcement/national security files

Certain features of law‑enforcement and national‑security files commonly predict—and the courts commonly accept—claims that nonexempt content is not reasonably segregable. These practical indicators guide both agencies and courts in applying the Mead standard.

One frequent indicator is the intermixing of exempt operational details and nonexempt factual data on a line‑by‑line basis. Investigative memoranda, interview reports, and intelligence analyses often embed contextual descriptions, source identifiers, investigative methods, and analytic judgments in the same paragraphs. Redacting the operational details may leave isolated factual fragments that—although nonexempt when standing alone—would, in the agency’s assessment, reveal the existence of sources, tactics, or the scope of an investigation if disclosed in context. Courts have accepted that where exempt and nonexempt information are so intertwined, segregation is not reasonable.

A second indicator is the presence of material whose disclosure would produce “mosaic” effects—where many innocuous facts, when disclosed together or in sequence, would allow a motivated adversary to reconstruct operational capabilities, witness identities, or investigative scope. In national‑security and counterterrorism contexts, the government often argues (and courts often accept) that even seemingly trivial dataset elements can be fused with other public information to produce a harmful picture. Mead’s insistence on meaningful information, rather than isolated words, dovetails with this concern: releasing isolated fragments that facilitate reconstruction is not a legitimate exercise of FOIA.

A third practical factor is volume coupled with the nature of the information. Large file collections assembled during long‑running investigations commonly contain repeated references to techniques, witness statements, and analytic inferences. Where the agency demonstrates by affidavit that these repeated references are pervasive and interlaced with nonexempt items, the court may conclude that segregable information is de minimis—or at least not “reasonably” segregable in light of the harms that would follow from piecemeal release.

Farahi embodies these indicators. The FBI located over 10,000 pages and 80 CDs potentially responsive to the request. The agency’s sealed declarations explained that the files were part of ongoing national‑security and terrorism investigations involving named terrorist actors and organizations—circumstances in which the agency plausibly showed that descriptions, context, and connective tissue among documents would reveal sensitive operational facts if released. The court accepted that the nonexempt material was not meaningfully separable without revealing exempt details or producing harms the exemptions were designed to prevent.

## Judicial tools: in camera review, sampling, and requiring granular justifications

To test an agency’s segregability claim, courts have developed several procedural tools that reconcile FOIA’s disclosure objective with agencies’ legitimate need to protect sensitive information. Three of the most important are in camera review, sampling, and requirements for granular justifications (Vaughn indices and detailed affidavits).

In camera review allows a judge alone to inspect the contested records. It is particularly valuable when the public record cannot fully reflect the issues because of the secrecy of the exempt material. A judge can decide whether an exemption applies and whether segregability is practical without compounding the problem by placing sensitive information on the public docket. In camera review, however, the court bears the risk of substituting its own assessment of operational harms without the agency’s context; nevertheless, the option exists as a potent remedial device when declarations are thin.

Sampling is a practical device in cases involving voluminous records. Rather than requiring an agency to index every page, the court may review a representative sample to test whether the agency’s descriptions are accurate and whether nonexempt content tends to be segregable. Sampling places a practical burden on the agency to demonstrate that its practice in the sample reflects its treatment of the larger corpus. Courts use sampling to avoid formulaic lawyering or overly generalized categorical descriptions.

Granular justifications take the form of Vaughn indices and detailed agency affidavits. A Vaughn index is an item-by-item description of withheld records showing the document type, the exemption(s) claimed, and the justification for withholding. The D.C. Circuit has emphasized that these indices or supporting declarations must supply information adequate for meaningful judicial review—sufficient specificity to connect the exemption to the content and to explain why segregability is not feasible. Declarations that merely parrot statutory language or offer conclusory assertions of “investigative harm” fail the test; those that provide a narrative of the investigation, explain the investigative significance of the withheld material, and describe why redaction would not protect the exempt content, fare better.

Farahi demonstrates how these tools interplay. The district court initially denied summary judgment and asked the FBI to update potentially stale declarations. The court’s request reflected the obligation to ensure that declarations remain current and to subject the agency’s claimed harms to scrutiny. The FBI supplemented its public affidavits with sealed, ex parte declarations and a more detailed showing on segregability. The district court applied those submissions (in camera) and concluded that the nonexempt material was not reasonably segregable; the D.C. Circuit accepted that approach and affirmed. The decision confirms that courts may, and sometimes must, demand refreshed and granular evidence before accepting an agency’s categorical or holistic segregability claim.

## Practical advice for counsel and courts

For agencies: When asserting nonsegregability, prepare a declaration that does more than identify the exemption. Describe the document types, explain the nature and stage of the investigation, identify the particular harms disclosure would cause, and explain why redaction is impractical or would leave only non‑meaningful fragments. If the agency relies on categories, describe the categories with concrete examples and explain the methodology used to assign records to each category. For national‑security and law‑enforcement records, describe how context and connections among documents would reveal sensitive information and explain why even small disclosures would be harmful.

For FOIA requesters: Challenge overbroad categories by demanding specificity. Seek in camera review or sampling where practicable. Insist on updated declarations when significant time passes, because assertions that an investigation is “ongoing” may lose force without current backing. If an agency uses sealed submissions, argue for at least a public summary that identifies the legal and factual bases for withholding without revealing sensitive facts, and press the court to sample or to inspect a representative set of documents in camera.

For courts: Balance deference to agency expertise with the statutory presumption of disclosure. Require reasonable specificity in declarations; where the agency’s public showing is insufficient, use in camera review or sampling to test the agency’s representations. Accept ex parte submissions when necessary to avoid revealing the very information the agency seeks to protect, but demand that sealed affidavits be probative and explain why segregability is not feasible. Remember Mead’s touchstone: the focus is on meaningful information, not on a substratum of isolated words that discloses nothing of substance.

## Conclusion

The segregability requirement is both doctrinally principled and pragmatically flexible. Mead’s “inextricably intertwined” concept gives substance to the statutory command to release “any reasonably segregable portion” by focusing inquiry on whether redaction would preserve meaningful information without disclosing exempt materials. The D.C. Circuit’s later decisions—CREW, Perioperative, Juarez, and Farahi among them—balance that principle against practical realities in law‑enforcement and national‑security contexts. Those cases teach that courts will demand reasonable specificity from agencies, will permit categorical withholdings where categories are sensibly drawn and justified, and will employ tools such as in camera review and sampling when the public record cannot safely reveal the sensitive details necessary to adjudicate the dispute. For students and practitioners alike, the lesson is straightforward: the duty to segregate is real, but its application must be attentive to the informational value of what would be released and to the legitimate operational harms that disclosure might produce.
