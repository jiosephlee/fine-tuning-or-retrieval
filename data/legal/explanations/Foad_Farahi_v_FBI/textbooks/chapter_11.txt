Chapter 11: # Practitioner’s Toolkit: Litigating and Resisting 7(A) Claims

This chapter uses Farahi v. FBI (D.C. Cir. 2025) as a teaching vehicle to show how Exemption 7(A) disputes are litigated in practice and to give concrete strategies for both requesters and agencies. The opinion shows recurring themes in 7(A) litigation: the government’s need to show records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated,” the court’s expectation that agencies update stale declarations, the role of ex parte/sealed submissions, and how courts evaluate segregability. Below are doctrinal rules framed as practical guidance, with examples and tactics grounded in Farahi and leading D.C. Circuit precedent (CREW, Juarez, CNSS, Campbell, Mead, Gallant, Khine).

## Requester strategies: demanding specificity, attacking overbroad categories, and leveraging staleness for updates

The requester’s fundamental task in a 7(A) case is to force the government to carry its burden with tangible specificity and to exploit any gaps—particularly gaps created by stale or conclusory declarations.

- Demand a particularized administrative record and a Vaughn-style disclosure. Do not let the government proceed with only a single conclusory affidavit. Ask for a Vaughn index (or an equivalent itemized log) describing withheld records by date, type, custodian, and exemption(s) claimed. CREW and Gallant require categories to be “sufficiently distinct” so a court can properly review the claimed exemptions; insist on that granularity early. In Farahi, the FBI supplied multiple declarations and ex parte material; Farahi’s failure to press specific category-based objections led to forfeiture. To avoid that, make concrete objections: identify particular categories you contend are overbroad and explain why.

- Attack conclusory or stale declarations. When a government declaration says an investigation is “ongoing” but provides no details, argue it is conclusory under CREW. Use the passage of time to demand updated declarations: move the district court to order refreshed attestations if significant time elapses between the declaration and the court’s decision. Farahi shows that courts often require updated assessments when there is delay—seek that remedy routinely. If the agency declines to update voluntarily, move to compel. Your motion should explain why the original declaration cannot answer whether enforcement proceedings remain “pending or reasonably anticipated” given intervening events or delay.

- Force detail about “interference.” The government must explain how disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” — examples include tipping off targets to investigative scope, revealing techniques or sources, or compromising cooperation. Ask for examples from the agency. Where public declarations are thin, contend they are insufficient unless supplemented by specific factual statements about investigative steps that would be compromised. In Farahi, the Hardy and ex parte declarations together supplied such detail; a requester should press to break that combination apart and force as much public specificity as possible.

- Narrow your request strategically. If the responsive set is enormous (e.g., 10,750 pages and 80 CDs in Farahi), propose narrower, staged requests targeted at categories less likely to implicate 7(A) (personal identifying records, immigration adjudicative documents, internal administrative communications unrelated to investigative strategy). Show the court that the agency could reasonably segregate and produce those narrower categories. This both reduces the government’s ability to hide behind voluminous records and focuses the litigation on discrete issues.

- Use procedural levers. Move promptly for summary judgment after the administrative record is closed; insist on a Vaughn index before summary judgment; file a motion to compel more adequate affidavits; and, if warranted, seek in camera review of withheld records or the ex parte declaration (discussed below). A requester should also propose protective orders or counsel-only review (including clearance-based access) as a way to let the court balance secrecy and adversarial testing.

- Preserve objections. If you intend to challenge the agency’s categorization or segregability, raise those objections specifically in the district court and on summary judgment. The D.C. Circuit enforces forfeiture rules (Khine), so ambiguous or belated objections are likely to be rejected. Spell out with citations to the record why a category is overbroad or why segregability is feasible; when possible supply redaction proposals to show practicality.

In short: press for specificity; use delays to force updates; narrow requests if practicable; and make specific, preserved arguments on categories and segregability.

## Agency strategies: drafting detailed but non‑prejudicial declarations and using categorical Vaughn submissions

Agencies must simultaneously be candid enough to meet the court’s burden‑of‑proof requirements while protecting information that would itself create the harm FOIA exemptions are meant to prevent.

- Structure declarations to establish the three core elements. For Exemption 7(A) the agency should: (1) show the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” (establish nexus to agency duty and to a possible violation); (2) explain how disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” (identify the nature of harm — e.g., tipping off targets, compromising sources); and (3) if the original declarations are old, provide contemporaneous updates confirming that the risk persists. The D.C. Circuit gives the FBI particular deference in law‑enforcement contexts (Campbell), but the agency still has the evidentiary burden. Farahi illustrates a successful approach: an initial Hardy declaration establishing the investigative nexus and an ex parte supplement providing detail, followed by an update to address staleness concerns.

- Use ex parte sealed submissions when necessary — but prepare to justify them. An agency may file ex parte declarations that give a court more detail than the public record can safely bear; Farahi’s outcome shows such filings are often decisive. However, filing ex parte material invites criticism that the plaintiff cannot test the claim. To reduce that risk, the agency should: (a) provide a thorough public declaration that establishes the basic elements; (b) make the ex parte declaration a carefully confined supplement that the court can use to resolve doubts; and (c) explain in the public record why the more detailed content must remain sealed. Courts will accept ex parte submissions when disclosure of their contents would itself cause the harm asserted (Perioperative Servs.; Farahi).

- Draft the public declaration with enough specificity to survive initial scrutiny. Even if the agency intends to rely on sealed material, the public declaration should: identify the investigation’s general subject matter (without revealing sources or techniques), assert with factual support that the investigation is ongoing or reasonably anticipated, describe in general terms the types of records at issue, and explain why disclosure would prejudice enforcement (e.g., by alerting targets, revealing investigative scope, or compromising cooperation). Saying only “ongoing” may not be enough if the declaration is old or unsupported.

- Use categorical Vaughn indices prudently. When there are thousands of pages, item‑by‑item descriptions are often impractical. Courts accept categorical descriptions when categories are “sufficiently distinct” to permit meaningful judicial review (CREW, Gallant). Choose categories that map neatly to exemptions (e.g., “correspondence from overseas source regarding fundraising” vs. “agent reports reflecting investigative steps and operational assessments”), provide representative examples (redacted or in camera), and explain why segregability of categories is infeasible (e.g., exempt material is interspersed line-by-line). The agency’s categorical approach is most credible if accompanied by a sworn statement that catalogues search and review methods and confirms the impossibility or impracticality of piecemeal redactions.

- Anticipate the court’s concern about staleness. If significant time may elapse between declaration and decision, the agency should plan to provide a short update (a paragraph or two) confirming that the factual predicate (e.g., ongoing investigation, pending grand jury) remains unchanged. Farahi demonstrates that an update like Bender’s — short but tied to an earlier, fuller declaration — can be sufficient when the earlier declaration contains the details.

- Show segregability analysis. The agency must explain with reasonable specificity why nonexempt portions are not reasonably segregable. Practical points to include: where exempt and nonexempt material are line-interspersed, why electronic redaction would reveal context and thereby harm the investigation; the technical infeasibility of redacting (especially in audio/video or scanned images); and why release of discrete excerpts would itself create the harm. The Mead rule that words “interspersed line-by-line” are often not reasonably segregable is a useful doctrinal anchor; but agencies should demonstrate that conclusion with examples, not merely assertion.

In short: prepare layered submissions — a robust public declaration, carefully tailored ex parte supplement if needed, and categorical Vaughn tied to clear, evidence-backed explanations of segregability.

## When and how to seek or oppose ex parte/in camera review

Ex parte and in camera review are powerful but sensitive tools. Courts allow sealed in camera review of sensitive records and sealed declarations when necessary to resolve FOIA disputes without public disclosure. But counsel must use these devices thoughtfully.

- When requesters should ask for in camera review. If an agency submits an ex parte declaration and the public record is inadequate, requesters should move for limited in camera review of either (a) the withheld records themselves or (b) the sealed declaration. This is most appropriate when the public declarations are conclusory and “update” mechanisms would be insufficient, or when the requester has offered a protective order that would allow limited disclosure to cleared counsel. Request an in camera review as narrowly as possible: ask the court to inspect a sampling of withheld records or to review the sealed declaration to determine whether the agency’s public justifications are pretextual. Be prepared to justify the request: explain the insufficiency of the public showing and propose precise bounds for the in camera inquiry.

- When requesters should oppose ex parte sealed filings. If the government seeks to rely on sealed, ex parte declarations to defeat disclosure, challengers should argue that the public record must before the court contain enough information to permit adversarial testing of 7(A) claims. Ask the court to require the government to provide a public summary of the sealed material that is sufficiently detailed to allow meaningful review. If the government resists, press for in camera review. But beware: courts often accept ex parte filings where public disclosure would itself cause the harms invoked — Farahi accepted ex parte material when it was necessary and appropriately restricted.

- When agencies should seek sealed submissions. Agencies should use sealed submissions when public descriptions would reveal operational details, identities of confidential sources, or investigative techniques that would themselves undermine enforcement. If such a submission is necessary, craft a public baseline showing the general contours of the investigation, and then explain why the sealed submission is essential to resolve the dispute. Anticipate the judge will inspect the sealed submission to confirm the public account; therefore the sealed submission must contain factual specifics that justify the claimed exemptions.

- How courts tend to allocate risk. Courts in this circuit accept that ex parte submissions handicap a plaintiff, but they have sanctioned their use where there is no workable alternative to resolving the dispute without disclosure of sensitive material (Perioperative Servs., Farahi). Requesters can mitigate the handicap by offering protective orders or counsel-only review, but courts will weigh the competing policy interests.

Practical tip: propose a staged approach — require the government to provide as much public detail as it can, demand a sworn public declaration explaining why disclosure would cause harm, and only then seek sealed ex parte review if that public baseline is inadequate.

## Framing segregability: demonstrating “inextricably intertwined” records versus proposing practical redaction regimes

Segregability is where many FOIA disputes live or die. Courts require agencies to release “any reasonably segregable portion” of a record. But the definition of “reasonable” depends on facts: the degree of interspersion, technical feasibility of redaction, and whether isolated disclosures would themselves cause harm.

- Establish the legal baseline. The statutory duty is unqualified: an agency must disclose “any reasonably segregable portion” of a record after deleting exempt portions. Courts will accept agency affidavits showing with “reasonable specificity” why segregability is impracticable; they will not accept mere boilerplate. Mead affords agencies room to argue that where nonexempt material is “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material, it is not reasonably segregable. Perioperative and Mead explain that random words or phrases scattered throughout thousands of pages are not necessarily “information” that FOIA protects; what matters is the meaningful information conveyed when fragments are combined.

- For requesters: propose redaction schemes and sampling. If the agency asserts that exempt and nonexempt information are line-interspersed and therefore inseparable, insist on a sampling approach. Ask the court to order production of a representative sample of redacted pages to test the agency’s claims. Offer feasible redaction regimes (for electronic documents: machine redaction + review; for scanned images: OCR + contextual redaction; for audio/video: transcript redaction). Demonstrate the practicality of redaction by proposing a timeline and methodology and identify whether technical limitations exist (e.g., if the records are on legacy media). Show that release of redacted samples would not compromise the investigation.

- For agencies: document the interspersion and technical impediments. If you contend that nonexempt material cannot be meaningfully segregated, provide concrete examples showing how redaction would reveal context that would itself harm ongoing proceedings. If redaction is technically infeasible (e.g., because scanned images or video cannot be redacted without revealing context), explain that with technical detail. Where the record contains small fragments of nonexempt material “interspersed line-by-line,” use Mead and Perioperative to explain why disclosure would not yield usable information and might reveal more than the fragments alone. Courts will accept an agency’s segregability analysis if it shows reasonable specificity; blanket claims will not suffice.

- Tactical compromise: offer to disclose narrow, depersonalized summaries. As a way to resolve disputes without full release, offer to produce summaries that do not reveal sensitive details (e.g., dates of interviews, general topics discussed without names or operational tactics). Agencies should consider whether such summaries would adequately serve FOIA’s transparency goals without undermining investigations. Requesters should be prepared to accept carefully worded summaries if they reveal meaningful information.

Bottom line: litigants should treat segregability as a technical exercise: the requester must show redactions are practical and would not cause harm; the agency must show why they are not.

## Preserving issues: avoiding forfeiture of category‑based objections and segregability challenges

Procedural vigilance is essential. The D.C. Circuit enforces forfeiture rules in FOIA litigation: arguments not raised with specificity in the district court may be lost on appeal.

- Make timely, specific challenges. If you are the requester and believe the agency’s categorical descriptions are overbroad, raise that objection with specificity—name the category, explain why it is overbroad, and suggest a remedial narrowing. In Farahi the court cited forfeiture (Khine) when the requester failed to explain why categories were objectionable. A passing or general objection is not enough.

- Use the summary‑judgment record to develop the record. FOIA is typically decided on summary judgment based on the agency’s affidavits and the administrative record. If you discover insufficiencies in the declaration, move to supplement the record (for example, by requesting a Vaughn index, sample documents, or court-ordered in camera review). Preserve any dispute about ex parte submissions at the district court level—challenge their adequacy and request an opportunity to respond under seal or by means of in camera inspection.

- Raise segregability issues early. Make a segregability argument in your opposition to summary judgment and request a specific remedy (production of redacted samples, in camera inspection to test the agency’s claims, or a required methodology for redaction). If you accept the district court’s reliance on sealed declarations without protesting, you have likely forfeited later appellate claims.

- Preserve alternative theories. If you believe some categories may be protected under 7(A) but other parts must be released under different exemptions or the FOIA’s request structure, present alternative proposals (e.g., partial releases, narrowed requests) to the district court. This gives the appellate court a record to review.

- Use protective orders strategically. If your inability to see sealed material is the principal obstacle to framing objections, propose an appropriate protective order allowing cleared counsel or counsel with need-to-know to review the material. If the court declines, preserve the record by asking for an explanation and asserting that the denial hampered your ability to challenge the government’s claims.

In short: be precise, timely, and proactive—procedural missteps can be fatal.

## Anticipating collateral proceedings (criminal, administrative, immigration) that bear on 7(A)

The “proceedings” protected by Exemption 7(A) are not limited to federal criminal charges; they include ongoing investigations that could reasonably lead to enforcement actions (criminal, civil, or administrative) and, as Farahi demonstrates, proceedings in contexts that cross agency lines (e.g., immigration) where the FBI’s records may be sought or used.

- Identify the relevant proceedings and show their nexus to the records. Agencies should describe what “proceeding” is at issue and how the requested records relate to it. In Farahi the FBI connected its investigative records to terrorism-related enforcement proceedings and to known prosecutions and detentions; that nexus made the 7(A) claim credible. When the proceeding is not a criminal prosecution (e.g., immigration proceedings), the agency must explain how the records relate to law enforcement duties and prospective enforcement steps.

- For requesters: attempt to show the absence of pending or anticipated proceedings. You can petition the court to require evidence that a proceeding is reasonably anticipated. Look for indications that an investigation is dormant (no recent investigative steps, closed files, no probable cause). Use public sources (dockets, press releases, declinations) to show no active enforcement step exists. If the agency points to another agency’s use of the records (e.g., ICE in immigration cases), seek clarification on whether the FBI is coordinating with that agency and whether prosecutions or other actions are reasonably anticipated.

- Anticipate cross‑agency coordination. Where records have been shared with other federal actors (e.g., DHS, prosecutors), the government’s ability to show anticipation of enforcement actions may be strengthened. Requesters should probe whether documents were used in actual enforcement proceedings (e.g., testimony at immigration hearings, evidence in trials). If some records have already been used in open proceedings, those records may no longer be covered by 7(A) with respect to the same proceedings—ask for specifics.

- Consider timing and predicate events. Investigations that have moved to grand jury presentation, charging decisions, or forensic analysis are easier for the agency to show as “pending” or “reasonably anticipated.” Conversely, long lags with no investigative steps strengthen a requester’s motion for updated declarations and disclosure. Farahi highlights that courts are sensitive to the passage of time, and a short confirming update (Bender) tied to an earlier substantive affidavit (Hardy) can bridge that gap for the agency.

- Use collateral proceedings as evidence. If you represent a requester and know the records were used in a tribunal that is public (e.g., immigration hearing), use that to argue waiver or public domain status for the portions used in those proceedings. But recognize that use in one forum does not necessarily waive all exemptions for the full file.

Practical posture: map the universe of potential proceedings early, and tailor litigation strategy (narrow requests, targeted demands for updates, requests for production of records already used in public proceedings) to exploit or blunt the government’s 7(A) claim.

## Checklists: Practical moves for each side

Requesters — checklist
- Serve a narrowly tailored or staged FOIA request if feasible; otherwise ask for a Vaughn index early.
- In FOIA litigation, demand a detailed public declaration that establishes: (i) law‑enforcement nexus; (ii) nature of the proceeding; (iii) how disclosure would interfere.
- If the declaration is old, move to compel an updated declaration; explain why the time lapse matters.
- Propose redaction regimes and request redacted samples or in camera review of a representative subset.
- Offer protective orders, counsel‑only review, or clearance‑based review to increase chances of accessing sealed material.
- Make timely, specific objections to categories and segregability; preserve these at the district court level.
- If the agency relies on ex parte submissions, move for an in camera inspection of those submissions or the records themselves.

Agencies — checklist
- Draft multi‑layered submissions: a detailed public declaration, a sealed ex parte supplement when necessary, and a categorical Vaughn with distinct categories.
- Explain the nexus between records and law‑enforcement duties; provide examples showing investigations are ongoing or reasonably anticipated.
- Anticipate staleness: plan to file short updates confirming that the investigative predicate persists.
- Provide practical segregability explanations showing why redaction is infeasible or would reveal context that defeats the exemption.
- When using categorical Vaughn, ensure categories are sufficiently distinct and link each category to specific exemption claims.
- If seeking to file sealed material, justify why public disclosure would itself cause harm and propose a confined sealed submission rather than broad secrecy.

Conclusion

Farahi v. FBI is a paradigmatic 7(A) opinion: it reaffirms the government’s burden to show the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings; it underscores the risk of stale declarations and the remedial need for updates; it recognizes a legitimate role for ex parte sealed submissions where the public record cannot safely contain necessary detail; and it enforces the segregability obligation while accepting the Mead principle when nonexempt material is inextricably intertwined with exempt material.

Successful practice in this area requires both sides to be technical and procedural: the requester must insist on specificity, propose feasible redaction regimes and preserve objections; the agency must supply a factual, layered record and be prepared to justify sealed submissions and categorical withholding. Litigation tactics that leverage the timing of declarations, the availability of protective orders, and the feasibility of redaction will often determine whether sensitive records remain secret or are partially released.
