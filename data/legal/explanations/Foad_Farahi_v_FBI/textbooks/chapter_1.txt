Chapter 1: # FOIA Fundamentals: Structure, Purpose, and Litigation Posture

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) is a disclosure statute. Its premise is simple but potent: government records are presumptively public, and information is to be released on request unless the statute expressly exempts disclosure. That presumption structures nearly every FOIA dispute—what an agency must do in response to a request, what it must prove when it withholds, how courts evaluate the agency’s proof, and how the balance between openness and other public interests is struck. The D.C. Circuit’s decision in Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation, No. 23-5243 (D.C. Cir. Sept. 30, 2025), illustrates many of those fundamentals in the context of a terrorism-related FOIA request and the often-difficult questions that arise when enforcement investigations and national-security concerns intersect with a requester’s interest in disclosure.

This chapter sets out the core FOIA structure and doctrine, explains the agency’s procedural and evidentiary obligations, and shows how courts resolve FOIA disputes—using Farahi to illuminate recurring issues such as Exemption 7(A), the agency declaration, updating stale affidavits, the use of ex parte submissions, segregability, and the summary-judgment posture in which most FOIA cases are decided.

## FOIA’s core mandate: disclosure as the rule, exemptions as the exception

FOIA’s starting point is a command of disclosure. The statute requires agencies to make records available to any person who requests them, subject to enumerated exemptions. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(3). That framework places on the requesting public the power to require government openness while recognizing that certain narrowly defined interests—such as personal privacy, law-enforcement effectiveness, and national-security secrets—may justify nondisclosure. The exemptions are therefore exceptions to the rule, and courts treat them accordingly.

The practical corollary is that FOIA disputes are necessarily protective of disclosure: an agency claiming an exemption bears the burden of demonstrating that the requested records fall squarely within an exemption. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). This allocation incentivizes care on the agency side—an agency must not simply assert an exemption in conclusory terms but must provide sufficient factual and legal explanation to support the withholding. If the agency fails to carry its burden, the records are released.

Farahi exemplifies these principles. The requester sought his FBI file—tens of thousands of pages the FBI had compiled while investigating suspected terrorist ties. The FBI withheld most of the material and invoked Exemption 7(A), a law-enforcement exemption that permits withholding of records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” when disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). The D.C. Circuit affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the FBI because the agency met its burden with detailed declarations and other evidence showing the records were law-enforcement records and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. The case thus underscores FOIA’s disclosure default and the agency’s affirmative obligation to justify nondisclosure.

## Statutory framework: 5 U.S.C. § 552(a) (requests, timing, appeals) and § 552(b) (exemptions)

To litigate FOIA intelligently, one must keep the statute’s structure in mind. Section 552(a) governs the request–response process. It requires agencies to make records available on request, sets procedural deadlines for agency responses, and creates obligations for indexing, recordkeeping, and administrative review procedures (where an agency has an appeal process). Section 552(b) enumerates nine exemptions that permit agencies to withhold information categorically or conditionally—ranging from classified national-security information (Exemption 1) and internal personnel rules (Exemption 2), to trade secrets (Exemption 4) and law-enforcement records (Exemption 7).

Two practical points follow from this statutory architecture. First, statutory timing matters. The statute establishes strict response deadlines—agency responses must typically issue within twenty working days of receipt, with limited extensions—so failure to comply can produce early litigation and court-ordered timelines. Second, the exemptions are discrete and must be read together; a particular factual record may touch multiple exemptions, and the agency must justify withholding under whichever exemption(s) it invokes.

In Farahi the timeline feature mattered immediately: the FBI missed its statutory response deadline, prompting the requester’s suit. The litigation then unfolded under the familiar FOIA posture—agency review and redactions, the government’s invocation of exemptions, the submission of agency declarations in support, and ultimately, judicial review under the statute’s standards.

## Agency duties: search adequacy, processing deadlines, and administrative appeal

FOIA imposes a set of affirmative duties on agencies. Three are especially important in litigation:

1. Search adequacy. An agency must conduct a search reasonably calculated to locate records responsive to the request. What counts as “reasonable” is fact-specific and courts will usually accept a nonconclusory declaration describing the search methodology, search terms used, locations searched, and efforts to identify potentially responsive offices or systems—so long as the declaration is reasonably detailed and plausible. Declarations that are conclusory or that omit basic facts about the search can be rejected as inadequate.

2. Processing deadlines. Agencies must adhere to statutory response deadlines (and to any agency-specific administrative-appeal timetables). Failure to meet those deadlines often triggers litigation. Even after litigation begins, courts may require agencies to complete their processing within court-ordered schedules, and courts sometimes require agencies to update earlier statements if substantial time elapses between initial declaration and the court’s decision.

3. Administrative appeal. Many agencies provide an internal administrative appeal. Pursuing such an appeal can be required before judicial review in some contexts; in others, the statute allows suit after 20 working days if the agency fails to comply. In all events, administrative processing matters because it may narrow issues and produce a contemporaneous administrative record that the court will later evaluate.

Farahi illustrates each duty. The FBI identified over 10,000 pages and 80 CDs responsive to Farahi’s request and conducted an extended review. The agency initially missed the statutory deadline and the requester sued. Before the district court resolved the matter, the FBI filed a declaration in support of withholding and later was required by the district court to update that declaration because sufficient time had passed to raise doubt about whether the factual premise underlying the exemption—ongoing investigations—remained true. The update requirement demonstrates that agencies cannot rely on stale, unsupported assertions about the status of investigations indefinitely; courts will require contemporaneous verification when appropriate.

## Burden of proof on the government and the de novo standard of judicial review

FOIA law places the burden squarely on the government to justify withholding. Section 552(a)(4)(B) specifies that the agency bears the burden of establishing that a withheld document falls within an exemption. In practice, agencies discharge that burden by submitting declarations and supporting material that explain the nature of the records, the exemption(s) invoked, and why disclosure would harm the identified interests. Courts do not accept bare formulas; the agency must provide a factual predicate adequate for meaningful judicial review.

At the same time, FOIA cases are reviewed de novo. Courts, pursuant to precedent, do not merely rubber-stamp agency labels. They independently evaluate whether the statutory criteria for nondisclosure are met. That review, however, is exercised against particular backgrounds of deference. When an agency addresses national-security or law-enforcement judgments—areas in which the executive branch has special expertise—courts afford a reasonable measure of deference to the agency’s assessments of harm and factual predicates. The key tension is thus visible: the court must independently determine whether the exemption applies, yet it should respect the agency’s expertise and the practical realities of executing sensitive investigations.

Farahi demonstrates this dynamic. The D.C. Circuit reaffirmed that it reviews FOIA grants of summary judgment de novo. But the Court also explained that because the FBI specializes in law enforcement—and because national-security concerns were implicated—its invocation of Exemption 7(A) received “a fair measure of deference.” The net result is not a rubber stamp: the FBI still had to produce detailed declarations showing both that the records were law-enforcement records and that disclosure would reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.

## Summary judgment as the typical vehicle in FOIA cases

Most FOIA disputes are resolved on motions for summary judgment. The reason is practical: FOIA disputes typically turn on legal questions—does an exemption cover the material at issue?—and on adjudication of the agency’s written declarations and indices that describe the withheld records. There are often no disputed factual issues requiring an evidentiary trial; the agency’s declarations (and any competing proof the requester can offer) create a record on which the court can decide the dispute.

Procedurally, courts treat the agency’s declaration like an affidavit. To win summary judgment, the government must show that the declaration supplies material facts that, taken together with any other evidence, entitle the agency to judgment as a matter of law. The requestor may counter by demonstrating genuine factual disputes (for example, by challenging the adequacy of the search or the veracity and sufficiency of the agency’s explanations). If the record contains unresolved factual disputes material to the exemption claim, the court may deny summary judgment and require further proceedings. But many cases resolve at summary judgment because the agency’s record is sufficiently detailed, and requesters rarely can create enough factual counterproof to raise a triable issue.

Farahi followed this path. The FBI moved for summary judgment with agency declarations in support. The district court initially denied the motion without prejudice because it wanted updated information addressing the passage of time (both about the continued pendency of investigations and segregability). After the FBI supplied supplemental declarations (including an ex parte one), the district court granted summary judgment. The D.C. Circuit reviewed that grant de novo and affirmed.

## The role of agency declarations and Vaughn indices in building the record

When a requester sues, the agency must provide a record sufficient for meaningful judicial review. Two primary devices serve this function: the agency declaration (often called an “affidavit” although it is usually sworn), and the Vaughn index (a document-by-document or category-by-category index describing each withheld record and the exemption(s) claimed). Each performs complementary functions.

- Agency declarations. A well-crafted declaration explains the agency’s procedures, the nature of the records, the reasons for withholding, and the factual predicates for harm if disclosure occurred. The declaration should be nonconclusory: it should identify the kinds of investigations involved, the nature of the harm that disclosure would cause, and any factual basis (for example, ongoing investigative steps) that supports the claim of harm. Declarations may be public or, in sensitive cases, filed ex parte and under seal to provide additional detail without revealing the very information the agency seeks to protect. Courts may rely on sealed ex parte declarations where necessary, but they recognize the tension that such submissions create for the requester.

- Vaughn indices. A Vaughn index lists withheld documents or categories of documents and, for each, describes the document’s type, date (if possible), general subject matter, and the exemption(s) supporting nondisclosure. Where agencies rely on categorical descriptions instead of a line-by-line index (common when volumes are large), the categories must be sufficiently distinct and specific so the court can determine whether the exemptions apply to each category.

Both devices must provide “reasonable specificity.” The D.C. Circuit has repeatedly said that courts may rely on government affidavits that show with reasonable specificity why documents cannot be segregated and why exemptions apply. Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008). But what amounts to sufficient specificity depends on the nature of the exemption and the case facts: national-security and law-enforcement contexts sometimes warrant more protection, but the agency still must identify factual grounds for its withholding.

Farahi is instructive on these points. The FBI’s initial submission included a declaration from the Section Chief of its Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Because significant time passed, the district court required updated confirmation that the investigations remained ongoing; the FBI supplied a public update and a sealed ex parte declaration containing more details. The court explained that supplemental sealed material could properly address concerns—particularly when national security and law-enforcement investigations were at stake—and that the agency’s material supplied the reasonable specificity needed for judicial review. The case thus shows how courts will use both public declarations and sealed ex parte submissions to balance openness with protection of sensitive investigative details.

## Where segregability fits in every FOIA case

Segregability is a central, often overlooked, doctrinal consequence of FOIA’s disclosure mandate. Although an agency may withhold records under an exemption, FOIA also requires disclosure of “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b); see also id. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii). That means the agency cannot hide behind a protective label and withhold an entire document if meaningful nonexempt information can be released after redaction. Courts therefore insist that agencies take “reasonable steps” to redact exempt information and disclose what remains.

How do courts analyze segregability? Several principles guide the inquiry:

1. The agency must demonstrate that it tried to segregate nonexempt material and explain why what remains is not meaningfully separable without revealing exempt material. This explanation typically appears in the agency declaration or the Vaughn index and should describe the redaction methodology and why further segmentation would be impracticable or would disclose sensitive information by inference.

2. Nonexempt fragments that are “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material need not be released. Where ostensibly benign words or phrases are embedded line-by-line in sensitive material—such that releasing them would still reveal the underlying exempt information—the agency may withhold entire passages or documents. The classic statement is Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. United States Dep’t of the Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 261 (D.C. Cir. 1977), where the court explained that the “focus of the FOIA is information, not simply words” and that random words scattered through a document are not necessarily “reasonably segregable” if their disclosure would communicate the protected information.

3. Courts may rely on agency affidavits that show with “reasonable specificity” why further segregation is not possible. Juarez, 518 F.3d at 61. But the agency’s explanation must be more than boilerplate; it must allow the court to evaluate whether the agency took the required steps.

Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs, 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023), and other recent D.C. Circuit cases illustrate that the inquiry is pragmatic: the court asks whether remaining nonexempt material would be “meaningful” and whether releasing it would, directly or by inference, disclose exempt information or hamper the government’s interests.

Farahi shows the segregability analysis in practice. Farahi argued that out of the tens of thousands of pages the FBI located, some nonexempt material must be segregable and releasable. The FBI acknowledged that some isolated words or phrases could, in theory, be released, but explained (in its ex parte submission) that such bits of information were interspersed line-by-line in clearly exempt investigative material and that revealing them would reveal or facilitate deduction of sensitive investigative facts. The district court reviewed the sealed explanations and concluded that the agency had provided a detailed justification for why no meaningful nonexempt material was reasonably segregable. The D.C. Circuit affirmed, noting that segregability obligations do not require agencies to release trivial fragments of information when doing so would compromise legitimate interests and that the agency’s sealed submission provided the necessary specificity.

The lesson is practical: when preparing to withhold, agencies should attempt reasonable redactions, explain their redaction methodology, and be prepared to show the court—if necessary, in camera or via sealed declaration—why the remaining material cannot be usefully separated from exempt material. Requesters challenging segregability should identify specific passages they believe are nonexempt and show why their disclosure would not reveal exempt information; generalized assertions that some nonexempt material must exist are often insufficient.

## Special issues illustrated by Farahi: Exemption 7(A), “pending or reasonably anticipated,” updating declarations, and ex parte submissions

Farahi centers on Exemption 7(A), which illustrates several recurring doctrinal points.

1. Elements of Exemption 7(A). The provision covers records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if their release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Courts analyze Exemption 7(A) in two steps: first, whether the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes (the “nexus” requirement); and second, whether release could reasonably be expected to interfere with “enforcement proceedings.” For the latter, this court requires that the proceedings be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” See Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ (CREW), 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

2. Proving “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The government may establish the existence of ongoing investigations and anticipated proceedings by attestation; an agency need not produce a charging document or detail every investigative step. CREW held that so long as the agency’s declaration is not merely conclusory and establishes an investigatory predicate, the agency may meet its burden by attesting that an investigation is ongoing and that certain harm—e.g., tipping off targets—would result from disclosure. Juarez confirmed that an investigation that continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case is a “proceeding” for these purposes. But CREW also warned that the “vague nature” of a bare assertion that an investigation is ongoing may not suffice; courts require enough factual content to evaluate the claim.

3. The need to update stale declarations. Time matters in Exemption 7(A) cases. If an agency submits a declaration stating that an investigation is ongoing, but significant time elapses, a court may require an updated declaration confirming that the investigation still exists. An initial declaration may “seem” to satisfy the prima facie need for withholding, but after a multi-year delay, a court is entitled to contemporaneous verification. Farahi’s litigation exemplifies this: after the FBI submitted its initial Hardy declaration in 2019, the district court in 2022 asked for an update, and the FBI supplied a supplemental public declaration and an ex parte declaration to confirm that investigations remained ongoing.

4. Use of ex parte and sealed submissions. Courts may permit agencies to file sealed ex parte declarations when public disclosure of the detail required to justify withholding would itself undermine the very interest the exemption protects. Ex parte submissions enable the court to see the factual predicate without disclosing the details to the requester. This is an imperfect solution—requesters understandably object to being deprived of the full record—but the D.C. Circuit recognizes that in national-security and sensitive law-enforcement contexts, ex parte material may be necessary and constitutionally permissible. In Farahi, the district court reviewed a sealed ex parte declaration and found it supplied the necessary factual specificity for both Exemption 7(A) and segregability. The D.C. Circuit affirmed the use of ex parte material where the requester had not objected to the procedure and where the sealed declaration legitimately filled gaps left by the public record.

5. Deference and limits. While courts defer to agency expertise, that deference is not limitless. Vague or conclusory assertions will not suffice; agencies must provide enough factual detail (in public or sealed submissions) to allow the court to determine whether the exemption applies. Farahi shows both sides: the court gave weight to the FBI’s expertise and the sensitive nature of the investigation, but it also required updated, specific attestations about the continued pendency of investigations before accepting the withholding.

## Practical guidance for applying FOIA doctrine to new fact patterns

A student confronting FOIA problems should take away the following practical rules of thumb:

- Treat disclosure as the norm. When drafting arguments for disclosure, emphasize the presumption of openness and press the agency for specific factual predicates for any exemption invoked.

- For agencies: prepare declarations with care. Declarations must provide a credible account of the nature of the records, the harm that disclosure would cause, and the specific statutory exemption(s) relied upon. Include factual bases that can be publicly stated without compromising sensitive information; for truly sensitive information, prepare a sealed ex parte declaration but avoid overreliance on secrecy to the point the public declaration reads as an empty label.

- Watch the clock. FOIA deadlines are strict; requests that are not timely answered may produce early litigation. If litigation is ongoing, remember that stale factual representations are vulnerable—courts often require contemporaneous confirmations, especially when attempts to withhold rest on the continued pendency of investigations.

- In litigating, press on segregability. Requesters should be specific when challenging an agency’s segregability claim. Point to particular pages or passages you believe are nonexempt and explain why their release would not reveal exempt information; general assertions that some nonexempt material must exist are less likely to prevail.

- Expect summary judgment. Build the record accordingly. If you represent a requester, shore up factual counterpoints to the agency’s declaration (e.g., show that the agency did not search a key location, that the agency’s timeline is inconsistent, or that information the agency claims is sensitive is already public). If you represent an agency, ensure your declaration explains not only that harm would occur but how—describe the nexus between the records and law-enforcement duties, and, where appropriate, explain why segregability is impracticable.

- Understand the doctrinal contours of specific exemptions. Exemption 7(A) is a good example: to succeed in withholding under 7(A) you must show the records are law-enforcement records and that release could reasonably be expected to interfere with proceedings that are pending or reasonably anticipated. “Pending or reasonably anticipated” is a distinct requirement that often hinges on whether an investigation is actively gathering evidence for a potential prosecution.

Farahi is a compact case study: the FBI carried its burden with detailed declarations (including sealed material), the district court required updates when reasonable doubts arose about stale assertions, and the segregability obligations were satisfied only after the court reviewed detailed sealed material explaining why nonexempt remnants were not meaningfully separable. The decision reflects the court’s dual role: enforcing FOIA’s mandate of openness while recognizing the executive’s expertise and the practical need to protect law-enforcement and national-security operations from disclosure that would compromise them.

In sum, FOIA litigation is a dance between transparency and protection. The statute presumes disclosure, places the burden of nondisclosure on the agency, and requires courts to undertake de novo review. But the statute also accommodates the realities of sensitive government functions: agencies may file detailed declarations and, when necessary, sealed submissions that provide courts with the information needed to determine whether an exemption truly applies. Understanding how these elements fit together is the key to analyzing and litigating FOIA disputes.
