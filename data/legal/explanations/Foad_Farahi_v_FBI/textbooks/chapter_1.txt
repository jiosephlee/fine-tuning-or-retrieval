Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Parties and context: Farahi’s immigration history and FBI involvement in DHS’s removal case (alleged terrorism links)
Foad Farahi is an Iranian national who has lived in the United States since 1993. In 2002, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) placed him in removal proceedings for overstaying his visa. During those immigration proceedings, DHS relied in part on material and live testimony from an FBI agent, asserting that Farahi had ties to high‑level terrorists and organizations the State Department has designated as foreign terrorist organizations. DHS’s filings in court and the Eleventh Circuit’s subsequent summary of the immigration record identify allegations that Farahi acted as a recruiter, had connections to Khaled Sheikh Mohammed and other al Qaeda associates, was linked to convicted or indicted terrorists (including Jose Padilla), and supported charities accused of financing terrorism. Those allegations provide the factual and investigative context for the FBI records at issue in this FOIA litigation.

## FOIA request scope and volume: approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs located
Farahi filed a Freedom of Information Act request seeking his FBI file. The FBI’s search located a very large body of potentially responsive material—about 10,750 pages and roughly 80 compact discs. That volume and the sensitive national‑security and counterterrorism context of the materials shaped how the FBI processed the request and how the courts handled the litigation.

## FBI’s initial processing, partial releases, and withholdings; missed statutory deadline leading to suit
The FBI reviewed the located materials over several years. It released some records to Farahi but withheld most of the pages and electronic records, invoking FOIA exemptions. The agency did not meet FOIA’s initial statutory response deadline, and Farahi sued to compel a timely response. After that suit was filed, the FBI continued its review, ultimately completing a substantive production and asserting exemptions for much of the material. The litigation became a typical FOIA summary‑judgment contest in which the agency relied on declarations to justify withholding while the requester contested the sufficiency of those justifications.

## The Hardy declarations: public and ex parte filings supporting Exemption 7(A) and categorical withholdings
When the FBI first moved for summary judgment (October 2019), it supported its motion with a public declaration from David Hardy, then Section Chief of the FBI’s Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Hardy’s public declaration asserted that the withheld records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings—principally investigations relating to national security and international terrorism. The FBI also sought leave to file a supplemental, sealed, ex parte declaration from Hardy that would provide additional factual detail the public declaration could not safely disclose. Farahi did not oppose that request, and the district court granted it in October 2020. Thus the agency’s initial withholding justification rested on both a public affidavit and a sealed ex parte submission that the court could review in camera.

## District court’s November 2022 order: apparent sufficiency on 7(A) but request for updated status and segregability details due to passage of time
In November 2022 the district court considered the FBI’s first summary‑judgment submission. The court found that the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and concluded the FBI had “seemingly” met its burden to show disclosure could interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. But the court was concerned about the passage of time: declarations submitted in 2019/2020 might no longer reflect the current status of any investigation. The court therefore denied summary judgment without prejudice and ordered the FBI to update its assessment of whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or were reasonably anticipated. The court also said it needed additional information to decide segregability (whether any nonexempt portions could be released), and it explained that the reasons it needed more detail could not be placed on the public record—hence the expectation of further sealed filings.

## Renewed motion with Bender declarations (public and ex parte) confirming ongoing investigations and addressing segregability
The FBI renewed its summary‑judgment motion with updated supporting material: a public declaration and a sealed ex parte declaration from Joseph Bender, who by then was Acting Section Chief of RIDS. The public Bender declaration explained that RIDS had contacted the FBI agent responsible for overseeing the Farahi file, and that agent confirmed the relevant investigations remained ongoing. The Bender ex parte declaration supplied more detailed, classified or sensitive factual information about the investigation and explicitly addressed the district court’s segregability concerns—explaining why meaningful nonexempt information could not be segregated and released without compromising the investigation. In short, the Bender submissions were intended to be updates to Hardy’s earlier public and sealed declarations: confirming that the FBI’s earlier representations remained true and providing the additional sealed detail the district court had requested.

## District court’s grant of summary judgment: crediting ongoing proceedings and accepting segregability showing
Relying on the Bender submissions (including the sealed ex parte declaration), the district court granted the FBI’s renewed summary‑judgment motion. The court credited the FBI’s confirmation that the relevant law‑enforcement proceedings remained ongoing and accepted the agency’s explanation—provided in camera—that the exempt material was not reasonably segregable from the withheld records. The court therefore concluded Exemption 7(A) applied and that the agency had satisfied its obligation to disclose any reasonably segregable, nonexempt material (the court found there was none that could be meaningfully released).

## Appeal to the D.C. Circuit
Farahi appealed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit. On appeal the principal disputes included whether the FBI had adequately shown the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” whether disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that were “pending or reasonably anticipated” given the passage of time, whether the FBI’s document categories were sufficiently distinct, and whether the agency had carried its segregability burden. The D.C. Circuit’s opinion (Katsas, J., Sept. 30, 2025) affirms the district court: it accepts that the records were law‑enforcement records, concludes the agency adequately showed an ongoing investigation that would be jeopardized by disclosure, and upholds the district court’s finding that no meaningful nonexempt material was reasonably segregable.
