Chapter 7: # National Security, Counterterrorism, and Deference under FOIA

This chapter examines how national security and counterterrorism concerns shape Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) litigation, with particular emphasis on Exemption 7(A). Using Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation, 23-5243 (D.C. Cir. Sept. 30, 2025), as the focal example, the discussion explains the doctrinal requirements for Exemption 7(A), the scope of judicial deference when national-security or counterterrorism investigations are implicated, the “mosaic” risks that justify withholding piecemeal information, strategic choices among FOIA exemptions, the role of parallel or collateral proceedings (including immigration removal), and the significance of public-source information versus official confirmation. Throughout, the chapter aims to teach the tests courts apply and the practical implications for both agencies and requesters.

## Deference to the Executive When National Security Is Implicated (CNSS and related authority)

The FOIA’s exemptions create a balance between disclosure and the legitimate government interest in secrecy. When national security or counterterrorism matters are involved, courts traditionally afford the Executive a pronounced measure of deference. The D.C. Circuit’s decision in Center for National Security Studies v. Department of Justice, 331 F.3d 918 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS), is a touchstone: it recognized that national security concerns often present factual and predictive questions for which courts should defer to the specialized expertise of agencies. Farahi reiterates that principle. The court emphasized that “deference to the executive in the FOIA context” is especially appropriate when Exemption 7(A) is invoked for counterterrorism-related records.

This deference manifests in a few distinct doctrinal ways. First, courts will generally accept an agency’s characterization that a record was “compiled for law enforcement purposes” when the agency is a law-enforcement body like the FBI, so long as a rational nexus between the investigation and law-enforcement duties is shown. Cases such as Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20 (D.C. Cir. 1998), and Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111 (D.C. Cir. 2017), recognize that the FBI’s professional judgment about the investigatory character of its files merits weight. In Farahi, the court accepted the FBI’s showing — including public and ex parte declarations — that the records were compiled in furtherance of investigations into national-security and terrorism-related conduct.

Second, deference affects the level of detail a court requires in agency declarations. Agencies must meet the burden of proof, but the D.C. Circuit allows agency affidavits to supply the requisite showings with “reasonable specificity” without requiring disclosure that would defeat the national-security interests at issue. Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54 (D.C. Cir. 2008), illustrates this approach: courts may “rely on government affidavits that show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated.” Farahi is illustrative: the FBI provided a public declaration that attested to the existence and purpose of the investigations and supplemented it with a sealed ex parte declaration that supplied detail the public declaration could not safely disclose. The court accepted that combination as adequate.

That said, deference is not an unbounded license. The D.C. Circuit has required enough factual specificity to permit a meaningful judicial assessment. CREW v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082 (D.C. Cir. 2014), established the “pending or reasonably anticipated” standard for Exemption 7(A) and warned against conclusory, vague assertions that an investigation is ongoing. Farahi demonstrates how courts approach this tension: the district court pressed the FBI for updated, non-stale attestations before accepting the Exemption 7(A) claim, and the D.C. Circuit endorsed that approach. Thus, while courts defer, agencies must still produce competent, timely declarations that provide a factual predicate for the claimed harm.

## The Mosaic Concern: How Seemingly Innocuous Details Can Reveal Sensitive Operations

One of the most important rationales for withholding information in national-security FOIA cases is what is often called the “mosaic” theory: discrete pieces of information, innocuous in isolation, may combine with other information (public or private) to reveal sensitive operational details or the scope of an investigation. Courts have repeatedly recognized that FOIA’s focus on “information” (not merely words) means that the context-sensitive risk of aggregation must be considered. Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242 (D.C. Cir. 1977), first articulated concerns about inextricably intertwined disclosures and the practical impossibility of redacting every harmless-looking data point when such redactions would still leave a usable mosaic.

The mosaic concern justifies both the withholding decision under Exemption 7(A) and the use of sealed ex parte declarations: public exposure of the agency’s reasoning, if it requires explaining the sensitivity of particular facts, may itself reveal the very operational contours the agency seeks to protect. In Farahi, the FBI’s decision to submit ex parte material was not just a litigation convenience; it was necessary to avoid producing an explanation that would tip off investigative targets. The D.C. Circuit accepted this approach, noting that ex parte submissions “handicap FOIA plaintiffs” but are sometimes “inherent” in cases where secrecy must be preserved. The court’s reasoning demonstrates the practical mechanics of the mosaic theory: a public declaration can state the general risk (e.g., “releasing information would tip off targets”), while sealed supporting material can demonstrate how even apparently trivial details could do so.

For students, the mosaic idea requires attention to context. An agency’s assertion that redaction or segregation is impracticable will be more persuasive when the record is a comprehensive, interwoven investigative file that includes timelines, corroborating identifiers, and operational planning. When relevant facts are “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” Mead Data and subsequent cases instruct that the small nonexempt fragments are not “reasonably segregable.” But the agency still must describe, with sufficient specificity, the nature of the interconnections so a court can assess whether the mosaic risk is real and substantial rather than speculative.

## Choice of Exemptions: Why Agencies May Rely on 7(A) Rather Than 1 or 3

Agencies have a menu of FOIA exemptions to choose from, and the selection often reflects the nature of the harm they seek to avert. Exemption 1 protects properly classified national-security information under an executive order; Exemption 3 covers matters specifically exempted by statute; Exemption 7(A) protects law-enforcement records whose release could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings. Each exemption has distinct prerequisites and evidentiary paths.

Exemption 1 hinges on classification decisions: the information must be properly classified under an executive order governing classified national security information (e.g., EO 13526). In contrast, Exemption 3 requires a separate statute that either forbids disclosure or sets a particular standard of non-disclosure. Exemption 7(A) requires that the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are pending or reasonably anticipated. Because Exemption 7(A) turns on the functional context of the records and the reasonable expectation of interference—rather than a formal classification decision or a narrowly tailored statutory prohibition—agencies frequently rely on 7(A) when the core concern is the interference that disclosure would create for investigations or prosecutions.

Farahi exemplifies this strategic choice. Although the case involves national-security and counterterrorism themes, the FBI relied principally on Exemption 7(A). Several reasons make that decision understandable. For one, the FBI’s files were investigatory and law-enforcement in nature: they were “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” a predicate that is easy for a law-enforcement agency to satisfy. For another, Exemption 7(A) directly addresses the harm of disclosure to investigative continuity and to the integrity of pending or prospective enforcement proceedings—harms that classification alone (Exemption 1) or a separate statutory bar (Exemption 3) may not precisely capture. Moreover, prosecutorial and investigative sensitivity often arises from procedural dynamics (e.g., tipping off targets) rather than simply classification, making 7(A)’s predictive interference analysis particularly apt.

That said, agencies sometimes invoke multiple exemptions in tandem. When information is formally classified, Exemption 1 provides an independent and often stronger basis for withholding because it rests on a distinct statutory–regulatory classification system. Conversely, agencies may prefer 7(A) when the central concern is operational interference or when the relevant information has not been classified but remains sensitive because of investigative implications. Students should appreciate that exemption choice is strategic and fact-dependent; courts will evaluate each claimed exemption on its own terms and will not permit agencies to rely on conclusory assertions. Farahi shows that the agency’s demonstration under 7(A) must include both the law-enforcement nexus and a plausible prediction that disclosure would interfere with “pending or reasonably anticipated” proceedings.

## Parallel Proceedings and Collateral Forums: Immigration, Criminal Investigations, and FOIA Justifications

FOIA disputes do not occur in a vacuum; the underlying records often connect to active or past proceedings in other fora. An especially salient example is immigration removal proceedings, which frequently draw on investigative files compiled by law-enforcement agencies. The “pending or reasonably anticipated” requirement in Exemption 7(A) is therefore frequently litigated in contexts where some form of enforcement activity—criminal investigation, immigration removal, administrative sanctions, or other proceedings—exists or could be anticipated.

CREW held that Exemption 7(A) requires the government to demonstrate that enforcement proceedings are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Subsequent cases, including Juarez and Farahi, have clarified that ongoing investigations that are actively gathering evidence for potential prosecution qualify as “proceedings.” Farahi shows the interplay between immigration proceedings and ongoing FBI investigations. Although Farahi’s immigration case is civil in nature, the FBI’s counterterrorism investigation was criminal and investigative. The court accepted that the disclosure of FBI materials could interfere with law-enforcement proceedings (criminal or prospective criminal ones), even though the requester’s most immediately relevant litigation context was immigration removal. Put differently, the existence of collateral proceedings does not prohibit the government from asserting 7(A) where an independent, ongoing law-enforcement investigation would be harmed by disclosure.

There are two practical points for students. First, a FOIA requester who is a party in a collateral proceeding should not assume that the needs of that forum (e.g., discovery rights in immigration proceedings) will trump general FOIA exemptions. The government can argue that disclosure in response to a FOIA request would differ in scale or publicity from targeted discovery and could undermine an investigation in ways that in-court disclosure under protective order might not. Second, an agency’s claim that disclosure would interfere with a law-enforcement proceeding must demonstrate a nexus between the records and possible enforcement activity. In Farahi, the government supplied both public and sealed evidence that the FBI was investigating ties to designated terrorist organizations and to convicted terrorists; the court accepted that nexus as sufficient to meet the Exemption 7(A) standard.

The doctrinal lesson is that “proceedings” under 7(A) are not limited to formal prosecutions and can include pending or reasonably anticipated criminal investigations and other enforcement activities. Collateral civil proceedings, like immigration removal, can increase the sensitivity of a file (if they reveal alleged associations or allegations publicized in different contexts), but they do not eliminate the government’s right to claim interference when criminal investigative interests are at stake.

## Public-Source Information versus Official Confirmation: Risks of “Confirmation” and the Value of Context

A common FOIA battleground concerns the relationship between information already in the public domain and the government’s assertion that disclosure would still be harmful. Requesters often argue that if facts are already public—through media reports, court filings, or other disclosures—then releasing agency records containing the same facts cannot harm national security or an investigation. Courts have recognized that public-source information can blunt an agency’s argument of harm, but they have also cautioned that official confirmation of facts or the aggregation of those facts in a government file can create distinct risks.

Farahi illustrates the nuance. The record shows that many allegations about the requester’s ties to terrorist organizations appeared in immigration proceedings and in public court records. Farahi argued that because some allegations were in the public record, the FBI could not reasonably claim that producing its file would interfere with an investigation. The D.C. Circuit rejected that argument. Two separate harms support that outcome.

First, official confirmation: when an agency produces its own records, it does more than repeat publicly available facts; it confirms the premises of those facts, sometimes in detail, and that confirmation can change the calculus for targets or co-conspirators who may not have previously known that the government is actively investigating. An official investigative dossier, even if its individual elements mirror public sources, signals investigatory attention and often reveals lines of inquiry. The court in Farahi accepted that even confirmation of public facts could tip off targets about the scope and focus of an investigation, thereby frustrating evidence-gathering.

Second, aggregation and context: public materials might be scattered, partial, or dated. The agency’s file aggregates, organizes, and potentially connects disparate items—timestamps, interview summaries, internal assessments, and linkages among individuals. That organization and the focus of internal analysis can reveal what investigators believe to be important and where they are placing their efforts. That is the mosaic risk in another guise: the same public pieces, when rearranged and contextualized, can become exponentially more informative.

For practitioners, these principles mean that requesters cannot rely solely on the existence of public allegations; they must show either that the information the agency seeks to withhold does not add materially to what is already public or that the agency’s asserted risk of harm is speculative. Conversely, agencies should explain why their records add value beyond public sources—whether by confirming an active focus, revealing temporal sequencing, or identifying unpublicized connections—and why that added value creates a realistic interference risk.

## Procedural Mechanics: Declarations, Updates, Ex Parte Submissions, Segregability, and Categorical Descriptions

Farahi also provides a useful lesson in the procedural mechanics that govern FOIA litigation. Agencies bear the burden of proving that an exemption applies, but courts allow certain practical tools to make that showing without compromising security. The following points summarize the key procedural doctrines demonstrated by the case.

- Declarations and Reasonable Specificity. Agency declarations must supply enough factual detail for a court to engage in meaningful review. The D.C. Circuit accepts affidavits that demonstrate with “reasonable specificity” why withholding is appropriate, but it will reject mere ipse dixit. In Farahi the FBI’s initial Hardy declaration and later ex parte supplementation were adequate when considered together; the court stressed, however, that the district court properly required an updated attestation because time had passed and factual circumstances could change.

- Updating Declarations. Courts expect agencies to update stale declarations. The district court’s denial without prejudice in Farahi was premised on the reasonable concern that facts and investigative status evolve over time, and that a declaration from years earlier may no longer be probative of whether a proceeding remains “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Agencies should therefore anticipate the need to refresh their factual showings if long delays occur. The Bender declaration in Farahi performed exactly that function: it confirmed that nothing had changed, thereby curing the district court’s concern about staleness.

- Ex Parte and Sealed Submissions. Courts may accept sealed, ex parte declarations when disclosure of the agency’s reasoning on the public record would itself cause harm. There is a tradeoff: the requester cannot test the sealed declaration publicly, but the court can review it in camera to ensure that the agency’s claims are genuine. Farahi recognizes this necessity for national-security FOIA disputes and validates the approach where ex parte materials are the only practicable way to resolve the case without further disclosure.

- Segregability and Inextricable Intertwining. FOIA requires agencies to disclose any “reasonably segregable” nonexempt portions of records. But courts accept that, in some investigative files, nonexempt material is “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material so that segregation would either be futile or would itself reveal sensitive linkages. Mead Data set the pattern for this analysis; later cases refine it. In Farahi, the court accepted the FBI’s explanation that meaningful, substantive nonexempt information was not segregable because it was scattered and interwoven throughout the record. The agency’s sealed explanation persuaded the court that a line-by-line parsing would not produce a releasable product that avoided operational risk.

- Categorical Descriptions and Specificity. When agencies withhold records category-by-category, they must describe categories with enough precision that a court can judge the applicability of claimed exemptions. CREW and Gallant require categories be “sufficiently distinct.” Farahi shows the limits of the requester’s challenge: because Farahi did not provide specific arguments explaining why the categories were overbroad, the court treated the objection as forfeited. Practitioners should note that a successful attack on categorical descriptions requires concrete examples and analytic argumentation.

## Practical Takeaways for Litigants and Counsel

For FOIA requesters challenging national-security-related withholdings, Farahi teaches several tactical lessons. First, attack agency declarations with particularity: identify where the agency’s factual assertions are stale, conclusory, or inconsistent, and ask the court to require updated, timely attestations. Second, when the agency relies on public-source material to justify non-disclosure, show precisely why the agency’s records do not add materially to what is already public or demonstrate that the agency’s confirmation would not meaningfully increase the risk of interference. Third, challenge categorical descriptions with concrete examples and explain how disclosure of representative records would not cause the predicted harm.

For agencies, the judgment is different. Provide a public declaration that sets out the law-enforcement nexus and the general harm of disclosure, and where necessary supplement with a sealed ex parte declaration that demonstrates, with greater specificity, the nature and risks of disclosure. Update declarations if litigation is protracted. When asserting non-segregability, explain why ostensibly nonexempt fragments are materially intertwined with exempt content such that partial release would itself be harmful or meaningless. And select exemptions strategically: use Exemption 7(A) when the predominant harm is investigatory interference, Exemption 1 when information is properly classified, and Exemption 3 when a specific statute applies.

## Conclusion

Farahi v. FBI exemplifies how courts apply Exemption 7(A) in the national-security and counterterrorism context: agencies receive a measure of deference, but courts still require factual showings that investigations are “pending or reasonably anticipated,” and they insist on reasonable specificity and timely attestations. The mosaic theory justifies withholding even when pieces of information are publicly available because official confirmation and contextual aggregation can meaningfully increase the risk to investigations. Procedurally, ex parte declarations and sealed submissions play a central role where openness would defeat secrecy, but agencies must use those tools prudently and be prepared to update their proofs. For students and practitioners, the case distills the principal doctrinal tests and practical strategies that govern FOIA disputes at the intersection of law enforcement and national security.
