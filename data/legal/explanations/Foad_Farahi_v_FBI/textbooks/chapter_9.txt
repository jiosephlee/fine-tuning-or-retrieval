Chapter 9: # Case Study: Farahi v. FBI as a Vehicle for Exemption 7(A)

This case study uses Farahi v. FBI to teach how courts evaluate Exemption 7(A) of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Exemption 7(A) permits an agency to withhold records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” The opinion illustrates: (1) the evidentiary showings an agency must make to satisfy the “compiled for law enforcement” and “pending or reasonably anticipated” requirements; (2) how courts treat public and ex parte (sealed) declarations, including the risks and benefits of ex parte submissions; (3) the scope and limits of the “tipping off” interference rationale; and (4) the practice and doctrine governing segregability and categorical withholdings. The case also shows how procedural posture—district court requests for updated declarations and appellate de novo review—shapes the litigation. The following sections walk through these topics, tie them to governing precedent, and explain how to apply the principles to new fact patterns.

## Factual background: FOIA request for an FBI file, immigration context, and counterterrorism allegations

Farahi is an Iranian national who, after years of immigration litigation, sought production of his FBI file by FOIA. The factual backdrop matters: DHS relied on evidence and testimony from an FBI agent in immigration removal proceedings that implicated Farahi in ties to individuals and organizations associated with terrorism. The FBI located roughly 10,750 pages and many CDs as potentially responsive. Over a multi-year review, the FBI released some material but withheld the bulk of the records under FOIA exemptions, principally Exemption 7(A).

These facts are important because Exemption 7(A) only applies to records “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” and the FBI’s asserted national-security and counterterrorism investigation creates the type of law-enforcement context in which courts traditionally afford the FBI deference. The immigration context also demonstrates how records compiled for one law-enforcement purpose (immigration enforcement) may be intertwined with broader counterterrorism investigations. For a student applying the law, the key lesson is that the underlying factual link between the subject and a suspected or investigated violation of law supplies the “nexus” courts require under Pratt and Campbell to treat the records as law-enforcement materials.

## The record: initial Hardy declarations (public and ex parte), district court’s staleness concern, and Bender’s updates

The agency supported its initial summary-judgment motion with a public declaration from David Hardy (RIDS Section Chief) and an ex parte, sealed Hardy declaration that provided fuller detail. The district court denied the FBI’s initial motion without prejudice, not because Exemption 7(A) was unavailable in principle, but because the court—sensitive to the passage of time—required the FBI to update its assessment that enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated. The court also said it needed more information to resolve segregability but could not state the reasons on the public record. The government then furnished updated public and sealed declarations from Joseph Bender (Acting RIDS Section Chief) to confirm that investigations remained “ongoing” and to elaborate on segregability. The district court accepted those submissions and granted summary judgment; the D.C. Circuit affirmed.

Two lessons emerge from this posture. First, courts may treat older agency declarations as stale if a long interval has elapsed between the declarations and judicial decision or appeal; agencies should refresh declarations. Second, agencies commonly use a two-tiered approach: a publicly filed declaration that gives general grounds for withholding, and a sealed ex parte declaration that supplies sensitive specifics the agency cannot disclose publicly. When a plaintiff does not oppose use of ex parte declarations, courts may rely heavily on them—particularly in national-security or sensitive law-enforcement contexts—although that reliance can limit the plaintiff’s ability to rebut the agency on the public record.

For practitioners, these facts teach a tactical rule: when the underlying investigations are protracted, agencies should anticipate judicial orders to update declarations; FOIA requesters should press for specificity and resist secrecy only when they can identify public indicators of staleness or inadequacy.

## Threshold met: compiled for law enforcement purposes under Pratt/Campbell

Exemption 7(A) is available only for records “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” The D.C. Circuit’s cases (Pratt and Campbell) require a demonstrable nexus between the records and the agency’s law-enforcement duties—i.e., that the records were created “in furtherance of” a law-enforcement investigation into suspected violations of law or threats to security. In Campbell, the court emphasized deference to the FBI’s determination because the FBI is a law-enforcement agency; but that deference is not limitless. The agency must supply a rational explanation tying the documents to investigatory duties and to a person or incident that plausibly implicates a law-enforcement interest.

In Farahi the FBI met this threshold: the public declarations described the records as compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism,” and the sealed declaration supplied substantive detail about the investigation. The court also relied on the broader record—Farahi’s own admissions in immigration proceedings that he had been investigated for ties to designated terrorist organizations and individuals convicted of terrorism-related offenses. Together, those facts establish the required nexus: the documents were not mundane administrative materials but part of an investigation into potential criminal or national-security violations.

To apply this standard in new fact patterns, ask whether the document’s purpose or provenance is investigatory (e.g., created by agents to gather evidence, reports about suspicious conduct, or intelligence summaries), and whether the agency explains the connection to its enforcement responsibilities. If the documents are plainly administrative (contract, personnel records unrelated to investigation), Exemption 7(A) will likely fail. Conversely, records generated as part of an agent’s investigatory activity will usually meet the threshold, especially when the agency furnishes concrete factual connectors.

## Pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings: combining public and ex parte detail; addressing CREW’s caution on vagueness

Exemption 7(A) applies only where disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW clarified that the government may establish this by showing the existence of an ongoing investigation, but cautioned against sustained, vague assertions that an investigation is “ongoing” without factual support. CREW warned that a bare or conclusory claim of an ongoing investigation may be insufficient.

Farahi shows how courts reconcile CREW’s caution with the practical limits of public disclosure. The FBI’s public Hardy declaration attested to an ongoing investigation and gave general reasons why disclosure would interfere; the sealed Hardy declaration provided the explanatory detail the public declaration lacked. When the district court later asked for an update because of the passage of time, the FBI’s Bender declaration confirmed the continuing pendency. The D.C. Circuit recognized the danger of treating a terse, standalone statement that an investigation “remains ongoing” as adequate under CREW, but it held that the Bender declaration need not be evaluated in a vacuum: if earlier (and contemporaneous sealed) submissions already established the scope and existence of an ongoing investigation, an update showing no change can be relatively concise.

For readers, the practical rule is this: (1) CREW requires more than a conclusory formula; (2) agencies can meet CREW by combining public attestations with ex parte disclosures that give the court the needed factual predicate; and (3) when a court requires an update, a short confirmatory declaration may suffice if the earlier record already provided the substantive detail. When applying these principles to new facts, consider the temporal gap between the agency’s showing and the court’s decision—long gaps will attract closer scrutiny—and evaluate whether the administrative record includes facts (even in sealed form) establishing a realistic prospect of enforcement proceedings.

## Interference rationale: tipping off targets and revealing scope of ongoing investigations

One paradigmatic reason why disclosure of law-enforcement records can interfere with proceedings is that it would “tip off” targets, allowing them to evade law enforcement, destroy evidence, or adapt their behavior. Courts frequently accept “tipping off” as a legitimate interference rationale in Exemption 7(A) cases, and Farahi reiterates that point. The public Hardy declaration explained that disclosure could reveal the scope of the investigation and tip off subjects; the sealed declarations provided further context making that explanation credible.

To apply this rationale to new situations, evaluate the content of the records and the nature of the investigation. Disclosure is most likely to interfere when the records reveal investigatory targets, lines of inquiry, investigative techniques, timing, or any details that would enable suspects to modify their conduct. Conversely, disclosure of purely historical summaries of concluded investigations, where no follow-on enforcement action is reasonably anticipated, is less likely to raise valid “tipping off” concerns. The court in Farahi accepted the FBI’s claim that the investigation remained broad and prospective in nature (including possible further prosecutions or intelligence actions), and so “tipping off” remained a realistic harm.

Importantly, the government must anchor the “tipping off” assertion in facts—either public facts or sealed explanatory material—so that the court can determine whether interference is reasonably expected. CREW warns against rubber-stamp claims; Farahi shows how sufficiently detailed sealed declarations can satisfy the court.

## Categorical withholdings and the requester’s forfeiture of category-specific challenges

When agencies withhold records by category (e.g., “investigative reports,” “interview memoranda,” “attachments”), courts require the categories to be “sufficiently distinct” so that the court can determine whether the claimed exemptions properly apply to each category. Cases such as Gallant and CREW articulate that principle. In Farahi, the FBI identified categories and justified withholding across them. Farahi challenged the breadth of those categories on appeal but did not articulate specific arguments about particular categories in a way that allowed the court to evaluate overbreadth. The D.C. Circuit therefore treated those arguments as forfeited under Khine.

The doctrinal lesson is double: (1) agencies should draft categories with clarity—each category should be intelligible and tied to a plausible exemption rationale—so the court can test the withholding; and (2) requesters must preserve and press specific category-by-category challenges with record evidence or reasoned legal argument. A generalized complaint that categories are “overbroad” is unlikely to prevail if the requester never shows why a specific category includes non-exempt material or how the agency’s rationale fails for a subset of documents. For students and litigators, the practical takeaway is to be granular: if you represent a FOIA plaintiff, identify specific records or categories that appear non-exempt and explain why; if you represent the agency, be prepared to show how each category maps to exemption criteria.

## Segregability: reliance on ex parte submissions and the “meaningful information” standard

Even when Exemption 7(A) covers a record, FOIA requires disclosure of any “reasonably segregable” non-exempt portions. The governing standard (Mead Data and later cases) is pragmatic: courts require that agencies disclose “meaningful information,” not isolated words or trivial items, and they may accept agency affidavits that explain why the non-exempt material is inextricably intertwined with exempt material. Farahi reiterates that if non-exempt material is scattered line-by-line throughout documents, removing it may not be “reasonably segregable” because the resulting redactions would not yield meaningful information and could themselves risk disclosure of sensitive context.

In Farahi the district court relied on Bender’s sealed ex parte declaration to conclude that the agency had satisfied its segregability obligation. The court was permitted to do so: a sealed declaration can describe, to the judge, why any non-exempt text is either trivial or so interwoven with exempt material that redaction would either be infeasible or would itself disclose protected information. The D.C. Circuit emphasized that a party who does not oppose ex parte filings cannot complain on appeal that the district court relied on sealed material. The court also explained that ex parte submissions are sometimes the only way to evaluate claims without revealing the very information the agency seeks to protect.

For students, the key principles for segregability analysis are: (1) the agency bears the burden to show what is reasonably segregable; (2) it may meet that burden with particularized affidavits, including sealed material where necessary; (3) courts assess whether the disclosed portion(s) would be “meaningful” to the requester; and (4) where non-exempt bits are intimately bound up with exempt content, courts may accept that segregability is not practicable. Practically, litigants should identify specific examples of allegedly non-exempt material, and agencies should provide concrete explanations—preferably examples in camera if publicly disclosable descriptions would compromise interests—showing why redactions would not meaningfully inform the public or would risk harm.

## Appellate posture: de novo review, deference in national security, and affirmance

FOIA summary-judgment decisions are reviewed de novo on appeal. The agency bears the burden of proving that the exemptions apply. Nonetheless, the D.C. Circuit has explained that courts should afford a “fair measure of deference” to the FBI in evaluating Exemption 7 claims because the FBI specializes in law enforcement; that deference is especially pronounced in national-security cases. Cases such as Campbell and CNSS reflect that doctrine: the court will not substitute its evaluation of investigative priorities for the FBI’s, but the agency must still supply sufficiently detailed factual predicates in its declarations.

Farahi typifies this balance. The D.C. Circuit reviewed de novo but accepted the district court’s in camera reliance on sealed declarations and credited the agency’s public and ex parte attestations as establishing both the law-enforcement nexus and the pendency of enforcement proceedings. The court also noted that the district court, after reconsidering due to time lapse, had required and received updated declarations. The appellate court affirmed, emphasizing both the agency’s burden and the permissible deference to agency expertise—especially where national-security implications justify limited public disclosure.

For litigation strategy, understand that appellate review will examine the sufficiency of the agency’s factual assertions and the district court’s explanation for relying on sealed material, but the court will not require the agency to publicly produce the very details that the agency says are sensitive. Practitioners should prepare record-level support (including in camera material if necessary) to withstand de novo appellate scrutiny.

## Practical lessons and application to new fact patterns

Farahi offers several practice-oriented rules students and practitioners should internalize:

- Timeliness and updates matter: courts may treat older declarations as stale and require updates. When a FOIA case lingers, agencies should refresh factual attestations; requesters should press for updates if they suspect a change in enforcement posture.

- Use of sealed declarations is permissible and often necessary in national-security or ongoing-investigation contexts, but reliance on sealed material reduces the plaintiff’s opportunities to rebut claims. Parties should anticipate that and plan accordingly.

- Specificity is required: although courts afford deference to the FBI, that deference is not blanket. Agencies should supply a factual nexus to law enforcement and an explanation of how disclosure would interfere with specific or plausible future proceedings.

- Segregability requires meaningful disclosure: agencies must disclose non-exempt information that is reasonably segregable in a way that yields meaningful content; trivial excision of single words is not sufficient.

- Categorical withholdings must be intelligible: an agency’s categories should be discrete and explained; requesters should press specific, targeted challenges instead of general assertions of overbreadth.

When applying these lessons to new facts, structure your analysis around the statutory elements and the relevant precedents: first determine whether the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes (Pratt/Campbell); then ask whether the government has shown the existence of pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings (CREW/Juarez); next examine the asserted interference rationale (e.g., tipping off), looking for factual support; then test the agency’s segregability showing (Mead Data and offspring); and finally evaluate whether the agency’s categorical descriptions permit meaningful judicial review. If an agency relies on sealed material, courts will often review that material in camera, but the party challenging the withholding should be prepared to point to public facts that undermine the agency’s claims or to identify discrete categories or documents that appear non-exempt.

Conclusion

Farahi v. FBI illustrates how Exemption 7(A) operates in the context of counterterrorism investigations: courts are willing to withhold large volumes of investigative material where the agency supplies a factual nexus to law enforcement, establishes the existence of ongoing or reasonably anticipated proceedings, and explains why non-exempt material is not reasonably segregable. The case also exemplifies the procedural dynamics of FOIA litigation—staleness concerns, updated declarations, and the judicious use of sealed ex parte filings—and confirms the practical and doctrinal boundary between judicial review and deference to national-security and law-enforcement expertise.
