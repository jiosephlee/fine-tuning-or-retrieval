Title: Blog about the opinion: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation

# Behind Closed Doors: Ex Parte Declarations, Waiver, and FOIA’s National‑Security Exception in Practice

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in Farahi v. FBI is less about a new gloss on Exemption 7(A) than it is about process — who gets to test an agency’s secrecy claims and on what record. The panel affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the FBI after the agency supplemented its public declarations with sealed, ex parte submissions. Significantly, the court endorsed the district court’s reliance on those sealed materials to find both that enforcement proceedings were “pending or reasonably anticipated” and that no meaningful, non‑exempt information was reasonably segregable. Farahi thus underscores the continued tolerance, at least in national‑security and counterterrorism FOIA disputes, for ex parte submissions as the means by which courts evaluate exemptions that would themselves be revealed by public explanation.

That tolerance has real stakes. Ex parte submissions can accomplish two things at once: they enable courts to adjudicate claims without publishing the closely held operational facts that could harm investigations, and they deprive requesters of an adversarial testing ground for the agency’s assertions. Farahi illustrates how courts will reconcile those tensions and highlights practical steps both requesters and agencies should take to protect their interests.

## The short procedural story

Foad Farahi, whose immigration record included allegations tying him to terrorism‑related activity, sought his FBI file under FOIA. The FBI located a massive set of potentially responsive records and, after years of review, withheld most of them under Exemption 7(A). The agency supported its summary‑judgment motions with a public declaration and with sealed, ex parte declarations from FBI officials. When the district court — concerned about the passage of time — asked the FBI to update its assertions about whether investigations remained ongoing, the FBI filed a terse public update plus a fuller ex parte submission. The district court, having reviewed the sealed material, concluded that the investigations remained ongoing and that meaningful segregable material was not reasonably separable; it granted judgment for the FBI. The D.C. Circuit affirmed, noting both the substantive sufficiency of the government’s showing and the procedural posture: Farahi had not opposed the government’s request to make parts of its filings ex parte.

## What the court said (and relied upon)

The panel applied familiar principles. Exemption 7(A) protects records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes when release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated” (CREW; Juarez). The D.C. Circuit reiterated substantial deference to the FBI’s characterization of its files as law‑enforcement records and to its judgment about interference where national‑security investigations are implicated (Campbell; CNSS). On segregability, the court accepted that densely interspersed, sensitive material may make disclosure of isolated words or phrases meaningless and therefore not “reasonably segregable” (Mead; Perioperative Services).

Two procedural points are critical to the panel’s decision. First, the FBI’s initial public and sealed declarations did the heavy lifting, and the later, brief public confirmation was treated as an update rather than an attempt to establish pendency anew. Second, Farahi did not oppose the district court’s order allowing sealed, ex parte filings; the panel treated that failure as fatal to his contention that the court should not have relied on the sealed material. The panel expressly invoked Perioperative Services for the proposition that ex parte submissions may be “inherent” when public reasoning would itself reveal the secrets at issue.

## Doctrinal footing for ex parte review — and its limits

Farahi sits comfortably on established precedents that permit courts to accept classified or sensitive ex parte submissions in FOIA cases. The doctrinal rationale has two prongs. Substantively, a court must be able to test the agency’s factual assertions (e.g., that an investigation is ongoing and would be harmed by disclosure) but doing so in public might itself disclose the very operational details that the agency seeks to protect. Procedurally, when a requester does not object to ex parte procedures — and when the sealed declarations are sufficiently detailed — courts have treated sealed filings as permissible and sometimes indispensable tools for adjudication.

That said, the decision also reflects limits that the D.C. Circuit has pressed in other contexts. Blanket, conclusory assertions are insufficient; public declarations must establish a rational nexus to law‑enforcement duties, and sealed declarations must give the court reasonable specificity to evaluate harm and segregability (see Campbell, CREW, Juarez). The court’s willingness to rely on sealed materials therefore depends on the quality of the sealed submission and the context of the case. Farahi’s outcome turned in part on the fact that the agency’s sealed declarations were not the only record — there were prior detailed public and ex parte declarations and a factual context from immigration proceedings that corroborated the FBI’s claims.

## The adversarial deficit and accountability concerns

Ex parte review inevitably produces an adversarial deficit. When requesters cannot see or rebut the factual foundations of the agency’s claim, a core function of adversarial litigation — testing credibility, pointing to omissions, offering alternative readings of the evidence — is constrained. Courts and agencies have long recognized this tension and attempted to mitigate it. The D.C. Circuit has required detailed affidavits and has permitted in camera review and other protective mechanisms to preserve the integrity of judicial fact‑finding without public disclosure.

Farahi demonstrates how procedural choices by requesters can compound the deficit. The panel noted that Farahi did not oppose the FBI’s motion to file sealed ex parte declarations; that concession foreclosed a later attack on the practice. In other words, even where courts accept ex parte submissions as sometimes necessary, requesters who acquiesce in that path forego later procedural arguments and limit their appellate options. Moreover, courts should be conscious of the cumulative institutional effect: routine reliance on sealed material without meaningful public explanation risks eroding FOIA’s transparency function.

## Practical strategies for requesters

Requesters facing national‑security FOIA denials should approach ex parte procedures with care. If an agency moves to file sealed declarations, requesters who are genuinely concerned about the sensitivity of specifics can still preserve adversarial testing by timely objecting to purely ex parte procedures and proposing alternatives. A requester should consider asking the court for in camera review of representative samples of the withheld records, for the appointment of a special master or amicus with the requisite security clearance, or for the production of a redacted but informative public declaration that outlines factual bases without operational specifics. Where feasible, requesters should press for a particularized showing that the sealed material is both necessary and sufficiently detailed, and they should preserve the record by formally opposing ex parte treatment if they intend to challenge it on appeal.

Requesters should also press on segregability with specificity. Generic assertions that there “must be” segregable material in a voluminous file are unlikely to prevail. Instead, requesters should identify categories or document types that, in their view, ought to be non‑exempt and ask the court to order the agency to justify, category by category, why those materials are intertwined with exempt content.

## Practical strategies for agencies

Agencies that need to rely on sealed submissions can strengthen both their litigation position and public legitimacy by providing as much non‑sensitive information as possible in the public record. A robust public declaration that sets forth the legal framework, the general nature and nexus of the investigation to law‑enforcement duties, and a categorical explanation of withheld material reduces the sense that ex parte filings are a black box. Agencies should use sealed declarations to supply operational detail that would be damaging if publicized, but those sealed filings ought to be detailed and specific so courts can meaningfully evaluate harm and segregability.

Agencies should also be responsive to district court questions designed to limit stale or conclusory assertions. Farahi’s outcome turned partly on the agency’s willingness to update its representations about the pendency of investigations and to explain why segregability was impracticable. Where possible, agencies should anticipate judicial scrutiny by preparing category‑by‑category rationales and by proposing less intrusive alternatives (sample in camera submissions, redacted summaries, disclosure to cleared counsel or special masters) when full ex parte treatment might be contested.

## Downstream implications

Farahi will likely be cited as a practical exemplar for courts and agencies navigating FOIA disputes that touch national security: sealed, ex parte submissions remain a permissible and sometimes necessary part of the toolkit, and a requester’s failure to contest their use can be dispositive of later procedural challenges. But the decision does not erase the underlying judicial responsibilities. Courts remain obligated to demand specificity, to guard against conclusory tropes, and to require meaningful public justification when secrecy is not strictly indispensible. For litigants and courts alike, Farahi is a reminder that process matters: who asks for secrecy, who agrees to it, and how judges scrutinize the sealed record will often determine whether FOIA’s transparency promise is vindicated or sealed shut.

## Conclusion

Farahi reaffirms the D.C. Circuit’s pragmatic accommodation of secrecy in FOIA litigation involving counterterrorism and national security. It also delivers a clear, if subtle, lesson to requesters: procedural concessions matter. The ruling should prompt thoughtful litigation planning on both sides. Requesters should preserve the record and press for alternatives to unilateral ex parte submissions; agencies should provide as much public detail as safely possible while using sealed filings to supply the specifics courts need. The balance between secrecy and accountability is delicate — Farahi demonstrates how courts will mediate that balance, but it also shows that litigants can shape the outcome long before the judge looks behind the curtain.
