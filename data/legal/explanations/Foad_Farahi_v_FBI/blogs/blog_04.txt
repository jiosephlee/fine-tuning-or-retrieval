# What Counts as a "Proceeding" Under 7(A)? The Elastic Standard of "Pending or Reasonably Anticipated"

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit's decision in Farahi v. FBI (No. 23-5243, Sept. 30, 2025) is the latest installment in a long-running line of FOIA jurisprudence about Exemption 7(A): when may an agency withhold records because their disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings”? At stake is the tension between FOIA’s disclosure mandate and a law-enforcement interest in keeping ongoing or prospective investigations from being compromised. Farahi matters because the court reaffirms two important (and related) doctrines that shape that balance: (1) ongoing investigative activity counts as a “proceeding” for Exemption 7(A) purposes, and the government may show the existence of such proceedings largely by agency attestation; and (2) courts may rely on ex parte, sealed declarations to resolve both the pendency/anticipation inquiry and segregability questions when national-security or investigative sensitivities make public explanation infeasible.

Those holdings can make it significantly easier for agencies—especially the FBI—to resist disclosure of large volumes of records without revealing the very facts FOIA requesters seek. For requesters and courts committed to transparency, Farahi thus raises familiar (but acute) worries about how robust the government’s evidentiary showing must be, and how much public-facing factual particularity FOIA plaintiffs can realistically demand.

## What the court did

The facts are straightforward and stark: Farahi, long the subject of immigration proceedings, sought his FBI file—some 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. The FBI withheld most of the material under Exemption 7(A), supported by a public declaration (David Hardy) and an ex parte, sealed declaration elaborating on investigative details. After an initial denial without prejudice (prompted by the passage of time), the FBI submitted an update (a brief, public attestation from Joseph Bender plus additional ex parte material) confirming that relevant investigations remained “ongoing.” The district court, having reviewed the sealed submissions, concluded the FBI had shown the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes, that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings, and that little or nothing was meaningfully segregable. The D.C. Circuit affirmed.

In doing so, the court reiterated settled principles: Exemption 7(A) applies only to records “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” and the “proceedings” whose interference Exemption 7(A) protects must be “pending or reasonably anticipated,” a standard the court traces to CREW v. DOJ (746 F.3d 1082 (D.C. Cir. 2014)) and its predecessors. The court emphasized deference to the FBI’s judgments, particularly in national-security or counterterrorism contexts, and rested its conclusion on the combination of the public declarations (which attested to the existence of ongoing investigations) together with sealed, ex parte declarations that supplied the factual detail the public record could not.

On segregability, the court accepted the FBI’s explanation—again largely in sealed form—that any nonexempt material was “interspersed line-by-line” such that meaningful disclosure would still jeopardize the investigation or reveal the exempt material. The court invoked long-standing precedents (Mead Data; Juarez) that permit non-disclosure where nonexempt portions are inextricably intertwined with exempt material and where agency affidavits show with reasonable specificity why further segregation is impracticable.

## The “proceeding” question and the elasticity of “pending or reasonably anticipated”

The doctrinal core of Farahi is the court’s treatment of what counts as a “proceeding.” Two features of the opinion deserve careful attention.

First, the court reaffirms that ongoing criminal (or counterterrorism) investigations themselves qualify as Exemption 7(A) proceedings. That is not new—this Circuit has said so before—but the point matters because it means that agencies need not await an actual charging decision, grand jury, or other formal step before claiming that disclosure could interfere with a prospective enforcement action. The court cites Juarez and CREW for the proposition that “so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence, Exemption 7(A) applies.” That is an expansive view of what it means to be “pending or reasonably anticipated”: a sufficiently active, evidentiary investigation is enough.

Second, and more consequentially, Farahi continues the D.C. Circuit’s practical deference to agency attestations about whether proceedings are pending or reasonably anticipated. CREW warned against “vague” and conclusory assertions of “ongoing” investigations that lack detail. Yet Farahi illustrates how that qualification is often met in practice: the court accepted that an initial, more detailed declaration plus a later, terse confirmatory attestation sufficed, because the later filing was an update, not the sole basis for the government’s claim. In short, a public declaration that describes the investigation’s nature and attests to ongoing enforcement activity, supplemented by sealed ex parte material giving the court necessary specifics, will usually pass muster.

That approach is defensible in many circumstances. The obvious pragmatic problem is that public specificity about investigative targets, methods, or evidence would itself risk the harm Exemption 7(A) seeks to prevent. The court’s solution—allow the agency to provide sensitive detail ex parte to the judge—is a long-standing accommodation balancing transparency and security. But it does vest considerable weight in agency submissions (and in judges’ secret review), and may leave requesters and the public without a robust way to test whether the agency truly faces a concrete prospect of enforcement or is simply invoking Exemption 7(A) to shield embarrassing or politically sensitive material.

The upshot is an elastic “pending or reasonably anticipated” standard: an agency that can show (publicly or in camera) that it is actively gathering evidence, and that a formal enforcement step might reasonably follow, will typically be able to claim Exemption 7(A). Absent egregious governmental overreach or a public record that contradicts the agency’s claims, courts are likely to defer.

## Foreseeable-harm, evidentiary burdens, and ex parte submissions

Farahi sits at the intersection of two related evidentiary questions: how strong must the government’s showing be that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere” (the foreseeable-harm standard), and how much of that showing must be in the public record? The opinion subtly lowers the practical bar for public-facing detail by validating reliance on sealed, ex parte declarations for the hard facts. The court explicitly recognizes that ex parte submissions “handicap” FOIA plaintiffs, but treats that handicap as “inherent” where disclosure of the very materials sought would undermine the investigation. The court does not create a new legal test allowing conclusory assertions in all cases, but its willingness to accept relatively brief public updates (so long as detailed ex parte material exists) tends to reduce the observable burden on the government.

From the requester’s vantage point, this is worrying. CREW’s admonition about vague assertions remains good law, but Farahi illustrates how agencies can meet the CREW standard while still keeping nearly all operative factual content off the public docket. That outcome is arguably consistent with FOIA’s statutory architecture—the Act contemplates in-camera review and authorizes exemptions that protect law enforcement—but it is nonetheless a practical constraint on transparency.

Judges thus remain the gatekeepers: their in-camera review must be sufficiently searching to ensure the agency’s withheld-material claim is not a pretext. Yet in many FOIA cases, the court’s review occurs behind seals and protective orders, which offers no external accountability. Farahi implicitly trusts district judges (and the D.C. Circuit on review) to perform that function, but the reputational and institutional pressures on judges to grant deference in national-security and counterterrorism contexts are real.

## Segregability and the “inextricably intertwined” problem

Farahi also underscores how segregability analysis can become practically moot where the agency shows that nonexempt material is diffused throughout records in a way that disclosure would itself disclose exempt information. The court relied on precedent permitting the withholding of documents in toto when meaningful segregability is impracticable—again pointing to the Mead Data “intertwined” concept and Juarez. That doctrine is doctrinally solid: FOIA requires disclosure of information, not isolated words; revealing snippets can itself reveal their context.

But the effect is that huge swaths of records may be kept secret as “not reasonably segregable” even when an imaginative or resource-intensive redaction project might yield some releasable material. The court’s reasoning reflects a practical balancing: the marginal public value of releasing stranded words or phrases is low relative to the incremental risk to a live investigation. For transparency advocates, the remedy is institutional: courts should continue to insist that agencies make a detailed, document-by-document showing that meaningful redaction cannot yield public information without harm, and should scrutinize any bare assertion that releasable content is “randomly interspersed” rather than systematically separable.

## Downstream effects

Farahi will be invoked in future FOIA fights—especially in national-security and counterterrorism contexts—to justify withholding of voluminous files where investigations are ongoing or plausibly prospective. Expect several practical consequences.

First, agencies will be encouraged to frontload a solid public declaration explaining the investigation’s nature and the grounds on which disclosure would interfere, then use sealed, detailed submissions to supply supporting facts. That approach reduces the public-facing showing required while preserving judicial review.

Second, FOIA plaintiffs will increasingly press procedural challenges: demanding in-court redaction demonstrations, contesting the sufficiency of categorical descriptions, or arguing that the passage of time defeats any realistic claim of an anticipated proceeding. Courts will face recurring methodological questions—how much time can elapse before an “ongoing” investigation becomes stale? Farahi suggests that a subsequent, even terse confirmation of continuity will be enough when initial declarations were detailed; plaintiffs will need to be ready to point to concrete indicia of investigative dormancy to succeed.

Third, Farahi reinforces a broader trend of deference in national-security-adjacent FOIA litigation, which may tilt the balance toward nondisclosure in cases implicating terrorism, espionage, and intelligence-gathering—even when the requested records relate to individuals like Farahi who are litigating immigration claims and may have professional or reputational interests in disclosure.

## Where the tension remains

Farahi does not and cannot erase the underlying tension of Exemption 7(A): the need to prevent interference with legitimate law-enforcement activity versus FOIA’s core purpose of public access. The D.C. Circuit has again articulated a framework that preserves judges’ ability to inspect sensitive materials in secret, while giving agencies a workable path to protect ongoing investigations. That approach is defensible on categorical and pragmatic grounds, but it presses courts to be careful, candid, and methodical in their in-camera reviews. The real question going forward is institutional: will courts assertively police agency assertions in sealed filings, or will the default of deference—especially in terrorism-related matters—harden into a near-permanent shield?

For FOIA practitioners, the lesson is pragmatic. To challenge 7(A) with any hope of success, a requester should: (1) force the agency to produce a detailed public declaration where possible; (2) challenge the scope and specificity of asserted categories and segregability on the public record; (3) marshal public evidence of investigative dormancy or lack of reasonable anticipation of enforcement; and (4) where appropriate, litigate the sufficiency of in-camera detail (asking courts to describe, at a minimum on a non-confidential basis, the nature of the sealed showing). Those steps are uphill but not futile; Farahi accepts sealed submissions but does not license carte blanche.

In the end Farahi is a reminder that Exemption 7(A) remains an elastic tool. It is elastic by necessity: law-enforcement secrecy sometimes cannot be reconciled with full public disclosure. But that elasticity is only legitimate if courts remain vigilant—examining sealed justifications closely, demanding particularized showings where the public record permits, and refusing to allow the exemption to become a reflexive blanket against accountability.
