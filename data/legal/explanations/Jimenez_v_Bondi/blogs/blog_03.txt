# Unable or Unwilling, or Just Unreported? The Tenth Circuit Tightens the Reporting Expectation

## Why this case matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi—denying asylum to a Colombian family who received threats and some physical intimidation—sends a clear signal about how this circuit will treat the “unable or unwilling” prong of asylum law when the applicant did not make robust attempts to involve local authorities. At its core, the court’s analysis reinforces a practical rule: if an applicant does not show that she reported persecution to government authorities (and that reporting would have been futile or dangerous), the BIA’s finding that the home government could have protected the applicant is likely to survive review. That emphasis tightens the evidentiary expectation for many asylum seekers, particularly in contexts of organized crime or quasi-state actors where distrust of police is common.

Practitioners and adjudicators should take note. The opinion foregrounds two pieces of evidence that sufficed to defeat the applicants’ claim: (1) the petitioners’ limited steps to seek protection (essentially a couple of conversations with local officers), and (2) affirmative government action against the criminal enterprise plaintiff’s client encountered—namely prior searches and seizures at the property linked to the persecutors. The combination allowed the BIA and the Tenth Circuit to conclude that the Colombian government was neither unwilling nor unable to control the persecutors, and that internal relocation would be feasible. The decision refines, and in practice constrains, the contours of the futility-and-danger exception in this circuit.

## What the court held and how it reasoned

The family’s claims rested on allegations of threats, an assault at gunpoint, and an incident where a man accosted the petitioner’s young son—conduct they attributed to a narco-trafficking leader (“Don Rafa”) and his associates. The IJ and BIA found the family credible but concluded: (1) the harms did not amount to past persecution; (2) the threats were motivated more by the petitioner’s interference with Don Rafa’s economic interests than by their political activity; (3) the Colombian government was not shown to be unable or unwilling to protect them; and (4) internal relocation was reasonable.

On the government-protection point, the court framed the inquiry around two central facts it deemed dispositive: whether the government tried to stop the persecutors and whether the applicant sought or was offered protection. The Tenth Circuit emphasized precedent that notice to authorities matters because, without notice, the record cannot show how the police would have responded. Although the petitioners spoke with two officers who advised them not to “seek trouble,” the court viewed those isolated interactions as insufficient to establish systemic police failure. The court also pointed to evidence that Colombian authorities had previously attempted “several searches and seizures” at the property linked to Don Rafa, which undercut the claim that the persecutors were aligned with or protected by the state. Because the petitioners did not pursue wider reporting—through other police channels, prosecutors, or leveraging the petitioner’s own family contacts in law enforcement—the court held the BIA’s finding that the government could have protected them was supported by substantial evidence.

## Analysis: a tightening, not a new test

The Tenth Circuit’s opinion does not announce a new legal test: it applies longstanding principles about reporting and futility. But it does sharpen the evidentiary tilt against applicants who provide only limited evidence of attempts to engage the state. Two interpretive points matter.

First, the decision underscores the circuit’s insistence on a fact-specific showing that reporting would have been futile or dangerous. The futility/danger exception remains available, but the petitioner bears a meaningful burden to either (a) produce evidence that reporting would have been ineffective (for example, objective evidence of collusion between persecutors and authorities), or (b) show that reporting itself would have put the applicant at immediate risk. Here, the court found neither: the record included at least some official action against the property, and the petitioners did not show systemic collusion or that the officers they contacted were representative of the government’s response.

Second, the opinion elevates corroborating government activity as a counterweight to the applicant’s narrative. Evidence that officials had investigated the very property connected to the persecutors provided the court a tangible reason to credit the government’s ability to act—a contrast to cases where the state’s absence from the scene is the dominant fact. In other words, affirmative, documented government efforts can be a powerful component of an IJ/BIA credibility and protection analysis.

The Tenth Circuit’s approach is defensible: asylum law contemplates, as a matter of fairness and feasibility, that governments be given an opportunity to protect their citizens unless doing so would have been pointless or perilous. But the decision also raises practical concerns. In many contexts of organized crime or endemic corruption, a petitioner’s reluctance to report stems precisely from reasonable fear that the report will bring retaliation, or from a realistic belief that officers are compromised. Requiring compelling evidence of systemic corruption may be a classic catch-22: applicants who cannot safely document corruption for fear of exposure will struggle to meet the evidentiary bar.

## How this compares across circuits

Circuits vary in how aggressively they treat the reporting expectation and the futility exception. The Tenth Circuit’s emphasis on affirmative governmental action and on the need for more than an isolated, unhelpful officer remark aligns it with the Seventh Circuit’s skepticism of one-off negative interactions (see, e.g., Osorio-Morales), and with decisions that require systemic evidence of state complicity rather than anecdotal police apathy. Other circuits—particularly the Ninth—have sometimes been more receptive to a broader set of indicators of futility or danger, such as demonstrated patterns of corruption in a region, credible reports of collusion between local police and organized crime, or explicit testimony showing that reporting would have risked immediate retaliation.

Jimenez thus situates the Tenth Circuit toward the stricter end of the spectrum: an applicant cannot rely solely on subjective fear or limited, localized refusals from officers to demonstrate that reporting would have been futile. That divergence means asylum strategies that work in, say, Ninth Circuit filings may need supplementation in the Tenth Circuit.

## Practitioner takeaways: documenting the inability or unwillingness to protect

For attorneys representing asylum seekers from countries with organized crime, corruption, or weak rule of law, Jimenez is a practical reminder to build the record on both process and background evidence. Key strategies include:

- Document attempts to notify authorities: contemporaneous police reports, copies of any written complaints, docket numbers, names and ranks of officers contacted, dates, and, where possible, signed receipts. If reporting was done informally, memorialize details as soon as feasible and corroborate with witnesses.

- Preserve contemporaneous evidence of danger in reporting: if an applicant feared reporting because an assailant threatened retaliation for contacting police, get that testimony on the record with specifics (who said what, when, in front of whom). Corroborate with witnesses, medical records, or contemporaneous communications.

- Collect objective country- and region-specific evidence of corruption or collusion: credible NGO or U.S. government reports (Human Rights Watch, Amnesty, State Department country reports), local press accounts documenting police-collusion incidents, or academic studies tying criminal groups to state actors. In Jimenez the court gave weight to evidence of the government’s actions against the property; the converse—evidence of government inaction or complicity—should be developed with equal care.

- Use professional and institutional contacts: where the applicant or family members have ties to law enforcement or prosecutors (as in Jimenez), trace how those contacts were used, whether higher-level reporting channels were pursued, and why those channels were or were not viable. If the applicant sought help from a local officer who shrugged them off, attempt to show whether that officer’s response reflected broader practice (e.g., multiple citizens reporting similar experiences).

- Consider strategic declarations from experts or former officials: affidavits from Latin America country experts, former prosecutors, or police accountability organizations can paint a picture of systematic problems that go beyond the applicant’s immediate neighborhood.

- If reporting is truly futile, show why: affidavits that explain the risk of retaliation tied to reporting (e.g., “in this municipality, those who report are subsequently attacked”) or documentary evidence that others who reported suffered reprisals can be persuasive.

Finally, where feasible, counsel should make a record of internal-relocation feasibility contemporaneously: lay out where the applicant could go, whether they have family there, and what steps would be required to relocate safely, because the government can often rebut a fear-of-return claim through relocation evidence as the court did here.

## Broader implications and likely downstream effects

Jimenez will likely produce three predictable effects. First, asylum applicants in the Tenth Circuit—particularly those fleeing organized criminals or non-state actors—will face a higher hurdle unless they can either show that reporting would have been futile/dangerous or that the government was systemically compromised. Second, immigration courts and the BIA will lean on similar fact patterns (e.g., any documented police activity against perpetrators) to deny claims, framing those exercises as evidence of the state’s capacity to protect. Third, attorneys operating in the Tenth Circuit will increasingly front-load detailed reporting evidence and country-specific corroboration into administrative records and initial asylum applications.

At the doctrinal level, the opinion reinforces the centrality of the “unable or unwilling” inquiry to the asylum claim and underscores the interplay between credibility, corroboration, and standard of review. It also reminds us that the BIA and courts will treat governmental action—or its absence—as a pivotal, often outcome-determinative fact. The potential risk is that applicants who reasonably avoid formal reporting because fear is rational will be asked to produce an evidentiary record they cannot safely create. That tension between rational fear and evidentiary demands will be fertile ground for future litigation and may prompt calls for more nuanced guidelines on what kinds of background evidence suffice to establish futility when direct reporting is dangerous.

## Conclusion

Jimenez v. Bondi does not rewrite asylum law, but it signals a narrower pathway for applicants in the Tenth Circuit who rely on the futility-and-danger exception to explain why they did not seek state protection. The decision places a premium on contemporaneous reporting efforts, corroborating evidence, and objective indicators of state capacity or incapacity. Practitioners should respond by documenting reporting attempts and by assembling robust country- and region-level materials showing why reporting would have been pointless or perilous. For courts, the opinion reflects a familiar judicial impulse to require some demonstrable effort to engage state remedies unless the record convincingly shows that such efforts would have been futile or dangerous. The practical consequence is a heightened evidentiary bar—one that will shape asylum litigation strategy across the Tenth Circuit going forward.
