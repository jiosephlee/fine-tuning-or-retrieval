# Substantial Evidence as the Silent Decider: Deference and Record-Building in Asylum Appeals

## Why this case matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi (Oct. 7, 2025) is not a dramatic new doctrinal pivot. But it is a clear, practical reminder of how often—and how decisively—immigration appeals lose at the petition-for-review stage because of the substantial-evidence standard and related doctrines of deference. For practitioners, the opinion is a compact lesson in what arguments typically flounder on appeal and what kinds of record-building can make the difference between reversal and the court shrugging that “substantial evidence” supports the Board’s view.

The panel denied review of the BIA’s affirmance of an IJ’s asylum and withholding denials for a Colombian family, emphasizing three core points that recur across asylum jurisprudence: (1) threats and emotional distress alone rarely amount to past persecution; (2) mixed-motive claims fail when the record supports a predominant non-protected motive; and (3) applicants who do not meaningfully seek or document state protection undercut an “unable or unwilling” showing. Layered over those substantive holdings is the appellate court’s deference to the BIA—and its willingness to consult the IJ’s fuller reasoning under Uanreroro when a single-member BIA order affirms. For lawyers preparing asylum cases, the decision is a primer on appellate vulnerability and on practical steps to shore up the record.

## What the court did and how it reasoned

The petitioners (a Colombian family) presented a fact pattern familiar in asylum litigation: repeated threats, an assault on a factory employee looking for them, an incident with their child at school, and an alleged nexus to politics because of campaign activity. The IJ and BIA found the family credible but concluded (a) the cumulative harm did not rise to past persecution, (b) any harm was motivated primarily by a private actor’s economic and criminal concerns (not the family’s political opinion), (c) the Colombian government was not shown to be unable or unwilling to protect them, and (d) internal relocation was reasonable.

The Tenth Circuit affirmed on substantial-evidence grounds. Key strands of the court’s reasoning:

- Persecution threshold: The panel reiterated that threats, psychological distress, and precautionary measures, without more, generally do not meet the circuit’s definition of persecution. The court cited its precedents—Karki, Ritonga, Brandy and others—underscoring that, even where credible, phone threats and stress-related symptoms often fall short absent more invasive physical harm or sustained, severe mistreatment.

- Nexus and mixed motives: The court emphasized that an applicant must show a protected ground was “at least one central reason” for the harm. Although mixed motives are permissible, the record here—escalation after a property dispute; statements pointing to economic or law-enforcement-related motives; absence of evidence tying earlier campaign-related threats to the same perpetrators—supported the BIA’s conclusion that the persecutor’s predominant motive was retaliation for interfering with criminal enterprise revenue, not political animus.

- Government protection and reporting: Because the applicant did not meaningfully document attempts to seek help beyond unhelpful individual officers and did not use available contacts, the court found substantial evidence supporting the BIA’s conclusion that the government was not shown to be unable or unwilling to protect them. The opinion relied on the principle that notice and opportunity to respond matter; absent reporting or evidence that policing would have been futile or dangerous, the “unable or unwilling” claim was weak.

- Deference and record consultation: Critically for appellate practitioners, the court made clear it was applying the substantial-evidence standard to the BIA’s factual determinations and, because a single-member BIA affirmed the IJ, it was appropriate under Uanreroro to consult the IJ’s more complete explanation. That consultation reinforced the BIA’s findings and limited the court’s ability to overturn them.

## Analytical perspective: deference is the engine that does the real work

Two doctrinal features dominate the outcome: the substantial-evidence standard and the mixed-motive/nexus burden. Substantial-evidence review is inherently backward-looking—asking whether a reasonable adjudicator could reach the BIA’s factual conclusions based on the record—so the appellate court’s role is narrow. Once the IJ and BIA make a factual determination that sits comfortably within precedent, reversal requires either legal error or record evidence so compelling that no reasonable factfinder could agree. Jimenez demonstrates both limitations.

The panel’s willingness to consult the IJ under Uanreroro is also significant. Single-member affirmances are common because they conserve administrative resources. But they can frustrate petitioners on appeal if the BIA’s terse order hides key factual reasoning. Uanreroro allows courts to consult the IJ’s fuller opinion, which in practice often reinforces the agency’s conclusions rather than undermining them. In Jimenez, the IJ’s detailed findings—about the timing of threats, statements by the perpetrators, the limited steps petitioners took with law enforcement, and the government’s prior efforts against the property—filled out a narrative that made reversal unlikely.

The consequence is structural: appellate courts will frequently be the site where deference does the decisive work. Appeals that rely on reweighing credibility, recharacterizing motive from the same facts, or arguing harm is “severe enough” will stumble unless the record undermines the IJ’s factual foundation or reveals legal mistakes in applying asylum standards.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Jimenez does not shift legal standards, but it will likely reinforce a set of practical inferences in future asylum appeals:

- “Threats plus fear” cases are vulnerable. Circuits often treat threats and psychological effects as insufficient alone; Jimenez continues that pattern. Practitioners should treat threat-only fact patterns as high risk unless accompanied by more acute harm, pervasive violence, or sustained coercion.

- Motive evidence matters. Mixed-motive fact patterns require careful proof that a protected ground was a central reason. Evidence that attackers acted to protect criminal revenue or retaliate for property disputes can be dispositive. Thus, showing contemporaneous statements tying harassment explicitly to political activity—or demonstrating the perpetrators have a history of targeting political actors—will be essential.

- Government-protection claims hinge on reporting and systemic evidence. Casual conversations with an indifferent officer won’t suffice; the record must show meaningful attempts to seek protection, evidence that reporting would be ineffective or dangerous, or proof of systemic police corruption or collusion. Country conditions reports and testimony that the applicant attempted to use official channels (and those channels failed) become vital.

- Single-member BIA decisions are not appellate “black boxes.” Uanreroro permits appellate courts to consult the IJ’s opinions, which in many cases will be the more detailed source of the factual findings that courts ultimately uphold.

Taken together, these doctrinal pressures nudge asylum litigation toward a robust contemporaneous record: police reports, medical and psychological documentation, documentary or testimonial evidence of motive, and corroboration of government response—or lack of it.

## Practical tips for practitioners seeking to avoid the “substantial-evidence” dead end

Jimenez offers several concrete lessons about what to develop pre-hearing and in the hearing record.

1. Contemporaneous reporting and documentation. File police reports, request written incident reports, and obtain copies. If the client reasonably believed reporting would be futile or dangerous, document attempts, communications, or credible reasons (e.g., prior known collusion or prior complaints proving ineffective). Notes of conversations with police officers should include names, dates, and the substance of the officers’ advice.

2. Corroborate psychological harm and physical risk. Medical and mental-health records, evaluations from qualified clinicians, and school records showing a child’s panic attacks or changed attendance can make emotional harms more tangible. For physical threats, photographs, contemporaneous written notes, witness statements, and employer testimony (e.g., a coworker who saw the assault at the factory) strengthen the cumulative-harm argument.

3. Build motive evidence. When nexus is contested, contemporaneous statements by the persecutors are gold. Seek third-party witnesses who heard statements like “death to snitches” or “this comes from Don Rafa,” collect any written threats (brochures, text messages), and obtain evidence about the perpetrator’s affiliations, patterns of targeting political actors, or prior acts against party members. Country reports, local press, and nonprofit documentation showing that a particular criminal group targets political activists can shift the mix toward political motive.

4. Demonstrate futile or dangerous reporting where applicable. If reporting would endanger the applicant, obtain testimony or documentation that shows the police are corrupt, or that victims who reported were retaliated against. Documentation from NGOs or media about police collusion can help, but the applicant should also show attempts to seek help or a reasoned fear about doing so.

5. Preserve the IJ record and seek fuller findings. When the BIA issues a single-member affirmance, the IJ opinion can be dispositive on appeal. Push for detailed findings at the IJ level—on nexus, on chronology, on why the government would or would not have been able to help. Cross-examine government witnesses, and ask the IJ to make explicit credibility and factual conclusions in the record.

6. Use corroboration to address relocation. If government evidence shows alternatives in-country exist, challenge their reasonableness with economic, familial, or safety-based proof—employment prospects, family threats in other locales, or logistical barriers to relocation.

7. Anticipate mixed-motive arguments and address them head-on. If there are non-protected motives, explain why the protected motive was central: show timing (e.g., harassment tied to campaigning), the content of threats referencing political activity, or that the persecutor’s animus toward the protected characteristic follows an identifiable pattern.

## Conclusion

Jimenez is a reminder that appellate victory in asylum cases often depends less on rhetorical flourish than on the record’s texture. Substantial-evidence review will routinely sustain the BIA when the IJ’s findings sit comfortably within precedent and when the record lacks contemporaneous reporting, corroboration, or direct motive evidence. Uanreroro’s permission to consult the IJ’s fuller explanation reinforces the lesson: make the IJ record as robust and explicit as possible. For advocates, the takeaway is practical and blunt—anticipate appellate deference at the outset, and build a record that leaves little room for “a reasonable adjudicator” to disagree.
