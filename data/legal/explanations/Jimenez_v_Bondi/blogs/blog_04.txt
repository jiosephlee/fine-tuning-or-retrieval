# Internal Relocation as a Decisive Tool: Short-Term Safety, Family Residence, and Regulatory Reasonableness

Why this matters: In Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez v. Bondi, the Tenth Circuit denied asylum and withholding of removal for a Colombian family whose political activity and an ensuing property dispute drew violent threats. After rejecting past persecution, nexus, and government unwillingness to protect, the court also held — briefly but decisively — that internal relocation within Colombia remained a reasonable option. That conclusion rested in part on two relatively thin factual predicates: (1) the family safely stayed for three weeks at a rural residence in the Bogota countryside before fleeing to the United States, and (2) family members still reside in Colombia without apparent harm. The decision illustrates how courts can lean on safe-relocation doctrine to dispose of asylum claims even where persecution is plausible, and it highlights important doctrinal tensions about what makes relocation “reasonable” under 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i).

The court’s treatment of relocation: The Tenth Circuit’s analysis follows the familiar two-part framework by which the government may rebut an asylum applicant’s claim — either by showing a fundamental change in circumstances or that internal relocation would avoid future persecution. After concluding petitioners failed to establish past persecution or government inability/unwillingness to protect, the court addressed relocation only briefly. It emphasized that (a) relatives still live in Colombia unharmed; (b) Jimenez’s father remains employed in the criminal justice system; and (c) the family had already lived safely with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks. The court also noted that the threats the family received had told them to leave their neighborhood, not the country, and thus were geographically limited in reach. On that record the court found relocation reasonable and feasible, and thus confirmed denial of asylum and withholding (and implicitly disposed of any remaining asylum presumption).

Why the concise treatment is consequential: Relocation is often the government’s most practical defense to asylum claims. The Tenth Circuit’s reliance on relatively modest indicia of safety — a three-week stay and the presence of unharmed relatives — signals a willingness to accept short-term and geographically focused evidence of safety as dispositive of “reasonableness.” That approach can be efficient, but it also raises doctrinal questions about how courts should apply the regulatory factors that govern feasibility and reasonableness.

Doctrinal friction: 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i) and the accompanying caselaw require courts to weigh a range of considerations when determining whether internal relocation is reasonable: the nature of the feared harm, the geography and size of the home country, the applicant’s ability to relocate (including age, health, family ties and economic prospects), whether state protection exists in the proposed area, and any foreseeable hardships that could result from relocation. The Jimenez opinion cites several traditional touchstones — family presence and a prior temporary relocation — but does not delve into many of these other factors. Specifically:

- Economic and administrative burdens. The court mentions Petitioners’ cursory briefing that relocation would impose administrative and economic burdens but does not engage those claims substantively. For families uprooted from urban livelihoods (a factory, local political networks, and potential professional licensure issues), relocation may mean loss of income, hurdles to transfer or recognition of credentials, and the need to reestablish housing and schools. These costs can be particularly acute for single-income or low-margin households. The regulation and many circuits look to such burdens when assessing “reasonableness,” but the Tenth Circuit’s opinion treats them as peripheral given the court’s other findings.

- Duration and representativeness of a safe stay. A three-week stay in the countryside is persuasive on one level — it is direct, contemporaneous evidence that the family could avoid immediate harm by moving — but it is limited as proof of sustained safety. The court accepts the three weeks as evidence that relocation is “feasible,” but does not interrogate whether that short interval reliably predicts long-term safety (e.g., whether the threats were truly localized or whether persecutors could easily follow or surveil the family).

- Child-focused concerns and schooling. Petitioners raised stress and panic attacks in a child, yet the opinion does not consider the practical impact of relocation on the children’s healthcare, schooling continuity, or psychological care. Courts increasingly recognize that relocation carries distinct burdens for minors that can factor into the reasonableness calculus.

- Security reach and perpetrator mobility. The opinion relies on threats that appeared neighborhood-specific. But it does not analyze how organized criminal actors or paramilitaries in Colombia move and whether geography alone insulates potential victims. Given the alleged ties between local narco-trafficking elements and political actors, assessing the credible mobility and reach of the perpetrators is often central to a relocation inquiry.

Practical implications for practitioners and future litigants: Because relocation evidence can decide claims, applicants should build a fuller record on feasibility and reasonableness when opposing the government’s safe-relocation defense. A richer record should, at minimum, include:

- Detailed testimony and documentary evidence about employment, licensure, and earnings history; prospects for employment elsewhere in-country; and costs of relocation (housing, transportation, school enrollment). For professionals like provisional lawyers, evidence about whether licensure or law-student status transfers or is recognized elsewhere is important.

- Longitudinal safety indicators. Short stays matter, but applicants should show sustained safety or explain why a brief safe period nonetheless demonstrates durable safety (for instance, by showing the persecutor’s lack of regional reach or local law enforcement’s effectiveness in the new area).

- Child welfare and education impacts. Medical or school records demonstrating ongoing psychological harm or treatment needs, plus evidence about availability of services in the proposed relocation area, can meaningfully inform the relocation reasonableness analysis.

- Country-condition evidence about perpetrator mobility and local government capacity. Local police responsiveness, investigations after similar threats, and reports about the reach of criminal gangs can frame whether a localized threat is likely to migrate with the target.

- Evidence of futile reporting or systemic corruption if applicants cannot or did not report to authorities; conversely, if prior reporting occurred, documentation of the government’s inadequate response can undercut a relocation defense.

Broader doctrinal stakes and downstream effects: Jimenez exemplifies how courts use relocation doctrine to cabin asylum claims without resolving harder questions about nexus or government complicity. That can be appealing for courts seeking efficient resolution, but it risks turning relocation into a near-per se escape hatch where a minimal showing of temporary safety or family presence will extinguish a claim. Over time, that trend could incentivize more fact-intensive, prophylactic government defenses and place a premium on asylum counsel’s ability to litigate nuanced feasibility facts that go beyond headline allegations of threat.

Conclusion: The Tenth Circuit’s use of relocation in Jimenez underscores the doctrine’s practical potency. The opinion is consistent with the governing regulation, but its succinct treatment leaves open important questions about how courts should balance short-term safe stays, economic and child-centered burdens, and the mobility of persecutors. Practitioners should anticipate this battleground and prepare robust relocation records — not only to rebut government assertions of safety but also to show why temporary refuge or family presence does not equate to a reasonable, sustainable option under 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13.
