# From Campaigning to ‘Snitching’: Calibrating Political-Opinion Claims in Narco/Gang Contexts

Why this case matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi (Oct. 7, 2025) is a useful, if familiar, exemplar of how U.S. courts navigate asylum claims that sit at the intersection of political activity, criminal actors, and allegations of cooperation with state institutions. At bottom, the court rejected an asylum and withholding-of-removal claim by a Colombian family who had campaigned for a presidential candidate and later were threatened and harassed after the husband, a provisional lawyer and prosecutor’s intern, confronted a property controlled by a locally powerful narco-trafficking figure. The court found (1) the harm did not rise to past persecution; (2) even taken together, the evidence showed the persecutors were motivated primarily by retaliation against the husband’s interference with their revenue and security—not by the family’s partisan political opinion; (3) there was insufficient evidence that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect the family; and (4) internal relocation was feasible.

The opinion is noteworthy because it highlights two recurring fault lines in asylum practice: when does partisan political activity (or anti-corruption work and “snitching”) translate into a protected political opinion, and how courts distinguish politically motivated persecution from criminally motivated retaliation. The court’s analysis underscores the evidentiary and doctrinal hurdles claimants face in narco/gang contexts, and it gives practitioners a roadmap—both warning signs and strategies—for how to sharpen nexus proofs going forward.

How the court reasoned

The Tenth Circuit applied settled asylum law. To prevail on asylum based on past persecution, an applicant must show (1) persecution (more than episodic threats or non-persistent harassment), (2) that the harm was “on account of” a protected ground (here, political opinion), and (3) that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors. A showing of past persecution generally gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of future persecution; absent that, a claimant must show a well-founded fear of future persecution.

On the first element, the court accepted that the family endured significant threats, a brochure reading “death to snitches from Liberal Party,” intimidating armed confrontations, the assault of a factory employee, and an incident involving the husband’s young son being grabbed at school. But the court emphasized precedent limiting persecution findings based solely on threats and psychological distress, noting petitioners conceded no lasting physical harm. In the court’s view, the cumulative record did not compel a finding of past persecution under Tenth Circuit standards.

On nexus—the centerpiece of the opinion—the court concluded the persecutors’ predominant motive was economic/personal retaliation tied to the husband’s intervention at Don Rafa’s property, not the family’s partisan campaigning. Even though petitioners had been active members of the Liberal Party and had campaigned for a presidential candidate, the escalation coincided with the husband’s property dispute and the men’s explicit references to him as a “snitch” and a prosecutor’s collaborator. The court stressed that a protected ground must be “at least one central reason” (not merely incidental or subordinate) for the harm. The court treated the snitching allegations as instrumental and directly tied to Don Rafa’s asserted loss of revenue and security; that, in the court’s view, made political opinion a tangential rather than central motivation.

On government protection, the court relied on the fact that law enforcement had, at least in part, attempted to address criminal activity at the property, and that the petitioners had limited efforts to seek protection beyond two unhelpful officers. The court pointed to the absence of systemic evidence showing government complicity or a pattern of failure. Finally, on internal relocation, the court saw corroborative facts: family members remained safely in Colombia, the petitioners had temporarily stayed with kin in the countryside, and the threats were neighborhood-specific (“move out of the neighborhood”), not countrywide.

Analytical perspective: doctrines, distinctions, and evidentiary fault lines

This opinion hews closely to established Tenth Circuit precedent and illustrates how courts operationalize two doctrinal distinctions that often decide cases: (1) political opinion versus non-protected motives in mixed-motive contexts, and (2) “snitching” framed as a political stance (e.g., anti-corruption or pro-rule-of-law expression) versus “snitching” as a functional accusation implicating private disputes or criminal enforcement (i.e., economic or personal motives).

First, nexus demands “one central reason,” which puts a heavy thumb on the scale in mixed-motive settings. Practically, courts ask whether the persecutor’s grievance stems from the target’s ideas, status, or messages, or instead from the target’s interference with the persecutor’s economic or criminal interests. Here, the temporal sequence mattered: threats existed early in the campaign, but the escalated, violent incidents followed the husband’s involvement at the property. The persecutors’ contemporaneous statements—calling him a “snitch” and blaming him for searches—proved decisive. That kind of explicit contemporaneous attribution is powerful because it links motive to conduct, but crucially not to partisan views. For courts, “snitch” commonly signifies cooperation with state enforcement rather than a political ideology, so claimants must bridge that gap.

Second, the court’s analysis signals skepticism about reading anti-corruption or pro-law-enforcement activity as political opinion absent evidence that the persecutor understood and targeted the claimant because of that opinion. In many contexts—especially where criminal actors view enforcement as a commercial threat—conduct that looks political to the applicant (or to outside observers) will be perceived by the persecutor as a threat to revenue. This dynamic is particularly acute in places where criminal groups wield local governance power and have ambiguous relationships with official actors.

Third, the opinion reaffirms the evidentiary premium courts place on applicant reporting to authorities, systemic evidence of state collusion, and efforts to seek protection beyond the immediate neighborhood. The Tenth Circuit treated the petitioners’ limited engagement with state institutions and the existing evidence of law-enforcement attempts to police the property as undermining the “unable or unwilling” claim. The court’s reliance on such higher-level indicators—government action at the property, family still resident in Colombia, temporary safe relocation to the countryside—reflects a pragmatic assessment of safety and avoidability.

Practical litigation strategies for claimants in similar narco/gang contexts

Counsel preparing claims that involve narco/gang actors should incorporate strategies that address these doctrinal fault lines directly. The Jimenez opinion highlights several avenues to strengthen a political-opinion nexus and to rebut a court’s inclination to view “snitching” as purely instrumental retaliation:

1. Establish contemporaneous evidence that persecutors tied the harassment to political expression or partisan activity, not merely to interference with criminal revenue. The strongest nexus proof is a persecutor’s statement linking the target’s political message or party affiliation to the attack. So: collect, document, and emphasize contemporaneous threats, flyers, social-media posts, or intercepted communications that connect the violence to political views (e.g., “you support X party” or “you campaigned for Y”).

2. Show that the claimant’s political activities were public, message-driven, and visible to the persecutor. Campaigning that included public rallies, leafleting, public statements, or other public-facing acts is easier to frame as political opinion than private door-to-door canvassing—particularly if the persecutor had knowledge of those activities.

3. Contextualize anti-corruption or law-enforcement cooperation as expressive political conduct when possible. If a claimant intervened publicly to denounce corruption or to advocate for law-and-order reforms—rather than merely conducting a private property dispute—highlight those public elements. Demonstrate that the claimant’s actions conveyed an opinion about governance, rule of law, or public policy rather than being limited to a private contractual or property interest.

4. Capture and emphasize any evidence that the persecutor’s “snitch” label was deployed to suppress political expression. In many settings, criminal actors use the “snitch” epithet to discredit or intimidate civic actors; when evidence shows the label was used to chill political speech (for example, against neighborhood political organizers, journalists, or anti-corruption activists), it strengthens the political-opinion narrative.

5. Use corroborating country conditions and expert testimony to show systemic targeting of political activists, or patterns where criminal groups target a political party or movement. If there is documentary evidence or credible reporting that criminal organizations have targeted a political party, or that state actors have failed to protect party activists, that can rebut an inference that the motive was purely economic.

6. Document attempts to seek protection beyond single officers and establish that doing so would have been futile or dangerous. Exhausting remedies is not always required, but courts give weight to applicants who sought help through multiple channels (local police stations, prosecutors, internal affairs, national-level bodies, or documented complaints) or who can show credible reasons why reporting would have been futile (e.g., history of collusion, threats tied to police, membership of persecutors in paramilitary or political networks).

7. Demonstrate the personal costs of relocation and contrast neighborhood-specific threats with broader persecution. Where relocation is genuinely infeasible—due to economic, familial, or targeted patterns—build a factual record (affidavits, economic assessments, child-care or medical needs) showing that internal relocation would not, in practice, eliminate risk or would impose intolerable hardship.

Where courts will likely push back

Even with careful preparation, some structural tendencies in judicial review make these claims difficult. Courts are rightly wary of treating ordinary criminal retaliation as a protected ground; asylum is not a remedy for victims of generalized criminal violence unless the violence is targeted for a protected reason. Mixed-motive situations are thorny: courts will parse sequencing and statements, and they will credit other plausible motives (revenge, economic harm). The onus is on claimants to convert private disputes or economically-inflected threats into evidence of a politically motivated campaign of persecution. That often requires contemporaneous and documentary proof that may not exist in dangerous, low-recordkeeping settings.

Conclusion

Jimenez v. Bondi is not a novelty—it mirrors many published circuit decisions grappling with the line between political targeting and criminal retaliation. But it is a useful refresher on what courts expect from claimants who say they were targeted for political opinion in contexts dominated by violent nonstate actors. The opinion underscores a simple lesson for practitioners: where “snitch” is alleged, turn the focus away from the label and toward what the persecutor understood the label to mean. Was the conduct and message political, public, and expressive? Did the persecutor act to suppress that expression? Did the state systematically fail to protect those with that expressive profile? Answering those questions through contemporaneous statements, broader patterns of targeting, and evidence of futile or dangerous attempts to seek protection will be essential to prevail in future cases that sit at the fraught intersection of partisan politics, corruption, and organized criminality.
