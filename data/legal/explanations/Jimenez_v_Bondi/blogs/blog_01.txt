# Threats, Panic, and the Persecution Line: The Tenth Circuit’s Hard Look at "Threats‑Only" Asylum Claims

## Why this case matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi (Oct. 7, 2025) is a significant reaffirmation of the circuit’s skeptical approach to “threats‑only” asylum claims. The panel denied asylum and withholding of removal to a Colombian family who fled after repeated death threats, an assault at gunpoint, an attempted abduction of a child, and ongoing psychological distress. Although the Immigration Judge found the family credible, the Board of Immigration Appeals and the Tenth Circuit concluded that the combination of threats and psychological harms did not, as a matter of law, rise to past persecution; nor did the record show that the family had a sufficient nexus to a protected ground or that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them.

For practitioners and adjudicators, Jimenez crystallizes several practical points: the Tenth Circuit continues to treat threats and emotional suffering as seldom enough for asylum absent more tangible physical harm or a clear showing of systemic failure of protection; where the persecutor’s motive plausibly flows from a private dispute tied to criminal profit rather than a protected characteristic, the nexus inquiry will doom a claim; and failure to seek broader government assistance—or to document why doing so would be futile—will sharply undercut unable/unwilling arguments. The case thus serves as a warning about how to frame evidence and theory when representing clients who primarily suffered threats and fear.

## What the court did and why

The court’s decision tracks a familiar Tenth Circuit path. It analyzed the claim under the statutory framework for asylum and withholding of removal: (1) did the applicants suffer past persecution (which creates a rebuttable presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution)? (2) was any persecution on account of a protected ground (here, political opinion)? and (3) was the persecution attributable to actors the government is unwilling or unable to control? The court applied the familiar substantial‑evidence standard to the BIA’s factual findings.

On past persecution, the court stressed that threats alone, even when repeated and accompanied by serious psychological effects, generally do not meet the threshold for persecution in the Tenth Circuit. The opinion relied on earlier Tenth Circuit decisions—Karki (threats alone insufficient), Brandy (threats and non‑lasting harms insufficient), and Chhetri (even detention and threats did not necessarily satisfy persecution)—to hold that the petitioners’ experience, “in the aggregate,” did not count as persecution. The court noted the petitioners conceded they suffered no lasting physical injury and emphasized that, while psychological distress is relevant, it rarely by itself satisfies the persecution standard in this circuit.

On nexus, the court focused on motive. Petitioners argued that their work on a presidential campaign for a rival to Colombia’s president made them targets for politically motivated violence. The court, weighing the record, concluded that the escalation of threats and violence coincided with the principal applicant’s confrontation of a local criminal leader (Don Rafa) over property used for illicit activity. The threats thus appeared aimed at punishing or protecting the trafficker’s revenue and security, not at suppressing a political opinion. Because petitioners’ political activity was at most tangential—and because the record tied the persecutors’ animus principally to the property dispute—the political‑opinion nexus failed.

Finally, on government protection and internal relocation, the court observed sparse efforts by petitioners to seek help beyond telling local officers in their neighborhood. Isolated, apathetic responses from individual officers did not demonstrate a systemic inability or unwillingness of the Colombian state to protect them. The court also pointed to evidence that law enforcement had previously attempted searches of the implicated property, undermining any claim that the persecutor was untouchable. The record showed petitioners could temporarily relocate within Colombia (they had done so for three weeks) and had family who remained in Colombia without harm—evidence the court treated as bolstering the availability of internal relocation.

## How the Tenth Circuit’s approach compares to other circuits

Jimenez is representative of the Tenth Circuit’s more demanding posture on threats‑based persecution claims. That posture contrasts with more claimant‑friendly decisions in some other circuits—most notably the Ninth Circuit—which have sometimes found death threats and repeated terrorizing conduct sufficient to establish persecution, depending on the totality of circumstances.

The Ninth Circuit has on several occasions been willing to treat serious, detailed death threats and violent targeting as persecution when taken with other contextual factors (e.g., pattern of violence in the region, inability of police to protect, and an individualized risk). By contrast, the Tenth Circuit’s cases cited in Jimenez (Karki, Brandy, Chhetri, Rivera‑Barrientos) show a consistent hesitancy: threats, even repeated and frightening ones, will not often pass the line unless accompanied by physical injury, detention, torture, or clear evidence that the threats are part of a broader campaign of punitive violence that places the victim in imminent, substantial danger.

That divergence matters in practice. An applicant whose record is strongest on threats and psychological injury may have a significantly better chance in a jurisdiction that has recognized threats as persecution in the right circumstances, while the same record may founder in the Tenth Circuit unless it also includes corroborating evidence of physical harm or government impotence.

## Analytical perspective: strengths and vulnerabilities of the panel’s reasoning

There is force to the Tenth Circuit’s concern about policing the boundary between “serious harassment” and “persecution.” Asylum status confers significant immigration benefits; treating every credible, but non‑physical, threat as persecution risks stretching the refugee definition beyond the international norm that underlies it. The court also properly leans on motive—if the central reason for the harm is a private dispute tied to criminal profits, categorizing it as persecution on account of political opinion or another protected ground would flatten the statute’s particularity requirement.

At the same time, the court’s treatment of psychological harms and threats invites criticism. The opinion reiterates that psychological injury “rarely, if ever” suffices absent physical harm. That rule can risk underprotecting victims whose lives are fundamentally destabilized by non‑physical but severe and protracted campaigns of intimidation—especially where physical harm is imminent or where cultural or contextual factors make physical injury less likely but fear and disruption are pervasive. Moreover, the line between “threats only” and “threats plus” is factually thin: an armed in‑person confrontation at a workplace and an attempted abduction of a child may be experienced and functionally equivalent to physical violence for family safety and livelihood.

The court does engage with cumulative‑effects doctrine, citing Ritonga and noting that incidents are assessed together. Yet its application seems to give relatively little weight to the child’s attempted abduction and the in‑person gunpoint incidents—events that some jurists might regard as approaching physical persecution. The Tenth Circuit’s heavy reliance on the lack of lasting physical injury and the apparent private motive means that in mixed‑motive or mixed‑harm cases, claimants may face a narrow window to show persecution.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Several doctrinal implications follow from Jimenez:

First, practitioners in the Tenth Circuit must be prepared to document either physical harm or a tightly woven narrative showing that threats are part of an ongoing campaign aimed at inflicting severe harm (e.g., attempted abduction or explicit, corroborated execution orders) and that such conduct is likely to recur.

Second, the nexus analysis in Jimenez underscores the importance of tracing motive. When private actors rather than the state—or state‑adjacent criminal groups—are involved, it is critical to develop evidence showing that the persecutor’s animus was at least partly directed at a protected ground. Where the persecutor’s statements or actions expressly reference political activity, or where political actors are known to target campaigners, that evidence will be determinative. If the persecutor’s statements instead tie harm to a private dispute (property, debt, business competition), the political‑opinion theory will be vulnerable.

Third, the government‑protection and internal‑relocation holdings emphasize the evidentiary value of (a) reporting to authorities or documenting the futility/danger of doing so, and (b) showing attempts and inability to obtain effective protection beyond isolated, unhelpful officers. Practitioners should compile documentary proof of systemic failures (news reports, human‑rights reports, prior unsuccessful complaints, contemporaneous police reports) when claiming inability or unwillingness of protection. A client who speaks to only one patrol officer and then departs risks a finding that the state had the capacity to protect them.

Fourth, Jimenez signals that Tenth Circuit adjudicators will weigh family and relocation facts—such as relatives remaining safely in-country and prior successful short‑term internal relocation—heavily against claims that internal relocation is infeasible.

## Practical evidentiary takeaways

For lawyers preparing cases where threats and psychological harms are central, Jimenez suggests concrete strategies:

- Corroborate threats with as much contemporaneous evidence as possible: police reports, medical or mental‑health records documenting panic attacks or PTSD, witness statements, physical evidence (e.g., damaged property), and contemporaneous communications (voicemails, text messages, flyers). The court’s emphasis on substantial evidence means the record matters.

- Where physical harm is absent, document why it was prevented (e.g., near‑misses, attempted abduction, in‑person gunpoint threats) and the specificity of execution orders. The more the threats are targeted, specific, and repeated, the likelier a court will treat them as more than ephemeral intimidation.

- Demonstrate nexus: collect evidence that persecutors referenced political views, campaign work, or other protected characteristics. If the persecutor’s stated motivation appears mixed or private, produce contextual evidence showing political content in the persecutor’s statements or broader patterns of political violence against campaigners.

- Seek systemic corroboration for inability/unwillingness claims: formal complaints with police and written responses (or the lack thereof), FOIA or other documentary evidence showing prior unsuccessful enforcement efforts, expert country‑condition declarations explaining why a police response would likely be ineffective, and evidence that higher‑level officials have connections to or tolerate the persecutors.

- Address relocation defensibly: show why internal relocation would be unsafe or unreasonable (e.g., nationwide presence of persecutors, economic or administrative barriers, family ties jeopardized) through concrete examples rather than speculation.

## Conclusion

Jimenez reaffirms that, in the Tenth Circuit, credible threats and significant psychological harms alone rarely breach the statutory line into persecution. The decision underscores the critical importance of documenting physical manifestations of danger, tying persecutors’ motives to protected grounds, and proving the futility of seeking protection. For claimants whose experiences are dominated by threats, Jimenez cautions that careful evidentiary scaffolding—not simply credible testimony of fear—will be essential to clear the asylum bar in this circuit.
