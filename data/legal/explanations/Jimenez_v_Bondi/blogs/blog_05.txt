Title: Blog about the opinion: Jimenez v. Bondi

# The Perils of Under‑Developed PSG Claims: What Wasn’t Briefed in Canon Jimenez

Why this case matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Canon Jimenez v. Bondi is an object lesson for practitioners: if you hope to win asylum or withholding of removal on the basis of membership in a particular social group (PSG), you must define and develop that theory fully in the administrative record and on appeal. The petitioners in this Colombia case presented a fact pattern—campaigning for an opposition party, alleged threats and violence from organized criminal actors, and one petitioner’s role as a provisional lawyer and occasional prosecutor’s office intern—that could plausibly fit within several PSG formulations that have traction in Latin American and gang‑related contexts. But the family never pressed a PSG argument in briefing below or on appeal, and the court refused to reach it. The result: the petitioners lost on alternative grounds (no past persecution, no nexus, government protection/relocation feasible), and the opinion serves as a cautionary blueprint on how omission can be dispositive.

What the court did — and did not — decide

The Tenth Circuit’s analysis is straightforward and anchored to the administrative record. The court affirmed the BIA’s conclusion that the petitioners failed to prove past persecution and, in any event, failed to show that any harm was “on account of” a protected ground. Critically, the court limited its review to the political‑opinion nexus because petitioners “did not brief any arguments regarding the existence of their particular social group,” and therefore the PSG avenue was forfeited. That procedural point mattered: without a concrete PSG on the table, the court had no occasion to apply the tripartite PSG test (particularity, social distinction, and nexus) as articulated in Supreme Court and circuit precedents such as Crespin‑Valladares.

The practical upshot for practitioners is stark. PSG theories are often the most viable path in cases involving non‑state or quasi‑state persecutors—gangs, paramilitaries, criminal syndicates—and where political opinion is not a dominant or clearly articulated motivation. But PSG claims do not survive on generalities; they must be pled, developed, and supported with tailored evidence.

Plausible PSG formulations in Colombia cases — and what they require

Looking at Colombia litigation more broadly, several PSG formulations have been recognized (or at least frequently litigated) as potentially viable:

- “Witnesses/informants” or “people who cooperate with law enforcement.” This group is natural where the targeted conduct stems from perceived cooperation with prosecutors or police investigations. To carry this theory a practitioner should show (1) that the applicant actually performed roles or functions that would make them be perceived as a cooperating witness or informant (e.g., assisted prosecutors, reported crimes, initiated complaints, or helped facilitate searches/seizures); (2) that society—particularly the persecutor community and the applicant’s local community—recognizes and treats such persons as a distinct group (social distinction); and (3) that the persecutor targeted them because of that perceived role (nexus).

- “Family members of those who oppose or have opposed gangs/organized crime” (or “family members of those who cooperate with the state”). Family‑based PSGs arise where violence targets relatives as a way to punish, intimidate, or neutralize a perceived opponent. Establishing such a group requires showing that the persecutors understood the family relationship and targeted the family because of it, that the family is seen as a distinct social grouping in the relevant context, and that the family members bear the stigma or threat that the persecutor intended.

- “Social leaders” or “community leaders” — often used in contexts where NGOs, human‑rights defenders, local politicians, or community activists are targeted. Petitioners in Colombia who are political activists or locally prominent volunteers sometimes fit this mold. To succeed, the applicant must show that (i) they occupy a role that distinguishes them from the broader community; (ii) that role is recognized (and feared) by the persecutors and neighbors; and (iii) the targeting is at least in part because of that role.

What Crespin‑Valladares and related doctrine demand

The Supreme Court’s ruling in Pereira/Crespin‑Valladares (which practitioners routinely cite for PSG analysis) and subsequent circuit decisions require careful attention to three elements:

- Particularity: The PSG must be defined with sufficient clarity and discrete boundaries so it is administrable. “All poor people in X region” is too amorphous; “women who report sexual violence to police in Municipality Y” may be sufficient if framed with evidentiary anchors. Particularity asks whether the group is internally cohesive and readily identifiable.

- Social distinction: The group must be perceived as a social grouping within the claimant’s society. This is an empirical question, and courts look for evidence — expert reports, country‑condition literature, testimony from community members, and corroborating documentation — showing that ordinary people in the claimant’s society would recognize the group.

- Nexus: The persecutor’s motive must be at least one central reason for the harm. For PSG claims, that means demonstrating that the persecutor targeted the claimant because of their membership in the group, not merely because of criminality, economic motive, or private vendetta.

Crespin‑Valladares and its progeny show that courts will scrutinize both legal formulations and supporting proof. Where a PSG is thinly defined or unsupported by the record, the risk of rejection increases.

Evidence that practitioners should gather (and marshal)

The Canon Jimenez record illustrates recurring evidentiary weaknesses in PSG cases. Below are practical pointers on building a record that lets a PSG theory live or die on the merits rather than on procedural omission:

- Define the group early and concretely in the written asylum application and in the IJ hearing. Don’t wait until appeal. If a claimant could plausibly be an “informant” or “family of a targeted leader,” say so and explain why based on facts.

- Fact detail matters for particularity: describe the attributes that make membership identifiable (e.g., “persons who have filed formal complaints with the Fiscalía in Municipality X,” “family members of people who have testified against narco‑traffickers,” “provisional lawyers who have assisted in seizures of day‑rental housing used for trafficking”).

- Social‑distinction proof: procure expert declarations (country experts, sociologists, former local officials), contemporaneous news reports, NGO reports describing threats to informants or families, and affidavits from members of the claimant’s community. Courts respond well to multiple corroborating sources showing that the group is recognized and targeted.

- Nexus proof: keep correspondence, screenshots, threats that mention the claimant’s role, witness statements from neighbors or coworkers describing what the persecutors said (e.g., “you work with the prosecutor”), and documentation that connects the persecutor’s stated rationale to the claimant’s group membership. When persecutors explicitly label someone a “snitch” or “informant,” that can be powerful if the record demonstrates that the persecutor understood and acted on that label.

- Government response: because inability or unwillingness to protect is critical, show police reports, attempts to seek protection, evidence that reporting would be futile or dangerous (e.g., complaints to police that produced no action, evidence of collusion), or proof of systemic failure in the relevant locality. If the applicant did not report because it would have been futile, explain why and back the claim with documentation.

Why omission is costly: waiver and missed doctrinal analysis

Canon Jimenez’s procedural posture is a reminder that courts routinely decline to consider unbriefed claims. The Tenth Circuit expressly limited its PSG review because the petitioners “did not brief any arguments regarding the existence of their particular social group.” That procedural forfeiture is consequential: it prevented an analysis that might otherwise have unpacked the facts under the particularity/social‑distinction/nexus rubric and could have yielded a different outcome had the record been developed.

Beyond forfeiture, the opinion underscores another common hazard: trying to shoehorn a dispute into a political‑opinion theory when the facts are more naturally characterized as an informant/witness/PSG problem. The court here concluded the dominant motive was economic/personal (threat to Don Rafa’s revenue/security) tied to a property dispute and alleged interference with illicit operations, not a political opinion central to the harm. When motives are mixed or ambiguous, explicit PSG advocacy can preserve an alternative path.

Downstream effects and strategic takeaways

Canon Jimenez will be cited as a cautionary tale in removal adjudications involving criminal or quasi‑state persecutors. For practitioners, the takeaways are pragmatic:

- Put your PSG theory on record early and develop it fully — both legally and factually. Don’t assume a political‑opinion argument alone will carry the day when non‑state actors are the primary aggressors.

- Build a multi‑layered evidentiary record: contemporaneous documents, police reports, expert declarations, community witness statements, and media/NGO reporting that supports particularity and social distinction.

- Anticipate and rebut the government’s potential repositioning of motive (economic, personal vendetta, or criminally opportunistic) by corroborating evidence that ties the persecutor’s actions to the claimant’s group membership.

- Where reporting to police occurred, document it; where it did not occur, explain and document the futility, fear, or risk of reporting.

- On appeal, don’t sacrifice alternative theories. If you have a viable PSG claim, brief it even if you emphasize political opinion below; appellate courts are less forgiving of new theories not developed in the administrative record.

Conclusion

Canon Jimenez is not a decision that reimagines PSG doctrine. Instead, it reaffirms an enduring lesson: asylum outcomes often turn on the quality of legal framing and the depth of factual development. In countries like Colombia, where organized criminal actors target a range of people—informants, community leaders, family members—the PSG route is often the most doctrinally and factually apt. But PSGs are not self‑executing; they require definition, proof of social recognition, and a clear link between membership and harm. For practitioners, the case is a prompt to be thorough, early, and strategic in both pleadings and evidence collection—because what you fail to brief can determine whether the merits are ever reached.
