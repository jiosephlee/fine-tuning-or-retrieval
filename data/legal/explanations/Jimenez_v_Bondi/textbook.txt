Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Jimenez v. Bondi

# Parties, Facts, and Procedural Posture

## Petitioners and their political activity
The petitioners are Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two minor sons. The family are natives and citizens of Colombia who lived and worked in Bogota until December 2022, when they left for the United States. From 2012 onward they were members of the Colombian Liberal Party, and in 2022 they actively campaigned—going door-to-door—for Federico Gutiérrez, a presidential candidate opposed to the eventual president. The political activity is central to the asylum claim because Petitioners assert they were targeted, at least in part, because of their political opinion and party affiliation; that alleged motive is the primary statutory “ground” (political opinion) they rely on in the immigration proceedings.

## Jimenez’s professional roles and their relevance
At the time of the events, Jimenez was a law student with a provisional law license and was working as an intern at Colombia’s National Prosecutor’s office, helping victims of domestic abuse, extortion, and other crimes. Those roles are important to the factual narrative the petitioners rely upon: Jimenez’s work for victims and his contact with law enforcement are the basis for statements by his alleged persecutors that he was a “snitch” for prosecutors or police. The IJ and BIA treated these professional connections as relevant both to motive (why private actors targeted him) and to whether the Colombian government would be able or willing to protect him.

## The property dispute and “Don Rafa”
The factual escalation pivoted on a June 2022 property matter. A woman from San Bernardo retained Jimenez to intervene informally with a tenant who had not paid rent for over a year. Investigating the title led Jimenez to discover the tenant was her ex-husband, Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros—known in the record as “Don Rafa.” The IJ and BIA accepted evidence in the record that the property was associated with criminal activity: the health department had sealed the third floor; police reports tied the premises to day-rental migrants, narco-trafficking, prostitution, and incidents of kidnapping and murder; and law enforcement had attempted searches and seizures of the property. Don Rafa is described in the record as a leader of a narco-trafficking guerrilla group allegedly politically aligned with Colombia’s president. This dispute is the factual fulcrum of the case because the petitioners began receiving more direct and violent threats only after Jimenez’s involvement with the property and after the tenant’s identity became known.

## The pattern of threats and escalating intimidation
The record shows an escalation of intimidation over a few months in 2022. Beginning around late May, Jimenez received threatening phone calls about his involvement with the Liberal Party; other local party members also received threats. In June a colleague who had reported threats was kidnapped and murdered. Later that month Jimenez received a brochure at his factory that read, “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” In July, two men held Jimenez at gunpoint at the family’s furniture factory and told him to leave the neighborhood, calling him a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office. Two months later those same men returned while the family was away, assaulted a factory employee, drew a gun, and demanded Petitioners’ whereabouts, again saying the order came from Don Rafa. Days after that incident a man accosted Jimenez’s younger son outside school, put his arm around the boy and covered his mouth, and told the child to “run to your dad”; the son escaped after Jimenez intervened. These incidents—threatening calls, a menacing brochure, armed confrontations and an attack on a child—make up the alleged past persecution and form the core facts the IJ and BIA evaluated.

## Interactions with law enforcement in Colombia
Petitioners report two kinds of interaction with Colombian authorities. First, the record reflects prior law enforcement activity directed at the property associated with Don Rafa: authorities had attempted searches and seizures; the property had been closed by the health department for unlawful uses. Second, after the incident with his son and other threats Jimenez told a police patrol officer what had happened. According to Petitioners, that officer said nothing could be done and advised the family to leave, implying police apathy or inability to help. The IJ and BIA relied on both aspects when analyzing whether the Colombian government was “unable or unwilling” to protect Petitioners: prior enforcement efforts against the property undercut an argument that authorities were wholly aligned with the persecutors, while the limited direct reporting by Petitioners and the officer’s single discouraging comment led the agencies to conclude there was not sufficient evidence of systemic government failure.

## Immediate departure, internal relocation, and entry to the United States
After the schoolyard incident and the other threats, the family moved temporarily to Jimenez’s mother’s home in the Bogota countryside for about three weeks. There they decided to leave Colombia and travel to the United States. Petitioners entered the United States in December 2022 without being admitted or paroled by an immigration officer. That manner of entry bears on the removal proceedings that followed: DHS served Notices to Appear charging them as removable, and the immigration courts adjudicated their requests for relief.

## Procedural history through the immigration courts
Three months after arrival, DHS served each family member with a Notice to Appear charging removability under the Immigration and Nationality Act. At the merits hearing Petitioners admitted the truth of DHS’s allegations and the IJ found them removable. They applied for asylum, withholding of removal, and protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT); the two minor sons proceeded as derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s asylum claim. The Immigration Judge found Petitioners credible but denied relief. The IJ concluded Petitioners had not carried their burdens for asylum and withholding because they failed to show (1) past persecution, (2) persecution on account of a protected ground (nexus), (3) that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them, and (4) that internal relocation was not reasonably available. The IJ also denied CAT relief and rejected Petitioners’ due process arguments about counsel.

Petitioners appealed to the Board of Immigration Appeals, which affirmed the IJ and dismissed the appeal. The BIA expressly considered asylum and withholding and rejected the due process claim; it also affirmed denial of CAT protection. Petitioners then sought review in the Tenth Circuit.

## Scope of the appeal and issues preserved
On appeal to the Tenth Circuit, Petitioners limited their challenge to the denial of asylum and withholding of removal. They do not press the CAT claim or their due process arguments in the circuit. The issues properly before the court, therefore, are whether the BIA erred in concluding (1) Petitioners did not suffer past persecution, (2) any harm they suffered was not on account of a protected ground—specifically political opinion, and (3) Petitioners do not have a well-founded fear of future persecution because the Colombian government is able and willing to protect them and internal relocation is feasible. The court’s disposition turned on those contested legal elements as applied to the factual record articulated above; because the asylum claim fails, the higher standard for withholding of removal necessarily fails as well.

# Jurisdiction, Review, and Governing Asylum Framework

This chapter explains the procedural and substantive law the Tenth Circuit applied in Jimenez v. Bondi when reviewing the Board of Immigration Appeals’ denial of asylum and withholding of removal. Focus is on (1) the court’s jurisdiction and the limits of its review; (2) the deferential substantial-evidence standard for factual findings; and (3) the statutory and doctrinal elements that govern asylum and withholding claims—especially the role of past persecution, nexus to a protected ground, mixed-motive rules for political opinion, and the relationship between asylum and withholding standards. The explanations tie the legal rules to how the court used them in Jimenez.

## Jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252 and consolidated review of asylum claims

The Tenth Circuit invoked its jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252 to review a final order of removal. As the court emphasized, § 1252(a)(1) and (b)(9) provide jurisdiction over final removal orders and permit “consolidated review of asylum claims” that are part of those orders. In practice, this means that when an alien is ordered removed and the BIA’s decision is final, the court of appeals is the forum to challenge the BIA’s denial of relief (asylum, withholding, etc.). Jimenez shows the typical pathway: DHS issued Notices to Appear; the immigration judge (IJ) denied relief; the BIA affirmed; the petitioners then sought review in the circuit court under § 1252.

## Record-based review: limitation to the administrative record

The statutory review is confined to the “administrative record on which the order of removal is based” (8 U.S.C. § 1252(b)(4)(A)). The Tenth Circuit in Jimenez reiterated that limitation: the court’s review of the BIA’s denial of asylum is limited to the evidence and record before the IJ and BIA. Practically, this means a petitioner cannot expand the factual record in the federal court; arguments must be evaluated against the evidence assembled and explained in the administrative proceedings.

## Scope of review when the BIA issues a streamlined affirmance; consulting the IJ’s reasoning

When a single BIA member issues a brief affirmance of an IJ’s decision, the court primarily reviews the BIA’s ruling but is permitted to consult the IJ’s more detailed opinion for context and reasoning. Jimenez follows Uanreroro v. Gonzales on that point: the BIA’s decision controls, yet the IJ’s fuller findings and analysis can be used to understand the factual and legal bases the BIA adopted. That rule prevents appellate courts from reweighing the record while ensuring they can see the IJ’s reasoning where the BIA’s explanation is terse.

## Substantial-evidence standard for factual findings; when reversal is compelled

Findings of fact by the BIA are reviewed under the substantial-evidence standard. As the court described it in Jimenez (quoting Rivera-Barrientos), a factual finding is “conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.” Under this deferential standard, the reviewing court does not reweigh evidence or substitute its judgment for the agency’s credibility assessments or inferences unless no reasonable factfinder could have reached the agency’s conclusion. In Jimenez the court repeatedly applied that standard to uphold the BIA’s determinations on persecution, nexus, government protection, and internal relocation.

## Asylum eligibility: refugee definition and applicant’s burden

Asylum is discretionary relief that an applicant must prove by a preponderance of the evidence. The court in Jimenez relied on the statutory framework: the asylum statute (8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)) places the burden on the applicant, and the INA’s definition of “refugee” (8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A)) requires that the applicant be “unable or unwilling to return to” and “unable or unwilling to avail” herself of national protection “because of persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution on account of” one of the five protected grounds (race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion). Jimenez cites Niang for this statutory definition and frames the applicant’s burden as proving refugee status by a preponderance.

## Past persecution and the rebuttable presumption of future persecution: the three elements

A successful showing of past persecution gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution. The court described the familiar three-element test for past persecution (drawing on Rivera-Barrientos and Niang): the applicant must show (1) incidents that rise to the level of persecution; (2) that the incidents were on account of a protected ground; and (3) that the persecution was committed by the government or by forces the government is unwilling or unable to control.

The opinion also explains how courts assess “persecution.” The INA does not define the term, so Tenth Circuit case law supplies the meaning: persecution requires the infliction of suffering or harm that is offensive—more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats (citing Wiransane). Courts consider cumulative events (Ritonga) rather than isolating each incident, but there are limits: threats standing alone often do not constitute persecution (Karki), and purely emotional or psychological harms are rarely sufficient by themselves (Brandy). Jimenez applied these principles and concluded that, even aggregated, the petitioners’ threats and psychological distress did not meet the threshold of past persecution under the deferential substantial-evidence standard.

## Nexus to a protected ground and the mixed‑motive rule for political opinion

Nexus requires a causal link between the persecution and a protected ground. For political-opinion claims, the petitioner must show that the persecutor’s motive included that protected ground as “at least one central reason” for the harm (the court cited Rivera-Barrientos and 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)). The Tenth Circuit’s mixed‑motive approach permits that persecutors can have multiple motives, but a protected motive cannot be merely incidental, tangential, superficial, subordinate, or playing only a minor role (Dallakoti; Rivera-Barrientos). In mixed-motive cases, the protected ground must be sufficiently central to the persecutor’s decision to target the applicant.

Jimenez applied this principle concretely: although the petitioners were politically active, the record showed the escalation of threats occurred only after Jimenez intervened in a property dispute with an alleged criminal leader and the threats referenced his alleged role in searches and seizures. The BIA and IJ reasonably inferred that the primary motive was retaliation for the property dispute and interference with the trafficker’s revenue/security—not the petitioners’ political activities. Under the substantial-evidence standard the court declined to overturn that factual inference, holding the political-opinion motive was tangential rather than central.

## Government protection inquiry and the role of reporting

The third past-persecution element—government involvement or the government’s inability/unwillingness to control persecutors—requires inquiry into whether the state can and will provide protection. Jimenez follows Singh in identifying two heavily weighted considerations: (1) whether the government stopped or attempted to stop the persecutors; and (2) whether the government offered protective measures to the applicant (for example, protective custody). The analysis also depends on whether the government had notice of the persecution and a reasonable opportunity to respond; if the applicant never reported the abuse, the record provides no basis to infer governmental nonresponse. Failure to report can undercut a claim that the government was unwilling or unable to protect, although it is not automatically fatal if the applicant can show reporting would have been futile or dangerous (Singh; Aviles‑Gonzalez).

In Jimenez the court found substantial evidence that petitioners did not meaningfully seek help beyond brief, unhelpful conversations with individual officers—despite familial connections to the criminal justice system—and that law-enforcement had attempted searches and seizures at the disputed property. Given that record, the court concluded petitioners failed to show the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them.

## Internal relocation and regulatory rebuttal of asylum claims

Even when an applicant shows a well‑founded fear, the government can rebut the claim by proving either a fundamental change in circumstances or that the applicant could reasonably relocate to another part of the country to avoid persecution (8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i); Rivera‑Barrientos). Jimenez applied the relocation rule: the court accepted that petitioners had safely stayed on a relative’s property in the countryside for weeks and that other family members remained in Colombia unharmed, supporting the BIA’s finding that internal relocation was reasonable and available.

## Withholding of removal: the higher standard

Withholding of removal is a form of mandatory (not discretionary) protection with a higher evidentiary standard than asylum. The Jimenez opinion summarizes this relationship: because petitioners failed to meet the asylum standard (well‑founded fear), they necessarily failed to meet withholding’s higher burden. The opinion cites circuit precedent (Carias‑Mejia; Uanreroro) to show withholding requires a greater showing of likelihood of future persecution—so failure on asylum is typically dispositive of withholding claims as well.

Conclusion

Jimenez exemplifies how jurisdictional limits, record‑based review, and the substantial‑evidence standard frame appellate review of asylum denials. Substantively, the opinion illustrates the critical interplay among the required elements—persecution, nexus, and government protection—how mixed‑motive analysis constrains political‑opinion claims, and why failure to satisfy the asylum standard usually forecloses withholding relief. For students, the case is a clear application of deferential review to mixed fact‑law questions in immigration adjudications.

# Past Persecution: Threats, Psychological Harm, and Cumulative Assessment

This chapter explains how the Tenth Circuit evaluated whether the petitioners’ experiences amounted to past persecution in Jimenez v. Bondi. The court framed the question under settled Tenth Circuit precedent: what level and type of harm—considered individually and cumulatively—suffices to qualify as “persecution” for asylum purposes. The analysis demonstrates how the court applies familiar doctrinal markers (severity, offensiveness, and cumulative effect) to a fact pattern of repeated threats, armed confrontations, property-related disputes with criminal actors, and alleged psychological injury.

## Persecution concept: more than mere restrictions or threats; offensiveness and severity requirement

The court began from the basic rule that “persecution” is not defined in the INA, so courts look to what sort of suffering is “regarded as offensive” and that is more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats. The opinion relies on Wiransane v. Ashcroft for this principle: persecution requires the infliction of suffering or harm on those who differ in some protected respect in a way that is “regarded as offensive” and that rises above mere threats or restrictions. This standard means courts evaluate both the quality (how offensive or severe) and quantity (how chronic or cumulative) of mistreatment. The court also reiterated that whether private persecution occurred is a factual question reviewed under the substantial-evidence standard, so the BIA’s factual findings are conclusive unless no reasonable adjudicator could reach the same conclusion.

Two important corollaries follow from the Tenth Circuit’s precedent and are applied in this opinion. First, physical injury is not strictly necessary to show persecution—the absence of bodily harm does not automatically doom a claim (see Karki)—but the overall harm must still be sufficiently severe. Second, mere threats, standing alone, typically do not qualify; the threats must be part of a pattern or accompanied by other serious mistreatment that together cross the persecution threshold.

## Evidence presented: repeated threats; armed confrontations; brochure; assault of employee; child intimidation; absence of actual physical injury to petitioners

The petitioners’ factual record included a sequence of hostile acts: repeated threatening phone calls (weekly), distribution of a brochure saying “death to snitches from Liberal Party,” two in-person armed confrontations (one holding Jimenez at gunpoint and ordering him to leave the neighborhood; another assault on a factory employee seeking the petitioners), and a frightening child-intimidation episode in which a stranger put his arm around the petitioners’ young son and covered his mouth. Petitioners also had a prior confrontation between Jimenez and a local criminal figure (Don Rafa) over a property dispute; the criminal’s associates later told petitioners they had received orders from Don Rafa.

Crucially, petitioners conceded before the IJ and elsewhere that they themselves were never physically injured. That concession is important because, although physical injury is not required, it is a significant factor in assessing severity. The court emphasized the absence of actual physical harm to the petitioners in applying the persecution standard.

## Petitioners’ claims of emotional and psychological harm (insomnia; child’s panic attacks)

Petitioners relied in part on psychological effects—Duarte’s insomnia and the son’s panic attacks—to argue the cumulative harm met the persecution threshold. The court acknowledged that emotional and psychological harms are relevant to the persecution inquiry, but treated those allegations as insufficient here. It pointed out that the Tenth Circuit has “rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone,” citing Brandy and other precedent. In short, while such harms can contribute to a broader showing, they seldom tip the scales when the underlying incidents consist principally of threats and non-injurious confrontations.

## Cumulative-evidence approach vs. isolated incidents

The court reiterated that incidents are not analyzed in isolation; instead, courts consider the cumulative effect of multiple incidents (Ritonga). That cumulative approach recognizes that a pattern of less-severe harms can, together, amount to persecution. But the cumulative test is not automatic: the combined incidents must, in total, meet the qualitative and quantitative thresholds—i.e., be sufficiently severe and offensive. Here the court applied that cumulative lens and still concluded the aggregate of threats, confrontations, and psychological stress did not reach persecution.

To understand the line the court drew, it is helpful to compare petitions the opinion discussed. The court cited Karki (threats alone insufficient), Chhetri (telephone threats and a vague in-person threat insufficient), and Carias-Mejia (machete threats and repeated robberies insufficient because the threats were conditional on compliance). Those cases show that even multiple threats or episodic criminal acts do not necessarily constitute persecution absent a level of harm or particularized targeting that meets the statutory test.

## Tenth Circuit guidance: threats alone typically insufficient; physical harm not required but severity matters

The Jimenez opinion synthesizes Tenth Circuit guidance: threats alone generally do not constitute persecution; physical injury is not a sine qua non but is a highly probative indicator of severity; and psychological injury—while relevant—rarely suffices by itself. The court also reaffirmed that the record must compel a different outcome to overturn the BIA under the substantial-evidence standard. The opinion notes that petitioners’ counsel conceded at oral argument that Tenth Circuit precedent has rejected asylum claims even in more extreme factual permutations, signaling that the bar is not easily met.

## Comparative case references: Karki; Brandy; Chhetri; Carias-Mejia

The opinion anchors its analysis with prior Tenth Circuit decisions. Karki establishes that threats alone are ordinarily insufficient. Brandy underscores the courts’ hesitancy to grant asylum based solely on emotional or psychological harms stemming from threats. Chhetri and Carias-Mejia provide concrete examples where multiple threats, detentions, or property-related violence did not suffice to show persecution because either the harms were not severe enough, were conditional, or lacked the requisite nexus and severity. Jimenez applies those precedents to conclude the present facts fall on the non-persecution side of the line.

## Holding on this element: substantial evidence supports BIA’s finding that, even in the aggregate, the harms did not rise to persecution

Applying the law to the facts, the Tenth Circuit concluded that substantial evidence supports the BIA’s finding that petitioners did not suffer past persecution. The court emphasized the petitioners’ own concessions (no physical harm), the nature of the incidents (threats, intimidation, an assault on an employee, and child-targeting), and the limited probative force of alleged psychological effects. Even when viewed cumulatively, the harms did not meet the threshold established in Tenth Circuit precedent. Because the petitioners failed to establish past persecution, they could not rely on the rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution that such a finding would create—an outcome that also bears on the separate but related withholding-of-removal claim.

Takeaway for students: when reading asylum opinions like Jimenez, focus on (1) the court’s articulation of the persecution standard (severity/offensiveness, more than threats), (2) whether incidents are considered cumulatively, (3) any concessions or absences of physical harm in the record, (4) how alleged psychological harms are weighed, and (5) the standard of review (substantial evidence). The Jimenez decision illustrates how a string of serious but non-injurious threats, even with distressing psychological consequences, can still fall short of the statutory concept of persecution under controlling Tenth Circuit authority.

# Nexus: Political Opinion Versus Personal Retaliation

## Protected grounds at issue; abandonment of particular social group
The petitioners presented two potential grounds for asylum in their briefing before the BIA, but on appeal they expressly abandoned any argument about membership in a particular social group. The only protected ground the Tenth Circuit therefore evaluated was political opinion. That narrowing is important: once a petitioner abandons a ground, the appellate court confines its review to the remaining ground(s). Here, the question presented was not whether petitioners belonged to a cognizable social group, but whether the mistreatment they experienced was “on account of” their political opinion.

## Legal test: political opinion must be at least one central reason; mixed motives
The opinion applies the familiar statutory and case-law standard: to establish persecution “on account of” political opinion, the applicant must show that the political opinion was “at least one central reason” for the harm. The court repeatedly cites Rivera‑Barrientos and Dallakoti to emphasize that the protected motive cannot be “incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate” or play only “a minor role.” At the same time, the court acknowledges that persecutors may have mixed motives; a protected ground need not be the sole reason, but it must be a central one. Thus, when motives are mixed the adjudicator asks whether a protected ground was a substantial causal factor in the persecutor’s decision to target the applicant.

This is a fact-intensive inquiry. The adjudicator looks to direct and circumstantial evidence of motive—statements by persecutors, timing of events, the factual context that would link the victim’s protected activity to the attackers’ purpose, and any competing plausible motives. The standard of proof for asylum eligibility is a preponderance of the evidence, but factual findings about motive are reviewed on a substantial‑evidence basis on appeal; the court asks whether a reasonable factfinder would be compelled to reach a contrary conclusion.

## How the court read the record on motive
The opinion analyzes the record for evidence that political opinion was a central reason for the threats and other mistreatment. Two strands of evidence are relevant on the record.

First, petitioners received politically tinged, collective threats early in the campaign season: starting around late May 2022, Jimenez and other Liberal Party members received threatening phone calls and a brochure that said “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” A colleague who had also received threats was later kidnapped and murdered. Those earlier incidents are at least facially political in character.

Second, a distinct escalation occurred after Jimenez, in his provisional-lawyer role, became involved in a private property dispute with a tenant who turned out to be Don Rafa, an identified leader of a narco‑trafficking group. After Jimenez visited the property and inquired about the tenant, the threats intensified and took on a different tenor: the men who accosted Jimenez later accused him of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for searches and seizures at Don Rafa’s property. When the attackers returned to the factory they assaulted an employee and, at gunpoint, demanded to know the family’s whereabouts, stating that the order came from Don Rafa.

The court treated these two factual strands as distinct and asked which better explains why petitioners were targeted. The early threats lacked any record evidence linking them to Don Rafa or to the particular men who later threatened the family. By contrast, the timing and content of the later threats tied directly to Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s criminal enterprise and to Jimenez’s official/professional role (his internship with the prosecutor’s office and his work on the property dispute).

## Why the perpetrators’ statements mattered
Adjudicators rely heavily on persecutor statements that reveal motive, and the opinion follows that practice. The men’s explicit accusations that Jimenez was a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and their attribution of the threats to Don Rafa show a retaliatory, personal/professional motive: they were upset about Jimenez’s interference with an illicit revenue stream and about law‑enforcement activity at the property. Those statements are probative of a nonpolitical motivation because they describe a concrete grievance tied to criminal activity and to Jimenez’s role in invoking—or being perceived as invoking—law enforcement against the perpetrators.

The court contrasted those declarations with the earlier, generic political threats and noted the absence of evidence that Don Rafa was behind the early political communications. Because the most direct and contemporaneous statements identified a personal/professional grievance, the court concluded that the protected political opinion was not a central reason for the mistreatment reflected in the record.

## Temporal and causal linkage to Don Rafa’s enterprise
Timing and causation are central to the court’s nexus analysis. The opinion emphasizes that the sequence of events supports a causal connection between Jimenez’s dealings with Don Rafa’s property and the escalated threats. Petitioners continued political campaigning despite earlier threats; the escalation occurred only after Jimenez confronted the tenant and investigated the property; the perpetrators’ remarks referenced searches and seizures at that property; and they identified Don Rafa as the source of the order. This temporal and causal pattern supports the BIA’s finding that the predominant motive was to protect the traffickers’ revenue and safety rather than to punish petitioners for their political views.

Conversely, the record lacks evidence tying Don Rafa to the initial politically motivated threats—so the mere coincidence of early political threats does not establish that the later, more particularized retaliation was politically motivated. Under the “at least one central reason” standard, the court found that the political motive was at most subordinate or tangential in the context of the later, more dangerous conduct.

## Court’s conclusion and doctrinal takeaways
The court affirmed the BIA’s finding that petitioners failed to show a nexus between their mistreatment and a protected political opinion. Applying the “central reason” test, it held that substantial evidence supports the view that the persecutors were primarily motivated by personal/professional retaliation tied to Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s illegal operations, not by the petitioners’ campaign activity. Although a persecutor can have multiple motives, the record here showed the political motive was tangential or subordinate.

For students learning to parse nexus issues, this opinion illustrates three practical lessons anchored in the court’s reasoning: (1) focus on contemporaneous statements and conduct by persecutors—explicit attributions of motive are highly persuasive; (2) analyze timing and causation—an escalation tied to a non‑political event undercuts claims the harm was politically motivated; and (3) distinguish earlier, generalized political threats from later, particularized reprisals—absence of evidence linking actors across those phases can be fatal to a political‑opinion nexus. Finally, remember the appellate constraint: where the BIA’s factual inferences are supported by substantial evidence, the court will not reweigh competing inferences to find a protected‑motive nexus.

# Government Unable-or-Unwilling Requirement and Internal Relocation

This chapter explains how the Tenth Circuit analyzed two related asylum questions in Jimenez v. Bondi: (1) whether the Colombian government was “unable or unwilling” to protect the petitioners from private persecutors, and (2) whether safe internal relocation within Colombia was a reasonable alternative. Both inquiries turn on particular evidentiary showings and on the procedural posture established by the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) and the Immigration Judge (IJ). The court’s reasoning tracks controlling Tenth Circuit precedents and illustrates how courts weigh sparse reporting to authorities, allegations of state complicity, and evidence of safe alternatives inside the home country.

## Unable-or-unwilling framework

To qualify for asylum based on past persecution (or a well-founded fear of future persecution), an applicant who is targeted by private actors must show that those actors were able to harm them because the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors. The Tenth Circuit summarizes the inquiry as focusing on two kinds of government response evidence: (1) whether authorities stopped or tried to stop the persecutors, and (2) whether authorities offered direct protection to the applicant (for example, protective custody). Singh v. Bondi is the primary articulation cited in the opinion. Closely related to that analysis is whether the government had notice of the persecution and an opportunity to respond—without such notice, courts cannot determine whether the state would have acted. This notice element makes reporting to police an important, though not invariably dispositive, part of the applicant’s burden.

The courts treat a failure to report as undermining an “unable-or-unwilling” claim because, lacking a formal report, there is no administrable record of how police would have reacted. But the failure to report is not automatically fatal: an applicant can avoid reporting when doing so would be futile or dangerous. When an applicant makes that claim, the record must supply evidence supporting futility or danger; generalized assertions that suspects have connections to the government are generally insufficient absent corroboration of systemic corruption or proof that reporting would have posed a real, immediate threat.

## Petitioners’ limited outreach to authorities

The Jimenez petitioners made only minimal, informal efforts to secure government protection. They spoke to two individual police officers after incidents occurred; their testimony was that those officers advised them not to seek trouble and, in one instance, told them to leave. The petitioners did not file formal police reports, despite the fact that Jimenez and his father had government or law-enforcement ties that could have provided reasonable avenues for doing so. The IJ and BIA relied on these facts to conclude the government had not been given a meaningful opportunity to respond, and the Tenth Circuit accepted that view under the substantial-evidence standard.

The court emphasized that conversations with a couple of individual officers do not necessarily show the government’s institutional unwillingness to act. While an apathetic officer can sometimes indicate broader government failure, the opinion follows Osorio-Morales in requiring evidence of systemic failure rather than isolated unhelpful interactions.

## Futility/danger exception and why it failed here

Petitioners argued that reporting would have been futile or dangerous because their persecutors were “closely aligned” with parts of the Colombian government. The Jimenez court recognized that the futility/danger exception can excuse a lack of reporting, but it requires record evidence showing reporting would have been pointless or unsafe in the circumstances. General allegations of ties between persecutors and government officials are not enough.

In this case the record contained countervailing facts that undermined a futility claim. Notably, the property tied to the persecutor (Don Rafa) was already on law‑enforcement radar: authorities had attempted searches and seizures, and the property had been closed by the health department for criminal uses. Those official actions showed that the government was not entirely complicit or powerless with respect to the persecutor’s activities. Moreover, petitioners did not seek help beyond their immediate neighborhood or use Jimenez’s law-enforcement contacts; they also had relatives who still worked in government. Given those facts, the court concluded the record did not support that reporting would have been futile or dangerously counterproductive. The court therefore found substantial evidence to uphold the BIA’s determination that the government was not shown to be unable or unwilling to protect the petitioners.

## Countervailing evidence of government action

A critical piece of the court’s analysis was the existence of prior government attempts to address the criminal activity at the persecutor’s property. Searches, seizures, and an administrative closure by the health department indicated that governmental institutions were aware of and attempting to counter the illicit conduct connected to Don Rafa. That evidence undercut the petitioners’ central contention that the persecutors were effectively beyond the reach of the state because of political ties.

The court treated those official efforts as powerful evidence that the state could have acted to protect the petitioners if (1) the state had received adequate notice of the threats directed at them and (2) petitioners had sought protection through established channels. Thus the record supported the BIA’s conclusion that the government was capable of protecting the petitioners had it been properly engaged.

## Conclusion on government protection

Summing up the unable-or-unwilling analysis, the Tenth Circuit held that substantial evidence supported the BIA’s finding that the Colombian government was not shown to be unable or unwilling to protect the petitioners. The petitioners’ informal, minimal outreach to police, combined with the absence of formal complaints and the presence of prior government enforcement actions against the persecutor’s property, led the court to affirm the BIA’s conclusion. The futility exception was not borne out by record evidence sufficient to excuse the lack of reporting.

## Internal relocation standard

Even if an applicant establishes past persecution or a well‑founded fear, the government can rebut the asylum claim by showing, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the country. The regulation implementing this rule (8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)) and Rivera-Barrientos govern this relocation inquiry. The relevant question is whether relocation would be reasonable under the circumstances—that is, whether it would be both feasible (practically possible) and reasonable (not imposing unreasonable hardship or placing the applicant in danger).

In assessing reasonableness courts look to factors such as availability of family or community ties in alternative locations, evidence of safety while temporarily relocated, and whether the persecutors’ scope of influence reaches the whole country.

## Evidence supporting relocation in Jimenez

The record in Jimenez contained multiple facts supporting the BIA’s finding that internal relocation was a reasonable alternative. Family members and other relatives remained in Colombia without harm, indicating there were places within the country where the petitioners could likely live safely. The petitioners themselves had successfully stayed with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks without incident; that episode demonstrated that relocation to an area outside their neighborhood was practicable. Further, the threats were framed by the persecutors as orders to leave a particular neighborhood—there was no evidence the persecutors threatened the family to leave Colombia or that they maintained nationwide reach that would make any domestic relocation futile.

Although petitioners raised administrative, economic, and judicial concerns about moving within Colombia, the BIA and the court treated those arguments as insufficiently developed in the record and, more importantly, as unpersuasive in light of the tangible evidence of safe, short-term relocation and the continued presence of unharmed relatives.

## Result: feasibility and reasonableness of relocating within Colombia

Even assuming petitioners had a well‑founded fear of future persecution, the BIA’s conclusion that internal relocation was reasonable and feasible is supported by substantial evidence. The combination of family ties, a safe three‑week stay in the countryside, and the narrowly targeted nature of the threats led the court to affirm that relocation within Colombia was a viable alternative to asylum. Because petitioners failed to show both government inability or unwillingness to protect them and the impracticability of internal relocation, the Tenth Circuit denied the petition for review on the asylum and withholding claims.

Taken together, Jimenez illustrates two practical lessons for asylum applicants complaining of private-party persecution: (1) meaningful reporting to authorities or persuasive evidence that reporting would have been futile is important to the unable-or-unwilling analysis, and (2) concrete evidence of feasible alternatives inside the home country—family ties, demonstrated short-term safety, or localized threats—can defeat a claim that internal relocation is unreasonable.

# Disposition, Withholding of Removal, and Key Takeaways

## Disposition: petition for review denied
The Tenth Circuit denied Jimenez v. Bondi for failure to meet the statutory elements for asylum and withholding of removal. The court exercised jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252 and reviewed the Board of Immigration Appeals’ (BIA’s) decision under the substantial-evidence standard. Because a single-member BIA affirmed the immigration judge (IJ), the court reviewed the BIA’s decision while consulting the IJ’s fuller reasoning where helpful. The bottom line: the administrative record supplied substantial evidence supporting the BIA’s adverse findings, and the petition for review was therefore denied.

## Asylum denial rested on multiple independent grounds
The court affirmed the BIA’s denial of asylum on several independent bases, each supported by substantial evidence in the record:

- Past persecution: The court concluded that the threats, psychological stress, and related incidents the family endured did not rise to “persecution” under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The opinion applied controlling Tenth Circuit precedent holding that threats and emotional harm, standing alone and in the absence of more severe physical harm or a sustained campaign of abuse, typically do not meet the statutory threshold. Although the court considered the incidents cumulatively, it found the aggregate mistreatment insufficient.

- Nexus to a protected ground: Petitioners had argued political opinion as the protected ground. The court held that the record supported the BIA’s finding that the predominant motive for the threats and intimidation was retaliation for Jimenez’s confrontation with a criminal actor (Don Rafa) over property and interference with that actor’s revenue—not because of the family’s political activities. Because the persecutor’s motive was primarily nonpolitical, the required “at least one central reason” link between harm and a protected ground was not established.

- Government unable or unwilling to protect: The court found the record did not show the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect Petitioners. The opinion emphasized that Petitioners’ contact with law enforcement was minimal, that they did not file formal complaints beyond limited conversations with local officers, and that the government had previously attempted searches or seizures at the implicated property. The court treated a single, unhelpful officer’s comment as insufficient to demonstrate systemic government failure or complicity.

- Internal relocation: Even assuming some risk existed, the court concluded the record supported the BIA’s finding that internal relocation within Colombia was reasonable and feasible. The family had safely stayed with relatives in the Bogota countryside for several weeks, family and employment connections remained in Colombia, and the threats were localized to the neighborhood rather than to the country as a whole.

Because each of these findings independently supported denial and was sustained by substantial evidence, the asylum claim failed on multiple fronts.

## Withholding of removal: necessarily fails given asylum denial
The court explained that withholding of removal requires a higher showing than asylum: an applicant must demonstrate a clear probability (more likely than not) of persecution on a protected ground. Because Petitioners failed to establish the lower asylum standard—no past persecution, no nexus, and no showing the government could not protect them—their withholding claim necessarily failed as well. The court therefore denied withholding of removal for the same reasons that defeated asylum.

## Unappealed issues: CAT and due process claims not before the court
Two claims in the administrative record were not pressed on appeal: (1) protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT) and (2) the Petitioners’ asserted due process claim that the IJ violated their right to counsel. The appellate decision explicitly states those issues were not appealed, and therefore the court did not address them. Practitioners should note that claims omitted from briefing are generally forfeited on appeal.

## Practice lessons from the opinion
This opinion reinforces several concrete advocacy and evidentiary lessons for asylum and withholding practice:

- Document severe harm beyond threats. The Tenth Circuit reiterated that threats and transient psychological distress frequently do not meet the statutory definition of persecution. When possible, develop evidence of physical harm, sustained or escalating campaigns of targeted abuse, objective medical or psychological evaluations, and contemporaneous records that establish the severity and permanence of injury.

- Tie the persecutor’s motive to a protected ground with concrete proof. Vulnerable mixed-motive cases require evidence that a protected ground (e.g., political opinion) was at least one central reason for targeting. Mere membership in a political party or temporal overlap between political activity and threats is often insufficient. Seek direct statements, corroborating witness testimony, contemporaneous writings or communications from persecutors, or other evidence that shows the persecutor’s motive was political rather than principally personal, professional, or criminal.

- Report to authorities, or substantiate why reporting was futile or dangerous. The opinion stressed the importance of government notice and opportunity to respond. If an applicant did not make formal reports, counsel should either obtain and present police reports and other official responses or develop evidence explaining why reporting would have been futile or would have placed the applicant in additional danger (e.g., credible evidence of systemic police corruption, a documented pattern of retaliation for reporting, or prior incidents showing reporting produced harm).

- Corroborate claims of government complicity with systemic evidence, not just anecdote. Individual unhelpful encounters with officers are weak evidence of state-wide unwillingness or inability to protect. To show government complicity or systemic failure, assemble country-condition materials, independent investigations, multiple witness statements, media reports, or other documentation that demonstrates a pattern of official inaction or collusion rather than isolated officer conduct.

- Address internal relocation concretely. When the government asserts internal relocation is reasonable, respond with specific evidence showing why relocation would be impracticable or unsafe: proof of pervasive reach of persecutors, lack of economic means, absence of family or community ties elsewhere, evidence that threats extend beyond the local area, or evidence that relocation would impose intolerable hardship. General assertions of administrative or economic burdens, or hypothetical risk, are unlikely to overcome evidence showing safe relocation is feasible (for example, temporary stays with relatives, work connections, or lack of countrywide targeting).

These lessons reflect the court’s focus throughout the opinion on the quality and specificity of evidence required by the INA and Tenth Circuit precedent and the deference owed to the BIA under the substantial-evidence standard.

## Conclusion
Jimenez v. Bondi illustrates how multiple, independently sufficient shortcomings in an applicant’s proof—insufficient severity of harm, failure to show nexus to a protected ground, inadequate evidence the state could not or would not protect, and feasible internal relocation—can cumulatively doom asylum and withholding claims. Under the substantial-evidence standard, appellate review will not reweigh the record; practitioners must therefore build a record at the agency level that directly addresses each legal element with concrete, corroborated evidence.
