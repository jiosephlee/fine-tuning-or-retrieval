A Casebook Chapter on: Jimenez_v_Bondi

# Orientation to U.S. Protection Law: Asylum, Withholding, and CAT

This chapter introduces the statutory framework, institutional actors, and core doctrines that govern humanitarian protection claims in the United States: asylum, withholding of removal, and protection under the United Nations Convention Against Torture (CAT). A working command of these concepts — the statutory elements, burdens of proof, evidentiary tests, and the roles of the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), immigration judges (IJs), the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA), and the federal courts of appeals — is indispensable before analyzing individual decisions. We use the Court of Appeals decision in Jimenez v. Bondi (10th Cir. 2025) to illustrate how these rules are applied in practice and to show common pitfalls in framing a protection case.

## Statutory bases: 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101(a)(42)(A), 1158, 1231(b)(3) and 8 C.F.R. pt. 1208

The INA defines the starting point for asylum law. Section 1101(a)(42)(A) defines a “refugee” as any person who is outside his or her country of nationality and who is unable or unwilling to return because of past persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. Section 1158 implements asylum relief: it authorizes the Attorney General to grant asylum to eligible applicants and prescribes procedural rules for applications. Section 1231(b)(3) sets a different protection — withholding of removal — which bars return to a country where the alien’s life or freedom would be threatened on account of one of the same five protected grounds. Regulations implementing asylum, withholding, and CAT protections appear primarily in 8 C.F.R. part 1208.

Although the statutory categories of protected grounds are narrowly enumerated, the statutory language hides important complexities. “Persecution” is not defined in the INA; courts and the BIA have developed the concept through precedent and regulation. The well‑founded fear standard, the distinction between past and future persecution, the particularity and social visibility inquiries for “particular social group,” and the “central reason” test for nexus (see 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)) are all interpretive constructs that flow from the statutory core. The regulations codify procedures (for example, 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13 addresses changed country conditions and internal relocation) and define key terms in administrative practice.

Jimenez v. Bondi provides concrete applications of these statutory touchstones. The court framed the claim under § 1101(a)(42)(A) and § 1158 for asylum and under § 1231(b)(3) for withholding, and relied on regulatory guidance (for example, on internal relocation and changed country conditions) to resolve contested issues such as whether the petitioners suffered “persecution,” whether the harm was “on account of” a protected ground, and whether the government was unwilling or unable to protect them.

## Institutional structure: DHS, IJs, the BIA, and the courts of appeals

The contemporary adjudicative sequence for asylum and related protection claims typically begins with DHS. DHS (through U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services for affirmative asylum applications, or through immigration enforcement components for defensive applications raised in removal proceedings) institutes removal proceedings and the filing of applications. When the claim is raised defensively before removal, an immigration judge (an Executive Office for Immigration Review adjudicator) conducts the merits hearing, receives evidence, makes credibility and factual findings, and issues a decision.

Appeals from an IJ’s decision go to the Board of Immigration Appeals, which reviews legal and factual determinations and issues precedential or non‑precedential decisions. A single BIA member may affirm an IJ’s reasoned decision; where that happens, courts of appeals generally review the BIA’s decision but may consult the IJ’s opinion for its fuller factual exposition. The final step for judicial review is an appeal to the appropriate court of appeals under the judicial review provisions of 8 U.S.C. § 1252; such review is constrained by the statutory standards that govern what counts as final agency action and by limitations on claims that may be raised.

In Jimenez, DHS initiated removal proceedings, the IJ heard the family’s asylum and withholding applications and denied relief, and a single BIA member affirmed. On appeal to the Tenth Circuit, the court reviewed the BIA’s decision under the familiar administrative‑law rubric: legal questions are reviewed de novo in many respects, but findings of fact — including credibility determinations and the existence of persecution — are reviewed under the substantial‑evidence standard. The court explicitly limited its review to the administrative record, consistent with 8 U.S.C. § 1252(b)(4)(A). This institutional path — DHS → IJ → BIA → court of appeals — is the standard procedural ladder for defensive protection claims.

Understanding the roles and incentives of each actor is important for litigation strategy. IJs and the BIA are fact‑finders operating within the Executive branch; they must apply legal precedents but are not courts. The courts of appeals play a supervisory role with deferential review on factual questions, which means that a well‑developed administrative record and targeted legal arguments are essential where the outcome hinges on fact‑intensive inquiries like nexus, persecution, and government protection.

## Relief versus protection: discretionary asylum, mandatory withholding, and CAT protection

Asylum, withholding of removal, and CAT relief are distinct remedies that serve overlapping but different functions.

Asylum is discretionary. An applicant must first establish eligibility by proving refugee status under § 1101(a)(42)(A) — a showing that the applicant was persecuted in the past or has a well‑founded fear of future persecution on account of a protected ground — and then the Attorney General (now acting through delegated officials) may exercise discretion to grant asylum. Because asylum is discretionary, adverse discretionary factors (e.g., criminal history, security concerns, delay in seeking protection) can defeat relief even when statutory eligibility is established. In Jimenez, the court’s denial of asylum rested on the conclusion that the applicants failed to meet the statutory eligibility thresholds; because the threshold was not met, the court did not reach the discretionary balancing inquiry.

Withholding of removal under § 1231(b)(3) is mandatory relief for noncitizens who meet the statutory standard: the government may not remove an alien to a country where the alien’s life or freedom would be threatened on account of a protected ground. The practical consequence is important: withholding protects against removal but does not confer the broader benefits associated with asylum (such as eligibility for adjustment to lawful permanent residence). The evidentiary standard for withholding is higher than for asylum — the applicant must generally show it is “more likely than not” that he would face persecution (or, under some caselaw formulations, that there is a clear probability of persecution) — and withholding, unlike asylum, is not discretionary; if the standard is met, the government must withhold removal.

CAT protection is distinct from both asylum and withholding. CAT obligations arise from treaty and implementing regulations (8 C.F.R. § 1208.16), and require the applicant to establish that it is more likely than not that he would be tortured if returned to the proposed country of removal. CAT protection applies irrespective of nexus to a protected ground and is primarily concerned with the nature and probability of torture by the state or persons acting with the state’s acquiescence. CAT relief is also non‑derivative and is available only when the statutory and regulatory standards are met.

Jimenez illustrates the differences in practice. The family applied for asylum (discretionary), withholding (mandatory if the higher standard were met), and CAT (separate standard and inquiry). The IJ and BIA denied all three; the Tenth Circuit addressed the asylum and withholding claims, rejecting them on statutory grounds (insufficient persecution, insufficient nexus, and insufficient evidence government was unwilling or unable to protect). The court did not need to reach CAT because petitioners did not press the CAT claim on appeal; but the opinion clarifies that CAT standards and withholding standards differ and require separate proof.

## Core burdens and standards: preponderance for asylum eligibility; well‑founded fear; more‑likely‑than‑not for withholding and CAT

Understanding burdens of proof and their substantive content is central to protection law.

Asylum eligibility requires proof by a preponderance of the evidence that the applicant is a “refugee.” That means the applicant must persuade the adjudicator that it is more likely than not, when all evidence is weighed, that the applicant meets the statutory refugee definition — either because she suffered past persecution on account of a protected ground, which gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of future persecution, or because she has a well‑founded fear of future persecution on account of a protected ground.

“Well‑founded fear” is a hybrid standard: it has a subjective component (the applicant sincerely fears persecution) and an objective component (a reasonable person in the applicant’s situation would fear persecution). Courts have interpreted “well‑founded” in different ways, but the operative practical rule is that an applicant can meet the standard either by showing past persecution (which creates a rebuttable presumption of future persecution) or by showing a significant possibility of future persecution. The Tenth Circuit in Jimenez emphasized that past persecution gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of future persecution, but the board and courts will carefully scrutinize whether the underlying harm actually constitutes persecution.

What counts as “persecution”? The INA does not define it, and courts have generally required something more than harassment, discrimination, or sporadic threats; persecution normally requires the infliction of suffering or harm considered serious and offensive — physical harm, prolonged detention, torture, or severe economic deprivation when inflicted for a protected reason. Courts evaluate incidents cumulatively; repeated non‑severe harms can, in aggregate, constitute persecution. But threats, standing alone, rarely satisfy the persecution element. In Jimenez, the Tenth Circuit concluded that the pattern of threats, a violent encounter at the factory, an assault on an employee, a menacing interaction with the petitioners’ son, and attendant psychological anxiety nonetheless did not rise to past persecution. The court relied on precedent holding that threats, even when repeated, and psychological distress alone are frequently insufficient.

Withholding of removal requires a higher standard of proof: the applicant must show it is “more likely than not” (i.e., a greater than 50% probability) that his life or freedom would be threatened in the proposed country of removal on account of a protected ground. This burden is commonly described as the “clear probability” standard arising from Supreme Court precedent. The higher standard reflects withholding’s mandatory nature and its narrower consequences (it prevents removal but does not afford the broader benefits of asylum). As a consequence, even applicants who meet the asylum standard may fail to meet the standard for withholding if the evidence does not cross the higher probability threshold.

CAT protection likewise demands a “more likely than not” showing that the applicant would be tortured if returned. But unlike asylum and withholding, CAT protection does not require a nexus to a protected ground; the inquiry focuses on the probability of torture, and whether the torture would be inflicted by or with the acquiescence of a public official. The scope of “torture” is defined in regulations and treaty practice and generally requires the intentional infliction of severe pain or suffering by or at the instigation of government actors.

In Jimenez, the court explained these burdens and their relationship: because the petitioners failed to establish asylum’s lower standard (no past persecution; no well‑founded fear on account of a protected ground), they could not meet the higher standard for withholding. The court did not decide CAT because petitioners did not press that claim on appeal; but it underscored that CAT would require a separate, non‑derivative showing (and a higher probability showing).

## Nexus and causation: “at least one central reason” and mixed‑motive cases

Establishing that persecution is “on account of” a protected ground — the nexus requirement — is often the most litigated element. The statutory text and caselaw require that a protected ground be at least one central reason for the persecution. Courts have made clear that the protected ground need not be the sole reason, but it must be a substantial and central motivating factor; the harm cannot be merely incidental or tangential to a dominant non‑protected motive.

When a persecutor has mixed motives, courts ask whether a protected ground was at least one central reason for the harm. The “at least one central reason” formulation (as recited in Jimenez) is a demanding standard. Evidence that the abuse was motivated primarily by personal disputes, criminal motives (e.g., extortion, property disputes), or general criminality will often undermine a claim of nexus to a protected ground. The record must include particularized evidence tying the persecutor’s actions to the protected characteristic: explicit statements, consistent patterns targeting others who share the protected trait, or credible inferences from objective circumstances.

Jimenez shows the application of this rule. The petitioners claimed persecution on account of political opinion — their membership in and campaigning for the opposition Liberal Party. The IJ and BIA found, and the Tenth Circuit agreed, that the petitioners’ targeting was predominantly a response to Jimenez’s legal intervention in a property dispute and to that intervention’s adverse effect on a criminal actor’s revenue and security. The escalated threats occurred only after Don Rafa (a narco‑trafficking leader) learned that Jimenez had investigated the property and allegedly caused searches and seizures. The men who threatened Jimenez accused him of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office, and the threats appeared tied to that alleged role rather than to his political activities. The petitioners’ continuing political campaigning earlier in the election — despite prior threats — and the temporal connection between the property dispute and the intensification of harassment further supported the agency’s conclusion that political opinion was tangential. The decision illustrates how courts weigh motive evidence and why generalized assertions of political views, without a clear causal connection to the persecutor’s conduct, often fail.

## Government protection: reporting, notice, futility, and systemic failure

A required element of many asylum and withholding claims is that the persecution is inflicted by actors the state is unable or unwilling to control. The “unable or unwilling” inquiry considers whether the national government could have protected the applicant and whether the applicant sought state protection or whether doing so would have been futile or dangerous.

Key components of the government‑protection analysis:

- Notice and opportunity to respond: Courts look for evidence that the government had notice of the persecution and a real opportunity to act. If an applicant never reported the persecution, the agency has no record that the government had an opportunity to intervene, and courts will give weight to the absence of reporting. That absence can be excused, however, where reporting would have been futile or dangerous.

- Evidence of police or judicial actions: The government’s actual efforts — arrests, investigations, protective custody, enforcement actions against perpetrators — are probative. Conversely, evidence of systemic corruption, collusion between officials and persecutors, or a documented pattern of non‑response can support a conclusion that the state is unwilling or unable to protect.

- Individual versus systemic failures: Isolated incidents of unhelpful local officers are less persuasive than widespread government inaction. Courts require a showing of systemic inability or unwillingness, or evidence that the applicant would face particularized risk and that reporting would not have been effective.

In Jimenez, the petitioners did approach police officers and received discouraging responses: one officer reportedly told Jimenez that “nothing could be done” and that the family should leave. Yet the court found those anecdotal interactions insufficient to prove systemic governmental failure. Moreover, important facts undercut the petitioners’ theory that the persecutors were aligned with the government: law enforcement had attempted searches and seizures at the property associated with the persecutor, indicating some degree of governmental action against the criminal enterprise. The petitioners also had not exhausted or documented attempts to seek help beyond their immediate neighborhood or via the petitioners’ law‑enforcement connections. The court therefore concluded the record supported the agency’s determination that the Colombian government was not unable or unwilling to control the persecutors.

This application shows two practical lessons: first, claimants should document efforts to seek official protection (police reports, victim‑witness statements, correspondence), or explain convincingly why such efforts were impossible or dangerous; second, generalized assertions of corruption or collusion require evidence of systemic failure or a reasoned explanation why the particular victim would not receive protection.

## Internal relocation: feasibility and the government’s rebuttal

If an applicant cannot show past persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution in the applicant’s home location, the government may rebut a presumption of a well‑founded fear by showing either (1) a fundamental change in country conditions such that the applicant no longer has a well‑founded fear, or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the country, if internal relocation is reasonable. The regulation implementing this rule is 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i), and courts analyze whether (a) alternative locations are accessible, (b) the applicant could reasonably settle there without undue hardship, and (c) relocation would meaningfully reduce the risk of persecution.

The relocation inquiry is case specific and considers ties to the proposed relocation area, availability of housing and employment, language and cultural barriers, and the reach of the persecutor. It is not enough for the government to say, in the abstract, that relocation is possible; it must show by a preponderance of the evidence that relocation is reasonable.

In Jimenez, the IJ and the BIA found that internal relocation was feasible. The petitioners had family in Colombia who remained unharmed, and Jimenez’s father continued to work in the criminal justice system, which made relocation to another area of the country more plausible. The family had in fact moved temporarily to the mother’s home in the Bogota countryside for several weeks without harm. The threatening men told the petitioners to leave the neighborhood but had not ordered them to leave the country. These facts allowed the agency and the court to accept internal relocation as a reasonable alternative, thus undercutting the petitioners’ fear of future persecution.

For practitioners and students, this emphasizes that demonstrating the unavailability of safe internal relocation often requires showing that the persecutor’s reach is nationwide, that the persecutor is aligned with authorities throughout the country, or that specific hardship (economic, administrative, social) would render relocation unreasonable.

## Derivative asylum; rider respondents; non‑derivative nature of withholding and CAT

Family structure matters in protection claims, but the rules differ for asylum, withholding, and CAT.

Asylum allows for derivative benefits: certain family members can be included on a principal applicant’s asylum application and derive asylum status if the principal applicant is granted asylum. Children, spouses, and qualifying dependents can be “rider respondents” in defensive proceedings; their claims hinge on the principal’s eligibility and the family relationship. In contrast, withholding of removal and CAT protection are non‑derivative in the sense that each person seeking those forms of protection must establish eligibility on his or her own. A parent’s success on withholding or CAT does not automatically cloak children or a spouse; each must demonstrate the higher requisite standard.

In Jimenez, the two minor sons proceeded as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s asylum application. That is why the Tenth Circuit’s findings against Jimenez and his wife on asylum eligibility directly affected the children’s claim to derivative asylum. Because the family failed to meet the asylum standard, the children could not derive asylum through Jimenez. With withholding and CAT, the court noted that petitioners had not carried their burdens and, because withholding requires a higher showing, the asylum failure logically foreclosed withholding. The non‑derivative aspect of CAT means even if an applicant’s family member applies successfully, any other family members seeking CAT relief would still need to establish the CAT standard individually.

For students, the distinction means that litigation strategy must treat family members differently depending on the relief sought: derivative asylum may allow economies of proof and a focus on the principal applicant’s case, whereas withholding and CAT pleadings must ensure each individual’s circumstances and risk are separately articulated and proven.

## Appellate review and practical consequences

Finally, remember how substantially deferential review affects case outcomes. Factual findings by the IJ and BIA — including credibility determinations, whether harm rises to persecution, whether a protected ground was a central reason for the harm, and whether internal relocation is reasonable — are reviewed under the substantial‑evidence standard. This means that the courts of appeals will not reweigh evidence or substitute their view for the agency’s where reasonable adjudicators could reach different conclusions. Thus, advocacy before the IJ and BIA should aim to build a clear, well‑documented factual record that ties the claimed mistreatment directly to a statutory ground and anticipates the government protection and internal relocation arguments.

The Jimenez opinion exemplifies these dynamics. The petitioners presented credible testimony and documentary evidence of threats and stress, but the IJ and BIA — and ultimately the Tenth Circuit — found that the evidence did not cross statutory thresholds. The decision reinforces several practical lessons: threats alone are often insufficient; demonstrating nexus requires concrete ties between persecutor motive and the protected ground; documenting attempts to seek government protection (or explaining why such attempts would have been futile) strengthens claims; and showing that internal relocation is infeasible requires persuasive evidence of persistent risk or lack of safe alternatives.

Conclusion

This orientation has laid out the statutory frameworks, the institutional processes, and the principal doctrinal tests that govern asylum, withholding, and CAT protection. Jimenez v. Bondi serves as a useful case study of how these rules operate — and where many claims fail. Mastery of the statutory definitions, the burdens of proof, and the evidentiary strategies to establish persecution, nexus, government protection failures, and the infeasibility of internal relocation is essential for both academic analysis and effective advocacy in protection cases.

# Elements of an Asylum Claim

Asylum doctrine is structured to answer a deceptively simple question: should a noncitizen who fears mistreatment if returned to her country of nationality be allowed to remain in the United States? The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) resolves that question by defining a “refugee” and by setting out the elements an asylum applicant must prove. This chapter explains those elements, the ways they interact, and how courts evaluate claims in practice. We use the Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi to illustrate how the doctrinal tests operate and why certain evidentiary gaps commonly prove fatal.

At the outset, a few framing points. Asylum is a discretionary remedy; an applicant must first show statutory eligibility before the Attorney General may grant relief. The applicant bears the burden of proving eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. The law recognizes two alternative routes to refugee status: a showing of past persecution (which creates a rebuttable presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution) or a showing of a well‑founded fear of future persecution without past persecution. Both routes require a nexus to one of the statutorily protected grounds. The BIA and immigration judges (IJs) make factual determinations under the substantial‑evidence standard, and courts reviewing the BIA will overturn factual findings only if the record compels a contrary conclusion.

## Defining a refugee under § 1101(a)(42)(A): past persecution or well‑founded fear on account of a protected ground

The INA defines a “refugee” as someone who is outside his country and “unable or unwilling to return” because of “persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution on account of” race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. That language yields several discrete doctrinal components: (1) persecution (past) or a well‑founded fear (future); (2) causal nexus to a protected ground; and (3) that the persecution is inflicted by the government or by private actors whom the government is unable or unwilling to control. An applicant who proves past persecution is entitled to a rebuttable presumption that he also has a well‑founded fear of future persecution. If an applicant cannot show past persecution, she may still prevail by establishing both the subjective and objective components of a well‑founded fear.

Jimenez illustrates the practical application of these components. The family presented evidence of threats, harassment, and one violent incident involving their child. The IJ and BIA found the family credible but concluded their cumulative harm did not rise to past persecution, and that the evidence did not establish a nexus to a protected ground or a lack of governmental protection. Because asylum eligibility hinges on these interrelated elements, failure on any single component is often dispositive.

## The three‑part past persecution showing: harm rising to persecution; nexus to a protected ground; by government or private actors the government is unable or unwilling to control

An applicant asserting past persecution must prove three things: (1) an incident, or cumulative incidents, that rise to the level of persecution; (2) that the harm was inflicted “on account of” (i.e., because of) a protected ground; and (3) the persecutor was either the government or private actors that the government could not or would not control.

Harm rising to persecution. Persecution is not precisely defined by statute, but courts require more than ordinary harassment, economic disadvantage, or sporadic threats. The relevant inquiry asks whether the harm inflicted amounts to suffering or harm that an objective observer would view as serious and offensive. Physical violence commonly meets this bar, but physical injury is not necessary; severe psychological harm, confinement, or prolonged, targeted abuse can also constitute persecution. Importantly, adjudicators assess incidents cumulatively: a series of threats and non‑fatal mistreatment may, when viewed together, reach the threshold even if no single event would suffice.

In Jimenez the court emphasized that threats alone frequently fall short. The family received recurring threats, a brochure calling for “death to snitches,” and a few harassing and violent confrontations, but no lasting physical injury. The Tenth Circuit relied on precedent holding threats and psychological stress, without more, rarely amount to persecution. Thus, even where applicants are credible and understandably frightened, courts often require either physical harm or a pattern of severe conduct to find past persecution.

Nexus to a protected ground. Persecution is actionable only if it is “on account of” one of the protected grounds. That means the protected ground must be at least one central reason for the persecution—not merely incidental or tangential. Showing the requisite nexus can come from direct evidence (persecutors’ statements indicating a political motive, for example) or from circumstantial evidence (timing, patterns of targeting of persons who share the protected trait, or contextual country conditions). Mixed‑motive situations are common: persecutors often have several motives. After the REAL ID Act, the causation standard requires that a protected ground be at least one central reason for the persecution. Put differently, the protected ground must play a central (not minor) causal role in the harm.

In Jimenez, the court concluded that the predominant motive was personal and economic retaliation for the applicant’s interference with a criminal’s business (a property dispute that threatened “Don Rafa’s” revenue and security), not political opinion. Although the family had been politically active, the escalated threats correlated temporally with the property dispute and included references to prosecutorial activity—evidence that the persecutors were motivated principally by Jimenez’s role as a provisional lawyer and his interference with criminal enterprises. The court therefore found the political‑opinion nexus lacking.

Government or government‑unable/unwilling private actors. The third prong asks whether the persecutor is the state, or private actors whom the state cannot or will not control. Where private actors inflict the harm, the applicant must show that a reasonable effort to seek state protection would have been futile, dangerous, or impossible—either because the state lacks the capacity to respond or because it is complicit. The key factual issues are whether the applicant reported the abuse and how the state responded (or would likely respond), and whether there is evidence of systemic failure rather than a one‑off indifferent officer.

Jimenez underscores how critical the reporting/notice inquiry can be. The applicant told a police officer about the threat; the officer replied that “nothing could be done” and suggested they leave. The record also showed government authorities had pursued searches and seizures at the relevant property and that some police activity had occurred. The court viewed the government’s investigative efforts against the applicant’s assertion of state collusion, concluding the record did not support a finding that Colombia was unable or unwilling to protect the family.

## Well‑founded fear: subjective and objective components; role of country conditions

If an applicant has not suffered past persecution, she may still qualify for asylum by showing a well‑founded fear of future persecution. This standard has both subjective and objective components. The subjective component requires that the applicant genuinely fears returning. That is satisfied by credible testimony that the applicant fears persecution. The objective component asks whether a reasonable person in the applicant’s circumstances would fear persecution—that there is a reasonable possibility of suffering persecution on account of a protected ground.

The objective prong is typically satisfied by showing a reasonable possibility of persecution, which courts have variously described as a “reasonable chance,” a “reasonable possibility,” or a nontrivial likelihood. Country conditions evidence—country reports, human‑rights documentation, expert testimony, and corroborating evidence—plays an important role in demonstrating the objective risk. Where an applicant has suffered past persecution, the BIA presumes a well‑founded fear of future persecution unless the government rebuts that presumption by showing a fundamental change in circumstances or that the applicant could relocate safely within the country.

Jimenez illustrates these dynamics. Because the court found no past persecution, the presumption never arose. The family therefore had to establish both subjective fear and the objective likelihood of future persecution. The court found no objective basis: the persecutors’ motives leaned non‑political, the government had taken some action against the criminals’ property, and the family had previously relocated within Colombia without harm—facts that undercut the claim of a reasonable possibility of future persecution.

A related point: withholding of removal requires a higher showing than asylum. An applicant seeking withholding must demonstrate it is “more likely than not” (a clear probability) that she would be persecuted if returned. If an applicant fails to establish a well‑founded fear for asylum, she necessarily fails to meet the withholding standard.

## Protected grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group (PSG), political opinion

The five protected grounds—race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, and political opinion—define the permissible reasons an applicant can offer for persecution. Race, religion, and nationality are typically straightforward categories. Political opinion and membership in a particular social group often raise the most contested legal and factual disputes.

Political opinion. To succeed on a political‑opinion claim, the applicant must show that the persecutor perceived her as holding a political opinion (this can include imputed opinions) or that she actually holds a political opinion that motivated the persecution. Courts look for evidence that persecutors targeted the applicant because of her political activities, affiliations, or expressions. Statements by persecutors referring to the applicant’s politics, signs of targeted attacks against a class of politically active persons, or temporal correlation between political activity and escalated harm can all support the required nexus.

In the Jimenez case, petitioners argued that campaign activity for a presidential candidate and membership in a political party established a political‑opinion nexus. The court acknowledged their political engagement but found the critical threats escalated after and because of Jimenez’s involvement in a property dispute with a criminal leader and his role as a provisional lawyer. Because the persecutors’ statements and the timing of the threats pointed to retaliation for interference with criminal activity rather than political beliefs, the political‑opinion claim failed.

Particular social group (PSG). PSG claims require not only that the group be cognizable under immigration law (generally defined by immutable characteristics, shared past experience, or characteristics fundamental to individual dignity), but also that the applicant be a member of that group and that the group be a central reason for the persecution. PSGs have been a litigation hotspot. As the Jimenez opinion shows, an applicant who settles on political‑opinion theory but fails to brief a PSG will forfeit that argument on appeal. Counsel should plead and develop any PSG theories at the administrative level and explain how the group is defined and why persecutors targeted the group.

Practical implication: when an applicant is targeted for overlapping reasons (political activity, social leadership, or a profession such as lawyering), counsel should identify alternative grounds and develop record evidence for each—by eliciting persecutor statements, showing patterns of attacks on similarly situated individuals, and using country‑condition evidence.

## REAL ID Act causation: “one central reason” and its application to mixed motives

The REAL ID Act clarified the causation standard for asylum claims. Under the Act, an applicant must demonstrate that the protected ground was “at least one central reason” for the persecution. This is a causation standard that recognizes persecutors may have multiple motives but requires that a protected ground be a central—not merely incidental—reason for the harm.

How this standard works in practice can be subtle. Evidence that a persecutor knew of an applicant’s political activity and expressed animus toward it is strong proof of political motive. But where the record shows stronger or alternative motives—economic gain, personal vendetta, criminal retaliation—the protected ground must still be shown to play a central role. The key questions are: what did the persecutor say? What actions were taken and in what context? Is there a temporal or causal link between the applicant’s protected characteristic and the mistreatment? Courts will examine both direct statements from persecutors and circumstantial patterns.

The Jimenez court applied this principle to conclude that political opinion was merely tangential. The persecutors’ references to “snitches” and to prosecutorial activity, the escalation of threats after Jimenez’s direct interference with the criminal’s property and the men’s explicit threat that the order came from Don Rafa strongly suggested that retaliation for legal/professional interference—rather than political activism—was the central motive. Even though the family had engaged in political campaigning earlier (and faced more general threats), the pivotal acts that escalated into targeted threats related to conduct that threatened the criminal enterprise’s business. Under REAL ID Act analysis, that dispositive context meant the political‑opinion claim failed.

Mixed‑motive cases are therefore often resolved on the relative strength of competing motives and the evidence demonstrating causation. Where protected‑ground motive is one of several reasons, an applicant should develop evidence that the protected ground was not subordinate—by eliciting overtly political statements, evidence that only politically active individuals were targeted, or documentary proof showing the persecutors’ political objectives.

## Interplay of burdens, presumptions, and the government’s rebuttal

Two important procedural rules shape asylum adjudication. First, an applicant bears the initial burden to prove eligibility by a preponderance. A showing of past persecution creates a rebuttable presumption that the applicant has a well‑founded fear of future persecution; the government then can rebut that presumption by proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, either a fundamental change in country conditions or that the applicant could safely relocate to another part of the country. Second, the government bears the burden of demonstrating that internal relocation is reasonable and that the applicant could avoid persecution by moving within the country.

Although these burdens seem straightforward, facts control. The government’s ability to rebut a presumption often depends on country‑conditions evidence, testimony about the applicant’s familial and social ties elsewhere in the country, and evidence of state responses to the persecutors. In Jimenez, the government’s position that internal relocation was reasonable was bolstered by the family’s successful temporary relocation to Jimenez’s mother’s home in the countryside, the continued safety of relatives who remained in Colombia, and evidence of government operations directed at the criminal property. Those facts allowed the BIA and the court to conclude that relocation would be reasonable.

Finally, appellate review matters. The BIA’s factual findings—about whether the harm rises to persecution, whether the government is unable or unwilling, and whether internal relocation is reasonable—are reviewed under the substantial‑evidence standard. Courts will not reweigh the evidence where the record supports the BIA’s conclusions. Jimenez demonstrates this principle: the family’s credibility did not compel a finding of persecution, and the appellate court deferred to the BIA’s factual judgments.

## Practical considerations for building an asylum case

The doctrine distilled above suggests several practical evidentiary rules for counsel and applicants:

- Gather specific evidence of persecutors’ motive: statements, notes, threats that reference political activity or protected status, or contemporaneous witnesses who heard the persecutors’ rationale.
- Document the severity and cumulative pattern of harm: medical records, psychological evaluations, police reports, photographs of injuries or property damage, and affidavits from neighbors or colleagues.
- Preserve evidence showing government notice and response (or lack thereof): police reports, written complaints, correspondence with authorities, and records indicating state investigations or their absence.
- Develop country‑conditions evidence that corroborates the objective risk; expert declarations can explain local politics, the power of criminal actors, or patterns of state collusion.
- If advancing a PSG claim, clearly define the group and develop evidence that persecutors target that group as such; PSGs require careful legal framing and factual development.
- Anticipate mixed‑motive arguments and be prepared to show that the protected ground was at least one central reason for the mistreatment.
- If internal relocation is an issue, document feasibility: family or community ties elsewhere, employment prospects, housing availability, and the geographic scope of the persecutors’ reach.

Conclusion

The asylum statute establishes a nuanced, narrative‑driven inquiry. Applicants must translate personal fear into legal elements: persecution (or a well‑founded fear), nexus to a protected ground, and the role or impotence of state protection. Courts evaluate these components holistically: credible fear alone is insufficient; the applicant must show that the fear rests on a legally cognizable ground and a realistic risk of future harm. Jimenez v. Bondi is a useful example of the interaction among these elements. There, credible testimony did not overcome the evidentiary gaps on persecution, nexus, and government protection; the Tenth Circuit therefore denied relief. Understanding the doctrinal tests and how they play out in practice is essential for effective advocacy in asylum proceedings.

# What Counts as Persecution?

This chapter examines the central but elusive question that arises in virtually every asylum claim: when does harassment, threat, or other mistreatment cross the line into persecution? The United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi (the opinion excerpted above) supplies the immediate doctrinal scaffolding for this inquiry. Jimenez illustrates how courts apply settled Tenth Circuit tests—drawing on Wiransane, Ritonga, Rivera-Barrientos, Karki and related precedents—to distinguish persecution from lesser wrongs, how adjudicators aggregate multiple harms, and what kinds of proof are necessary when physical injury is absent but psychological injury or threats are alleged.

The chapter is organized to explain the term of art “persecution,” the importance of cumulative assessment, the limited circumstances in which threats alone will suffice, the evidentiary challenges when reliance is placed on psychological injury rather than physical harm, the special weight given to family- and child-targeted incidents, and the contours of inter-circuit variation. Throughout, we use the Jimenez facts as a running example: repeated telephone and in-person threats, a threatening brochure, an assault on an employee seeking the family’s whereabouts, an attempted interference with the family’s child, and the murder of a fellow campaigner—but no physical injury inflicted on the petitioning family themselves.

## Persecution as a term of art: more than restrictions or sporadic threats; “offensive” harm

Persecution is a term of art in asylum law, not a synonym for any mistreatment or inconvenience. In the Tenth Circuit it requires "the infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ (in race, religion, or political opinion) in a way regarded as offensive," and must "entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty." That language, adopted from Wiransane and repeatedly applied in later decisions, emphasizes two linked points. First, the harm must be meaningfully serious—something beyond ordinary criminality, neighborhood disputes, or attenuated harassment. Second, the harm must be of a kind that an adjudicator would regard as offensive in the context of the protected characteristic alleged: it is harm targeted at the applicant because of who they are or what they believe.

Jimenez illustrates the application of that standard. The family received repeated threats, a brochure reading “death to snitches from Liberal Party,” and at least one violent in-person encounter in which men pointed guns and told them to leave the neighborhood. Nevertheless, the Tenth Circuit concluded that the cumulative harm—absent actual physical injury to the petitioners—did not meet the court’s definition of persecution. The panel emphasized the distinction between threatening conduct and persecution: threats may be alarming and destabilizing, but the key inquiry is whether they rise to the level of suffering offensive enough to meet the statutory standard. In Jimenez the court found the threats and associated incidents fell short.

For practice, keep in mind that persecution should be evaluated against the surrounding facts. The same verbal threat may be persecution where delivered by state forces to a marginalized political dissident with prior history of targeted violence, but merely criminal intimidation in local gang-controlled neighborhoods where the harms are not connected to a protected ground. The "offensive" component therefore is contextual: identity or opinion-based targeting, repeated or escalatory conduct, and the severity of consequences are all relevant.

## Assessing harm cumulatively rather than incident-by-incident

Adjudicators must analyze patterns of abuse holistically. Tenth Circuit precedent requires consideration of incidents in the aggregate because the cumulative effect of multiple smaller harms may constitute persecution even if any single incident would not. Ritonga emphasizes that adjudicators should not parse incidents in isolation; rather, they must examine frequency, escalation, and the totality of consequences to the claimant’s life.

Jimenez demonstrates the cumulative approach at work. The family experienced a string of events over a period of months—phone threats, the men’s first armed visit, a later armed search for the family, an employee assault, and an attempted interference with the child. The IJ and BIA evaluated these events jointly and so did the Tenth Circuit on review. Nonetheless, the panel concluded that even considered together the harms lacked the requisite gravity or offensive character: the threats had not culminated in violence against the petitioners, and the psychological effects, while real, were not shown to be sufficiently severe by the record presented.

When you analyze a new fact pattern, assemble incidents chronologically and assess whether they show a pattern of sustained, targeted coercion that materially altered the applicant’s life—loss of liberty, forced displacement, severe injury, forcible expropriation, or sustained attempts to terrorize. A checklist to apply in cumulative analysis: number and frequency of incidents; escalation from threats to physical acts; corroboration (witnesses, contemporaneous reports); adverse consequences (job loss, forced relocation, functional impairment); and the nexus to a protected ground. Cumulative harms that combine to deprive the person of basic security or liberty are more likely to be treated as persecution.

## Threats alone: when are they insufficient, and what aggravating factors matter (specificity, imminence, repetition, capability, accompanying actions)?

A core, recurring rule in the Tenth Circuit is that threats alone—without more—generally do not constitute persecution. Karki and other decisions reflect the view that while threats are a relevant part of the calculus, threats unaccompanied by further objective indicators of serious mistreatment usually fall short. But threats are not categorically irrelevant: their character and context determine their legal significance.

Several aggravating features can elevate threats into persecution-sufficient harm:

- Specificity: Vague or generic warnings are weaker than threats that name the target, specify a timeframe, or declare an explicit intention to kill or harm.
- Imminence: A threat that is both specific and immediately actionable—that is, signaling an impending attack—carries more weight than speculative or conditional threats.
- Repetition and duration: Threats repeated over time, showing persistence and an inability to escape them, are more threatening in the persecution sense than one-off utterances.
- Capability: The sender’s demonstrated capacity to carry out the threat—possession of weapons, control of territory, prior successful attacks—makes the threat credible and dangerous.
- Accompanying actions: Threats followed by acts such as assaults on property, kidnapping or murder of associates, or attempted abductions of relatives are more likely to be treated as persecution because they show willingness to act.
- Targeting of vulnerability: Threats directed at children, the elderly, or that leverage personal details (addresses, routines) amplify risk.

Applying these factors to Jimenez: some aggravators were present—the threats were repeated; men pointed guns; a colleague was kidnapped and murdered; assailants assaulted an employee and searched for the family; a man intercepted the younger son at school and put his hand over the child’s mouth. These accompanying acts and the murder of a nearby campaigner are significant aggravating facts. Still, the Tenth Circuit found that, as recorded in the administrative record, those facts did not cross the line into persecution for the petitioners themselves. The court stressed the absence of physical injury to the petitioners, the limited scope of the incidents, and the lack of certain corroboration (for example, evidence that the threatening group had already harmed or attempted to harm the petitioners) as reasons for finding the threats insufficient.

For practitioners, arguable persecution cases based principally on threats should emphasize specificity, implicated actors’ demonstrated capability, contemporaneous reporting, and any follow-through acts. Evidence that the persecutors have controlled territory, conducted prior kidnappings or murders, or made credible attempts to seize or harm the claimant increases the chance the threats will be treated as persecution.

## Physical harm not required: the evidentiary challenges of proving severe psychological harm

It is settled that physical injury is not a necessary component of persecution; psychological and emotional harm can suffice. But the Tenth Circuit’s jurisprudence imposes a demanding evidentiary standard when claimants rest their case primarily on psychological injury rather than on physical abuse. Cases such as Karki and Chhetri show that courts require persuasive, objective evidence demonstrating severity and functional impairment, not mere assertions of fear, insomnia, or stress.

In Jimenez, Petitioners described anxiety, panic attacks in a child, and disrupted sleep. The family endured stress and altered behavior (monitoring children’s routes, moving temporarily to a relative’s house). Despite that, the court characterized these psychological effects as insufficiently severe and insufficiently documented to constitute persecution. The decision recognized the reality of psychological injury, but emphasized that psychological harms are rarely, if ever, the sole basis for an asylum grant in the Tenth Circuit absent robust evidence.

What does “robust evidence” look like? Effective proof of severe psychological harm typically includes:

- Clinical documentation from qualified mental-health professionals (diagnoses such as PTSD, major depressive disorder), contemporaneous treatment records, and treatment plans;
- Objective measures of impairment: hospitalization for psychiatric care, prescriptions and compliance records, documented inability to work or care for self or family;
- Corroborating testimony that links psychological symptoms temporally and causally to the persecutory acts;
- Expert affidavits that explain the severity of the condition, its typical causes, and its expected persistence if the claimant were forced to return.

Absent this type of corroboration, courts will often treat complaints of fear, anxiety, and sleeplessness as insufficient unless they are extreme and clearly disabling. Practitioners relying on psychological harm should therefore secure early psychiatric or psychological evaluations and contemporaneous treatment documentation, and explain how the symptoms meet standard diagnostic thresholds and materially impair the claimant’s life.

## Family-targeted threats and child-focused incidents: how they factor into the aggregate assessment

Threats or acts that implicate a claimant’s family, especially children, can intensify the assessment of persecution because they bear on the severity and scope of harm and can demonstrate the persecutor’s willingness to attack noncombatant targets. Courts have recognized that an attack on a family member or a child can reflect a broader campaign of intimidation and may make otherwise equivocal threats more credible and severe.

In Jimenez, the attempted interference with the younger son—where a man put an arm around the child, covered his mouth, and told him to “run to your dad”—was a salient incident. The family reasonably perceived this as a threat to a vulnerable child, prompting them to relocate temporarily and ultimately to depart the country. Nevertheless, the Tenth Circuit found that, when combined with the other incidents, this child-focused incident did not tip the balance toward persecution. The court noted that the assailant did not abduct or physically injure the child and that the family had not been physically harmed despite other neighborhood violence.

When analyzing child- and family-targeted incidents in other cases, focus on two elements. First, the nature and severity of the action directed at family members: attempted abduction, injury, or murder of a relative is plainly more weighty than verbal intimidation near a school. Second, the effect on the family’s overall security and ability to remain: did the family have to flee their home or relocate long-term? Did a child suffer lasting psychological trauma or physical injury? Courts are more likely to find persecution where children are explicitly targeted in acts that demonstrate the persecutor’s operational capacity and intent to harm noncombatant family members.

Finally, evidence that a persecutor intentionally seeks to exploit a claimant’s emotional ties (for instance, threatening children to coerce political silence) will be treated as more aggravated than threats that merely happen to affect family members tangentially.

## Comparative notes: varying circuit approaches to threats and psychological injury

Although the Tenth Circuit’s approach—requirement of more than mere threats, close scrutiny of psychological-only claims, and an emphasis on corroboration—reflects mainstream federal adjudication, there is inter-circuit variation in both emphasis and threshold. Some circuits are stricter in requiring physical injury or clear evidence of an intention to carry out threats before treating them as persecution. Others are more willing to treat sustained, targeted threats—especially when combined with demonstrated capability and a credible history of violence against similarly situated individuals—as persecution without actual physical injury to the claimant.

The Tenth Circuit, as shown in Jimenez, falls toward the cautious side: threats are significant but normally insufficient standing alone; psychological harm must be well-documented; and the cumulative assessment must show that the applicant was subject to offensive suffering. The Seventh Circuit has likewise cautioned that a single unhelpful police response does not automatically prove systemic government failure to protect, undercutting claims based on threats without reporting or corroboration. Other circuits have sometimes accepted asylum where the record demonstrated that a persecutor had the capacity and propensity to carry out threats and where the threats were specific and imminent—especially where the claimant’s social or political visibility made them an obvious target.

For a practitioner or student, the practical lesson is to study the controlling circuit’s precedents and to tailor evidence-gathering accordingly: in the Tenth Circuit, prioritize concrete proof of violent follow-through or clinical corroboration of severe psychological harm; in courts that give greater weight to the context of threats, emphasize the persecutor’s demonstrated capacity and community-level consequences.

## Practical implications and a litigation checklist

Jimenez confirms that credible fear and even genuine psychological distress will not automatically equal persecution. To present the strongest possible claim where persecution is alleged but physical injury is absent, counsel should assemble a record that attends to both the objective and subjective elements of persecution:

1. Corroborate threats and confrontations: contemporaneous police reports, factory logs, witness affidavits, medical or school records documenting incidents involving children, photographs, or communications (texts, brochures).
2. Demonstrate persecutor capability and propensity: evidence that the group has engaged in kidnappings, murders, or control of territory; local press accounts or country reports showing similar attacks on political activists.
3. Document psychological harm: evaluations and diagnoses from licensed mental-health professionals, records of counseling or medication, evidence of functional impairment (inability to work, attend school, or care for children).
4. Show pattern and escalation: establish chronology demonstrating repetition, escalation in severity, and lack of effective remedy.
5. Report to authorities and document attempts to secure protection: police reports, record of contacts with prosecutors, and evidence that reporting was futile or dangerous if reporting did not occur.
6. Demonstrate nexus where required: connect the persecutor’s statements or conduct to the applicant’s protected characteristic (e.g., political activity), and counter alternative motives that might explain the mistreatment.

Conclusion

Persecution is a high-threshold claim with important remedial consequences. Jimenez demonstrates that even an assortment of threats, disturbing incidents, and attendant psychological harms may not be enough in the Tenth Circuit absent stronger corroboration of severity, demonstrable capability to carry out threats, or convincing clinical evidence of debilitating psychological injury. Adjudicators assess harms cumulatively, but the aggregated misconduct must amount to suffering "regarded as offensive" in light of the statutory purposes of asylum. For law students and practitioners the central tasks are doctrinally familiar—focus on severity, nexus, and corroboration—and factually exacting: assemble credible, contemporaneous, and clinical evidence to bridge the gap between alarming harassment and legally cognizable persecution.

# Causation and Nexus: Political Opinion, Mixed Motives, and Personal Disputes

Nexus — the causal connection between harm and a statutorily protected ground — is the lynchpin of many asylum and withholding claims. Congress and the courts have made clear that not every harm inflicted by nonstate actors or political actors qualifies as persecution on account of a protected characteristic. The REAL ID Act requires that a protected ground be “at least one central reason” for the harm. That language narrows the universe of successful claims: it rejects asylum theories in which a protected ground plays only an incidental, subordinate, or tangential role. This chapter explains the law of nexus, unpacks the “one central reason” standard as applied to political-opinion claims, and shows how factual realities — timing, perpetrators’ statements, comparative treatment, and overlapping motives — determine whether a petitioner can or cannot carry the burden. The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi provides a concrete illustration: on these facts, the court concluded that the petitioners’ political activity was not a central reason for the threats and harassment they suffered because the perpetrators’ statements and the escalation of threats were tied to a personal, economic, and professional dispute.

## The legal standard: REAL ID’s “one central reason” test and rejecting incidental, tangential, or subordinate motives

The REAL ID Act of 2005 modified the statutory structure for asylum nexus, adding the phrase that an asylum applicant must establish that a protected ground was “at least one central reason” for persecution. See 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i). Courts interpret that phrase strictly: a claimant cannot prevail if the protected ground was merely incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another motive for harm. The phrase does not demand that the protected ground be the sole or dominant reason; mixed motives are permissible. But it does require that the protected ground be central — a significant, principal, or core reason why the persecutor acted.

Because nexus is a factual determination, administrative bodies and reviewing courts apply substantial-evidence review to the Board of Immigration Appeals’ (BIA’s) and Immigration Judge’s (IJ’s) factual findings. The question on review is whether substantial evidence supports a determination that the protected ground was not a central reason. In practice, adjudicators look to the totality of the record to see whether the evidence shows that persecutors targeted the applicant principally for a protected characteristic, or principally for other reasons (e.g., robbery, revenge, economic competition, personal animus).

In Jimenez, the Tenth Circuit applied these principles. The petitioners were active in party politics (the Colombian Liberal Party) and had engaged in door-to-door campaigning for a presidential candidate. But the court upheld the BIA’s determination that the attacks and threats were principally tied to a separate, private dispute: Jimenez’s involvement in investigating and attempting to resolve a property dispute that affected a powerful local criminal leader (Don Rafa). The court emphasized that the threats escalated after Jimenez’s intervention in the property matter, that the perpetrators’ statements accused him of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and responsible for searches and seizures at that property, and that there was no evidence connecting the actor(s) who escalated the harassment to the earlier politically motivated threats. Those facts, the court said, supported the conclusion that political opinion played only a subordinate or tangential role.

For a practitioner or student, the lesson is straightforward: under REAL ID, a claim that political opinion caused persecution must do more than show the applicant held or expressed a political view. The record must show that the persecutor’s conduct was meaningfully motivated by that political view such that it was a central reason for the harm.

## Political opinion: actual versus imputed; evidentiary markers in the record

Political-opinion claims come in two main varieties: claims of an applicant’s actual political opinion (the applicant genuinely holds and manifests a belief) and claims of an imputed political opinion (the persecutor believes — correctly or incorrectly — that the applicant holds a political opinion, and persecutes the applicant for that perceived view). Courts treat both as cognizable protected grounds, but the evidentiary contours differ subtly.

An actual political-opinion claim requires showing the applicant expressed or acted on a political belief and that the persecutor targeted the applicant because of that belief. Evidence that favors an actual-opinion nexus includes contemporaneous expressions of opinion (campaigning, public statements), threats referencing political affiliation, patterns of targeting persons with the same opinion, or the identity and ideology of the persecutors (for example, an organized group that systematically targets supporters of a particular party).

Imputed political-opinion claims arise where perpetrators infer an applicant’s politics from association or conduct (for example, membership in a political party, employment in a prosecutor’s office that is seen as aligned with an opposing political faction, or association with a particular demographic group). The key in an imputed-opinion claim is proving that the persecutor perceived the political stance and acted on that perception. Here too, the persecutor’s statements, the context of the harm, and patterns of treatment provide support.

The kinds of evidentiary markers that adjudicators rely upon to determine whether political opinion was a central reason include: explicit persecutor statements linking the threats to political views; contemporaneous messages or pamphlets referencing a party or ideology; timing of the harassment in relation to political activity; and comparative treatment — whether others who shared the applicant’s politics were also targeted while others were not. In Jimenez, for example, the record contained a threatening brochure that read “death to snitches from Liberal Party.” That brochure, if tied to the later actors, could provide a strong textual link to political motivation. But the court noted that the later, more violent threats explicitly accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and referenced searches and seizures at a contested property; the identity of the threats’ sender and the timing — escalation after the property dispute — pointed more strongly to retaliation for lawyering and interference with criminal activity than to pure political animus. The court was careful to note the absence of evidence connecting the initial politically themed threats to the later private-actor threats.

Comparative treatment is particularly useful: showing that political actors or campaigners were systematically singled out (while non-political actors in similar circumstances were not) strengthens a claim. Conversely, evidence that the persecutors treated the applicant differently for non-political reasons undercuts a political-opinion nexus.

## Mixed motives: proving a protected ground amid criminal, economic, or personal disputes

PERSECUTION OFTEN HAS MULTIPLE MOTIVES. The mixed-motive framework recognizes that persecutors can act for mixed reasons — for example, an extortionist may target someone both to eliminate resistance to their economic control and because that person has acted politically against them. The REAL ID Act does not foreclose mixed-motive claims: the protected ground need not be the sole reason. But the protected ground must be a central reason.

To win a mixed-motive claim, the applicant must marshal evidence showing that the persecutor’s calculus included the protected ground as one of the principal drivers. Evidence that helps is direct statements by perpetrators acknowledging political animus, a consistent pattern of targeting persons because of political activities even where other motives are plausible, or contemporaneous indicia that political activity triggered the persecution (for example, political activity immediately followed by threats that explicitly reference the political activity).

Conversely, where a persecutor’s statements and the chronology of events point to an economic or personal motive — such as retaliation for intervening in a business, a land dispute, or professional actions that harmed the persecutor’s revenue — courts will generally conclude that the protected ground is not central. The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez is paradigmatic: the persecutor accused the applicant of being a “snitch” and of causing law enforcement searches at the persecutor’s property, the threats escalated after the applicant’s intervention in the property dispute, and there was no persuasive evidence linking the actors who issued the later threats to the initial campaign-related threats. Under those circumstances the court sustained the BIA’s determination that the perpetrators’ motive was primarily economic and retaliatory, not political.

A practical point emerges: in mixed-motive cases the adjudicator will attempt to parse causation from the perspective of the persecutor. Did the persecutor act substantially because of political reasons, or because of personal revenge, profit, or some other unprotected reason? Evidence that illuminates the persecutor’s subjective motive — perpetrators’ words, organizational communications, orders or directives from political actors, or contemporaneous threats — can tip the balance.

## When professional or personal entanglements eclipse protected grounds

Many asylum claims arise from events that look political at first glance but have deeper roots in professional or personal conflict. Professionals — lawyers, prosecutors, judges, human-rights workers, union organizers — often attract hostile reactions from criminals or private actors when they interfere with illicit activities. When the persecutor’s motive springs primarily from the applicant’s professional role or personal involvement in a dispute, a nexus to a protected ground is difficult to establish unless the applicant can show the persecutor’s animus was fundamentally political.

Jimenez’s work as a provisional lawyer and an intern with the national prosecutor’s office illustrates the problem. His involvement in resolving a landlord-tenant conflict and visiting a property connected to narcotrafficking led to threats and violence. The perpetrators labeled him a “snitch” and linked him to searches and seizures. Those statements and the timing of the harassment supported the conclusion that the persecutors sought to protect their revenue and criminal operations, and that their motive was retaliation for professional interference rather than politics. Even though Jimenez also engaged in partisan campaigning for a presidential candidate, the escalation that produced credible danger followed the professional entanglement — and the record lacked a link showing the persecutors’ actions were driven by the campaign activity.

When professional or personal motives eclipse political motives, claimants must be prepared to show why the persecutor’s animus nonetheless had a substantial political component. That may be possible where, for instance, the persecutor is an arm of a political faction seeking to eliminate opponents, or where the professional action was taken because of the applicant’s political alignment (for example, a prosecutor acting against criminals because they are aligned with the applicant’s party). But absent clear connections, adjudicators will treat such conflicts as non-political and therefore outside asylum’s protected grounds.

## Developing and presenting nexus evidence: best practices

Nexus is largely an evidentiary inquiry. Practitioners should aim to build a record that illuminates the persecutor’s motive, the sequence of events, and any connections between the persecutor and political actors. The following best practices, derived from applicable law and the lessons of Jimenez, will help counsel frame and prove nexus.

First, contemporaneous documentation is critical. Collect and preserve threats (letters, brochures, text messages, social-media posts), police reports, medical or mental-health records reflecting the timing and content of incidents, and any workplace or municipal complaints. In Jimenez, a threatening brochure saying “death to snitches from Liberal Party” was potentially powerful evidence of political targeting — but it needed to be tied to the actors who later made threats. Counsel should authenticate such materials and build a chain linking them to the relevant perpetrators.

Second, elicit and record persecutor statements. What an attacker says often controls nexus. Direct evidence — the persecutor shouting “we’ll kill you for being a [political affiliation]” — is highly persuasive. Where persecutors make statements tying the applicant to political actors or to conduct that the persecutor perceives as political, preserve that testimony and seek corroboration from witnesses. In Jimenez the perpetrators' statements accusing him of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and asserting responsibility for searches was a decisive factual marker supporting a nonpolitical motive.

Third, develop comparative and contextual evidence. Show whether others who shared the applicant’s political activities were also targeted, and whether non-political actors in similar situations were left alone. Country-condition evidence, local news reports, and human-rights organization documentation can demonstrate patterns of political targeting or, conversely, demonstrate that criminal groups retaliate against interference without political coloring. If perpetrators are known to align with a particular political faction, gather that evidence (links between the perpetrator and state actors, membership in paramilitary or political groups, social-media posts celebrating political leaders).

Fourth, obtain declarations from perpetrators or witnesses when possible. A sworn statement from a perpetrator — even post hoc — admitting political motive is powerful. Witness declarations from neighbors, co-workers, or victims who heard threats or saw targeting can corroborate an applicant’s story. Similarly, contemporaneous notes or travel records that show the applicant’s political activity and subsequent targeting (for example, a schedule of campaign events followed by threats) create a timeline that supports nexus.

Fifth, anticipate and address alternative motives. If there are plausible non-political reasons for the persecution (business disputes, criminal retaliation), the claimant must explain why political motive remains central. That may require showing that the persecutor invoked political language when making threats, that the political activity increased the risk, or that the persecutor targeted others primarily for politics. If the temporal sequence shows political activity preceded and was followed by isolated, politically framed threats (as opposed to threats that only arose after a private dispute), emphasize that chronology.

Sixth, authenticate and explain police responses or lack thereof. Reports to police can show notice and a potential state response. But poor or indifferent responses by individual officers do not automatically prove state inability or unwillingness to protect, unless there is systemic evidence. In Jimenez, the petitioners’ limited contact with police and the absence of evidence that reporting would be futile undercut the claim that the state was unable or unwilling to protect. Counsel should therefore document all attempts to seek protection, any institutional failures (patterns of impunity), and any evidence linking persecutors to state actors.

Finally, integrate country condition reporting and expert testimony where helpful. A seasoned country expert can explain the political landscape, the relationship between criminal groups and political actors, and whether particular threats are ordinarily politically motivated or merely criminal reprisals. That context helps evaluators interpret ambiguous facts.

## Practical application: an evidentiary checklist for nexus

To synthesize, a practitioner preparing a political-opinion nexus claim should assemble the following types of evidence and be ready to link each item to the persecutor’s motive:

- Contemporaneous hostile communications (pamphlets, texts, social-media posts) that explicitly reference political affiliation or express political animus.
- Perpetrator statements that reference politics, party labels, or the target’s political acts; contemporaneous witness statements corroborating those utterances.
- Chronology showing political activity followed by immediate, targeted reprisals linked to the same actors.
- Pattern evidence demonstrating that others with similar political views were targeted.
- Evidence of the persecutor’s organizational identity and ideological alignment (affiliation with a political faction or militia).
- Documentation of attempts to report to authorities, and evidence of systemic police failure or state complicity when arguing government inability or unwillingness to protect.
- Evidence rebutting competing motives (e.g., demonstrating that a property dispute was minor compared to the applicant’s substantial political visibility, or showing that perpetrators used explicitly political rhetoric).
- Expert and country-condition reports clarifying local dynamics and the significance of certain messages or actors.

In the absence of these elements, adjudicators will scrutinize whether the alleged political motive is central or merely incidental to another, more immediate motive. The Jimenez decision highlights that when the record reveals an intervening event (a property dispute) that precipitates the escalation, and when perpetrators’ statements tie harm to that event (blaming searches and “snitching”), the BIA and courts will often find nexus lacking.

## Conclusion

Nexus doctrine requires careful parsing of motive. The REAL ID Act’s “one central reason” test rejects thin or speculative connections between protected grounds and persecution. In practice, adjudicators look for direct statements, contemporaneous evidence, patterns of treatment, and clear temporal connections that point to political animus. Mixed motives are permitted, but the protected reason must be central, not marginal. When an applicant’s professional role or a personal dispute better explains the persecutor’s conduct, the political-opinion claim will likely fail unless the record convincingly ties the persecutor’s actions to the applicant’s political stance. Effective advocacy anticipates the government’s alternative explanations and is built on documented, contemporaneous evidence that makes the persecutor’s motive visible.

# Private Actors and the Government ‘Unable or Unwilling’ Standard

Private-party persecution is one of the most common bases for asylum claims in contemporary practice. The Immigration and Nationality Act contemplates relief for persons who are persecuted by non‑state actors, but only when the government of the applicant’s country is either unable or unwilling to control those actors. That limitation—the “unable or unwilling” prong—frequently decides cases that turn on factual nuance: Did the victim give the state notice and an opportunity to respond? Did the police respond in any meaningful way? Is the government’s failure to protect an isolated instance of indifference or evidence of a systemic collapse or collusion? This chapter explains the doctrinal test the circuits apply, how applicants must prove the government’s response (or lack of it), when failure to report is excused, how adjudicators distinguish isolated apathy from systemic inability or corruption, and how country‑condition materials fit into the analysis. The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi, decided October 7, 2025, provides a contemporary illustration of these principles in application.

## Doctrinal test: government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors; government offered protection

The statutory definition of a refugee requires persecution “by the government or forces the government is either unable or unwilling to control.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A). In practice, courts and the Board of Immigration Appeals have distilled that statutory phrase into two related inquiries when the alleged persecutor is a private actor. First, did the government stop or at least attempt to stop the persecutors? Second, did the government offer protection to the applicant in a form that would have been effective (for example, placing the applicant in protective custody or otherwise securing his or her safety)? If the answer to both questions is “yes,” then the government has satisfied the statutory requirement; if the answer to both is “no,” then the applicant may establish the third element of an asylum claim—governmental inability or unwillingness to control the persecutors.

The Tenth Circuit has repeatedly framed this inquiry as an evidentiary assessment of how the state responded to a claimant’s situation. In Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder—still the leading statement in this circuit—the court described the three elements of a past‑persecution claim, emphasizing that past persecution must be “committed by the government or forces the government is either unable or unwilling to control.” 666 F.3d 641, 645–46 (10th Cir. 2012). More recently, in Singh v. Bondi, the court articulated that two kinds of information weigh “heavily” in evaluating the government’s response: (1) whether the government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors, and (2) whether the government offered protection to the applicant, such as protective custody. 130 F.4th 848, 861 (10th Cir. 2025).

It is important to appreciate that these two inquiries are evidentiary rather than formalistic. There is no magic checklist that an applicant completes by merely filing a police report; the adjudicator must evaluate the quality and effectiveness of the government’s response. An arrest that leads to prosecution and removal of the threat will commonly defeat a claim that the government is unable or unwilling to protect. Conversely, superficial police gestures or words of sympathy without any investigatory or protective follow‑through may be insufficient. The distinguishing feature is whether state action meaningfully reduced the risk of harm to the applicant.

Application in Jimenez. The Tenth Circuit applied this twofold frame in Jimenez. The record showed that law enforcement had tried to address criminal conduct at the property tied to the persecutors—“the government was aware of the property at issue and had attempted several searches and seizures to address the ongoing criminal conduct there.” That affirmative evidence of government action undercut Petitioners’ contention that the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect them. Equally important, the IJ and BIA found no evidence that the government had offered protective measures to Petitioners because Petitioners did not seek help outside their immediate neighborhood. Taken together, substantial evidence supported the agency’s finding that the government’s response did not amount to an inability or unwillingness to protect.

## Notice and opportunity to respond: importance of reporting to authorities

Notice to the government of the persecution is central to the unable‑or‑unwilling inquiry. Courts require reasonable evidence that the state had both notice of the threat and an opportunity to act before concluding the state failed to protect. The logic is straightforward: without notice, the government cannot be faulted for inaction. Thus, an applicant who did not notify authorities faces an uphill evidentiary task.

The Tenth Circuit explained this principle in Singh: the inquiry “considers evidence about a government’s response to an applicant’s past persecution,” and “a key fact to the analysis is whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government.” 130 F.4th at 861–62. If an applicant reported and the government did nothing, that is powerful evidence the state is unwilling or unable to help. If the applicant did not report, the absence is not dispositive, but the applicant must explain why reporting was not done and the agency is entitled to treat the omission as evidentiary support for the government’s ability to protect.

This emphasis on reporting does not mean a claimant is strictly required to file a formal police report in every case, but the claimant must provide persuasive evidence that either reporting was attempted or that reporting would have been futile or dangerous. An applicant’s testimony that she called the police, made a complaint to local officials, or reached out to a prosecutor can be enough if corroborated, credible, and tied to subsequent lack of state action. Conversely, vague assertions that an applicant “didn’t think it would help” are weak evidentiary substitutes for an explanation grounded in facts—threats, prior reprisals, or governmental corruption—that would have made reporting pointless or perilous.

Application in Jimenez. Jimenez did tell at least one police patrol officer about the assaultive incident involving his son, and local officers allegedly told him “nothing could be done” and that his family “should leave.” But the IJ and BIA emphasized that Petitioners did not pursue help beyond those neighborhood exchanges or make use of Jimenez’s own law‑enforcement contacts. Because the record reflected only minimal, two‑officer level contact and no formal complaints or documented protective requests, the agency reasonably declined to infer systemic failure from an absence of evidence showing the government had notice and an opportunity to respond.

## When failure to report is excused: proving futility or danger with record evidence

Although reporting is important, failure to report can be excused when the applicant proves that reporting would have been futile or would have endangered the applicant. The futility/danger exception recognizes that some victims reasonably decline to engage authorities because the authorities are complicit with the persecutors, will retaliate, or have demonstrated a pattern of indifference that makes formal reporting useless.

The claimant bears the burden of proving futility or danger by credible evidence. Courts have identified several types of evidence that make a futility or danger claim persuasive: prior attempts to seek protection that resulted in harassment or worse; credible threats by persecutors warning the applicant not to report (especially if combined with evidence that police are known to collude with those persecutors); evidence of systemic corruption or collusion documented in independent country reports; and credible testimony that police explicitly warned the applicant that making a complaint would increase risk. Where an applicant can show that prior complaints produced no meaningful response—or where independent records show police tied to criminal organizations—an adjudicator may reasonably conclude that reporting would have been futile or dangerous.

The Tenth Circuit’s caselaw reflects both sides. In Aviles‑Gonzalez v. Garland, the court observed that “a failure to report can undercut a claim that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors,” but it is “[n]ot necessarily fatal” where the applicant proves reporting would have been futile. No. 23‑9547, 2024 WL 3066987, at *2 (10th Cir. June 20, 2024). Conversely, in Chhetri v. Rosen, the court upheld denial of relief where the applicant did not report threats and could not show why reporting would have been futile, even though the facts included arrest and detention. 844 F. App’x 23, 27–28 (10th Cir. 2021).

What constitutes sufficient proof of futility or danger? The answer is fact‑intensive. Examples of persuasive proof include: contemporaneous police reports showing no follow‑up despite obvious danger; medical and psychological evidence that the claimant was too traumatized to report coupled with objective indicators of government collusion; affidavits from credible witnesses who describe patterns of corruption or direct threats by officials; documentary evidence of official links between the persecutors and the government; or credible news and human‑rights reports corroborating allegations of impunity in the relevant location. Where applicants rely solely on generalized assertions of corruption or fear without documentary or testimonial support, adjudicators are likely to find the exception inapplicable.

Application in Jimenez. Petitioners argued that reporting would have been futile because the persecutors were “closely aligned and associated with the Colombian government,” and a local police officer allegedly told Jimenez he “should leave.” The IJ and BIA required more than that. The record contained evidence that the government had attempted searches and seizures at the property from which the persecutors operated—evidence that undermines the claim of futility. Because Petitioners did not provide stronger evidence of official collusion, prior reprisals for reporting, or independent documentation of systemic corruption in their locality, the agency’s decision that the futility exception did not apply was supported by substantial evidence.

## Evaluating government efforts: distinguishing isolated apathy from systemic inability or corruption

Adjudicators must distinguish between isolated failures of individual officers and systemic governmental inability or unwillingness. A single apathetic or unhelpful officer does not automatically mean the state is unable or unwilling to protect. The law requires evidence of broader patterns, unless the single encounter itself demonstrates official complicity or a direct instruction that reporting is futile or dangerous.

Osorio‑Morales v. Garland illustrates the point: the Seventh Circuit noted that while police apathy can indicate state unwillingness, “a one‑off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim.” 72 F.4th 738, 743 (7th Cir. 2023). The reasoning is practical: governments are complex institutions; a single negative encounter is consistent with routine bureaucratic failure. To establish state incapacity or collusion, applicants usually must present evidence showing that non‑response reflects a pattern: repeated refusals to investigate, a history of impunity in similar cases, evidence of official links to criminal groups, or macro‑level indicators that the rule of law is weak where the applicant lives.

What counts as meaningful government efforts? Arrests, prosecutions, convictions, seizure of criminal property, and documented use of protective services weigh heavily in favor of the government. Publicly accessible police reports, prosecutor memoranda, or court dockets that show investigation and prosecution are strong evidence that the government attempted to control the persecutors. Conversely, public reports from reputable organizations documenting widespread impunity, corroborated declarations by local residents or officials, or official admissions that the state lacks capacity in the region can establish systemic failure.

Evaluation of government efforts also depends on the claimant’s plausibly available avenues for protection. If an applicant complains only to neighborhood police and makes no attempt to report to higher authorities or prosecutors when practicable, an adjudicator may reasonably infer the applicant did not give the state a genuine opportunity to help. The converse is also true: if the applicant demonstrates that higher authorities are inaccessible, collusive, or ineffective, then localized reporting may have been all that could be done.

Application in Jimenez. The IJ and BIA treated the police response as isolated and limited: Petitioners told only neighborhood officers and did not seek help from other government channels despite Jimenez’s own connections in the criminal justice system. The record, moreover, showed state attempts to address the criminality at the property through searches and seizures. That pattern—some action against the property but no evidence the state refused to act against the persecutors as a policy—led the agency to conclude the government’s failures were not systemic toward Petitioners specifically. Under the substantial‑evidence standard, the Tenth Circuit sustained that assessment.

## Country conditions as corroboration; leveraging human rights and rule‑of‑law indicators

Country‑condition materials often play a pivotal role in prosecutions and asylum adjudications alike. Law enforcement records, news articles, human‑rights reports, State Department country reports, and NGO memoranda can corroborate an applicant’s account that the government is unable or unwilling to control non‑state actors. These materials are not a substitute for individualized proof, but they can place isolated incidents in a broader context and permit an adjudicator to infer systemic problems when local evidence is thin.

Useful country‑condition evidence tends to be specific and local. Generalized national statistics about corruption or crime are helpful background, but the most persuasive evidence links national trends to the applicant’s particular locale and persecutors. For example, a State Department human‑rights report that documents collusion between security forces and guerrilla groups in a claimant’s municipality is far more probative than a general crime statistic. Likewise, investigative reporting or court cases showing law‑enforcement officers in the claimant’s region were charged with collusion or obstruction goes straight to the futility question.

When assembling country‑condition proof, counsel should prioritize the following: contemporaneous government documents (police logs, prosecutor correspondence, court dockets), local and national news coverage of the persecutors or of official investigations, international and domestic human‑rights reports that address patterns of impunity or official corruption in the relevant geographic area, and expert declarations that can situate the events in a broader political and security context. Medical and psychological evaluations can corroborate trauma and the severity of threats, which in turn can support claims that the applicant reasonably feared reporting.

Evidentiary weight matters. Agencies evaluate country evidence for relevance and specificity and may afford greater weight to materials that are corroborating, contemporary, and causally connected to the applicant’s circumstances. Under substantial‑evidence review, courts will sustain agency credibility findings that run counter to country‑condition submissions unless the record compels a contrary conclusion.

Application in Jimenez. Petitioners did not place substantial country‑condition evidence in the record showing systemic collusion between the persecutors and the Colombian state in their particular locality; instead, the record showed government activity directed at the property where criminality was based. In that factual setting, national‑level claims of political alignment between members of criminal groups and high officials were insufficiently tied to the victim’s local circumstances to establish futility or systemic failure. The absence of compelling, locality‑specific corroboration contributed to the agency’s finding that the Colombian government was neither unable nor unwilling to protect Petitioners.

## Practical guidance: what an applicant should show and what counsel should assemble

The legal rules sketched above translate into practical tasks for counsel and adjudicators. If a claimant alleges private‑actor persecution and contends the state is unable or unwilling to help, the record should ideally include:

- Clear testimony and timeline of threats and events, identifying dates, locations, and the persons involved.
- Evidence of any reports made to police or prosecutors, including copies of police reports, complaint receipts, or correspondence with authorities. If a formal report was not made, a detailed explanation supported by corroborating evidence about why reporting was futile or dangerous.
- Witness affidavits (neighbors, family members, coworkers) corroborating the incidents and any interactions with police.
- Medical and psychological evaluations documenting the physical or mental harms suffered and, where relevant, documenting fear of reporting.
- Localized country‑condition materials—news articles, NGO reports, human‑rights documentation—that address patterns of impunity, police collusion, or the presence of criminal groups in the claimant’s municipality.
- Documentation of government action (or lack thereof) against the persecutors: arrest records, seizure orders, search warrants, prosecutor filings, or court dockets.
- Expert declarations when factual patterns are complex or when the nexus between national political dynamics and localized threats needs explanation.

Counsel should remember that an applicant does not have a formal duty to exhaust all government remedies in the country of origin; rather, the applicant must produce evidence adequate to show that the government had notice and an opportunity to act, and if not, to show why reporting would have been futile or dangerous. The better the documentary record on these points, the less likely a tribunal is to treat an appellant’s failure to report as dispositive.

## Conclusion: balancing individualized proof and systemic indicators

The “unable or unwilling” standard mediates between two competing concerns. On the one hand, it prevents the asylum regime from becoming a backdoor for ordinary criminal victimhood when the state remains capable and willing to protect. On the other hand, it recognizes that private‑actor persecution is often an intrinsically law‑enforcement problem, and that state impotence or collusion makes asylum protection necessary. Courts evaluate this balance through an intensely fact‑bound inquiry: whether the government tried to stop the persecutors, whether it offered protection to the applicant, whether the applicant gave the state meaningful notice and opportunity to respond, and whether country‑condition materials corroborate or undercut claims of futility and danger.

Jimenez v. Bondi exemplifies how those elements interact. The Tenth Circuit sustained the agency’s finding that evidence of some state action (searches and seizures at the criminal property), coupled with Petitioners’ limited engagement with authorities and lack of locality‑specific documentation of collusion, supported denial of relief. The teaching is practical and principled: claimants alleging private‑actor persecution must do more than describe threats and harms; they must situate those harms against the government’s actual, provable response or rebut the presumption that reporting would have been effective. When applicants and counsel assemble a contemporaneous, corroborated record—police reports, witness statements, localized country evidence—the futility exception can be proved. Absent such evidence, adjudicators are entitled to infer that the state either was able to help or was never given a genuine opportunity to do so.

# Internal Relocation and Reasonableness

Internal relocation—also referred to as internal flight alternative or internal protection—is a central doctrine in asylum and withholding-of-removal adjudication. It asks whether an applicant who fears persecution in some part of his country of nationality could avoid that persecution by reasonably relocating to another part of the same country. The doctrine occupies the point where the individualized facts of a petitioner’s claim intersect with generalized country conditions, and it often determines whether an asserted fear of future harm is “well‑founded” or whether a presumption raised by past persecution is rebutted.

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi (10th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025) exemplifies how courts apply the internal relocation inquiry in practice. There the court affirmed the Board of Immigration Appeals’ conclusion that the petitioners could reasonably relocate in Colombia, relying on family ties, a short safe stay the family had already undertaken, evidence of police activity against the persecutor’s property, and the absence of record evidence showing the persecutor’s national reach or governmental collusion. This chapter explains the regulatory framework, the allocation of burdens, the factors that determine reasonableness, the evidentiary avenues available to both sides, and practical lessons from Jimenez and related precedent.

## Regulatory framework: 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1) and (b)(3)

The regulatory baseline for internal relocation is 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1) (and related provisions). That regulation provides two principal defenses the government may use to rebut an applicant’s claim of a well‑founded fear: (1) a “fundamental change” in country conditions such that the applicant no longer has a well‑founded fear of persecution; or (2) that the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country of nationality and that it would be reasonable for the applicant to do so. The text gives the government the burden of proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, either a fundamental change or the reasonableness of internal relocation.

Section 1208.13(b)(3) contains the analogous instruction for applicants who seek withholding of removal: the effect of country-condition changes and internal relocation is accounted for in determining whether there is a “clear probability” the applicant would be persecuted. Thus internal relocation considerations may affect both asylum (a “well‑founded fear” standard) and withholding adjudications (a higher “more likely than not” standard), although relief standards and consequences differ.

The regulation therefore creates a two-step structure: an applicant must first establish refugee status—either by showing past persecution (which gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution) or by establishing a well‑founded fear without the benefit of that presumption. Once the applicant has done so, the government can rebut a claim by showing that relocation is reasonable (or that changed circumstances remove the fear). The gravamen of adjudication becomes reasonableness: would a typical, prudent person in the applicant’s circumstances be required to relocate, and, if so, could that relocation be expected to provide safety and basic subsistence?

Jimenez illustrates this regulatory structure. The Tenth Circuit laid out the regulation’s mechanics and upheld the BIA’s ruling that the government carried its rebuttal burden: the family had safely stayed elsewhere in Colombia, relatives remained in the country, law enforcement had attempted to act against the persecutor’s property, and petitioners did not present evidence that relocation would be unreasonable.

## Burden‑shifting: effect of establishing (or not) past persecution

Understanding who bears which burden at each stage is essential.

- Applicant’s initial burden: To obtain asylum, an applicant must prove by a preponderance of the evidence that he is a refugee—i.e., that he is unable or unwilling to return to his country because of persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution on account of a protected ground. That showing can be made in one of two ways: (a) by demonstrating past persecution, which creates a rebuttable presumption that the applicant has a well‑founded fear of future persecution; or (b) by demonstrating a well‑founded fear without the benefit of a presumption.

- Government’s rebuttal burden: If the applicant establishes past persecution or otherwise shows a well‑founded fear, the government may still prevail by proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, that circumstances have changed or that internal relocation is reasonable and would avoid future persecution. The regulation specifies that the government must produce sufficient evidence to persuade the trier of fact that relocation is both possible and reasonable.

- Applicant’s rebuttal-opportunity: If the government meets its rebuttal burden, the applicant may present counter‑evidence showing that relocation is not feasible or would not be reasonable under his particular circumstances (for example, because the persecutor has nationwide reach, because relocation would prevent the family from earning a livelihood, or because cultural or social barriers would make life intolerable in the proposed area).

The practical effect of this burden‑shifting is that a finding of past persecution gives the applicant a significant initial advantage. That presumption can be neutralized only if the government makes the preponderance showing that an applicant could reasonably relocate or that country conditions have fundamentally changed. In Jimenez, however, the IJ and BIA concluded that petitioners had not proven past persecution; even without relying on the rebuttable presumption, the BIA found that petitioners failed to show a well‑founded fear of future persecution and that, in any event, relocation would be reasonable. The Tenth Circuit accepted the substantial‑evidence determination.

A few doctrinal points follow from precedent and practice. First, the presumption from past persecution is rebuttable, not conclusive; the government’s showing need not prove the applicant’s fear will fail with absolute certainty, only that the applicant would be safe by relocating. Second, the ultimate question is objective: would a reasonable person in the applicant’s position be expected to relocate? The answer depends on the totality of circumstances, not on purely speculative or hypothetical hardships. Third, the allocations of burden are legal matters that parties must address in briefs and at hearing—failure to properly develop the record on these points can be fatal.

## Reasonableness factors: family ties, economic viability, safety, social/cultural barriers, and access to services

Reasonableness is built from multiple, interrelated considerations. Adjudicators weigh the totality of circumstances, with attention to a non‑exclusive set of factors that recur in the case law and in agency practice. The most important of these are family ties, economic viability, personal safety and the reach of the persecutor, social and cultural barriers, and access to essential services.

Family ties and social support. One of the strongest indicators that internal relocation is feasible is the existence of relatives, friends, or community networks in the proposed safe area. Family ties can provide housing, initial subsistence, and local knowledge necessary for integration. In Jimenez the BIA emphasized that family members remained in Colombia and that the family had safely stayed with Jimenez’s mother in the countryside for three weeks—facts that supported the conclusion that internal relocation was feasible. That kind of evidence is powerful because it demonstrates a concrete, immediate option for the applicant rather than a speculative idea of relocating somewhere unknown.

Economic viability. Relocation does not require an applicant to become wealthy or to retain his previous social status, but it must be reasonable in light of basic subsistence. The question is whether the applicant can secure housing, employment, or some means of support, or receive help from relatives. Courts have frequently found relocation unreasonable when applicants would face total destitution or when documentation, such as identity papers or work permits, would be impossible to obtain. Evidence relevant to economic viability includes proof of job prospects, prior experience working in other parts of the country, and any offers of assistance from relatives. The absence of any feasible means of sustaining oneself after relocation can render relocation unreasonable.

Personal safety and the persecutor’s reach. The core inquiry remains whether relocation would remove the risk of persecution. If the persecutor’s reach is geographically limited—gangs or criminal leaders who operate in a particular city or region—relocation to an area outside that sphere may be effective. Conversely, if the persecutor is a national organization, or if the persecutor has political or institutional connections allowing for pursuit anywhere in the country, relocation is likely unreasonable. In Jimenez the BIA relied on record evidence indicating the persecutor (Don Rafa) operated from a particular property and that law enforcement had attempted searches of that property, supporting the inference that the persecutor did not have nationwide reach.

Social and cultural barriers. Social stigma, caste‑like group ostracism, entrenched discrimination, language dialects, or social roles may render relocation unreasonable. For example, women who fear gender‑based persecution may face renewed risk in communities where gender norms facilitate abuse; LGBTQ applicants may be unable to hide their identity in some regions; ethnoreligious minorities may be known across broad geographic swaths. The relevant inquiry is whether the applicant, because of the protected characteristic that is the basis of the fear, would be unable to live safely and with minimal subsistence in the proposed area.

Access to services and institutions. Practical concerns—availability of schools for children, healthcare, identification papers, and police protection—matter. If relocation would place an applicant out of reach of essential medical care or would expose minors to educational disruption that the family cannot reasonably bear, these factors weigh against reasonableness. Courts also look at whether the government in the proposed safe area would be capable and willing to offer protection.

Adjudicators balance these factors. A single factor rarely decides the issue; rather, the question is whether, on balance, relocation would be reasonable for a person in the applicant’s circumstances. In Jimenez, the totality—family present in country, a prior safe stay with a relative, police efforts against the persecutor, and no convincing evidence of persecutor reach or governmental collusion—weighed in favor of reasonableness.

## Evidence of persecutor reach and the significance of safe stays elsewhere within the country

Two evidentiary themes recur in the relocation inquiry: establishing the persecutor’s geographic scope, and distinguishing temporary safe stays from durable relocation.

Persecutor reach. Applicants who can show that their persecutors are able to track and attack them beyond the immediate locality are in a strong position to resist a relocation defense. Evidence demonstrating national or transregional reach includes: testimony and affidavits stating the persecutor’s prior actions in multiple regions; documentation of the persecutor’s membership in national criminal organizations; news reports describing the group’s operations across the country; and expert testimony linking the persecutor to nationwide networks or to official structures that would facilitate pursuit. Conversely, evidence that the persecutor’s activity is localized reduces the applicant’s ability to resist a relocation argument. In Jimenez, the threats and violent incidents centered on a particular property and neighborhood; petitioners did not show that Don Rafa could or would follow them nationwide, and the record showed law enforcement had taken actions against the property—both factors supporting a finding that relocation could be effective.

Safe stays versus durable relocation. Courts often treat a prior safe stay in another part of the country as probative because it demonstrates in concrete terms the feasibility of relocation. But adjudicators must distinguish a temporary, short‑term refuge from a durable, reasonable relocation. The relevant inquiry asks whether the prior stay shows more than momentary safety; does the applicant have realistic prospects for long‑term residence and subsistence, or was the stay an ad hoc, non‑sustainable escape? In Jimenez, the family’s three‑week stay with Jimenez’s mother was relevant but did not stand alone; it combined with other evidence—family ties and available protection—to support a persuasive inference that longer relocation was feasible. If a prior stay is brief and unsupported by continuing family or economic ties, an IJ or the BIA may find it insufficient to establish that reasonable relocation is available.

Adjudicators therefore scrutinize the quality of evidence of safe stays and the persecutor’s reach. Applicants who rely solely on the fact that they stayed for a brief period in another town—without evidence of permanent housing, employment prospects, or continuing family support—will find such evidence less persuasive.

## Using country conditions and expert testimony to contest relocation

Because internal relocation inquiries often turn on country‑specific realities, country‑condition evidence and expert testimony can be decisive. Applicants and the government may present State Department Country Reports, human rights organization reports (e.g., Human Rights Watch, Amnesty International), local news articles, scholarly analysis, and expert declarations. The evidentiary role of such materials is to illuminate whether: (1) a persecutor has national reach; (2) local law enforcement is capable and willing to offer protection across regions; (3) structural obstacles (administrative, economic, social) prevent meaningful relocation; or (4) the government has the capacity to suppress the persecutor so that relocation will be effective.

To be persuasive, experts should not merely reiterate country‑report conclusions. Effective expert declarations explain how country conditions interact with the applicant’s particular characteristics. For instance, an expert can tie a persecutor’s operational footprint to particular regions, explain whether identification and employment documents can be obtained in rural areas, discuss whether victims of particular crimes risk stigmatization that would follow them across regions, or describe the practical limits of police protection in remote municipalities.

Jimenez shows the consequence of an underdeveloped country‑condition record. The petitioners relied principally on their personal testimony and asserted ties between the persecutor and the government, but they did not supply comprehensive evidence or convincing expert analysis to show systemic inability or unwillingness of government authorities to protect them countrywide. The BIA and the court found the record insufficiently developed to refute the government’s showing that relocation was reasonable.

Practical guidance on evidence:

- For applicants seeking to resist relocation, gather contemporaneous documentation: police reports, medical records, arrest reports, corroborating affidavits from neighbors or local officials, and evidence of threats indicating the persecutor’s broad reach. If collusion with government actors is alleged, evidence such as public records, investigative news reporting, documentation of official visits or contracts, or testimony about official benefactions to the persecutor may be relevant.

- Present expert testimony that connects generalized country reports to the applicant’s individual situation—e.g., explaining how internal displacement patterns or gang recruitment practices would make relocation impossible for someone of the applicant’s profile.

- Demonstrate why family ties are inadequate or why previous safe stays were not sustainable—e.g., show that relatives are unable to host the applicant long term, or that staying would expose relatives to reprisals.

- Anticipate government arguments about economic viability and produce evidence that obtaining housing, employment, identification papers, or accessing health care would be impractical in the proposed area.

## Procedural and strategic implications—lessons from Jimenez

Jimenez provides several practical lessons:

1. Develop the record on relocation early. Petitioners in Jimenez did not present robust country‑condition or expert evidence on persecutor reach, government collusion, or economic barriers to relocation. Because the government carries the burden to prove relocation is reasonable, an applicant who does not counter with concrete evidence weakens his position.

2. Use tangible indicia of inability to relocate. Generalized fears or assertions that a persecutor has “ties to government” will not suffice. The more an applicant can show documentary evidence, corroborated testimony, or technical reports illustrating systemic failure of protection or nationwide reach, the better.

3. Emphasize durable—not temporary—obstacles. Short, ephemeral stays in safe places are useful but must be shown to be scalable into sustainable, long‑term residence. Conversely, if an applicant has already tried to relocate within the country and was followed or threatened, this is probative against reasonableness.

4. Tie country‑condition evidence to the applicant’s particular protected characteristic. A country‑wide phenomenon (e.g., generalized violence) does not automatically make relocation unreasonable unless the applicant shows why that phenomenon will specifically endanger him because of his race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

5. Build evidence regarding government notice and response. Whether the government had notice of past persecution and what steps it took is relevant both to the separate “unable or unwilling” protection inquiry and to relocation: if the government has acted against the persecutor in some regions, that supports relocation; if the government is complicit or systematically corrupted nationwide, that undermines the relocation defense.

## Conclusion

Internal relocation is an objective, fact‑intensive inquiry that can determine the fate of asylum and withholding claims. It requires careful attention to statutory and regulatory commands, careful presentation of corroborating evidence and expert analysis, and focused proof of how generalized country conditions affect the applicant’s specific circumstances. Jimenez underscores that a relocation defense grounded in family ties, safe prior stays, and evidence of law‑enforcement activity can satisfy the government’s burden, while petitioners who rely only on generalized fear and limited testimony face a steep uphill battle. For practitioners, the lesson is straightforward: document, corroborate, and contextualize.

# Standards of Review and Appellate Procedure

This chapter explains how federal courts review agency decisions in immigration cases—particularly denials of asylum and withholding of removal—and how procedural doctrines such as issue preservation, the scope of the administrative record, harmless error, and remand interact with those standards. The Tenth Circuit’s opinion in Jimenez v. Bondi (No. 24‑9573) illustrates these concepts in a routine but instructive setting: the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) affirmed an Immigration Judge (IJ) and the court denied review under the familiar substantial‑evidence standard. Read together, the statutory text, circuit precedent, and the Jimenez opinion teach practical and doctrinal rules that govern appellate advocacy and judicial decisionmaking in removal cases.

## Jurisdiction and scope: 8 U.S.C. § 1252; consolidated review

Review of final orders of removal is governed by 8 U.S.C. § 1252. Section 1252(a)(1) provides that courts of appeals have jurisdiction to review final orders of removal, and § 1252(b)(9) centralizes review in the courts of appeals for “a petition for review of an order of removal” while limiting piecemeal collateral attacks. The statutory scheme creates a single consolidated vehicle in the court of appeals for challenges arising from a removal order (for example, denials of asylum, withholding, and protection under the Convention Against Torture), subject to particular exhaustion and jurisdictional provisions elsewhere in the INA.

Jimenez illustrates these jurisdictional mechanics. The petitioners asked the Tenth Circuit to review the BIA’s denial of their asylum and withholding claims. The court began by invoking § 1252 and circuit precedent (Singh v. Bondi) to establish that it had jurisdiction to entertain a consolidated review of the immigration claims presented in the administrative record. That is the ordinary route for challenging an IJ/BIA removal order: the court of appeals evaluates the administrative proceedings as a unit, not by treating each stage as a separate litigation.

A crucial corollary to the consolidated review is the limited scope of review: courts generally review only “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” 8 U.S.C. § 1252(b)(4)(A). That provision requires litigants to present their case to the agency and to rely on the evidence assembled in the administrative proceedings; it also constrains the court’s factfinding capability. If a party wants a court to consider material outside the record, the party must normally persuade the court to remand to the BIA so that the agency can receive and assess the new evidence. Jimenez follows this rule: the Tenth Circuit limited itself to the administrative record that the IJ and BIA had developed.

Practical point for advocates: because the court receives only the administrative record, litigants must present their best factual and legal case at the agency level and preserve issues properly—see the sections below on issue preservation and waiver.

## Substantial‑evidence review of BIA factfinding; deference on credibility versus sufficiency

The critical substantive standard in most immigration appeals is the “substantial evidence” standard. The courts of appeals review the BIA’s factual determinations under this deferential test: a factual finding is “conclusive” unless the record compels a contrary conclusion. As the Tenth Circuit explained in Jimenez (quoting Rivera‑Barrientos and other precedent), the reviewing court must uphold an adverse factual determination unless “any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.” In practice this means that the reviewing court does not reweigh evidence or substitute its judgment for that of the agency when the record supports more than one permissible view.

This deference governs credibility determinations and factbound questions—whether the petitioner suffered past persecution, whether a persecutor was motivated “on account of” a protected ground, whether government protection was unavailable, and whether internal relocation is reasonable. In Jimenez the BIA (and the IJ) found that, even when taken together, the threats and related incidents did not rise to the level of persecution; the Tenth Circuit upheld that decision because the record contained evidence supporting the BIA’s interpretation of the cumulative facts. The court emphasized that petitioner’s concession of no physical harm and the character of the threats made it reasonable for the agency to conclude the mistreatment did not amount to persecution.

But the substantial‑evidence standard does not apply to legal questions. Pure questions of law (for example, whether a particular legal test should govern or how statutory language should be interpreted) are reviewed de novo. That distinction—de novo review of law and substantial‑evidence review of facts—is central. When an outcome depends on the application of a legal standard (for instance, the legal meaning of “particular social group” or the interpretation of nexus requirements), the court will review whether the agency applied the correct legal test. When an outcome hinges on whether the petitioner’s testimony was believable or whether the incidents alleged meet a fact‑intensive threshold (for example, whether the harm is sufficiently severe or recurring), the court applies substantial‑evidence review.

Credibility findings receive particular deference because they are quintessentially factual. A credibility determination based on demeanor, internal consistency, and corroboration is reviewed for substantial evidence. If the agency’s credibility determination rests on a legal error—if, for example, the agency applied an incorrect legal standard or excluded relevant corroboration improperly—then the reviewing court will scrutinize that aspect as a legal question and may remand.

Jimenez demonstrates both points. The IJ found petitioners credible, but nevertheless concluded their harm did not rise to persecution; the court treated those factual conclusions as within the agency’s domain and sustained them under the substantial‑evidence standard.

## Reviewing BIA decisions with reference to IJ reasoning

The BIA’s opinion is the primary document for appellate review, but the administrative record typically contains the IJ’s decision, which often contains more detailed factual findings and reasoning. When the BIA issues a short opinion—affirming an IJ’s decision without a fresh, full exposition—courts routinely consult the IJ’s decision to understand the basis for the BIA’s ruling. Uanreroro v. Gonzales is the leading example and is cited in Jimenez: when a single member of the BIA “affirm[s] the IJ’s decision,” a reviewing court adjudicates the BIA’s decision but is “not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds.”

This practice has two consequences. First, the IJ’s written findings remain important even if the BIA’s summary affirms; a reviewing court will look to the IJ’s analysis to determine whether the BIA’s conclusions rest on substantial evidence or a legal mistake. Second, litigants must preserve arguments and develop the record at both levels: an argument not adequately presented to the IJ or to the BIA is unlikely to be revived on appeal simply because the IJ articulated helpful legal reasoning.

In Jimenez the BIA issued a summary affirmation, and the Tenth Circuit explicitly consulted the IJ’s fuller explanation to assess whether the BIA’s factfinding satisfied the substantial‑evidence test. That approach enabled the court to evaluate both the agency’s factual conclusions and its application of law without remanding for further explanation.

Practical point for students: when reading agency opinions, always read the IJ decision where present. It often contains the contours of the agency’s factual basis and legal reasoning and can be decisive on appeal.

## Issue preservation and waiver on petition for review

Issue preservation is a threshold matter in immigration appeals. Courts generally will not consider an argument raised for the first time on petition for review; a petitioner must present the issue to the agency (to the IJ and then to the BIA) so that the agency has an opportunity to address the matter. Section 1252(d) and related doctrines embody the principle that the administration should be allowed to address and correct alleged errors before judicial review. Failure to press a claim at the agency stage typically results in waiver and forfeiture on appeal.

Jimenez contains several concrete examples of preservation and waiver. Petitioners did not appeal the denial of protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT) or their due process claim, and they therefore “do not appeal” those findings to the court; the Tenth Circuit accordingly declined to consider them. Similarly, petitioners had not briefed an argument based on membership in a particular social group and thus the court limited its review to the political‑opinion nexus; the omitted argument was treated as waived. The court also noted that petitioners “haphazardly briefed” other points, which weakened their appellate claim. In short, a claim not pressed in the agency proceedings or not developed in the appellate brief is unlikely to survive scrutiny.

Practical advice for advocates: preserve every argument you intend to raise on appeal. Raise it at the merits hearing before the IJ, renew or reframe it on appeal to the BIA, and brief it carefully in the petition for review. If you abandon a claim at any stage, you should expect waiver.

A few nuances complicate the general rule. For constitutional claims and questions of law, some statutes and precedent ensure review even where an issue has not been exhaustively briefed, but those exceptions are narrow. Moreover, an agency’s failure to adjudicate a claim at all—or a jurisdictional error by the agency—may constitute grounds for immediate judicial review even if the party failed to preserve a particular argument. But as a practical matter, the preservation rule governs the majority of ordinary removal appeals.

## Harmless error and remand principles in immigration appeals

When the agency errs—procedurally or legally—a reviewing court must decide whether the error requires reversal or whether it is harmless. The harmless‑error doctrine asks whether the error affected the petitioner’s “substantial rights” or otherwise had a prejudicial impact on the outcome. If the error is harmless, the court may affirm despite the irregularity; if the error is prejudicial, the court will typically vacate or remand.

In immigration appeals, harmless error and remand analysis are informed by the substantial‑evidence standard. If the agency applied the correct legal standard and the record would have compelled the same outcome notwithstanding the misstep, a court may deem the error harmless and deny relief. Conversely, where the agency applied the wrong legal standard, conflated legal and factual questions, overlooked material evidence, or reached a conclusion unsupported by the administrative record, the court commonly remands for the agency to reconsider under the correct standard.

Jimenez illustrates these principles by the converse: the court found no legal error that would require remand; the agency’s factual findings were supported by the record, so no prejudicial error existed. By contrast, a case in which the BIA misapplies a legal test for persecution or nexus—e.g., by equating political motive with a mere “tangential” factor without explaining why evidence of mixed motives fails the statutory test—would ordinarily be remanded for further agency analysis.

Remand is especially appropriate where the BIA fails to address material arguments or omits necessary findings. Because the BIA is the agency with national jurisdiction and expertise, courts defer to its resolution of factual conflicts and its expertise in applying immigration law, but they also require reasoned decisionmaking. If the BIA’s opinion fails to offer an adequate basis for review (for example, if the BIA ignored an argument that could change the outcome), the appellate court will often remand rather than supply missing reasons itself.

Practical tips on seeking remand or affirmance on harmless‑error grounds:
- If you represent a petitioner, identify and emphasize in the record the evidence that would require reversal if considered properly. If an IJ or the BIA overlooks that evidence, argue that the omission was prejudicial and ask for remand.
- If you represent the government, demonstrate how any error was harmless because the record unambiguously supports the agency’s result (the Jimenez opinion shows that courts will affirm when the record contains evidence that reasonably supports the BIA’s conclusions).
- When seeking relief based on extra‑record evidence, present a convincing reason why the evidence was unavailable before the agency and why the BIA should consider it on remand.

Finally, courts sometimes address the interplay of discretion and remand. Asylum is a discretionary remedy; even when an applicant establishes refugee status, the Attorney General retains discretion whether to grant asylum. Courts generally lack authority to reverse or direct grant of purely discretionary relief, although they may remand for the agency to exercise discretion if legal or procedural errors infected the agency’s discretionary denial.

## Practical implications and closing observations

The Jimenez opinion is a representative example of how courts synthesize statutory text, agency findings, and record constraints to decide removal appeals. Several takeaways follow:

- Jurisdiction under § 1252 allows consolidated review of removal claims, but the court’s review is ordinarily confined to the administrative record developed before the IJ and the BIA.

- Fact‑intensive questions—credibility, past persecution, nexus, government protection, and internal relocation—are reviewed for substantial evidence. Appellate courts will affirm if reasonable adjudicators could reach the agency’s conclusion.

- Legal questions are reviewed de novo; when a legal error infects an agency’s factfinding (for example, an incorrect legal test), remand is usually required.

- Where the BIA’s opinion is terse and it “affirm[s] the IJ,” courts will consult the IJ’s fuller explanation to discern the basis for the agency’s ruling.

- Issue preservation matters. Claims not raised before the agency or not properly briefed on appeal are typically waived.

- Harmless‑error and remand doctrines are the mechanisms courts use to correct agency mistakes without overstepping. If the error could have affected the outcome, remand is the common remedy; if the error is harmless in light of the record, the court may affirm.

For students and practitioners, the practical lesson is plain: build a complete administrative record, raise and preserve every viable legal theory at each administrative level, and anticipate the deferential posture a court will adopt on factual issues. With those prerequisites met, appellate arguments can focus on legal error, inadequacy of agency reasoning, and instances where the record—rather than mere disagreement—compels reversal.

# Case Study: Canon Jimenez v. Bondi (10th Cir. 2025)

This chapter uses the Tenth Circuit’s decision in Canon Jimenez v. Bondi to examine how federal appellate courts apply the key elements of asylum and withholding law: past persecution, nexus to a protected ground, government unwillingness or inability to protect, and internal relocation. The court denied review of the Board of Immigration Appeals’ (BIA) affirmance of the Immigration Judge (IJ), concluding that substantial evidence supported the agency’s findings that the petitioners had not suffered persecution, that their mistreatment was not on account of a protected ground (political opinion), and that safe internal relocation and state protection were realistically available. The court’s analysis provides a compact example of how courts evaluate cumulative threats and psychological harm, mixed-motive claims, the importance of reporting to authorities, and the interaction between asylum and withholding claims.

## Factual narrative

The petitioners are a Colombian family—Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two young sons—who entered the United States in late 2022 and applied for asylum and withholding of removal. The facts the IJ and BIA credited, and the Tenth Circuit relied upon, are straightforward: the family had been active in local politics as members of the Colombian Liberal Party and canvassed for a presidential candidate in 2022. Separate from that political activity, Jimenez (a provisional lawyer and law student who had interned at the national prosecutor’s office) became involved in a civil dispute on behalf of a client seeking to reclaim rental property. The tenant turned out to be a man known as “Don Rafa,” described in the record as a leader of a narco-trafficking group with a local power base and with reported ties to prostitution, migrant day‑rental activities, and violent crime.

Beginning in late spring 2022 Jimenez received recurring threatening phone calls about his party activity. The threats escalated after his unsuccessful efforts concerning the property associated with Don Rafa. The family received an anonymous brochure reading “death to snitches from Liberal Party,” unknown assailants twice accosted Jimenez at the factory where the family worked—once holding him at gunpoint and ordering him to leave the neighborhood—and assailants later assaulted a factory employee while seeking the family. In a particularly alarming incident, a stranger put his arm around the petitioners’ younger son as he left school, placed a hand over the boy’s mouth, and told him to “run to your dad.” The family temporarily moved to Jimenez’s mother’s home in the countryside and ultimately fled to the United States after being told by a police officer that “nothing could be done” and that they should leave.

These facts raise a mix of criminal retaliation-type conduct (the property dispute and alleged threats by narco-affiliates) and politically connected activities (campaigning for a presidential candidate and membership in a political party). The legal question posed by the petitioners’ narrative is whether their mistreatment—and the risk of future harm—is persecution on account of a protected ground, whether the state is unable or unwilling to protect them, and whether they could avoid harm by relocating internally.

## Procedural history and posture

Procedurally, the petitioners were charged with removability, admitted the allegations of the Notices to Appear, and sought asylum and withholding of removal (they did not press Convention Against Torture protection on appeal). The IJ found the petitioners credible but denied relief: the IJ concluded that (1) the harm did not rise to past persecution; (2) there was no nexus to a protected ground (political opinion or a claimed particular social group); (3) the Colombian government was not shown to be unwilling or unable to protect them; and (4) internal relocation was feasible. The BIA affirmed on the same grounds, and the petitioners appealed only the asylum and withholding denials. The Tenth Circuit reviewed the BIA’s decision under the substantial-evidence standard and denied the petition for review.

This procedural posture matters: the IJ’s credibility findings were not disturbed, so the dispute turned on legal and mixed-fact issues—how to assess cumulative threats and psychological harm for persecution, how to determine motive (nexus), and how to weigh evidence of state response and relocation feasibility. Because the IJ and BIA are factfinders, the appellate court’s role was limited: it must affirm unless no reasonable adjudicator could have reached the agency’s conclusions.

## Persecution analysis: threats and psychological distress assessed cumulatively yet found insufficient

The court began with the threshold question for asylum through a past‑persecution route: did the petitioners suffer persecution? Per the statute and precedent, a refugee must be unable or unwilling to return because of persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution on account of a protected ground. A showing of past persecution gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of future persecution; conversely, a failure to show past persecution means the applicant must demonstrate a well‑founded fear of future persecution without the benefit of that presumption.

The term “persecution” is not statutorily defined, and the Tenth Circuit follows precedents requiring an “infliction of suffering or harm . . . in a way regarded as offensive” and more than mere “restrictions or threats to life and liberty.” The court treats incidents cumulatively—multiple non‑severe harms can, in combination, amount to persecution—but also recognizes that threats standing alone rarely meet that threshold. In Canon Jimenez, the court accepted that the petitioners experienced serious and frightening conduct (continuing threatening calls, a brochure threatening “death to snitches,” two armed episodes, an assault of an employee seeking the petitioners, and a child‑targeted intimidation). The IJ found the petitioners credible and the court accepted that finding.

Despite this accumulation, the Tenth Circuit concluded that substantial evidence supported the agency’s determination that the conduct did not amount to persecution. The critical points were that the petitioners conceded they suffered no physical injury and that the traumatic effects—sleep disturbance, panic attacks—while real, were not the type of severe, lasting physical or psychological harm that the court’s precedents treat as sufficient in the absence of physical harm. The court relied on its prior decisions refusing to equate threats (even repeated, serious ones) and consequent emotional distress with persecution when the record lacks more invasive or sustained physical harm or other indicia of systematic, severe mistreatment. Thus, this case illustrates how courts evaluate psychological harm: documentation of diagnosis, treatment, or functional impairment can matter, but generalized fear and stress following threats are frequently held insufficient unless accompanied by additional severe acts or systemic targeting.

Two pedagogical points follow. First, cumulative analysis matters: a single threat may not be persecutory, but repeated, escalating actions can be. Second, applicants who rely on psychological injury should support their claims with contemporaneous evidence—medical or psychological records, expert testimony, or objective corroboration—that shows the harm’s severity and lasting impact. Absent that, appellate courts are likely to defer to the agency’s finding that threats plus stress do not meet the persecution standard.

## Nexus analysis: criminal/professional conflict as the primary motive; political opinion deemed tangential

Nexus—the requirement that persecution be “on account of” a protected ground—is often litigated when persecutors may have multiple motives. The statutory and precedential test requires an applicant to show that a protected ground was “at least one central reason” for the harm. The court’s approach in Canon Jimenez demonstrates how motive is inferred from the totality of evidence: threats’ content, timing relative to political activity, any statements by the persecutors linking the attacks to a political stance, and whether the persecution escalated after non‑political conduct.

Here, the court emphasized several facts that undercut a political nexus. The petitioners had been politically active earlier and had received some early threats, but the more serious, escalated threats and episodes occurred after Jimenez became involved in the property dispute with the tenant identified as Don Rafa. The assailants explicitly accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for searches of the property. One of the brochures referred to “snitches from Liberal Party,” but the timing and the specific accusations by the attackers focused on alleged interference with Don Rafa’s business and security. The IJ and BIA concluded, and the Tenth Circuit agreed, that these facts show the primary motive was retaliation for Jimenez’s professional/personal conflict with a criminal actor, not political opinion. The political dimension—party membership and campaigning—was therefore “tangential and subordinate.”

The court also reiterated the mixed‑motive doctrine: an attacker can have multiple motives, but an applicant must show the protected ground was a central reason. Evidence that proves mixed motives tends to include direct statements by perpetrators (e.g., “we hate you for your support of X”), contemporaneous documents or threats linking the harm to the protected ground, or a context in which only those holding the protected attribute are targeted. In mixed‑motive cases, the analysis turns on which motive predominates, and the agency’s fact determination will be upheld if reasonable. In Canon Jimenez, the timing, the attackers’ statements, and the property‑specific escalation pointed toward private retaliation as the central motive.

For practitioners, this component of the case underscores the importance of eliciting and preserving evidence of the persecutors’ statements, threat language, and contemporaneous documents tying conduct to political activity. If a persecutor mentions politics, that strengthens nexus; if a persecutor’s statements relate to criminal retaliation, nexus to a protected ground becomes harder to establish.

## Government protection: minimal reporting; evidence of police action undermines claims of state complicity

For asylum based on persecution by private actors, an applicant must show the harm is committed by the government or that the government is unwilling or unable to control the persecutors. The Tenth Circuit in Canon Jimenez applied the familiar two‑part inquiry: (1) whether the government attempted to stop the persecutors or otherwise responded to complaints, and (2) whether the applicant sought and received protection or whether reporting would have been futile or dangerous.

The petitioners argued the state was complicit with persecutors or at least unwilling/unable to protect them, pointing to an officer’s discouraging response when Jimenez reported the incident and to political ties allegedly connecting Don Rafa to national actors. The agency concluded, and the court agreed, that the petitioners’ reports to authorities were minimal: they did not pursue complaints beyond a couple of officer interactions, and they did not produce police reports or evidence of systemic state complicity. Crucially, the record actually contained evidence that law enforcement had conducted searches and seizures at the property associated with Don Rafa, undermining the claim that the state was powerless or complicit. The court emphasized that a single discouraging conversation with an officer is not necessarily proof of systemic failure, and absent a showing that reporting would have been futile, applicants are generally expected to seek state protection.

This part of the decision highlights a central evidentiary rule: reporting to authorities matters. While courts recognize a futility exception—where applicants show that reporting would be ineffectual or dangerous because of state collusion—establishing futility requires evidence beyond the applicant’s subjective belief. Contemporaneous complaints, police reports showing refusal to investigate, affidavits showing threats by officials, country‑condition evidence of pervasive collusion, or prior unsuccessful complaints by similarly situated persons can all be persuasive.

In Canon Jimenez, the evidence of police investigative efforts against the property and the lack of comprehensive reporting by the petitioners left the agency free to conclude the government was neither unwilling nor unable to protect them. The court gave weight to the agency’s inference that, had petitioners sought help more forcefully or at higher levels, they might have obtained protection.

## Internal relocation: temporary move and family in-country support weighed against fear of persecution

The government may rebut a well-founded fear of future persecution by showing that internal relocation within the applicant’s home country is reasonable and safe. The two-prong test requires the government to establish either a fundamental change in circumstances or that the applicant could safely relocate.

The Tenth Circuit found substantial evidence supporting the agency’s determination that the petitioners could relocate within Colombia. The factual bases included the petitioners’ ability to move temporarily to Jimenez’s mother’s home in the Bogota countryside without incident, the presence of unharmed relatives in Colombia (including Jimenez’s father who continued to work with the criminal justice system), and the localized nature of the threats (the men told them to move out of the neighborhood rather than threatening them anywhere in the country). These facts suggested relocation was a realistic option and that the risk of harm was not nationwide or inescapable.

Relocation reasonableness depends on multiple considerations: the applicant’s age and health, financial resources, familial ties, evidence of the persecutor’s geographic reach, and country conditions. The Canon Jimenez decision illustrates that courts will consider whether the applicant actually attempted to relocate domestically or whether the record shows that others in similar circumstances live safely in other parts of the country. Here, the petitioners’ successful short‑term relocation bolstered the agency’s finding that relocation could mitigate risk.

For practitioners, when litigating relocation, it is important to document reasons why relocation would be dangerous or unworkable (e.g., pervasive national reach of perpetrators, family or economic ties making resettlement impossible, lack of access to livelihoods, or discriminatory practices in other regions). Conversely, if an applicant can show members of the same social or political group have been targeted nationwide, relocation is less likely to be considered feasible.

## Withholding claim’s dependency on asylum findings (higher standard)

Withholding of removal requires the applicant to prove a “clear probability” of persecution upon return—a higher standard than the “well‑founded fear” required for asylum. The Tenth Circuit reaffirmed the settled rule that failure to meet the asylum standard necessarily undermines withholding relief because an applicant who cannot satisfy the lower standard ordinarily cannot satisfy the higher burden. Thus, the court denied withholding as a corollary of its asylum ruling.

This point is doctrinally straightforward but practically important: asylum is both discretionary (if the statutory elements are met, relief is possible but not guaranteed) and uses the lower standard of proof. Because withholding provides mandatory protection only on a showing of a high likelihood of persecution, successful withholding claims typically require stronger factual records (more direct evidence of physical harm, systemic targeting, or unrebuttable country‑condition proof).

## What additional evidence might have altered the outcome

The outcome in Canon Jimenez turned on factual determinations—degree of harm, motive, government responsiveness, and feasibility of relocation—so different or stronger evidence could have produced a different result. The kinds of evidence that might have changed the adjudication include, but are not limited to, the following:

- Contemporaneous police reports or written complaints showing the petitioners reported threats and detailing official responses (or official refusals to act). Formal documentation of a complaint and a demonstrable refusal to investigate would bolster a claim of state unwillingness or inability to protect.

- Medical and mental‑health records diagnosing post‑traumatic stress disorder or other conditions directly linked to the incidents, together with expert testimony establishing the severity and expected persistence of psychological harm. Demonstrable, clinically significant psychological injury can convert threats-plus-stress into persecution in the right factual mix.

- Evidence of physical harm or more invasive attacks (assault with injury, abduction attempts, prolonged forced detention, torture, or forced disappearance) rather than threats and intimidation alone. Physical injuries or egregious mistreatment are more likely to cross the persecution threshold.

- Direct statements, messages, or documentation showing that the attackers targeted petitioners explicitly because of their political activity (e.g., threats that named the specific political activity and linked it to the reason for harm). Clear contemporaneous evidence that persecutors referred to political views or party membership as reasons for threats would strengthen nexus.

- Affidavits or testimony from witnesses (neighbors, employees, other political activists) that corroborate targeted political harassment or show a pattern of political actors being singled out, or evidence that other known Liberal Party members were attacked under similar circumstances.

- Country‑condition evidence showing systematic collusion between the government and the particular criminal group, or a pattern of police refusal to investigate crimes associated with that group. Reports from reputable NGOs, human-rights organizations, or governmental sources documenting state complicity in the persecutor’s region could support a futility claim.

- Documentation showing the perpetrator’s national reach or evidence that moving to another part of Colombia would not avoid the risk (e.g., affidavits that the perpetrators have connections throughout the country, incidents of cross‑regional attacks, or proof that the threats were not geographically limited).

- Proof that petitioners sought protection at higher levels (prosecutor’s office, national police headquarters) and were rebuffed, or that prior complaints by other victims were ignored or resulted in reprisals, demonstrating systemic inability to protect.

Collectively, this sort of corroborative, contemporaneous, and expert evidence reduces judicial reliance on demeanor and subjective testimony and makes it harder for an adjudicator to conclude that threats are merely localized criminal retaliation or that state protection was available.

## Practical takeaways for advocates and students

Canon Jimenez is a compact demonstration of familiar asylum law patterns and evidentiary weaknesses that frequently determine outcomes:

- Credibility is necessary but not sufficient. Even fully credible testimony will fail if the record lacks the particular legal elements—persecution, nexus, and government failure.

- Threats without physical injury can be persecutory, but bare threats + generalized fear often fail. Document and corroborate psychological injury and any physical incidents.

- Nexus is a fact‑intensive, motive inquiry; contemporaneous statements by persecutors and timing of escalation are crucial. Where criminal retaliation and political activity coexist, focus on evidence tying the harm to the protected ground.

- Report to authorities whenever safe and feasible; contemporaneous police reports and documentation of official inaction are powerful evidence of state inability or unwillingness to protect.

- Collect country‑condition materials and witness statements about state‑perpetrator relationships and the reach of criminal actors; these can support futility claims and rebut relocation arguments.

- Internal relocation arguments are fact sensitive. Show why relocation is not reasonable if you want to rebut the government’s relocation defense: economic barriers, lack of family or community support, occupational licensing barriers, or pervasive reach of persecutors can matter.

Finally, students should note how appellate deference to the agency’s factual findings operates here. The Tenth Circuit repeatedly emphasized the substantial‑evidence standard: so long as reasonable adjudicators could reach the IJ’s/BIA’s conclusions on persecution, nexus, and protection, the court will not substitute its view. That underscores the importance at the administrative stage of building a comprehensive, well‑documented record that anticipates and answers the agency’s questions about motive, harm severity, reporting, and relocation.

# Particular Social Group and Political Activism: Framing Alternative Theories

Asylum law requires careful legal framing and factual development to win relief. When a client has been targeted for civic or political activity—in communities riven by gangs, cartels, or corrupt officials—counsel must decide how to conceptualize the claim: as persecution on account of political opinion, as persecution on account of membership in a particular social group (PSG), or both. The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi (the opinion reproduced above) illustrates the risks of underdeveloped alternative theories. The court denied relief in part because the petitioners both failed to establish the required nexus to a protected ground and failed to develop or brief an alternative PSG theory. This chapter explains the PSG doctrinal tests, how they commonly arise in civic/political contexts, why alternative grounds must be preserved and developed, how imputed political opinion interacts with PSG claims against gangs and cartels, and how to plead and preserve mixed-motive cases strategically.

## PSG requirements: immutability, particularity, and social distinction

A “particular social group” is a statutory category of protected ground under the refugee definition. Courts have developed three central features that a claimed social group must satisfy: (1) immutability or fundamental characteristic; (2) particularity; and (3) social distinction. Each element is legal and factual; understanding what they require and why they matter is essential to drafting winners.

Immutability or fundamentality refers to a characteristic that a person either cannot change, should not be required to change, or that is so fundamental to human dignity that it is akin to an immutable trait. Sexual orientation is a paradigmatic example. But immutability also encompasses characteristics the person cannot or should not be required to alter: political belief is mutable as a matter of fact, but courts sometimes accept a PSG that is defined by conduct that demonstrates a protected characteristic (e.g., “former police officers”) when the conduct reflects a trait fundamental to identity or conscience. When asserting a PSG built on activism or civic leadership, counsel must show why the characteristic at issue meets the immutability/fundamentality prong—why the persecutor targets the claimant for something the claimant cannot or should not be forced to abandon.

Particularity requires that the group be discrete and definable as a group. The group must have clear boundaries so that members can be identified with reasonable certainty. Vague, amorphous descriptions—“people who oppose criminal organizations” or “social leaders” without more—risk being rejected as lacking particularity because they could encompass nearly anyone and do not identify an intelligible group. A successful PSG in a political context is often defined narrowly with reference to geography, specific political party or activity, or a distinct role (for example, “Liberal Party campaign volunteers in [named municipality] who engaged in door-to-door campaigning in 2022”). Narrow definitions help ensure the group is particular.

Social distinction is an empirical inquiry into whether the society in question perceives the group as a discrete class. This is not purely aspirational; adjudicators look to country conditions, media reports, community testimony, or other evidence showing that the relevant population recognizes the group as distinct. Evidence can be direct—testimony that perpetrators referred to the claimant by a label—or indirect—reports showing gangs target “community leaders,” news articles identifying municipal campaigners as targets, or expert evidence that civic activists are singled out in certain regions. Courts repeatedly emphasize that social distinction is a factual question and that a bare assertion without supporting evidence is unlikely to satisfy the requirement.

These three components are interrelated. A narrowly drawn PSG that reflects a fundamental characteristic and is recognized by the relevant society has the best chance of surviving adjudication. The Jimenez decision exemplifies the consequences of failing to present or develop a PSG theory: the petitioners “did not brief any arguments regarding the existence of their particular social group,” and the court limited review to their political-opinion theory. That omission proved consequential.

## Common PSG formulations in civic/political contexts (e.g., “social leaders”) and pitfalls

In civic and political contexts, lawyers commonly propose PSGs such as “social leaders,” “community organizers,” “local political activists,” or “members of [named party]’s grassroots campaign in [municipality].” These labels may capture the lived reality of vulnerability, but they also present pitfalls.

First, labels like “social leaders” are often too broad and indeterminate to meet particularity and social-distinction requirements. Who counts as a “social leader”? Does the label include anyone who speaks at community meetings, anyone who organizes a charity drive, or only those with a formal leadership title? Without a clear definition and supporting evidence that the society perceives and treats “social leaders” as a distinct class, adjudicators are likely to dismiss the group as amorphous. The Jimenez court explicitly noted that the petitioners “did not brief any arguments regarding the existence of their particular social group,” and the absence of such briefing allowed the court to avoid resolving a poorly defined PSG.

Second, PSGs tied to political activity can run into immutability problems: political opinions are mutable and voluntary. To overcome that, the PSG must be framed in terms of an immutable or fundamental characteristic or as a discrete role that members cannot or should not be compelled to abandon (for example, “Liberal Party campaign volunteers who engaged in door-to-door canvassing in 2022 in [neighborhood],” accompanied by evidence that the persecutors singled out such canvassers). Narrowing by geography, time, specific activities, or affiliation helps establish particularity and shows why members cannot feasibly dissociate to avoid harm.

Third, evidence must show social distinction. In gang/corrupt environments, persecutors may not use civic labels but may brand targets with epithets (“snitches,” “government collaborators”). Documentation that perpetrators labeled the claimant as a member of a specific group, or that community members recognized a discrete class of targets, strengthens the social-distinction showing.

Finally, watch out for groups defined by the persecutor’s motive alone—“people the cartel deems problematic” is circular and unconstitutionally broad. Instead, define the group in objective terms tied to immutable or fundamental traits or to discrete, recognizable roles.

## Preserving alternative protected grounds: briefing and record development

Jimenez is a cautionary tale about preservation. The petitioners primarily argued political opinion; they did not brief PSG theories and thus forfeited them. Federal courts will not consider an unbriefed theory on appeal, and adjudicators at the BIA and IJ level expect claimants to present and develop alternative grounds. Preservation requires more than a passing mention—it requires legal argument and factual support in the administrative record.

Record development must be active and intentional. If counsel intends to pursue a PSG theory, they should:

- Frame the PSG narrowly and precisely in written filings and at hearing. Avoid generic labels; define the group by specific traits (party affiliation, type of activism, geography, time period).
- Elicit and submit evidence showing why the group is immutable/fundamental, particular, and socially distinct: testimony about how neighbors, perpetrators, or local authorities identify the claimant; affidavits from community members; news articles; NGO reports; and country-condition evidence.
- Secure corroboration of key events. Police reports, contemporaneous complaints, witness statements, and documentation of threats or acts against similarly situated people are crucial.
- Use expert testimony where appropriate to show social recognition of the group, patterns of targeting, and the role of criminal organizations in political violence.

Failing to brief or document the PSG theory invites the result in Jimenez: the court declined to entertain a PSG claim that the petitioners had not argued or developed.

## Imputed political opinion versus PSG theories in gang and cartel environments

In contexts dominated by non-state actors—gangs, cartels, guerrillas—two doctrinal paths often compete: (1) the persecutor imputed a political opinion to the claimant (or perceived the claimant as cooperating with the government), or (2) the claimant belongs to a PSG (for example, “people perceived to oppose [criminal group]” or “people who cooperate with the state”).

An imputed political opinion claim focuses on the persecutor’s perception of the claimant’s beliefs. If a cartel or gang targets someone because it believes the person supports or cooperates with the state or a rival political movement, that targeting may be “on account of” political opinion even if the claimant never openly expressed such an opinion. The key is evidence that the persecutor’s actions were motivated—at least in part—by that perception. Statements by attackers, notes, pamphlets, or patterns of targeting campaign workers can show motive. The Tenth Circuit requires that a protected ground be “at least one central reason” for the persecution; in mixed-motive situations the protected ground must be a central, not incidental, reason.

A PSG theory in this setting might attempt to capture the same reality by defining the group as “persons perceived to be cooperating with the government” or “people who refuse to cooperate with the cartel.” These formulations can succeed, but they face particularity and social-distinction hurdles. “Persons perceived to be cooperating with the government” may be sufficiently particular if defined by geography or specific acts (e.g., “residents of [town] who reported kidnappings to local police in 2022”), and if evidence shows community recognition. But a PSG that is merely “people the cartel dislikes” is circular and unlikely to survive.

Which path to pursue depends on the facts and evidence. If attackers explicitly framed the victim as a “snitch” or a government collaborator, an imputed-political-opinion theory is natural and often more straightforward. If the claimant and others share a discrete, socially recognized status (e.g., “local election monitors”), a PSG claim may be preferable. Where possible, plead both and develop evidence for both: an imputation can often be shown with attacker statements, and a PSG with corroboration of social recognition.

## Strategic pleading in mixed-motive settings

Mixed-motive cases—where persecutors have both protected and unprotected reasons—are common. The governing rule is familiar: the protected ground need only be “at least one central reason” for harm. Strategically, counsel should aim to demonstrate protected grounds are central rather than incidental.

Begin pleadings by asserting all plausible protected grounds: political opinion (actual and imputed), PSG, and in appropriate cases race, religion, or nationality. Carefully define any PSG alternatives to satisfy immutability, particularity, and social distinction. Use a layered approach to the record:

- Collect contemporaneous statements by perpetrators (threat letters, pamphlets, recorded threats) that reference political alignment or label victims (e.g., “death to snitches from Liberal Party,” as in Jimenez). Such statements are powerful on both political-opinion and imputation theories.
- Document patterns of targeting: others affiliated with the same party, campaign, or role who were threatened, attacked, kidnapped, or murdered.
- Submit police reports and correspondence demonstrating reporting and the government’s response (or lack thereof). If the government discouraged reporting or law enforcement is linked to persecutors, collect evidence demonstrating systemic failure.
- Use affidavits and expert reports describing local dynamics and social perceptions to satisfy social-distinction and demonstrate the persecutor’s motives.

Finally, anticipate and counter obvious defenses. If the persecutor will argue the motive was private (a personal vendetta or property dispute), gather evidence tying the dispute to political activity or showing the property dispute was a pretext for targeting a political actor. In Jimenez, the court credited evidence that the escalation followed a property dispute with a local narco leader and found the predominant motive economic and personal; had counsel developed a tighter PSG or adducing stronger evidence that the threats were politically motivated (for example, contemporaneous statements tying the threats to campaign activity or a pattern of targeting campaigners), the result might have differed.

Conclusion — Practical checklist

- From the outset, decide whether to pursue political-opinion claims, PSG claims, or both. Preserve each theory in pleadings and at hearing.
- If asserting a PSG, define it narrowly and tie it to immutability/fundamentality, particularity, and social distinction. Avoid labels without definition.
- Develop the record: contemporaneous documentation, witness statements, police reports, country conditions, and expert testimony are often determinative.
- Treat imputed political opinion as a distinct and often powerful route in gang/cartel settings; plead it alongside PSG theories where appropriate.
- In mixed-motive cases, assemble evidence that the protected ground was “at least one central reason” for the harm. Perpetrator statements and pattern evidence are key.
- Preserve arguments and develop factual support at the administrative level; appellate courts will not rescue unbriefed or underdeveloped theories.

The Jimenez opinion underscores a recurring lesson: asylum outcomes turn on doctrine and on disciplined advocacy. Clear legal framing, narrow PSG definitions, robust factual development, and preservation of alternative grounds can mean the difference between relief and denial.

# Evidence, Credibility, and Corroboration: Building a Winning Record

A successful asylum or withholding claim turns on two linked inquiries: whether the applicant suffered (or reasonably fears) persecution, and whether that persecution is connected to a protected ground. Those legal questions are litigated through testimonial narrative and documentary proof. Courts give substantial deference to the Board of Immigration Appeals’ factual findings, so practitioners must assemble a record that not only persuades an adjudicator at the IJ level, but also survives the "substantial evidence" review at the BIA and the courts of appeals. The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi, decided on October 7, 2025, illustrates how gaps in reporting, corroboration, nexus proof, and evidence of failed internal relocation undermine claims even when petitioners are found credible. This chapter converts doctrine into practice: what evidence moves the needle on each legal element, how to develop and present it, and how to anticipate adverse findings.

Effective advocacy begins with understanding the legal standards the adjudicator will apply. As the Tenth Circuit reiterated, asylum applicants must prove refugee status by a preponderance of the evidence—either by establishing past persecution (which raises a rebuttable presumption of future persecution) or by showing a well‑founded fear of future persecution on account of a protected ground such as political opinion. The inquiry has three core components: (1) whether the harm rises to the level of persecution; (2) whether the harm is on account of one of the statutorily protected grounds; and (3) whether the persecutor is an agent of the state or the state is unable or unwilling to control the persecutor. Each element has evidentiary consequences that inform what documents, evaluations, affidavits, and expert testimony will be needed.

## Corroboration expectations post-REAL ID: police reports, medical and psychological evaluations, affidavits

The REAL ID Act reshaped credibility and corroboration analysis by emphasizing that an applicant’s testimony may be sufficient alone unless it is inconsistent, implausible, or contradicted, and by authorizing adjudicators to require corroboration where the applicant’s testimony is lacking. But that does not mean corroboration is optional; rather, adjudicators now make an individualized assessment whether corroborating evidence is reasonably available and whether the applicant’s testimony, standing alone, meets the statutory standard. In practice, adjudicators are more likely to credit testimony supported by contemporaneous and independent documents.

Police reports and complaint receipts are among the most persuasive forms of corroboration when available. They do three critical things: (1) show the applicant gave the government notice (a relevant fact under the "unable or unwilling" analysis), (2) document the timing and content of threats or attacks contemporaneously, and (3) supply official identifiers—dates, complaint numbers, and officer names—that can be used to follow up. In Jimenez, the petitioners’ weaknesses included limited reporting: they had only anecdotal encounters with individual officers and did not present official complaint numbers or a sustained paper trail, a deficiency the court emphasized. When police reports cannot be obtained, counsel should document the reasons—e.g., offices destroyed during violence, officials refusing to accept complaints, credible threats against reporting—so the adjudicator can consider a futility or danger argument.

Medical and psychological evaluations give shape and specificity to claims of physical injury, pain, and psychological trauma—precisely the types of harms courts scrutinize when assessing whether the conduct rises to persecution. A credible clinician’s diagnosis (for example, PTSD, anxiety disorder, or a panic disorder), anchored by standardized instruments and by a clear causal nexus to the incidents at issue, materially increases the odds that an adjudicator will treat emotional and psychological harms as more than "subjective fear." Jimenez highlights the court’s reluctance to treat subjective sleep loss and panic attacks, unsupported by clinical assessment, as sufficient. A strong mental‑health report will describe symptoms, assessments used (e.g., CAPS, CIDI), history of treatment, functional impairment (work or school problems), and an expert opinion tying symptoms to the hostile acts; it should also explain why the symptoms would persist or recur upon return.

Affidavits and witness statements remain indispensable. Employers, neighbors, co‑workers, teachers, and local community leaders can supply contemporaneous detail—who said what, when, and where—that lends credibility to the applicant’s narrative. The best affidavits are specific: dates, locations, verbatim hostile statements when remembered, physical descriptions of assailants, and the affiant’s basis of knowledge. Where possible, affidavits should be notarized and accompanied by documentary corroboration (photos, receipts, text messages). The Tenth Circuit’s treatment of the brochure saying "death to snitches from Liberal Party" suggests that a single piece of documentary evidence, standing alone and not clearly tied to the alleged aggressors’ later conduct, may lack persuasive force; the record should therefore include proof the document came from the persecutor or was distributed in the same context as subsequent confrontations.

Practical steps for assembling corroboration: obtain and certify police complaint numbers and follow‑up letters; secure contemporaneous medical records and arrange mental‑health evaluations promptly; collect and notarize third‑party affidavits explaining personal knowledge; preserve digital evidence (texts, voicemail, social‑media posts) with metadata when possible; and provide translations and, where necessary, expert witness statements authenticating documents.

## Documenting government inability or unwillingness: complaint receipts, follow-ups, ombudsman or prosecutor outreach

The state‑protection inquiry turns heavily on whether the government had notice and an opportunity to intervene, and whether it did so effectively. Singh—cited by the Tenth Circuit in Jimenez—explains that evidence of governmental response (or lack thereof) is key: whether authorities stopped or tried to stop the persecutors, and whether protective measures were offered. The applicant therefore should build a record that addresses those two prongs directly.

Start with proof of reporting: formal complaints, receipts, police report numbers, names and ranks of officers, and copies of any letters requesting help. Follow‑up communications—emails to prosecutors, documented visits to a precinct, certified mail, and contemporaneous notes of conversations—demonstrate persistence and give the adjudicator a timeline to evaluate whether the state had a reasonable opportunity to act. In Jimenez, the court noted that petitioners “spoke to two individual police officers who said that Petitioners should not seek trouble,” but there was no evidence of formal complaints or systemic official inaction; that gap undercut their claim that the state was unwilling or unable to protect them. Where the police give unhelpful oral responses, counsel should memorialize those interactions in writing immediately and seek a written response or refusal.

When ordinary police reporting is fraught, applicants should document outreach to other state institutions: the prosecutor’s office, an ombudsman (procuraduría or defensoría), the Human Rights Unit, or national authorities responsible for protection programs. Certified copies of complaint filings with prosecutors, acknowledgment receipts, and any correspondence from oversight bodies demonstrate that the applicant sought protection through formal channels. If those offices decline jurisdiction or otherwise fail to respond, obtain written refusals or contemporaneous proof of inaction. This creates a firmer record for invoking the futility exception to the reporting requirement.

Expert country‑condition declarations can tie particular local steps—e.g., known impunity in a municipality, evidence of police collusion with a paramilitary leader—to the applicant’s individualized experience. But such evidence must be specific and connected: a national human‑rights report describing generalized corruption is helpful background, but is typically insufficient by itself to prove the government was unable or unwilling to protect a particular applicant absent a showing that the particular officials who could have helped either declined to do so or were complicit. The Jimenez court emphasized this point by distinguishing isolated interactions with unhelpful officers from systemic failures: courts often require proof of systemic, not merely anecdotal, dysfunction to find the government unable or unwilling to protect.

Finally, document any real risks inherent in making reports—threats, retaliation, or evidence that complainants have been targeted after filing. If reporting would have been dangerous, describe the specific reasons, supported by affidavits or expert testimony, rather than relying on generalized assertions of corruption.

## Nexus proof: persecutor statements tying harm to protected grounds; timing and comparator evidence

Nexus is the sine qua non of a successful claim: an applicant must show the persecutor’s motive was at least one central reason for the harm. The Tenth Circuit applies the "at least one central reason" standard and treats motive as a factual question of mixed intent. Evidence of persecutor statements that link the conduct to a protected ground—contemporaneous threats, leaflets, shouted slogans, texts explicitly naming the victim’s party, religion, or social identity—are the strongest proof of nexus.

Direct evidence is best: contemporaneous utterances such as "we will kill you for supporting Candidate X" or written threats like the brochure in Jimenez, when clearly traceable to the perpetrators, provide powerful nexus proof. In Jimenez the brochure read “death to snitches from Liberal Party”—that might have supported nexus to political opinion had the court been convinced the brochure was tied to the later attacks and to the individuals who threatened the family. But the court found the timing and context (the property dispute and the tenant "Don Rafa") showed the primary motive was retaliation for alleged interference in criminal activity; the political wording was, in the court’s view, tangential. That illustrates the need to show not only the content of the message but also the identity of the author and contemporaneity with persecution.

Circumstantial evidence can also establish nexus. Timing is crucial: an escalation of violence that begins only after an applicant takes an overt political role strengthens the inference of a political motive. Comparator evidence—showing a pattern where others who share the protected characteristic were targeted—bolsters an inference of motive. For example, evidence that several local Liberal Party volunteers received similar threats or were attacked during the same campaign season supports the political nexus. Documentation such as police blotters, media articles reporting targeted attacks against a political group, and affidavits from other victims can help build this pattern.

Mixed‑motive cases require careful evidentiary framing. If a persecutor had multiple motives (personal revenge, economic gain, and hostility toward the applicant’s political activities), the applicant must show that a protected ground was at least a central reason. To do so, offer evidence showing the persecutor’s knowledge of the applicant’s political activity (campaign photos, membership cards, witness testimony that the persecutor used political labels during threats), and explain why political animus mattered (e.g., the persecutor targeted only those who campaigned). Equally important is rebutting alternative motivations the government might posit. Where the persecutor alleges a personal dispute (land, debt, criminal retaliation), record evidence that discounts that explanation—show the applicant had no prior animus, explain why a property dispute would not have led to lethal threats except when combined with political activity, or show a contemporaneous pattern that the property dispute alone does not explain.

In short: procure contemporaneous statements, preserve the chain linking the statement to the aggressor, show timing and patterns, and use comparator evidence to demonstrate motive was not incidental.

## Internal relocation rebuttal: expert declarations, relocation failures, persecutor networks’ geographic reach

Whether internal relocation is a reasonable alternative is a question adjudicators often decide early. The government can rebut the presumption of future persecution by showing the applicant could reasonably relocate to another part of the country. To defeat that defense, an applicant must show that relocation is not reasonable—because it is unsafe, impossible, or unduly harsh—or that the persecutor has the reach to find and harm the applicant wherever he or she goes.

The record should include expert declarations about the persecutor’s network and geographic reach. An expert can explain whether criminal groups or corrupt networks operate regionally or nationally, whether they have the capability to track and retaliate, and how they cooperate with or evade state authorities. In Jimenez the court credited evidence that the client safely stayed with family in Bogota’s countryside for three weeks and that relatives continued to live in Colombia unharmed; those facts supported the BIA’s conclusion that internal relocation was reasonable. To counter such a finding, evidence must show either that short‑term relocations are illusory (the persecutors followed the family and continued threats), that the geographic reach of the persecutor is broad and well‑documented, or that the claimant faces special obstacles to relocation (economic impossibility, lack of family or job prospects, medical or educational needs).

Document any prior attempts to relocate and their outcomes. If the applicant previously tried to move to another city and the threats continued, preserve contemporaneous evidence—new threats, surveillance incidents, or local officials’ inability to protect the applicant—that shows relocation failed. Financial and administrative burdens can also be relevant: provide leases showing unaffordability, employer letters documenting loss of income, proof of lack of legal immigration alternatives in the area of relocation, and expert testimony on barriers to safe integration in a new locale.

When arguing relocation is unreasonable because the government cannot protect against a persecutor operating nationally, tie that claim to concrete evidence: prosecutors’ and police reports showing failed enforcement, media accounts of organized crime operating across regions, and expert testimony about the group’s national reach. Avoid speculative assertions that a persecutor might find an applicant anywhere; courts require concrete proof that relocation would not solve the problem.

## Child-specific evidence and UNHCR guidance on child persecution

Children merit special evidentiary care. International guidance, including UNHCR’s country‑specific and child‑protection guidance, emphasizes the distinct ways children experience persecution and the need for age‑appropriate corroboration. Domestic courts recognize children’s vulnerabilities—school threats, abductions near school, forced recruitment, or psychological trauma—and the evidence should be tailored accordingly.

School records are often highly probative. Attendance records that show absences related to fear, notes from teachers documenting behavioral changes, and written reports from school administrators about an incident (for example, a stranger grabbing a child’s mouth as he exits school) are powerful. In Jimenez, a man put his arm around the petitioners’ younger son and covered his mouth as the boy tried to flee; the record contained testimony about the event and reported panic attacks but lacked clinical or school documentation that would have amplified the child‑specific harm. A contemporaneous letter from the school corroborating the incident—or a teacher’s affidavit—is especially persuasive.

Medical and psychological evaluations are crucial for children. Pediatricians, child psychologists, or psychiatrists can diagnose trauma‑related disorders, show functional impairment (sleep problems, school performance decline), and explain long‑term developmental consequences. Because children may not be able to describe experiences with the granularity adults can, clinicians’ explanations linking symptoms to documented incidents help fill the inferential gap. Clinicians should use child‑appropriate instruments and explain why the child’s symptoms stem from the events alleged.

UNHCR guidance recommends child‑sensitive interviewing techniques and emphasizes best‑interest determinations. Practitioners should ensure interviews with children are conducted in a manner that minimizes retraumatization and maximize reliability: short sessions, presence of a trusted adult when appropriate, use of child‑trained interpreters, and corroboration through independent child‑focused professionals. Where possible, identify child‑welfare or social‑service records, foster‑care documents, or reports from child protection agencies that document the risk or harm. These documents do double duty: they corroborate the child’s experience and provide evidence that returning the child would not be in the child’s best interests.

Finally, when the claim principally concerns a child, consider relief avenues that center on child welfare (e.g., withholding or Convention‑based relief where applicable) and be prepared to explain the child's needs in relocation analysis—whether education, specialized therapy, or family support necessary for safety.

Conclusion: translating doctrine into evidence

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez demonstrates the practical consequences of an evidentiary gap: credible testimony alone can be insufficient where key corroboration (police complaints, clinical evaluations, documents tying threats to a persecutor’s motive) is absent or ambiguous. To build a winning record, practitioners must think in advance about how to document each element:

- For past persecution: assemble contemporaneous reports, physical or clinical records, and third‑party affidavits that describe the incidents in detail and show cumulative harm beyond mere threats.
- For nexus: preserve persecutor statements, document timing and patterns, collect comparator evidence, and use experts to explain local dynamics linking the harm to a protected ground.
- For state protection: secure formal complaint receipts, document attempts to follow up with prosecutors or ombudsmen, and, if reporting was dangerous or futile, demonstrate why with concrete evidence.
- For internal relocation: obtain expert analysis of the persecutor’s reach, document any prior relocation failures, and provide evidence of economic or logistical barriers where relevant.
- For child claims: gather school and medical records, child‑sensitive psychological assessments, and use UNHCR guidance to craft child‑appropriate interviews and corroboration.

In every case, the overarching principle is connectivity: the record must connect the dots—who did what, when, and why; how the state responded; and why relocation does or does not solve the problem. Meticulous, contemporaneous documentation and carefully targeted expert evidence turn credible stories into adjudicable proofs.

# Comparative Perspectives and Circuit Variations

This chapter uses the Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi as its touchstone to map how federal appellate courts and the Board of Immigration Appeals (and Attorney General decisional authority) have defined and applied key asylum concepts: when threats become persecution, how to treat persecution by private actors (and when reporting to the state is required), how mixed motives are assessed after the REAL ID Act, the evolution of the “unable or unwilling” government-protection inquiry, and what these doctrinal differences mean for venue and litigation strategy. Jimenez is paradigmatic of a conservative, fact‑specific application of asylum doctrine: the court stressed that threats without physical harm and without strong proof of a protected‑ground motive do not ordinarily constitute persecution, and that an applicant’s limited efforts to seek state protection undercut claims that the government was unable or unwilling to protect them. Placing that approach alongside other circuits and BIA/AG guidance reveals both common law contours and important fault lines.

## Threats-as-persecution: stricter and more permissive approaches across circuits

A central question that arises repeatedly in asylum adjudication is when threats—standing alone or combined with other conduct—cross the line from criminal wrongdoing or harassment into persecution. The Tenth Circuit in Jimenez reiterates a familiar rule in that circuit: threats by themselves are rarely sufficient to amount to persecution. The opinion emphasizes precedent holding that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution” and that the applicant must show suffering or harm that is “offensive” and more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats. The court also focused on the absence of physical injury and the relatively commonplace nature of psychological distress (insomnia, panic attacks) to conclude the cumulative conduct did not meet the persecution threshold.

Across the circuits, the baseline analytic move—look at the cumulative severity, frequency, context, and consequences of threats—is widely shared. But circuits diverge in how readily they convert threats into persecution.

- At the stricter end (as illustrated by the Tenth), courts ask whether threats were concrete, imminent, and caused substantial physical or long‑term psychological harm. They emphasize the need to connect the threats to a pattern of serious interference with fundamental rights or to evidence of actual violence.

- More permissive courts have recognized that sustained, credible threats—especially when backed by the capacity and history of the persecutor to carry them out—can be persecution even without prior physical harm. In those courts, the assessors weigh (a) specificity and credibility of the threats, (b) the persecutor’s demonstrated capacity to act (e.g., organized criminal groups that have committed violence), and (c) credible evidence of significant psychological impairment or forced flight.

The practical takeaway is that identical facts—sustained telephone threats, a leaflet threatening “death to snitches,” a later in‑person confrontation at gunpoint, and a child’s being accosted at school—may produce different legal results depending on how a tribunal weighs the cumulative effect and the ordinary baseline for “persecution.” Jimenez shows the Tenth Circuit’s reluctance to stretch the persecution concept to cover the facts before it: the threats escalated, but lacked past physical injury and were linked, in the court’s view, primarily to a personal dispute involving a criminal actor rather than to a protected ground.

For students and practitioners: when evaluating (or pleading) a threats claim, assemble evidence on three axes—objective risk (capacity and history of the persecutor), concrete acts (dates, locations, witnesses, contemporaneous reports), and medical/psychological documentation of harm. The more each axis is populated, the better the odds of persuading circuits that are willing to recognize non‑physical—but severe—persecution.

## Private-actor persecution and the reporting requirement: how circuits assess futility and danger exceptions

The Immigration and Nationality Act requires that persecution be “by the government or forces the government is either unable or unwilling to control.” Thus many claims about private actors (gangs, criminal enterprises, private militias) turn on whether the state can realistically be expected to provide protection. Jimenez raises the reporting requirement squarely: the Tenth Court emphasized the applicant’s limited efforts to seek state protection and treated the failure to report (or to seek help beyond one or two neighborhood officers) as significant evidence that the record did not demonstrate the government was unable or unwilling to protect the family.

What courts demand of asylum seekers before accepting the contention that reporting would have been futile or dangerous varies.

- The Tenth Circuit’s approach (reflected in Jimenez and earlier cases the court invokes) places substantial weight on whether the applicant actually notified authorities and whether the authorities had notice and an opportunity to respond. If an applicant did not report, the court will normally require a compelling reason—such as credible evidence of systemic collusion between officials and the persecutors, or a clear and immediate danger from reporting—to excuse nonreporting. Single, unhelpful encounters with police do not ordinarily prove state incapacity or unwillingness unless supported by broader evidence.

- Other circuits have been more willing to accept a futility or danger exception on the basis of reasonably understood risks. Some panels have accepted that reporting would have been futile when the persecutor has known ties to state actors, when reporting would have exposed the claimant to immediate retaliation, or when there is substantial country‑condition evidence showing widespread impunity or pervasive corruption. Those decisions often rely on documentary country conditions, reports of the persecutor’s collusion with officials, or credible testimony demonstrating that police inaction is systemic rather than idiosyncratic.

- A third, middle approach treats the reporting requirement as contextual: courts will accept a failure to report when an applicant shows that reporting was impracticable, would have posed an unreasonable risk, or under the circumstances could not be expected. The showing required is nontrivial—the applicant must supply evidence that reporting would have been futile or dangerous—but courts apply the standard flexibly in light of individual circumstances.

Jimenez illustrates the Tenth Circuit’s insistence on more concrete engagement with state actors before excusing nonreporting: the applicants spoke with a couple of police officers who essentially advised them to leave, but they did not otherwise seek help from police beyond their neighborhood or use the legal contacts available to them. The court treated that as insufficient to establish government inability or unwillingness.

For lawyers: when representing clients persecuted by private actors, prioritize contemporaneous reporting where safe to do so, document all contacts with law enforcement, and collect corroborating country‑condition evidence showing systemic failure or collusion if reporting did not occur. If reporting would have been dangerous, build a record to prove that danger—witnesses, contemporaneous messages, and credible expert or NGO reports—because some circuits will not excuse nonreporting on the basis of one or two unhelpful officer statements alone.

## Mixed-motive causation after the REAL ID Act: recurring fact patterns (gang extortion, whistleblowing, political canvassing)

The REAL ID Act requires that an applicant show that a protected ground was “at least one central reason” for persecution. That statutory formulation tightened pre‑existing, somewhat malleable standards and created a concrete test for mixed‑motive cases: the persecutor can have multiple motives, but a protected ground must be a central one.

The Jimenez decision applies that standard in a common fact pattern: political canvassing by a member of a political party who is then threatened not only because of politics but also because of a separate civil dispute with a criminal actor (here, a property dispute with a narco‑group leader). The Tenth Circuit concluded the dominant motive was economic and personal—retaliation for suspected interference with the criminal enterprise—rather than political.

Three recurring patterns illustrate the significance of the “one central reason” test:

- Gang extortion and political activity. Extortion often looks like political persecution when the victim is politically active, but courts will scrutinize whether the gang’s motive is primarily economic. Evidence that the gang targeted multiple people regardless of political views, or that the gang’s statements and actions focused on money, tends to undermine a political nexus. By contrast, threats that explicitly reference political activity, come in the context of targeted, selective attacks against partisan actors, or coincide temporally with political campaigning, help sustain a political‑motive inference.

- Whistleblowing or cooperation with authorities. When a person is threatened after providing information to prosecutors, the persecutor’s animus may be tied to the individual’s role as a “snitch” rather than to a protected political opinion. The nexus is stronger if the government or the persecutor indicates the harassment was because of the applicant’s political expression or opinions; weaker if the persecutor is responding solely to the applicant’s cooperation with law enforcement and economic disruption. Courts will look for statements by persecutors, for the timing and singularity of the targeting, and for patterns of persecution limited to those engaged in the protected activity.

- Political canvassing and localized harassment. Small‑scale political activity—going door‑to‑door for a candidate—frequently precipitates threats from actors who resent any authority or who profit from quiet. The central question is whether the canvassing was a central reason for the harm. Evidence that the persecutor referenced the canvassing in threats, that only those engaged in comparable political activity were targeted, or that the persecutor had a history of politically motivated violence supports a nexus finding. Jimenez is illustrative: the court found that although Petitioners were active in the Liberal Party, the escalation of threats coincided with a property dispute that more plausibly explained the men’s conduct.

The practical implication of REAL ID Act jurisprudence is that litigants must prove that a protected ground was a causal center of the harm—not merely present or a minor pretext. Documentary evidence of persecutor statements, contemporaneous threats referencing political views, selective targeting of politically active individuals, and careful temporal sequencing will matter. Absent such proof, courts will treat competing motives (financial, revenge, criminal control) as dispositive against an asylum nexus.

## BIA and Attorney General guidance: evolution and current status of the ‘unable or unwilling’ test

The Board of Immigration Appeals (and when the Attorney General steps in, the AG) has long shaped the standards for asylum through decisions and, more recently, through high‑profile AG referrals that recalibrated doctrines governing private‑actor persecution.

- Historically, BIA decisions emphasized the need to show either state involvement in persecution or a failure of state protection that is systemic, pervasive, or demonstrated by the government’s repudiation or inability to control the persecutor. The BIA has balanced the need to prevent granting asylum for ordinary crime victims against the protection of individuals genuinely threatened by private actors that the state cannot control.

- In the last decade, Attorney General decisions have sometimes narrowed eligibility for asylum against private actors. The best known is Matter of A‑B‑ (AG decision), in which the Attorney General took a narrow view of asylum claims grounded in private‑actor domestic violence and emphasized state protection and political motive limitations. That decision sparked litigation and divergent circuit responses; many courts and commentators criticized it as overly restrictive and insensitive to the reality that victims of private violence can be politically persecuted or systemically unprotected.

- The cumulative effect is a patchwork: some BIA precedents (and AG decisions) remain influential; yet appellate courts retain authority to interpret the statute and have sometimes rejected the AG’s narrow readings. Jimenez reflects a BIA‑friendly, deferential posture to the immigration court’s factual findings and to the requirement that applicants either seek government help or show why seeking it would have been futile or dangerous.

At present, the “unable or unwilling” inquiry is a mixed question of law and fact. The legal framework is stable: demonstrate government collusion, incapacity, indifference, or futility. But outcomes turn on the quality of evidence and jurisdictional predispositions. The BIA and AG have encouraged careful documentation and conservative factfinding. Appellate courts vary in their willingness to find systemic governmental failure on a thin record.

For practitioners and students: do not rely solely on an applicant’s testimony that they feared the police; compile contemporaneous reports, formal complaints, medical records, and independent country‑condition sources (NGO reports, U.S. State Department reports, media accounts) that show patterns of impunity, police collusion, or state incapacity in the relevant region. If the persecutor has links to the state, prioritize proof of those links—financial, social, or institutional—to rebut the presumption that the state can and will protect.

## Forum selection consequences for asylum lawyering

The doctrinal variations across courts and the BIA’s evolving posture have direct implications for where and how asylum cases are litigated. “Forum shopping” in immigration cases—which circuit hears an appeal—can be determinative. For example, petitioners may face more favorable law in circuits that have recognized broader circumstances under which threats, private‑actor persecution, or futility exceptions suffice; conversely, circuits that require robust evidence of physical harm, state collusion, or actual reporting will be harder venues.

Practical consequences for lawyers:

- Pre‑filing assessment. Evaluate the client’s strongest claims against the doctrinal leanings of the local circuit. If the client’s case rests principally on threats and psychological harm, consider filing in jurisdictions with precedent more favorable to non‑physical persecution claims. If the main problem is private‑actor persecution but reporting did not occur, favor circuits with broader futility doctrine and higher receptivity to country‑condition evidence of systemic corruption.

- Evidence design. Anticipate the element most likely to lose (nexus, persecution, or state protection) and structure discovery and corroboration to target that issue. For nexus, collect persecutor statements and proof of selective targeting. For persecution, compile medical or psychological evaluations, employment or school disruptions, and witness statements. For state protection, document police reports, contact records with officials, and authoritative country‑condition reports.

- Preservation and framing. Courts defer to the IJ and the BIA on factual findings; however, appellate courts vary in reviewing legal errors. Preserve arguments about burden, evidentiary weight, and legal standards at the administrative level. Frame issues in a way that courts in your circuit have found persuasive: in the Tenth, emphasize objective evidence and reporting; in other circuits, emphasize systemic failures and the applicant’s reasonable fear.

- Appellate strategy. If the initial result is unfavorable, assess whether the circuit’s law provides ripeness for a favorable reversal. Consider whether to urge an en banc rehearing or a cert petition where circuits have split. When a case implicates a circuit split, it can be an asset in seeking rehearing or certiorari, but those remedies are difficult and exceptional.

A brief practical checklist for counsel informed by Jimenez and comparative doctrine:
- Immediately document threats with as much contemporaneous evidence as possible (photographs, recordings, witness statements).
- File police reports if doing so is safe; if not, document why—text messages, witness testimony, NGO reports, medical records.
- Evidence the persecutor’s motive: direct threats referencing political views, selective targeting, membership lists, or public statements by the persecutor.
- Collect corroborative country‑condition documentation about impunity, corruption, or collusion.
- Tailor litigation strategy to local circuit precedent on threats, futility of reporting, and mixed motives.

Conclusion

Jimenez v. Bondi is a useful exemplar of a conservative, fact‑intensive application of asylum doctrine: it illustrates the Tenth Circuit’s reluctance to treat threats and non‑physical harms as persecution, its insistence on nexus to a protected ground, and its skepticism toward claims of government inability absent stronger evidence or reporting. When compared with other circuits and the BIA/AG landscape, the decision highlights the doctrinal levers that determine asylum outcomes: the required quantum of harm for persecution, the evidentiary threshold for excusing reporting, and the precise proof needed to satisfy the REAL ID Act’s “at least one central reason” test. For students and practitioners alike, the lesson is that asylum law sits at the intersection of fact‑finding and jurisdictional doctrine—sound lawyering for asylum cases requires both meticulous fact development and strategic forum‑aware legal framing.

# Withholding of Removal and CAT: Relationship to Asylum

This chapter explains how withholding of removal and protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT) relate to—and differ from—asylum. Students must understand the doctrinal, evidentiary, and practical distinctions among these forms of protection because the same factual record can yield different outcomes under different standards. The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi, discussed below, provides a useful vehicle to illustrate these differences and to show how, in practice, asylum denial often—but not always—forecloses other forms of relief.

At the outset, recall three basic structural points. First, asylum is a discretionary form of protection rooted in the refugee definition in the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA): an applicant must prove she is a “refugee,” i.e., unable or unwilling to return to her country because of persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. By contrast, withholding of removal (statutory withholding) and CAT relief are mandatory forms of non‑refoulement relief if their statutory or regulatory standards are met. Second, the burdens of proof differ among the three forms of relief. Third, the substantive elements that an applicant must prove are not identical: withholding requires a higher likelihood of future harm and still depends on a protected-ground nexus; CAT requires proof of torture and state involvement or acquiescence but does not require a protected-ground nexus.

The Jimenez opinion exemplifies these themes. The petitioners appealed denial of asylum and withholding; they did not appeal the denial of CAT relief. The Tenth Circuit upheld the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA), first concluding that petitioners failed to show past persecution or a well‑founded fear of future persecution, and then stating that because petitioners failed to satisfy asylum’s standard for a well‑founded fear, they necessarily could not meet withholding’s higher “clear probability” standard. That reasoning reflects a common, but not universal, relationship among the remedies: asylum denial often predicts withholding denial, but withholding and CAT may still succeed in some circumstances even when asylum fails. The sections that follow explain the standards and the circumstances in which one form of relief may succeed where another fails.

## Withholding’s higher probability standard and non‑derivative nature

Doctrinal baseline. Asylum and withholding address related harms, but they are governed by different statutory provisions and different standards of proof. Asylum is discretionary and premised on the INA’s refugee definition; withholding of removal is mandatory relief under 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3) (formerly § 243(h)) for any individual who shows “a clear probability” (or “more likely than not”) that his life or freedom would be threatened on account of a protected ground if returned. The Supreme Court in INS v. Cardoza‑Fonseca made the critical point that the “well‑founded fear” standard relevant to asylum is meaningfully less demanding than the “more likely than not” standard that applies for withholding (and that this difference has consequences for proof and outcomes). Practically, then, meeting the asylum standard does not automatically satisfy withholding’s more demanding likelihood threshold.

What “higher standard” means in practice. The “well‑founded fear” standard for asylum requires a reasonable possibility of persecution. Courts have sometimes quantified this as requiring something less than a 50% chance—an objectively reasonable possibility—while withholding requires the applicant to demonstrate that it is more likely than not (i.e., greater than 50%) that she will be persecuted. Because the withholding standard requires a higher probability, an applicant who persuades an adjudicator that persecution is reasonably possible may obtain asylum (if discretion favors her) but still fail to meet withholding’s mandatory standard. Conversely, in limited circumstances where an adjudicator denies asylum for discretionary reasons despite finding persecution, withholding may nevertheless be granted if the higher likelihood requirement is established.

Non‑discretionary character and consequences. The mandatory nature of withholding is an important difference. If an applicant proves the withholding standard, the government is statutorily required to withhold removal to the persecuting country; the relief is not subject to the same form of favorable discretion that governs asylum. But the protections that flow from withholding are narrower. Withholding does not confer permanent lawful status or the same discretionary benefits (such as eventual adjustment to lawful permanent resident status) that asylum can open the door to; instead, it simply bars removal to the designated country while the noncitizen remains in the United States. It is therefore critical to understand both the legal standard and the real‑world consequences of a grant of withholding versus asylum.

How the Jimenez decision applies the principle. The Tenth Circuit described this relationship succinctly: because the petitioners failed to establish a well‑founded fear of future persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily could not meet the higher standard required for withholding. That is a straightforward application of the higher likelihood requirement. But the opinion is careful to treat the two inquiries as analytically distinct—failure under asylum does not mechanically foreclose withholding in every conceivable factual posture; rather, the higher standard is simply harder to meet on the same facts.

Practical tips for practitioners and analysts. When preparing a withholding claim, counsel should recognize that corroboration, documentary evidence, and clear testimony are especially important because of the higher probability requirement. If the record shows past persecution, that fact can create a rebuttable presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution and materially help on the withholding inquiry, but counsel must be prepared to show why future persecution is more likely than not. Where asylum rests on a thin record of threats or harassment, it will be difficult to meet withholding’s threshold unless more persuasive evidence appears.

## CAT’s separate elements: torture, state action or acquiescence, and non‑refoulement

CAT’s legal structure. Protection under CAT operates under a different legal regime. The United States implements the Convention Against Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment through federal regulation (8 C.F.R. § 1208.16(c)) and by statute that prohibits removal to any country where “it is more likely than not that the alien would be tortured.” CAT relief is independent of the refugee definition; it does not require a nexus to a protected ground. Instead, it requires proof of (1) torture as defined by the Convention—severe physical or mental pain intentionally inflicted for certain purposes; and (2) that the torture would be inflicted by, or at the instigation of, or with the acquiescence of, a public official or other person acting in an official capacity. If these elements are met to the requisite likelihood standard, non‑refoulement under CAT prohibits removal to the country where torture is likely.

Defining “torture.” The Convention defines “torture” as intentional infliction of severe physical or mental pain or suffering for purposes such as obtaining information, punishment, intimidation, coercion, or discrimination, and excludes pain arising only from lawful sanctions or inherent in lawful penalties. In practice, applicants must show both the quality of the harm (severe) and the intent and purpose behind it. Not all persecution or abuse will qualify as torture; the mistreatment must reach the extreme level contemplated by the Convention.

State action or acquiescence. A central and often difficult element of a CAT claim is establishing state involvement. CAT does not require that the government itself physically commit the torture; it is sufficient to show that state actors would be the torturers or that public officials would acquiesce—i.e., know of the torture and consent or remain willfully blind. Courts assess whether the government is the author of the harm, whether it has failed to control private actors, or whether it otherwise would be complicit. The Tenth Circuit in Jimenez discussed similar issues in the asylum context—government inability or unwillingness to control persecutors is relevant there—but CAT’s state‑action analysis is narrower and focused on the specific question of whether government acquiescence would facilitate torture.

Standard of proof. CAT uses the “more likely than not” standard (substantively similar to withholding’s higher likelihood standard). The applicant must show it is more likely than not that she would be tortured if returned to the proposed country of removal. Because CAT does not require a nexus to a protected ground, it can protect individuals whose prospective mistreatment stems from non‑political motives so long as the torture element and government involvement or acquiescence can be proved.

How CAT differs functionally from asylum and withholding. There are several important practical differences. First, CAT’s lack of a protected‑ground requirement means it is available to applicants who suffer grave abuse for reasons outside the five protected grounds. Second, the kind of harm required is more severe than what many asylum claims allege—CAT demands evidence of torture-level treatment. Third, CAT relief, like withholding, does not confer lawful permanent residence; it is an enforceable protection against removal to a specific country. Finally, the evidence and strategies to prove CAT differ: demonstrating police or military involvement, systemic impunity, or documented patterns of torture in the country are often crucial.

Jimenez and CAT. In Jimenez, the petitioners did not press their CAT claim on appeal, and the panel explicitly declined to reach it. The court’s opinion therefore does not speak to the CAT merits. That procedural posture illustrates a practical lesson: CAT claims, although different in substance from asylum and withholding, must be preserved and argued to obtain appellate review; failure to appeal CAT denial waives a potential protection that might succeed even if asylum and withholding fail.

## When withholding or CAT succeed despite asylum denial (e.g., discretion or nexus failures)

It is tempting to conclude that denial of asylum almost always predicts denial of withholding and CAT, but that inference is overbroad. There are important scenarios in which withholding or CAT may be granted even when asylum is denied.

1) Asylum denied on discretionary grounds. Because asylum is discretionary, an applicant might satisfy asylum’s substantive elements (including a well‑founded fear or past persecution) yet be denied because the immigration judge declines to exercise discretion in her favor (for example, because of adverse discretionary factors like a criminal conviction, delay in filing, or other negative equities). Withholding, by contrast, is mandatory: if the applicant proves the statutory standard (clear probability) for withholding, the government must withhold removal even if the IJ would have denied asylum in the exercise of discretion. Thus, if the record supports the higher likelihood standard, the applicant can prevail on withholding even when asylum is denied on discretionary grounds.

2) New or different evidence on appeal or after the IJ. Sometimes, an applicant’s asylum claim fails because of inadequate evidence or an unpersuasive nexus showing, yet, through supplementation of the record or further documentary proof, the applicant later can meet withholding’s likelihood requirement. If the IJ or BIA denies asylum for lack of corroboration or insufficient evidence, timely supplemental evidence can support withholding on remand or at the appellate stage if properly presented.

3) Asylum denial for lack of nexus; CAT success because no nexus is required. The asylum analysis requires nexus to a protected ground; withholding requires the same nexus. But CAT does not require that the harm be on account of a protected ground. Consequently, if asylum fails because the harm stems from criminal targeting, vendetta, economic motives, or a private dispute (i.e., no protected ground), withholding will usually fail for the same reason; however, CAT may still succeed if the applicant can show torture and government acquiescence or participation. A classic hypothetical: an applicant is targeted for kidnapping and torture by security forces or militia because of a private dispute unrelated to political opinion—no asylum or withholding—but CAT may apply if the government is complicit in or will not prevent the torture.

4) Government protection or relocation findings. Denial of asylum sometimes rests on a finding that the applicant could safely relocate within her country or that the government would protect her if notified. Withholding and CAT inquiries consider government protection and internal relocation, but the importance and standards differ. Because withholding requires a higher probability of harm, evidence that internal relocation is practical or that the government would protect the applicant may defeat withholding even more readily than asylum. Conversely, an asylum denial that turns solely on adverse discretion leaves open the possibility of a mandatory withholding grant if the higher likelihood is proved.

How to read Jimenez in this context. Jimenez demonstrates the common trajectory where asylum failure leads to withholding failure because the facts did not show past persecution, nexus, or an inability or unwillingness of the state to protect. The court’s analysis emphasized the insufficiency of the threats, the predominance of non‑political motives in the persecutors’ acts, and the lack of systemic evidence that Colombian authorities were complicit. Those same factual weaknesses killed both asylum and withholding claims. But the opinion also implicitly acknowledges that had the IJ found past persecution and that the persecutors acted with state acquiescence—facts sufficient to show torture or a clear probability— the result on withholding or CAT could have diverged.

## Strategic sequencing and fallback theories in removal defense

Given the doctrinal differences described above, practitioners must plan claims and present evidence strategically. A typical defensive posture coordinates asylum, withholding, and CAT claims and then tailors proof to each remedy’s elements.

1) Plead and preserve all possible claims. Counsel should file applications and pleadings that preserve asylum, withholding, and CAT claims at the earliest stage. Preserving arguments is essential for appeal; failing to raise CAT at the IJ or to appeal its denial may waive a potentially viable non‑refoulement claim. The Jimenez petitioners did not appeal the CAT denial; that foreclosed relief on that ground at the appellate level.

2) Recognize divergent elements and collect different kinds of evidence. Because asylum requires nexus to a protected ground, evidence of political activity, statements by persecutors citing that activity, or country conditions showing targeted groups are vital. For withholding, the same evidence is relevant but must be stronger in quality and quantity to meet the higher likelihood threshold. For CAT, the emphasis shifts: evidence of torture‑level abuse, state complicity or acquiescence, medical or psychological reports documenting severe harm, and country reports showing systemic torture or impunity are key.

3) Address government protection and internal relocation comprehensively. Government protection and internal relocation are common defenses the government uses to rebut asylum or withholding claims. Applicants should document efforts (or the futility of efforts) to seek police help, provide evidence that local authorities are unresponsive or complicit, and explain why relocation within the country is not reasonable (e.g., because perpetrators reach widely, lack of documentation, inability to gain work or subsistence, or family ties that cannot be severed). Counsel should be prepared to distinguish asylum relocation issues from the similar inquiry under withholding and to show why relocation is not a meaningful safety valve.

4) Use past persecution evidence strategically. Evidence of past persecution creates a rebuttable presumption of future persecution and is powerful for both asylum and withholding. If past persecution is established, the government must rebut the presumption. Past persecution is also potentially relevant to CAT if it involves torture or shows a pattern of severe abuse and state acquiescence. Because the adjudicator may view the same acts differently under the different frameworks, emphasize aspects of past harm that meet the highest standards (severe physical injury, evidence of state involvement) to preserve CAT and withholding potential even if asylum is shaky.

5) Prepare for mixed‑motive arguments and nexus disputes. Persecutors often have mixed motives (for example, criminal profit and political intimidation). Under asylum and withholding law, an applicant need only show that a protected ground was “at least one central reason” for persecution—not necessarily the only one. Gather statements, contemporaneous documentation, and corroboration that link the harm to the protected motive. The Jimenez court underscored that where the protected motive is tangential or subordinate to other motives, the nexus requirement is not met. Successful withholding or asylum claims will therefore present evidence showing the protected ground played a central role.

6) Be mindful of relief consequences. Counsel should explain to clients the practical differences among the forms of protection—discretionary relief and the potential for adjustment to lawful permanent residence in the case of asylum, the mandatory but narrower protection of withholding, and CAT’s bar to removal without pathway to permanent status. These distinctions affect long‑term planning and client expectations.

Concluding synthesis. As illustrated by Jimenez, asylum, withholding, and CAT are interrelated but distinct protections. Asylum’s lower substantive standard and discretionary nature mean it can sometimes be granted where withholding is not; yet asylum’s discretionary character can also mean withholding is available even after asylum denial if the applicant meets withholding’s higher probability threshold. CAT operates on a separate axis: it requires torture and state involvement but no protected‑ground nexus, and can therefore protect applicants who are ineligible for asylum or withholding for lack of nexus. Practitioners must therefore plead and develop each claim with its unique elements in mind, collect evidence tailored to each standard, and preserve all arguments for appellate review. The Jimenez decision underscores both the predictable relationship between asylum and withholding outcomes in many cases and the continuing importance of preserving and developing CAT claims as an independent avenue of protection.

# Significance and Doctrinal Trajectory of Canon Jimenez

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Canon Jimenez v. Bondi reiterates core asylum doctrines while sharpening practical rules for litigants whose claims rest on threats, mixed motives, and government protection. The panel denied asylum and withholding of removal to a Colombian family whose leader, a provisional lawyer and political canvasser, received repeated threats and experienced a series of menacing encounters after confronting the tenant of a client’s property—an individual tied to a local narco-trafficking boss. The court affirmed the Board of Immigration Appeals’ conclusions that (1) the conduct alleged did not rise to past persecution; (2) the petitioners failed to prove that political opinion was at least one central reason for the mistreatment; (3) they did not show the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect them; and (4) internal relocation in Colombia was reasonable. That framework embodies familiar Tenth Circuit rules, but the opinion also supplies practical guidance on evidentiary priorities and highlights several contested doctrinal contours likely to shape future litigation.

Below we analyze the opinion’s doctrinal holdings, their fit in circuit precedent, and the takeaways for advocates and adjudicators.

## Reaffirmation of a High Bar for Threats-Only Persecution in the Tenth Circuit

Canon Jimenez reaffirms the Tenth Circuit’s longstanding principle that threats, even when repeated and terrifying, rarely suffice on their own to establish “past persecution.” The court reiterated that persecution requires “the infliction of suffering or harm … that must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty,” and that claims grounded only in threats or in transient emotional harms generally fail the persecution prong. The opinion follows prior Tenth Circuit decisions—Karki, Chhetri, Brandy, and others—that decline asylum relief where the record shows threats, harassment, or short-term detention without enduring physical injury, permanent disability, or comparable severity.

The practical consequences are twofold. First, litigants should treat “threats-only” fact patterns as an uphill battle in this circuit. Counsel must present rich, concrete evidence to show that the threats produced harm of the kind historically understood as persecution: severe physical harm, prolonged detention with mistreatment, forced displacement, or demonstrable long-term disabling psychological injury corroborated by expert evidence. Second, the court’s emphasis on cumulative analysis (recognizing that multiple incidents may combine to amount to persecution) does not displace the underlying requirement of objectively severe harm; repeated threats must be shown to have produced substantial, enduring harm or been part of a pattern of objectively violent, coercive conduct to clear the threshold.

For practitioners that retain these claims, the lesson is clear: do not rely on the rhetorical force of threats alone. Augment threats with corroboration of physical incidents, injuries, credible medical/psychological evaluations showing diagnosis and causation, or evidence that threats were coupled with extortion, forced exile, or similar harms that collectively create an objectively intolerable environment.

## Clarifying Mixed-Motive Analysis Where Criminal/Economic Motives Predominate

A central legal issue in Canon Jimenez was whether political opinion was “at least one central reason” for the targeting—a statutory requirement for asylum. The Tenth Circuit reaffirmed the “one central reason” standard and applied it in a mixed-motive context where criminal or economic motives were plausibly primary. The court accepted that persecutors often have multiple motives, but emphasized that in mixed-motive cases the protected ground must be a central, not incidental, reason for the mistreatment.

The facts were determinative: Petitioners faced escalated threats only after the principal (Jimenez) intervened in a property dispute that threatened a narco-trafficker’s economic interests and the security of his illicit operations. Threats linked to the property dispute, explicit references to “snitching” and involvement with prosecutors, and the timing of escalating harassment supported the BIA’s finding that economic and security motives predominated over political animus. The court therefore credited a non-political explanation as the principal driver of harm.

Doctrinally, Canon Jimenez clarifies that where criminal actors can point to proximate, non-political triggers (for example, direct interference with revenue streams, eviction conflicts, or allegations of informing on criminal operations), adjudicators will generally accept criminal/economic motives as the central reason unless the applicant adduces specific evidence tying the persecutor’s conduct to political opposition. For applicants, the practical inference is straightforward: when criminal or pecuniary motives exist, counsel must develop affirmative, case-specific evidence showing that the persecutor was responding to political activity—statements tying the harassment to campaign work, contemporaneous documentation of threats referencing political labels, reliable witness testimony that the persecutor targeted the applicant for political reasons, or pattern evidence showing the persecutor targeted political actors beyond mere economic complaints.

## Emphasis on Reporting and Evidentiary Development for the ‘Unable or Unwilling’ Prong

A critical portion of the court’s analysis addressed whether Colombia was “unable or unwilling” to control the persecutors. Canon Jimenez underscores two evidentiary touchstones that often decide this inquiry in the Tenth Circuit: (1) whether the government had notice and an opportunity to act (normally demonstrated by formal reporting to law enforcement), and (2) whether the government actually took steps to stop the persecutors or offer protection.

The opinion criticized Petitioners for scant efforts to seek redress beyond isolated conversations with individual officers who expressed apathy. The court observed that one-off, uncorroborated encounters with unhelpful officers do not necessarily indicate systemic inability or unwillingness. Conversely, the record showed that authorities had investigated the contested property and undertaken searches and seizures—facts that undercut a claim that the state was complicit or powerless. The court thus treated reporting (and the related documentation) as a pivotal piece of the puzzle.

For litigators, Canon Jimenez sends several practical instructions. Counsel should aggressively document any contact with authorities: obtain police reports, complaints, reference or case numbers; preserve records of calls, emails, or official correspondence; secure statements from government officials if available; and, if reporting would have been futile, develop contemporaneous evidence to support futility—examples include threats of retaliation for reporting, prior complaints that led to retaliatory harm, or credible evidence that law enforcement is collusive with persecutors. Country-condition materials and expert testimony become important when asserting systemic corruption or complicity: detailed reports showing pervasive impunity, patterns of collusion between criminal groups and officials, or credible accounts of retaliation against those who report can help transform an individual officer’s apathy into persuasive evidence of systemic failure.

The opinion also demonstrates that alleging government inability or unwillingness without concrete, corroborative evidence will likely fail. Attorneys should therefore treat the government-protection prong as an evidentiary exercise: identify the specific governmental actors who could have protected the applicant, document the applicant’s attempts to inform those actors (or explain why attempts would have been futile), and supply independent corroboration of governmental dysfunction when necessary.

## Internal Relocation as a Practical Safety Valve in Neighborhood-Confined Threats

Canon Jimenez reiterates the government’s ability to rebut a well-founded fear of persecution by showing internal relocation within the country would be reasonable and safe. The court observed that the threats were localized—confined to the neighborhood around the factory and the contested property—and that the family had safely lived with relatives in the Bogota countryside for several weeks prior to departure. The presence of family ties, a history of safe temporary relocation, and no evidence that the persecutors sought to remove the family from the country supported the BIA’s finding that internal relocation was a feasible alternative.

This aspect of the opinion highlights the practical contours of the relocation analysis. Adjudicators assess the reasonableness of relocation by considering safety, access to livelihood, the applicant’s age and health, family ties, and whether the persecutor has reach beyond a particular locale. Localized threats—especially those where persecutors threaten only that the applicant should leave the neighborhood, not the country—weaken asylum claims if the state provides reasonably safe options elsewhere.

For practitioners: when forced to confront a relocation defense, build a record showing why relocation is not reasonable—evidence of national-level reach of persecutors, inability to secure housing or employment elsewhere, threats that explicitly apply nationally or are linked to mobile forms of persecution, or country reports showing pervasive targeting that undermines the plausibility of safe relocation. Conversely, if an applicant has already relocated within the country and remained safe, admit that as strong evidence that relocation is reasonable.

## Implications for Political Canvassers and Community Advocates Entangled with Criminal Groups

Canon Jimenez has immediate implications for political canvassers, community organizers, and lawyers who perform advocacy in environments where criminal actors exert control. The opinion demonstrates that so long as a persecutor can show a proximate, non-political reason for targeting (for example, protecting illicit revenue, retaliating for a property challenge, or preventing law enforcement action), an asylum claim premised on political persecution will face significant headwinds.

Advocates working in such contexts should pursue two parallel litigation strategies. First, if the persecution arises from political activity (campaigning, opposition organizing, public denunciation of criminal groups tied to political actors), develop clear, contemporaneous evidence that persecutors explicitly targeted the applicant for the political conduct: threats that reference the candidate, statements linking the harassment to campaign activity, contemporaneous witness statements, and documentation that the persecutor has targeted others specifically because of political activities. Second, if the danger is more plausibly economic or criminal, consider alternate asylum theories—particular social group (family, community leaders, etc.)—or CAT claims where the state’s complicity rises to the level of torture risk. Be mindful that the Tenth Circuit scrutinizes social-group claims and will require that the group be cognizable under prevailing test elements (immutability, social visibility, and particularity).

Canon Jimenez also signals caution for those whose political work overlaps with law-enforcement functions (e.g., being a prosecutor’s intern). When persecutors view an individual as an agent of law enforcement, persecutors may couch threats as retaliation for “snitching” or prosecutorial work—motivations that courts may characterize as criminal rather than overtly political. Counsel should therefore anticipate the mixed-motive inquiry and proactively develop evidence tying the persecutor’s animus to protected political viewpoints rather than merely to perceived law enforcement activity.

## Open Questions and Areas for Further Development

Although Canon Jimenez clarifies several issues, it leaves important open questions that will be litigated in subsequent cases.

- Imputed Political Opinion. The opinion did not fully explore whether the persecutors imputed a political opinion to the applicants based on their campaign involvement or association with the prosecutor’s office. Courts have recognized imputed political opinion claims where persecutors punish persons because they are perceived to hold a political view. Future cases will need to delineate when mere association (campaigning in a contested neighborhood, working briefly with a prosecutor) suffices for an imputation finding—particularly when the persecutor’s stated reasons reference anti-police or anti-snitch sentiments rather than explicit political labels.

- Proof of Systemic Corruption or State Complicity. Canon Jimenez demonstrates the Tenth Circuit’s demand for concrete evidence when applicants assert state inability or unwillingness. But what quantum and type of proof suffices to show systemic corruption? The court suggested that isolated encounters with unhelpful officers are insufficient; at the same time, it did not set a bright-line rule for when reports and country-conditions evidence overcome that burden. Future decisions will need to clarify thresholds for expert testimony, independent investigations, or detailed country reports to meaningfully prove systemic failure or complicity.

- Child-Focused Claims and Psychological Harm. Petitioners invoked children’s panic attacks and parental sleep deprivation, but the court found psychological harms insufficient without more. The opinion leaves open how courts should evaluate children’s psychological injuries in asylum claims—what kind of mental-health documentation, longitudinal therapy records, school reports, or expert testimony would convincingly demonstrate long-term, debilitating consequences rising to persecution. This area is particularly salient because threats directed at children or acts that impede a child’s safe education may warrant different treatment in future cases.

- Particular Social Group Theories. Petitioners did not press a particular social group argument here, and the court declined to analyze that theory. Given the evolving jurisprudence on family- and community-based PSGs—especially where crime groups target “social leaders” or “community advocates”—future litigation will likely revisit whether community leaders or canvassers constitute cognizable PSGs and how those groups overlap with political-opinion claims.

- Government Actors and Criminal-Political Alliances. The canonical tension between criminal groups’ independence and their political alignments remains unsettled. Canon Jimenez weighed evidence that the government executed searches and was not complicit—but many other cases will involve less clear-cut evidence and allegations of closer ties between criminals and state officials. Courts will continue to sort when criminal actors’ political alignments convert a private actor’s harms into state-action or when such alignments suffice to show state unwillingness.

## Practical Checklist for Litigators (What to Develop in the Record)

Canon Jimenez highlights several concrete pieces of evidence that frequently decide asylum claims in similar circumstances. Litigators should, early and deliberately, seek to develop the following:

- Documented reports to police or prosecutors (official complaint numbers, copies of reports, follow-up correspondence). If reporting did not occur, contemporaneous reasons (threats asserting retaliation for reporting, prior failed reports) and corroboration.

- Medical and psychological records that (a) diagnose specific conditions and (b) link causally the applicant’s symptoms to the alleged persecution. For children, school or counseling records indicating persistent functional impairment.

- Witness statements and affidavits from coworkers, neighbors, or family who observed threats, assaults, or the persecutor’s statements tying the mistreatment to political activity.

- Contemporaneous documentary evidence connecting the persecutor to the alleged criminal or political organization (news articles, police incident reports, municipal records showing enforcement actions at the property).

- Country-condition and expert evidence on systemic corruption, police performance, and the reach of criminal groups—tailored to the applicant’s locality and the persecutor’s network.

- Evidence of internal relocation attempts or success (temporary stays with relatives or moves to other parts of the country without incident).

- Strategic articulation of nexus: frame whether the claim rests on the applicant’s own political opinion, an imputed opinion, or membership in a particular social group—and develop evidence specific to that theory.

Canon Jimenez teaches that litigation is won or lost in evidentiary development. Courts will credit well-documented, specific showings over generalized or speculative assertions.

## Conclusion

Canon Jimenez should be read as an exemplar of the Tenth Circuit’s careful, evidence-driven approach to asylum law. The decision confirms that (1) threats alone rarely constitute past persecution in the Tenth Circuit; (2) mixed motives require the protected ground to be a central reason for targeting; (3) applicants must develop a factual record showing the government had notice and failed to act, or that reporting would have been futile; and (4) internal relocation remains an important, and often decisive, consideration where threats are localized. For practitioners, the case underscores the premium on early, thorough evidentiary development—police reports, medical and psychological documentation, corroborating witness statements, and persuasive country-condition proof. For the doctrine, Canon Jimenez narrows certain avenues for relief while leaving pivotal questions—about imputed political opinion, the thresholds for demonstrating systemic corruption, and the evidentiary proof needed for child-focused psychological claims—ripe for future development.

# Notes, Problems, and Discussion Prompts

This chapter uses Jimenez v. Bondi (10th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025) as a springboard for exercises that test students’ ability to apply the asylum doctrines discussed in the opinion: the elements of past persecution, the “on account of” nexus inquiry (with particular focus on political opinion), the “unable or unwilling” government-protection analysis, and internal relocation. The problems below present fact patterns that vary one or more salient features of Jimenez. Each problem is followed by an analyst’s note—an instructor’s answer sketch that identifies the governing law, how the law applies, what evidence an applicant should present, and what pitfalls to avoid. The discussion prompts explore doctrinal line‑drawing and evidentiary strategy; they are designed to spur classroom debate and written analysis.

Before turning to the problems, recall the core principles reiterated in Jimenez and the cases the court relied upon. To obtain asylum an applicant must show she is a “refugee,” defined as one who is outside her country and “unable or unwilling to return . . . because of persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution on account of” a protected ground (race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group). Past persecution gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution. The past‑persecution inquiry asks whether the applicant suffered harm “that entails more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty,” while the nexus requirement asks whether the protected ground was “at least one central reason” for the harm. Government protection is judged by whether the state had notice and an opportunity to respond and whether it acted to stop the persecutors or offered protection; an applicant’s failure to report can undercut but does not necessarily doom an “unable or unwilling” claim if reporting would have been futile or dangerous. Finally, the government can rebut an applicant’s fear by proving internal relocation is reasonable.

## Problem 1 — A Canvasser in a Different Region Receives Explicit Partisan Death Threats and Is Assaulted

Fact pattern
Sofia is a 28‑year‑old canvasser for a political party in Country X. She campaigned door‑to‑door for three months for a gubernatorial candidate opposed to a regional crime‑boss who publicly supports the incumbent. While canvassing she receives repeated phone calls demanding she stop; the callers say, “We’ll end your political game.” One night two masked men assault her outside a campaign office, beat her, and carve a cross on her forearm—a symbol the crime‑boss uses to mark enemies. The police take Sofia’s written complaint and promise an investigation. Two weeks later the police have made no arrests and tell Sofia the investigation needs more time. Sofia’s campaign supervisor is later found murdered; the murder is widely reported as revenge for supporting the opposition. Sofia flees to the United States and applies for asylum.

Questions
1. Does Sofia’s experience likely qualify as past persecution? Explain why or why not under the standards articulated in Jimenez and companion cases.
2. Is there an arguable nexus to political opinion? What evidence would be critical to prove that the crime‑boss targeted her “on account of” her political activity?
3. How should Sofia present the government‑protection issue to strengthen her claim that the authorities are unable or unwilling to protect her?

Analyst’s note (instructor’s answer sketch)
1. Past persecution. Under Jimenez and prior Tenth Circuit decisions, threats alone commonly do not suffice; however, physical assault—particularly when accompanied by symbolic marking and contemporaneous murders of political actors—can constitute persecution. The relevant test asks whether the inflicted harm is “suffering or harm . . . in a way regarded as offensive” and “more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty.” Sofia endured bodily violence (beating) and symbolic marking by a factional sign—facts that, considered together with the murder of her supervisor and repeated threats, are likely to meet the threshold for past persecution. The cumulative approach (considering incidents in the aggregate) favors finding persecution here because the assault is a concrete physical injury and the campaign supervisor’s murder shows a pattern of violent targeting of political actors. Thus, a reasonable adjudicator could conclude Sofia suffered past persecution.

2. Nexus to political opinion. The “at least one central reason” standard requires evidence that the persecutor targeted Sofia at least in part because of her political activity or opinion. Here, relevant evidence includes: (a) Sofia’s visible role as a canvasser (public, partisan activity); (b) the timing of the threats and violence during the campaign; (c) statements by perpetrators (“We’ll end your political game”); (d) the murder of another campaigner (pattern of targeting); and (e) any public statements by the crime‑boss endorsing the incumbent and denouncing opponents. Compared to Jimenez, where the court emphasized that escalation occurred after the respondent’s dispute with a criminal leader over property and that earlier politically themed threats lacked a clear link to the eventual violence, Sofia’s facts show contemporaneous threats explicitly framed in political terms and a campaign co‑worker’s murder. These facts tend to establish that political opinion was at least one central reason. The applicant should gather evidence of the carve’s meaning, media reporting linking the crime‑boss to political intimidation, and witness affidavits corroborating threats and the supervisor’s political activity.

3. Government protection. Sofia reported to police and the police accepted her complaint—this gives the government notice and opportunity to respond, which could hurt her claim if the police proved effective. But two ways to frame the issue to sustain an “unable or unwilling” claim: (a) show investigatory inertia or refusal to act—document that the police made no arrests despite evidence, offered no protective measures, or explicitly blamed Sofia for the violence; (b) present objective evidence that the police force is compromised or that the crime‑boss controls local officials—country reports, independent press accounts, or affidavits from other victims. Evidence of good‑faith but ineffective efforts by police (e.g., superficial investigation, no surveillance, failure to protect) supports inability to protect. Conversely, if Sofia’s evidence shows the police took reasonable steps and the crime‑boss is unusually powerful (e.g., with capacity to evade law enforcement), she can argue ineffectiveness rather than unwillingness. To avoid the Jimenez pitfall (where petitioner’s minimal reporting left no basis to judge state response), Sofia should compile documentary proof of her report, records of police inaction, and corroborative country information showing systemic impunity.

Class discussion points and courtroom strategy
- Stress the difference that physical harm (beating, carving) makes in moving from mere threats to persecution. Discuss what additional facts might be necessary to tip borderline cases (e.g., hospitalization, permanent injury, threats to family).
- Emphasize the importance of contemporaneous statements by perpetrators and public pattern evidence to prove nexus.

## Problem 2 — Reporting to a Specialized Anti‑Corruption Unit Versus Local Police

Fact pattern
Carlos is a mid‑level municipal official in Country Y who discovers and reports to the country’s independent National Anti‑Corruption Commission (NACC) that contractors working with a local mayor are taking kickbacks. The NACC opens a formal investigation and instructs Carlos to provide documents and attest under oath. The NACC tells Carlos it will provide whistleblower protection but eventually returns his evidence to the mayor’s office and reassigns his investigator. When Carlos is subsequently threatened, he complains to his local police precinct but is told to “leave politics to adults.” The mayor publicly denounces Carlos as a liar. Carlos files for asylum in the U.S., claiming persecution on account of his political opinion (anti‑corruption) and whistleblowing.

Questions
1. How should Carlos frame the “unable or unwilling” government protection inquiry: does reporting to the NACC help or hurt his asylum claim?
2. What documentary evidence should Carlos submit to prove the NACC’s failure and to show futility or danger in seeking further governmental protection?

Analyst’s note
Reporting to a specialized unit is a double‑edged sword. On the one hand, reporting to a recognized, independent national agency (NACC) often provides clearer evidence that the government had notice and an opportunity to respond—if the agency later took effective steps to protect the applicant, the government could rebut the applicant’s claim that it was unable or unwilling to protect. On the other hand, showing that a specialized unit received the complaint and then failed—especially if the failure was systemic or attributable to high‑level corruption—can strengthen an applicant’s claim that the state is unwilling or unable to protect.

How to frame the inquiry
- If the NACC genuinely investigated and offered protection but the threats continued and the protection proved ineffective, the applicant should focus on demonstrating the ineffectiveness of protection (e.g., reassigning investigators, returning evidence, leaks to perpetrators). That shows notice plus inability rather than mere nonreporting.
- If the NACC was captured by the mayor or the investigation was a sham, Carlos can argue the official procedures were a façade—documenting capture demonstrates systemic inability or unwillingness.

Critical documentary evidence
Carlos should collect: (a) written communications with the NACC (complaint, receipt of complaint, investigation file numbers, meeting notes); (b) the NACC’s formal findings or lack thereof; (c) correspondence showing the NACC returned documents or reassigned staff; (d) any whistleblower‑protection offers and the record of how protection failed; (e) press reports or NGO/IG reports (Transparency International, Human Rights Watch) that document NACC’s lack of independence or political interference; and (f) affidavits from NACC staff, co‑workers, or other victims showing a pattern of capture. Such documentation shifts the analysis away from the Jimenez problem (little or no reporting) to an affirmative showing of state notice plus systemic failure.

Tactical considerations
- Anticipate government arguments that the applicant “had a remedy” and failed to pursue it: avoid this trap by documenting initial reporting and subsequent futility or danger.
- If reporting increased danger (e.g., leaks to mayor’s office), emphasize the causation: reporting led to mayor’s denunciation and threats. Courts recognize that reporting can sometimes precipitate harm and that in such cases reporting is not a prerequisite to establish inability to protect.

## Problem 3 — A Nationwide Cartel With Documented Reach: Is Internal Relocation Reasonable?

Fact pattern
Mariana lives in a medium city in Country Z, where a powerful cartel runs extortion rackets in coastal regions and has recently expanded into the interior using national transport networks and corrupt contacts. Mariana refused to pay extortion, was beaten and told she would be killed if she left the city. She presents evidence that the cartel operates coast‑to‑coast, controls several police precincts through bribery, and uses national bus lines and cell‑phone tracking to find dissidents. Mariana has an aunt in a remote highland town who could house her, but there are no job prospects and the aunt’s home is small. Mariana seeks asylum and contends internal relocation is not reasonable.

Questions
1. Under the framework set out in Rivera‑Barrientos and Jimenez, how should a court assess whether internal relocation is reasonable in Mariana’s case?
2. What types of evidence would rebut or support the government’s (or applicant’s) position on reasonableness of relocation?

Analyst’s note
Legal framework and factors
The government bears the burden of proving by a preponderance that internal relocation is reasonable. Courts examine whether the applicant could avoid persecution by relocating to another part of the country taking into account such factors as the extent of the persecutor’s reach, feasibility and safety of travel, administrative or legal barriers to resettlement, family or community ties in the proposed relocation area, economic viability, and whether the relocated person would be able to live a life free from future persecution.

Applying the framework
- The cartel’s nationwide reach and sophisticated tracking methods weigh strongly against the reasonableness of internal relocation. If the applicant demonstrates the cartel operates full national networks, undermines local police even in remote towns, and has a pattern of following victims who relocate, a court may find relocation unreasonable. Evidence that the cartel controls police precincts—in particular if corroborated by independent country reports—supports an applicant’s claim.
- Conversely, if the government shows that other regions maintain effective law enforcement, have no documented cartel activity, and could offer protection or safe employment, the government may meet its burden to show relocation is reasonable. The aunt’s willingness to shelter Mariana is relevant but not dispositive—courts will inquire whether the aunt’s town provides safety and means of survival.

Evidentiary needs
Applicant should present: country‑condition reports demonstrating the cartel’s reach; reports of threats or attacks in multiple regions; communications showing pursuit of relocated victims; letters from potential hosts corroborating the offer of shelter; and documentation showing lack of jobs or social services in the new area. The government should present evidence of regions without cartel presence, functioning police records, and testimony from experts or NGOs attesting to pockets of safety within the country.

Practical assessment
Given the fact pattern (documented coast‑to‑coast cartel operations, corrupt contacts, ability to track victims), a court is likely to find internal relocation unreasonable unless the government can show particular safe zones where the cartel lacks influence and where the applicant could realistically rebuild life. The aunt’s offer alone is unlikely to satisfy the government’s preponderance burden to show reasonable relocation.

Class exercise
Ask students to draft a short memorandum arguing both sides on internal relocation—one paragraph for the applicant, one paragraph for the government—citing specific evidence types.

## Discussion Prompt 1 — Drawing the Line Between Whistleblowing on Crime and Expressing a Political Opinion

Background and doctrinal issues
The Jimenez opinion draws attention to an important practical and doctrinal problem in asylum law: whether the persecutor’s motive was retaliatory for a criminal or private dispute (e.g., property, law enforcement contact) or was because the victim held a political opinion. Whistleblowing—exposing corruption, crime, or wrongdoing—often sits on the border. On the one hand, speaking out against corruption may be a civic or law‑enforcement act; on the other, it can be political if it expresses a belief about the propriety of government policy or seeks to influence public decisionmaking.

Questions for class debate
1. What criteria should courts use to determine when whistleblowing constitutes a “political opinion” for asylum purposes?
2. How should courts evaluate the persecutor’s perception (was the persecutor motivated by politics) as distinct from the applicant’s subjective motive?
3. Can private acts of law enforcement (e.g., representing a client, filing a complaint) be political when the act challenges a politically powerful actor?

Discussion points and analytical guidance
- Adopt a perception‑focused approach: the key inquiry (per Jimenez) is often whether the persecutor perceived and targeted the victim for a protected ground. Thus, courts should examine statements by perpetrators, the context of the disputed act (public or private), and whether the act was intended to influence government policy or political outcomes.
- Publicity matters: public, outspoken denunciations of government corruption or participation in political rallies are more readily categorized as political opinion than private legal representation, which may be seen as professional conduct. The more public and expressive the speech, the stronger the claim that it reflects political opinion.
- Content and audience: statements that articulate political viewpoints (e.g., “I oppose the president’s use of cartels”) are political; discreet whistleblowing to a prosecutor about misappropriation may be private unless the whistleblower aims to change policy or rally public attention.
- Persecutor’s motive: look for evidence that the persecutor links the victim’s action to politics (e.g., calling someone an “opposition activist,” targeting campaign workers). Jimenez turned on the fact that the threats escalated after the respondent’s involvement in a property dispute and that earlier politically themed threats lacked clear connection to the escalation.
- Policy concerns: be mindful that treating all whistleblowing as political could expand asylum beyond intended limits; the line should therefore be fact‑sensitive.

Class assignment
Provide three short vignettes of whistleblowing (private complaint to prosecutor; publishing investigative articles; leaking documents to opposition party) and ask students to classify each as political or nonpolitical and justify their reasoning under the “persecutor’s perception” framework.

## Discussion Prompt 2 — The Role of Psychological Expert Testimony in Meeting the Persecution Threshold

Context and doctrinal stakes
Jimenez illustrates the court’s reluctance to grant asylum based solely on threats and resultant psychological distress. Psychological expert testimony, however, can be pivotal in close cases where physical harm is absent but psychological injury and functional impairment are significant. The question is when such testimony can transform subjective fears and emotional suffering into objective evidence of “suffering or harm” that constitutes persecution.

Key issues for analysis
1. What probative value does expert psychological testimony add to an asylum claim that rests largely on threats and nonpermanent harm?
2. What must an expert demonstrate to be persuasive—diagnosis, causation, severity, prognosis, and linkage to consequences in the applicant’s country of origin?
3. How do courts balance expert testimony against the normative statement in Jimenez that threats and emotional distress alone rarely suffice?

Analytical guidance
- Diagnosis and objective measures: An expert should provide a clear clinical diagnosis (e.g., PTSD, major depressive disorder) supported by standardized testing, clinical interviews, and observable functional impairment. Descriptions of sleep loss, panic attacks, or inability to work are more persuasive when tied to standardized measures and clinical findings.
- Causation and attribution: The expert must causally link the disorder to the persecutory incidents and explain why the disorder is not attributable to other sources. The expert should also opine whether the symptoms are likely to persist or worsen upon return to the home country.
- Severity and functional impact: Courts look for evidence that psychological harm is severe enough to amount to “suffering or harm” recognized as persecution—this includes severe PTSD symptoms, suicidal ideation, inability to function in daily life, or long‑term disability.
- Corroboration: Expert testimony is strongest when corroborated by contemporaneous records (medical notes, psychiatric treatment in the home country or in exile), witness statements, or other objective indicators of impairment.
- Limitations: Even strong expert testimony cannot alone prove nexus or government inability to protect; it addresses only the “suffering” element. It must be combined with objective evidence of targeting and state response.

Practical tips
- Prepare the expert to tie clinical conclusions to the legal standard: explain why the psychological harm rises to persecution and why returning to the applicant’s country would aggravate the condition.
- Anticipate cross‑examination: the government will probe for preexisting conditions, malingering, and alternative explanations. The expert should have data and methodology ready to counter such challenges.
- Use experts to address relapse risk on return: expert opinions about the likely psychological and social consequences of forced return can strengthen the overall claim of a well‑founded fear.

Class activity
Provide a redacted expert report and a respondent’s hearing transcript. Ask students to identify strengths and weaknesses in the expert’s testimony and to draft three follow‑up examination questions that would bolster the report’s linkage to the persecution standard.

Concluding note for students
These problems and prompts are designed to bridge doctrinal rules and real‑world adjudication. Jimenez is instructive for its emphases: (1) the line between threats and persecution turns on the seriousness and cumulative effect of harm; (2) nexus requires that a protected ground be a central reason, not a mere pretext; (3) the government‑protection inquiry is fact‑sensitive and depends on notice and response; and (4) internal relocation remains a feasible defense for the government if it can demonstrate safe alternatives. In applying these tests, careful factual development and documentary corroboration are decisive. Use these exercises to sharpen your ability to identify the critical facts an adjudicator needs to decide each element and to plan an evidentiary strategy that anticipates and neutralizes the government’s rebuttal.
