Title: Blog about the opinion: Jimenez v. Bondi

# When Death Threats Aren’t Enough: The Tenth Circuit’s Rigorous Persecution Threshold in Jimenez v. Bondi

## Why this case matters
Jimenez v. Bondi is a reminder that, in the Tenth Circuit, even repeated, armed death threats and an incident involving a child may fall short of asylum’s persecution threshold unless accompanied by more concrete indicia of severe harm or a clearer showing of motive and state failure. The case puts a fine point on three recurring asylum battlegrounds: what counts as “persecution”; how to establish nexus to a protected ground in mixed‑motive harassment by criminals; and when government protection or internal relocation is unreasonable. For practitioners and advocates, Jimenez signals that compiling documentary and corroborative evidence remains decisive—threats, standing alone and without stronger corroboration, are at perennial risk of being discounted on appeal.

## The court’s reasoning, in brief
The petitioners—Colombian nationals who campaigned for a presidential candidate and whose husband-law‑student later intervened in a property dispute with a narco-trafficking leader (“Don Rafa”)—suffered a series of menacing events: telephone threats, a brochure reading “death to snitches from Liberal Party,” two armed confrontations at the family’s factory (one directly telling them to leave), a later assault of an employee who was asked where the petitioners were, and a frightening encounter in which a man put his arm around the petitioners’ younger son and told him to “run to your dad.” The family briefly hid with relatives and ultimately fled to the United States after a police patrol officer said “nothing could be done” and advised them to leave.

The Tenth Circuit denied the petition for review, affirming the BIA’s decision on multiple grounds. First, on past persecution, the court applied its settled framework: persecution requires “infliction of suffering or harm . . . more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty,” and threats alone are generally insufficient. Although the panel acknowledged the incidents collectively, it concluded that (1) petitioners suffered no physical injury or lasting physical harm, a concession they made at the merits hearing, and (2) their psychological harms—sleep loss, panic attacks—were not the type of corroborated, severe psychological injury that the court has treated as rising to persecution. The court relied on Karki, Brandy, and related precedents to hold that the cumulative pattern here did not compel a different result.

Second, on nexus, the court treated this as a mixed‑motive case and required that a protected ground be “at least one central reason” for the harm. The panel found substantial evidence that the driving motive was not political opinion but retaliation and financial protection of Don Rafa’s criminal enterprise after Jimenez’s involvement in the property dispute. The escalation of threats only after Jimenez confronted the tenant/Don Rafa, the references by the perpetrators to searches and seizures at the property, and the absence of proof that the earlier politically‑tinged threats were attributable to Don Rafa all supported the BIA’s finding that political opinion was tangential.

Third, on government protection and relocation, the court emphasized the importance of reporting and notice: without a contemporaneous report or evidence that reporting would have been futile or dangerous, there is no basis to conclude the Colombian government was categorically unable or unwilling to protect the family. The record showed police had attempted searches at the property and that family members safely remained in Colombia, and the petitioners themselves had a short safe stay at a relative’s home—facts the court used to support the BIA’s conclusions that internal relocation was reasonable and that the state was not shown to be complicit or helpless.

The upshot: substantial‑evidence review sustained the BIA’s factual findings across these points, and the petitioners failed to meet the asylum (and, consequently, the higher withholding) standards.

## Why the court came out this way — and where that reasoning is contestable
Jimenez is faithful to the Tenth Circuit’s cautious posture on threat‑only claims. Two doctrinal currents drive the result. First, the court draws a line between menacing conduct and the type of targeted, severe infliction of suffering historically understood as persecution. That line matters for adjudicatory clarity and curbs overbroad asylum grants, but it also risks starving meritorious claims where non‑physical violence—terrorizing, repeated threats, or attempts to abduct a child—has life‑altering consequences.

Second, the court’s mixed‑motive analysis is rigorous: it will treat criminal motives (revenge, profit, silencing) as dispositive unless an applicant can point to evidence that political opinion was a central reason for targeting. Here, the timing of escalation after the property confrontation and the perpetrators’ statements about searches at Don Rafa’s house undercut a political‑motive theory. That focus on temporal sequencing and specific statements is legally defensible, but it imposes a high evidentiary hurdle for applicants who face amorphous, overlapping motives in contexts where criminal actors and political conflict intertwine.

There are plausible critiques. The court’s skepticism toward psychological harms, while anchored in precedent, can obscure situations where terrorizing tactics produce debilitating mental illness that effectively deprives victims of normal life and safety. Likewise, giving significant weight to the absence of contemporaneous police reports assumes reporting is feasible and safe; though the court recognized the futility exception, it demanded more concrete proof of futility here than the petitioners produced. That is often a hard evidentiary task for asylum seekers who flee quickly or fear reprisals from corrupt local officials.

## Evidence that might tip a similar case in the Tenth Circuit
Jimenez implicitly sketches the contours of what would have been persuasive. Representatives of such claims should, where possible, develop:

- Contemporaneous police reports, formal complaints, or other official documentation showing notice and the government’s inability or refusal to act. If reporting is dangerous or futile, an applicant should establish that with specific country‑condition evidence or prior instances showing systemic collusion or impunity.
- Medical records or psychological evaluations tying depression, panic disorder, or PTSD to the incidents. The Tenth Circuit permits psychological harm to be considered, but wants corroboration that the harm is severe and enduring.
- Corroboration of physical attempts to abduct or injure (photographs, surveillance footage, witness statements, hospital records), or other objective indicators (e.g., evidence of kidnapping rings operating in the area, contemporaneous media reports of similar attacks) showing the persecutor’s capacity and intent to cause severe harm.
- Clear evidence linking the persecutor’s motive to a protected ground—explicit statements by attackers (“we’re killing Liberal Party people”) or documentation of systemic targeting of party members in the applicant’s locale may satisfy the “central reason” requirement in mixed‑motive cases.
- Country‑condition reports and expert testimony establishing that local law enforcement is complicit with, or unable to control, particular criminal organizations—this helps make the futility argument when reporting is dangerous.

In short, the court wants tangible, contemporaneous, and corroborative evidence—especially where the alleged harms are largely threats and where motive is contested.

## Doctrinal and practical implications
Jimenez reaffirms several settled points in Tenth Circuit asylum jurisprudence: threats alone rarely suffice, psychological harm demands strong corroboration, mixed-motive inquiries require a protected ground to be a central reason, and applicants bear the burden of showing government inability or unwillingness to protect them. For asylum seekers from countries with violent non‑state actors who also have political dimensions to their targeting, the decision means litigators must be strategic and evidentiary: build the record early, document incidents contemporaneously when feasible, and marshal medical, expert, and country‑condition evidence.

More broadly, the case underscores cross‑circuit frictions. Some courts have been more receptive to threat‑based claims when threats were extreme, accompanied by near‑abductions, or set against a backdrop of demonstrated state complicity. The Tenth Circuit’s approach narrows that path, and will likely produce repeated appeals, particularly in factual patterns where criminal and political motives overlap. Immigration attorneys practicing in the Tenth Circuit should anticipate that adjudicators will probe motive and corroboration and that appellate courts will give substantial deference to the BIA’s factual conclusions.

## Conclusion
Jimenez v. Bondi is a useful, if sobering, exemplar of the evidentiary expectations the Tenth Circuit enforces in asylum cases centered on threats and intimidation. The panel did not deny the terror the family experienced, but it required more—either physical harm, stronger documentary corroboration of severe psychological injury, clearer linkage of motive to a protected ground, or firmer proof that reporting would have been futile—before finding persecution. For advocates, the lesson is practical: collect documentation, secure contemporaneous reporting when safe, obtain corroborating medical and expert assessments, and frame motive with precision. Absent that architecture of proof, even harrowing threats may not clear the circuit’s high bar.



# Mixed Motives, Central Reasons: Criminal Retaliation Versus Political Opinion in the Tenth Circuit’s Nexus Analysis

## Why Jimenez v. Bondi matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi (Oct. 7, 2025) is a useful, pragmatic application of the “nexus” requirement in asylum law when private criminal actors and political activity intersect. The panel denied asylum and withholding of removal because it concluded that the primary reason Petitioners were targeted was criminal retaliation—retaliation for a property dispute and interference with a narco-trafficking operation—rather than their political activity for the Colombian Liberal Party. For practitioners and adjudicators, the opinion crystalizes how timing, contemporaneous statements by persecutors, and escalation tied to a non-political incident can defeat a claim that political opinion was “at least one central reason” for harm.

## How the court reasoned

The court framed the dispute around the familiar statutory test: an applicant must show persecution “on account of” a protected ground, and in mixed-motive situations the protected ground must be “at least one central reason” for the harm. Drawing on Rivera-Barrientos and Dallakoti, and following its recent articulation of mixed-motive principles in O.C.V. v. Bondi, the Tenth Circuit acknowledged that persecutors may have multiple motives but emphasized that incidental or tangential political animus is not enough.

The panel relied heavily on the temporal pattern and content of the threats. Petitioners had engaged in political canvassing beginning in 2012 and supported a 2022 presidential campaign; earlier in that election season they received threatening calls and one of their colleagues was kidnapped and killed. Crucially, however, the threats escalated only after Jimenez intervened in a property dispute involving a tenant later identified as a narco-trafficker and local criminal leader known as “Don Rafa.” The two men who later menaced Jimenez explicitly accused him of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and of being responsible for searches of Don Rafa’s property. That the violence and more frightening in-person confrontations followed Jimenez’s professional and personal conflict with Don Rafa led the court to conclude that the predominant motive was protecting criminal revenue and security, not political animus. Because the record did not show that the earlier politically tinged threats were tied to the narco-traffickers, or that the perpetrators were motivated by Petitioners’ party work, the BIA’s finding that political opinion was merely “tangential and subordinate” was supported by substantial evidence.

## Analytical perspective: a sensible but demanding nexus inquiry

Jimenez is analytically straightforward: when an asylum applicant faces both political threats and criminal retaliation, the court will parse motive by the sequence of events and by what the persecutors actually said or did. That approach is sensible and administrable. It prevents asylum from becoming a refuge for victims of what is essentially private criminality where politics is a coincidental fact of biography.

At the same time, the decision highlights an evidentiary burden that can be difficult for asylum-seekers to meet. Persecutors rarely issue manifestos explaining their motives, and political animus can be expressed subtly or through local reputation and networks. The Tenth Circuit correctly reminded adjudicators that mixed motives are possible, but in practice the court’s insistence on demonstrable linkage—timing, perpetrators’ statements, and corroboration—raises the bar in cases where political activity predated or was contemporaneous with non-political conflicts.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Jimenez reinforces three durable doctrinal points. First, timing matters. Courts will weigh whether the persecutory acts correlate more closely with political activity or with an intervening event (here, the property dispute). Second, perpetrators’ statements are potent evidence; explicit references to politics or to protected characteristics will tip the balance toward nexus. Third, where private criminal actors are involved, adjudicators will be attentive to alternative, non-protected motives such as extortion, revenue protection, or retaliation for interference with criminal operations.

Practically, Jimenez may make it harder for applicants who combine political activity with roles that put them in conflict with criminal actors—journalists investigating corruption, lawyers assisting victims, or party activists who happen to confront local criminal interests—to obtain asylum unless they can link the violent acts to politics rather than to criminal revenge. The decision will likely push practitioners to anticipate more searching nexus fact-finding and to bring forward corroboration that ties the persecutors to political purpose, rather than merely to criminality.

## What practitioners should do differently

To survive the Tenth Circuit’s scrutiny in mixed-motive contexts, applicants should marshal evidence that makes political motive central, not ancillary. That evidence can take several forms.

First, establish contemporaneous linkage between early political threats and later violence: if the same actors or the same operational network made both sets of threats, that continuity is critical. Demonstrate that the people who issued early political threats were the same individuals or affiliates who later escalated violence.

Second, collect and emphasize persecutor statements that reference politics or political affiliations. Recorded threats, contemporaneous witness testimony recounting political slurs, or intercepted communications that tie the conduct to party activity carry weight.

Third, if the persecutors are publicly or privately affiliated with a rival political faction, produce documentation—news reports, prison/paramilitary records, social-media posts, or local election materials—showing those links. Expert country-conditions affidavits can be useful to explain how criminal actors and political operatives interact locally, and how a particular actor’s political alignment would make an applicant a target.

Fourth, corroborate the political nature of the applicant’s work. Campaign schedules, photos, testimony from other party members who received threats, and organizational records can show that the applicant’s political role made them visible and targeted.

Finally, report to authority and document the attempt. While reporting is not dispositive, evidence that the applicant sought official protection and was rebuffed, especially with contemporaneous reports that tie persecution to politics, helps prove both nexus and government inability/unwillingness.

## Conclusion

Jimenez v. Bondi is a clear application of the “at least one central reason” test in a messy real-world setting where politics and criminality converge. It affirms that asylum is not a backstop for private criminal retaliation absent a demonstrable political motive, but it also serves as a checklist for practitioners: in mixed-motive cases, make the political throughline explicit, contemporaneous, and corroborated, because timing and words from the persecutors often decide the case.



# Unable or Unwilling, or Just Unasked? Failure to Report, Police Apathy, and Internal Relocation in Jimenez

## Why this case matters

Jimenez v. Bondi is a compact but instructive decision about a recurring fault line in U.S. asylum law: when does a victim’s failure to secure police protection undercut a claim that her home government is “unable or unwilling” to protect her? The Tenth Circuit denied relief to a Colombian family who fled threats from narco-traffickers after a local dispute, accepting the Board of Immigration Appeals’ view that their limited outreach to authorities and a couple of unhelpful police encounters did not establish government inability or unwillingness to protect. The court reached that conclusion while tying it to its finding that internal relocation was reasonable — the family had safely stayed with relatives and others remained in Colombia unmolested.

The opinion is consequential for practitioners because it applies and clarifies the Singh v. Bondi framework in a fact pattern that will be familiar to many asylum seekers: threats from nonstate armed actors who may have, or be alleged to have, ties to local authorities. It underscores what the court treats as necessary evidence to trigger the “futility” exception to the ordinary requirement that applicants seek state protection, and it shows how a weak record on government complicity can carry through to defeat both asylum and withholding claims by supporting an internal-relocation finding.

## How the court reasoned

The panel applied settled Tenth Circuit law: to obtain asylum via past persecution or a well-founded fear of future persecution, an applicant must show (among other things) that the persecutor acted “on account of” a protected ground, and that the government is unable or unwilling to control the persecutor. The court affirmed the BIA on three interlocking conclusions.

First, on persecution, the court reiterated the familiar principle that threats and emotional distress, standing alone and even in the aggregate, do not always meet the threshold of persecution. Jimenez and his wife experienced threats, an assault on a factory employee, and a frightening encounter involving their child; the IJ found them credible. But the court relied on its precedents (Karki, Brandy, Chhetri) that place a relatively high bar on when threats and psychological harms suffice.

Second, on nexus, the court found substantial evidence that the persecutors were primarily motivated by Jimenez’s confrontation with “Don Rafa” over property and revenue, not by his—and his family’s—political activities. The timing of escalated threats after the property dispute, and the capture in the record of the attackers’ statements blaming Jimenez for searches, supported the BIA’s conclusion that political opinion was at best tangential.

Third, and most relevant for practitioners, the court rejected the argument that the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect the family. The decision leaned on two related points. The record showed the Colombian authorities had attempted searches and seizures at the problematic property, undermining an assertion of state capture. And crucially, Petitioners barely engaged with the state: they reported only limited contacts with two police officers who told them not to seek trouble, and they did not pursue broader avenues of protection (including using Jimenez’s law-enforcement contacts). Under Singh v. Bondi, the court emphasized notice to the state and the opportunity to respond; an applicant’s failure to report can undercut an inability/unwillingness claim unless the applicant shows that reporting would have been futile or dangerous. The Tenth Circuit found no such showing here. Finally, the court tied that result to internal relocation: family members still lived safely in Colombia, the family had spent three weeks safely at a relative’s home, and the threats were localized to their neighborhood — factors that supported the IJ’s conclusion that relocation within Colombia was reasonable.

## What the opinion signals about the “futility” and “unwilling or unable” inquiry

Jimenez illustrates the evidentiary contours that the Tenth Circuit expects when a claimant seeks to invoke the futility exception. A few discrete police encounters with indifferent officers will not, by themselves, establish systemic government failure. The court conspicuously distinguished isolated officer apathy from the sort of pattern or systemic evidence that courts find probative of inability or unwillingness. It cited Osorio‑Morales (7th Cir.) for the proposition that “a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show” government failure, and it relied on the presence of government efforts against the same criminal enterprise as counterevidence.

Put differently, to demonstrate that reporting would have been futile or dangerous, an applicant needs more than a generalized fear or a short, unproductive contact with police. Courts will look for indicia that the state had notice and either turned a blind eye or actively colluded: specific evidence of official complicity, a pattern of failed or abusive responses in the relevant region, prior reprisals for reporting, credible evidence that police take bribes from the persecutors, or expert testimony and country reports showing pervasive local corruption. Absent that record, courts are likely to insist that applicants at least attempt to obtain protection before conceding the state off-limits.

## How the government-protection analysis dovetailed with internal-relocation findings

The Tenth Circuit’s unwilling-or-unable analysis did double work: it not only negated a well-founded fear but also supported the BIA’s conclusion that internal relocation was feasible. The court relied on concrete indicators that relatives remained safely in Colombia and that the family had a prior safe stay in the Bogota countryside. It was consequential that the men who threatened the family told them to leave the neighborhood — not the country — a fact the court read as consistent with the availability of local relocation. The presence of civil‑service ties (Jimenez’s father’s continued work) and the government’s activity against the property amplified the court’s willingness to treat internal relocation as a realistic option.

This linkage illustrates a critical practical point: the weaker an applicant’s proof of systemic state failure, the easier it is for the government to rebut a fear-of-persecution claim both by denying a protection failure and by showing alternative safe avenues within the country. The two analyses are often mutually reinforcing.

## Practical implications and downstream effects

For asylum applicants and counsel, Jimenez is a reminder that the administrative record must include an affirmative showing on government notice and response — or persuasive proof of why reporting would have been futile or dangerous. Attorneys should document every effort to seek protection, preserve copies of police reports, and, when direct reporting is impracticable, assemble corroboration: contemporaneous communications, witness statements, proof of reprisals against others who reported, and expert affidavits explaining local patterns of corruption or collusion. Country reports, local NGO documentation, and prior litigation showing systemic failures in a particular municipality can help bridge a sparse personal reporting record.

Practically, evidence of prior reprisals for reporting, corroborated testimony that the persecutors retaliate against complainants, or proof that police are paid off are the kinds of facts courts find persuasive for the futility exception. Conversely, isolated evidence of apathy or a few unhelpful officers will likely fall short absent broader indicia of official complicity.

For courts, Jimenez follows a mainstream line — it aligns the Tenth Circuit with other circuits that require more than minimal contacts to establish systemic official failure. But it also signals the need for careful fact-sensitive work: the difference between “one unhelpful officer” and “systemic official complicity in [a region]” is often a matter of record development, not doctrine. The opinion therefore indirectly puts pressure on immigration adjudicators and advocates to develop fuller records early, lest appellate courts apply substantial-evidence review to an underdeveloped factual showing.

One potential downstream concern is a chilling effect: victims who reasonably fear retaliation for reporting may still be forced by courts to try to obtain protection and thereafter be penalized for not having done so. Jimenez reaffirms the futility exception but narrows the practical circumstances in which it will apply. Unless district‑ or country‑level evidence of systemic corruption becomes routine in the record, applicants from settings with localized or semi-covert collusion may face higher hurdles.

## Takeaways

Jimenez is a useful, if sobering, primer on how courts treat failures to report and isolated police apathy in asylum cases. It makes clear that (1) notice to the state and an opportunity to respond are central to the unable-or-unwilling inquiry; (2) isolated, one-off unhelpful police encounters are rarely enough to prove systemic state failure; (3) evidence that government authorities have acted against the same criminal enterprise undercuts claims of state capture; and (4) a weak record on government protection can dovetail with, and reinforce, an internal-relocation finding. For practitioners, the path to overcoming Jimenez is straightforward in theory — build the record on reporting, document reprisals, and secure expert or country-level corroboration — but demanding in practice.

In short, Jimenez reaffirms longstanding law in a familiar factual setting and puts a premium on meticulous record building in cases where applicants contend the state will not or cannot protect them.



# Deference All the Way Down: Substantial Evidence Review and Record‑Building Lessons from Jimenez v. Bondi

## Why Jimenez matters

Jimenez v. Bondi is a reminder of how decisive appellate deference can be in asylum litigation — even when an IJ finds a claimant credible. The Tenth Circuit’s opinion, handed down October 7, 2025, denies review of a Colombian family’s asylum and withholding claims after a single‑member BIA affirmance. The court stressed that substantial‑evidence review of fact findings leaves little room for an appellate court to reweigh competing inferences about persecution, motive, and state protection. For practitioners, the decision is a cautionary tale about the limits of credibility and the levers that actually win cases on review: tightly developed fact patterns, documentary corroboration of motive, proof of systemic state failure, and an administrative record that forecloses the most plausible alternate explanations offered by the government.

## What the court held (and how it got there)

The Tenth Circuit framed the case around the three familiar asylum building blocks: past persecution, nexus to a protected ground (political opinion here), and the government’s inability or unwillingness to protect the applicants (or the infeasibility of internal relocation). The court applied the usual deferential standards: it reviewed the single‑member BIA decision (while consulting the IJ for fuller reasoning) and applied the substantial‑evidence standard to factual findings.

On past persecution, the court acknowledged the family’s troubling experiences — repeated threats, a colleague’s political murder, a violent in‑person intimidation of the father and staff, and an episode in which a stranger accosted the child — but found the harms fell short of persecution as a legal matter. Citing circuit precedent, the panel reiterated that threats and psychological stress, standing alone, often do not meet the statutory standard unless they accumulate to a level of inflicted suffering that courts deem “offensive” rather than episodic or contingent. The petitioners conceded they suffered no lasting physical injury, which made the court’s path to denial easier under established Tenth Circuit authority.

On nexus, the court rejected the claim that the family’s political activity was a central reason for the mistreatment. The record, the court said, pointed to a different, non‑political motive: a property dispute and the perceived threat to criminal revenue and security posed by the father’s involvement with law enforcement and an eviction effort at premises linked to a local narco‑trafficker. The court emphasized that in mixed‑motivation settings the protected ground must be at least one central reason for targeting; where the evidence supports a dominant, alternate motive, the nexus fails under substantial‑evidence review.

Finally, the court upheld the BIA’s finding that the Colombian government was not shown to be unable or unwilling to protect the family. The record contained only limited interactions with individual, unhelpful police officers and no meaningful report filings, formal complaints, or corroborated evidence of systemic state collusion. Nor did petitioners develop a persuasive futility argument that establishing government notice would have been dangerous. The fact that law enforcement had undertaken searches at the implicated property further undermined the claim of state connivance. And since internal relocation was feasible — the family had briefly stayed safely with relatives outside the neighborhood — the court found another basis for denying asylum.

## Where petitioners’ case faltered: factual gaps that mattered

Jimenez illustrates how a few kinds of evidentiary and strategic weaknesses can be dispositive under substantial‑evidence review, even when claimants are believed at the outset. First, the petitioners did not pursue or preserve other potential legal avenues that might have offered complementary relief or at least forced a fuller record. They did not appeal the IJ’s CAT and due‑process conclusions, and they expressly declined to press those claims on appeal; that narrowing of issues removed alternative paths to relief.

Second, they failed to develop a particular‑social‑group theory. The asylum brief focused on political opinion but did not meaningfully argue or support membership in any comparator social group — a missed opportunity to present alternative protected‑ground theories.

Third, the record lacked strong corroboration tying the persecutor’s conduct to politics. The escalation in threats aligned temporally with a property dispute involving a narco‑trafficker; the perpetrators’ statements implicating the father’s role with prosecutors provided a law‑enforcement/professional motive that the BIA and the court found more compelling than political animus. Where an adverse actor has plausible non‑political motives, claimants must produce tangible evidence that political opinion was not merely incidental.

Fourth, petitioners offered only minimal proof that reporting to authorities would have been futile or dangerous. They relied on a couple of discouraging remarks from individual officers and general assertions about state‑criminal alignment, but the record also showed law‑enforcement activity at the contested property. Under the court’s framing, a single discouraging encounter with an officer — without corroboration of systemic failure or complaints showing pattern and practice — does not satisfy the unable‑or‑unwilling element.

Fifth, country‑conditions proof of state collusion or a pervasive inability to protect was thin. The Tenth Circuit treated the absence of systemic evidence (reports, NGO findings, or official documents showing state complicity) as a reason to credit the government’s theory that protection was available.

Finally, the court gave weight to safe‑relocation evidence: the family had stayed with relatives outside the immediate danger zone and relatives continue to live in Colombia without harm. Those facts helped the government rebut any presumption of future risk.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Jimenez does not break new doctrinal ground, but it reinforces several important lines of Tenth Circuit authority. It confirms that: (1) credibility findings alone will not trump the substantial‑evidence standard; (2) threats plus psychological stress are frequently insufficient absent a showing of severity or cumulative harm that courts recognize as persecution; (3) nexus in mixed‑motive contexts requires political opinion to be a central reason; and (4) the unable‑or‑unwilling analysis turns on demonstrable government notice, a meaningful opportunity to respond, and systemic evidence where collusion is alleged.

Practically, the decision will sharpen litigators’ focus on record building below the IJ. Because single‑member BIA decisions are common and appellate review is so deferential, the facts and documentary record developed at the first instance will often determine outcomes on appeal. Jimenez will likely push advocates to be more proactive: file and preserve alternative legal theories (particular social group, CAT, due process), pursue contemporaneous reporting to local authorities with documentation, secure medical and psychological evaluations when relevant, and obtain expert country‑conditions affidavits that directly address state capacity and ties between criminal actors and officials.

The opinion also underscores an institutional reality: where a private criminal actor’s motive is plausibly economic or personal, courts will default to those non‑protected explanations unless claimants can produce persuasive evidence to the contrary. That is a tough burden in many Latin America contexts where criminal actors intentionally obscure political motive.

## Practical record‑building guidance for practitioners

Jimenez is instructive as a checklist for what to press for at the IJ stage if you want an asylum claim to survive substantial‑evidence review on appeal. The list below reflects the kinds of record items the Tenth Circuit explicitly and implicitly treated as decisive:

- Build motive evidence. When political opinion is the asserted ground, gather contemporaneous documentary proof that political activity was known to and targeted by the persecutor: campaign schedules, witness statements corroborating political messaging in threats, social‑media posts, pamphlets or leaflets explicitly linking the victim’s politics to threats, and testimony or affidavits from local political figures. Where a persecutor has mixed motives, triangulate to show political animus was at least a central reason.

- Document government contacts and responses. File police reports and obtain copies; seek written receipts of complaints, investigation reports, or refusal letters. If reporting would be futile or dangerous, preserve contemporaneous evidence explaining why (threats tied to prior complainants, corroborating affidavits, or evidence of reprisals after earlier reports). If officers are unhelpful, obtain written statements from multiple officers or supervisors rather than relying on a single anecdotal discouraging remark.

- Corroborate psychological and physical harms. Secure expert medical or psychological evaluations linking symptoms to the asylum events, and show any functional impairments. Where physical injury is absent, high‑quality corroboration of severe, enduring psychological harm can sometimes help but must be tied to established standards and the cumulative record.

- Develop country‑condition proof tailored to state protection. Obtain NGO, U.N., and academic reports that address government capacity and patterns of collusion with criminal actors, ideally focusing on the applicant’s locality. An expert affidavit that explains law‑enforcement practices in the relevant municipality and interprets local events for the adjudicator can be pivotal.

- Preserve alternative claims and procedural objections. Don’t abandon CAT, PSO, due‑process, or particular‑social‑group theories prematurely. Even if they seem unlikely, pressing and preserving these claims can open other legal routes and often forces a more probing factual record. If the BIA enters a single‑member affirmance, consider motions for reconsideration or to refer to the full Board where appropriate facts or legal errors require fuller review.

- Make the relocation analysis concrete. If internal relocation is implausible, provide economic, administrative, and social‑network evidence showing why safe movement is not feasible. Demonstrate how the persecutor’s reach, control over resources, or social ties would make relocation ineffective.

## Conclusion

Jimenez v. Bondi illustrates the practical effect of substantial‑evidence review: the appellate court will sustain the BIA’s factual inferences unless the administrative record compels a contrary conclusion. Credibility is necessary but not sufficient; claimants must anticipate the court’s instinct to accept non‑political explanations for violence or intimidation and to demand tangible proof of systemic state failure before overturning a BIA finding. For practitioners, the lesson is straightforward — invest in the record below. Exhaustive, contemporaneous, and corroborated factual development is the best hedge against “deference all the way down.”
