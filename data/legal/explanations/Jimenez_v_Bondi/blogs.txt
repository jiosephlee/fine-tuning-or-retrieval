# Threats, Panic, and the Persecution Line: The Tenth Circuit’s Hard Look at "Threats‑Only" Asylum Claims

## Why this case matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi (Oct. 7, 2025) is a significant reaffirmation of the circuit’s skeptical approach to “threats‑only” asylum claims. The panel denied asylum and withholding of removal to a Colombian family who fled after repeated death threats, an assault at gunpoint, an attempted abduction of a child, and ongoing psychological distress. Although the Immigration Judge found the family credible, the Board of Immigration Appeals and the Tenth Circuit concluded that the combination of threats and psychological harms did not, as a matter of law, rise to past persecution; nor did the record show that the family had a sufficient nexus to a protected ground or that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them.

For practitioners and adjudicators, Jimenez crystallizes several practical points: the Tenth Circuit continues to treat threats and emotional suffering as seldom enough for asylum absent more tangible physical harm or a clear showing of systemic failure of protection; where the persecutor’s motive plausibly flows from a private dispute tied to criminal profit rather than a protected characteristic, the nexus inquiry will doom a claim; and failure to seek broader government assistance—or to document why doing so would be futile—will sharply undercut unable/unwilling arguments. The case thus serves as a warning about how to frame evidence and theory when representing clients who primarily suffered threats and fear.

## What the court did and why

The court’s decision tracks a familiar Tenth Circuit path. It analyzed the claim under the statutory framework for asylum and withholding of removal: (1) did the applicants suffer past persecution (which creates a rebuttable presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution)? (2) was any persecution on account of a protected ground (here, political opinion)? and (3) was the persecution attributable to actors the government is unwilling or unable to control? The court applied the familiar substantial‑evidence standard to the BIA’s factual findings.

On past persecution, the court stressed that threats alone, even when repeated and accompanied by serious psychological effects, generally do not meet the threshold for persecution in the Tenth Circuit. The opinion relied on earlier Tenth Circuit decisions—Karki (threats alone insufficient), Brandy (threats and non‑lasting harms insufficient), and Chhetri (even detention and threats did not necessarily satisfy persecution)—to hold that the petitioners’ experience, “in the aggregate,” did not count as persecution. The court noted the petitioners conceded they suffered no lasting physical injury and emphasized that, while psychological distress is relevant, it rarely by itself satisfies the persecution standard in this circuit.

On nexus, the court focused on motive. Petitioners argued that their work on a presidential campaign for a rival to Colombia’s president made them targets for politically motivated violence. The court, weighing the record, concluded that the escalation of threats and violence coincided with the principal applicant’s confrontation of a local criminal leader (Don Rafa) over property used for illicit activity. The threats thus appeared aimed at punishing or protecting the trafficker’s revenue and security, not at suppressing a political opinion. Because petitioners’ political activity was at most tangential—and because the record tied the persecutors’ animus principally to the property dispute—the political‑opinion nexus failed.

Finally, on government protection and internal relocation, the court observed sparse efforts by petitioners to seek help beyond telling local officers in their neighborhood. Isolated, apathetic responses from individual officers did not demonstrate a systemic inability or unwillingness of the Colombian state to protect them. The court also pointed to evidence that law enforcement had previously attempted searches of the implicated property, undermining any claim that the persecutor was untouchable. The record showed petitioners could temporarily relocate within Colombia (they had done so for three weeks) and had family who remained in Colombia without harm—evidence the court treated as bolstering the availability of internal relocation.

## How the Tenth Circuit’s approach compares to other circuits

Jimenez is representative of the Tenth Circuit’s more demanding posture on threats‑based persecution claims. That posture contrasts with more claimant‑friendly decisions in some other circuits—most notably the Ninth Circuit—which have sometimes found death threats and repeated terrorizing conduct sufficient to establish persecution, depending on the totality of circumstances.

The Ninth Circuit has on several occasions been willing to treat serious, detailed death threats and violent targeting as persecution when taken with other contextual factors (e.g., pattern of violence in the region, inability of police to protect, and an individualized risk). By contrast, the Tenth Circuit’s cases cited in Jimenez (Karki, Brandy, Chhetri, Rivera‑Barrientos) show a consistent hesitancy: threats, even repeated and frightening ones, will not often pass the line unless accompanied by physical injury, detention, torture, or clear evidence that the threats are part of a broader campaign of punitive violence that places the victim in imminent, substantial danger.

That divergence matters in practice. An applicant whose record is strongest on threats and psychological injury may have a significantly better chance in a jurisdiction that has recognized threats as persecution in the right circumstances, while the same record may founder in the Tenth Circuit unless it also includes corroborating evidence of physical harm or government impotence.

## Analytical perspective: strengths and vulnerabilities of the panel’s reasoning

There is force to the Tenth Circuit’s concern about policing the boundary between “serious harassment” and “persecution.” Asylum status confers significant immigration benefits; treating every credible, but non‑physical, threat as persecution risks stretching the refugee definition beyond the international norm that underlies it. The court also properly leans on motive—if the central reason for the harm is a private dispute tied to criminal profits, categorizing it as persecution on account of political opinion or another protected ground would flatten the statute’s particularity requirement.

At the same time, the court’s treatment of psychological harms and threats invites criticism. The opinion reiterates that psychological injury “rarely, if ever” suffices absent physical harm. That rule can risk underprotecting victims whose lives are fundamentally destabilized by non‑physical but severe and protracted campaigns of intimidation—especially where physical harm is imminent or where cultural or contextual factors make physical injury less likely but fear and disruption are pervasive. Moreover, the line between “threats only” and “threats plus” is factually thin: an armed in‑person confrontation at a workplace and an attempted abduction of a child may be experienced and functionally equivalent to physical violence for family safety and livelihood.

The court does engage with cumulative‑effects doctrine, citing Ritonga and noting that incidents are assessed together. Yet its application seems to give relatively little weight to the child’s attempted abduction and the in‑person gunpoint incidents—events that some jurists might regard as approaching physical persecution. The Tenth Circuit’s heavy reliance on the lack of lasting physical injury and the apparent private motive means that in mixed‑motive or mixed‑harm cases, claimants may face a narrow window to show persecution.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Several doctrinal implications follow from Jimenez:

First, practitioners in the Tenth Circuit must be prepared to document either physical harm or a tightly woven narrative showing that threats are part of an ongoing campaign aimed at inflicting severe harm (e.g., attempted abduction or explicit, corroborated execution orders) and that such conduct is likely to recur.

Second, the nexus analysis in Jimenez underscores the importance of tracing motive. When private actors rather than the state—or state‑adjacent criminal groups—are involved, it is critical to develop evidence showing that the persecutor’s animus was at least partly directed at a protected ground. Where the persecutor’s statements or actions expressly reference political activity, or where political actors are known to target campaigners, that evidence will be determinative. If the persecutor’s statements instead tie harm to a private dispute (property, debt, business competition), the political‑opinion theory will be vulnerable.

Third, the government‑protection and internal‑relocation holdings emphasize the evidentiary value of (a) reporting to authorities or documenting the futility/danger of doing so, and (b) showing attempts and inability to obtain effective protection beyond isolated, unhelpful officers. Practitioners should compile documentary proof of systemic failures (news reports, human‑rights reports, prior unsuccessful complaints, contemporaneous police reports) when claiming inability or unwillingness of protection. A client who speaks to only one patrol officer and then departs risks a finding that the state had the capacity to protect them.

Fourth, Jimenez signals that Tenth Circuit adjudicators will weigh family and relocation facts—such as relatives remaining safely in-country and prior successful short‑term internal relocation—heavily against claims that internal relocation is infeasible.

## Practical evidentiary takeaways

For lawyers preparing cases where threats and psychological harms are central, Jimenez suggests concrete strategies:

- Corroborate threats with as much contemporaneous evidence as possible: police reports, medical or mental‑health records documenting panic attacks or PTSD, witness statements, physical evidence (e.g., damaged property), and contemporaneous communications (voicemails, text messages, flyers). The court’s emphasis on substantial evidence means the record matters.

- Where physical harm is absent, document why it was prevented (e.g., near‑misses, attempted abduction, in‑person gunpoint threats) and the specificity of execution orders. The more the threats are targeted, specific, and repeated, the likelier a court will treat them as more than ephemeral intimidation.

- Demonstrate nexus: collect evidence that persecutors referenced political views, campaign work, or other protected characteristics. If the persecutor’s stated motivation appears mixed or private, produce contextual evidence showing political content in the persecutor’s statements or broader patterns of political violence against campaigners.

- Seek systemic corroboration for inability/unwillingness claims: formal complaints with police and written responses (or the lack thereof), FOIA or other documentary evidence showing prior unsuccessful enforcement efforts, expert country‑condition declarations explaining why a police response would likely be ineffective, and evidence that higher‑level officials have connections to or tolerate the persecutors.

- Address relocation defensibly: show why internal relocation would be unsafe or unreasonable (e.g., nationwide presence of persecutors, economic or administrative barriers, family ties jeopardized) through concrete examples rather than speculation.

## Conclusion

Jimenez reaffirms that, in the Tenth Circuit, credible threats and significant psychological harms alone rarely breach the statutory line into persecution. The decision underscores the critical importance of documenting physical manifestations of danger, tying persecutors’ motives to protected grounds, and proving the futility of seeking protection. For claimants whose experiences are dominated by threats, Jimenez cautions that careful evidentiary scaffolding—not simply credible testimony of fear—will be essential to clear the asylum bar in this circuit.



# When Politics Is Tangential: Mixed‑Motive Nexus After Canon Jimenez v. Bondi

## Why this case matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Canon Jimenez v. Bondi is a useful, and in some ways cautionary, reminder of how difficult it can be for asylum seekers to satisfy the “nexus” requirement when private actors with mixed motives target them. At issue in the appeal was whether threats and harassment aimed at a Colombian family—who had been active in partisan campaigning and whose husband had professionally confronted a local narco‑trafficker about property—were “on account of” the family’s political opinion. The court affirmed the Board of Immigration Appeals’ (BIA) conclusion that the persecutor’s predominant motive was economic and retaliatory—protecting criminal revenue and security after Jimenez’s interference—rendering any political animus merely “tangential.” That conclusion defeated the applicants’ claim under the statutory requirement that a protected ground be “at least one central reason” for persecution.

The ruling has important implications for future asylum claims that arise where criminal actors, corruption, or anti‑corruption activities intersect with political activism. It underscores the evidentiary and framing challenges in mixed‑motive cases—particularly where the applicant’s conduct could plausibly have provoked private criminal retaliation for reasons other than political opposition.

## The court’s reasoning in a nutshell

The Tenth Circuit applied the familiar three‑part test for past persecution: harm rising to persecution, causation (nexus) to a protected ground, and government inability or unwillingness to control the persecutor. While the court found that the petitioners suffered threats and frightening incidents, it concluded these harms—taken in the aggregate—did not meet the jurisdiction’s established baseline for “past persecution.” More centrally for the nexus discussion, the court held that substantial evidence supported the BIA’s determination that the persecutor’s main reason for targeting Jimenez and his family was that Jimenez’s involvement in a property dispute interfered with the trafficker’s revenue and security interests. That interference, not the family’s partisan work for a rival presidential candidate, was the dominant motive.

The court relied on several fact‑based signals: the escalation of threats began only after Jimenez confronted the tenant (who was linked to the trafficker), the persecutors explicitly accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” and of working for the prosecutor’s office, and the timing and content of the threats connected the harassment more closely to the property dispute than to the family’s prior campaigning. Against that backdrop, the court saw Petitioners’ political activity as tangential rather than a central causal reason. The opinion reiterates the settled principle that when a persecutor has multiple motives, at least one protected ground must be a central reason for the harm—not merely present or subordinate.

## Putting the ruling in doctrinal context

The decision tracks established Tenth Circuit doctrine—drawing on Rivera‑Barrientos and related precedent—about the “one central reason” standard for nexus. It also reflects an established reluctance to construe private‑actor violence as persecution on a protected ground unless the evidence shows a clear, central causal link to the protected characteristic or opinion.

There are two doctrinal points to highlight. First, mixed‑motive analysis remains fact‑intensive and deferential: courts evaluate whether the administrative record compels a different conclusion, and they do not reweigh credibility where substantial evidence supports the BIA’s reading. That means strategic evidentiary development at the administrative hearing stage is critical.

Second, claims premised on anti‑corruption, whistleblowing, or law‑enforcement‑adjacent activities face a particular challenge: persecutors will often couch retaliation as a response to interference with criminal economics or security rather than as punishment for political opinion. When applicants have engaged in both partisan activity and actions that might directly threaten private criminal interests, adjudicators may view the latter as the motivating factor. The Tenth Circuit’s opinion reinforces that inferential gap.

## Practical consequences and downstream effects

For practitioners and advocates, Canon Jimenez signals several practical realities. If an applicant’s political activity is temporally distant, diffuse, or shared with many others in an area—while confrontations with criminal actors are proximate, individualized, and tied to specific interference with criminal enterprises—adjudicators may find the political explanation weak. Equally, when private actors articulate non‑political reasons for violence (e.g., punishing a “snitch” or protecting revenue), decisionmakers may credit those motives absent strong, contrary evidence.

The decision may also be cited in future cases as support for a narrow reading of political‑opinion nexus where criminal‑economic motives are plausible. That could make it harder for clients who combine community leadership, anti‑corruption activities, or legal work with overt partisan campaigning to obtain asylum unless they can develop record evidence tying persecutors’ statements, patterns, or organizational incentives directly to political animus.

## Strategies for proving nexus in mixed‑motive settings

Canon Jimenez does not foreclose asylum grants in mixed‑motive cases, but it points to what litigants must show to get past the “one central reason” hurdle. A few practical strategies follow from the opinion:

- Seek contemporaneous statements or objective indicators of political animus. Persecutors’ own words—even if ambiguous—are potent. When the persecutor explicitly references the applicant’s political activities, that substantially shores up a nexus claim. Record any threats or materials that tie the violence to partisan identifiers (party membership, campaign role, slogans) rather than to business or private disputes.

- Emphasize timing and escalation tied to political acts. If threats or violence begin immediately after a political speech, protest, or campaign action, highlight that temporal link. Here, the court stressed that the threats escalated after a property confrontation; litigants should document and argue the reverse where the facts allow.

- Build structural context—show that the persecutor targets political actors systematically. Evidence that the group targets rivals of a political faction, or has a pattern of suppressing partisan opponents, helps place individual incidents in a political pattern rather than in isolated criminal retaliation.

- Distinguish professional or anti‑corruption activity from neutral private disputes. When an applicant’s professional role involved representing victims, reporting crimes, or cooperating with law enforcement on matters that overlap with public‑interest lawyering or civic political action, emphasize how those roles are political in your client’s country insofar as they represent opposition to entrenched power or policy.

- Use corroboration beyond testimony: police reports, contemporaneous news articles, NGO reports, and expert country‑condition evidence can show that local criminal actors operate as political enforcers or that certain groups are informally allied with political factions—countering the inference that motives were purely economic.

## Conclusion

Canon Jimenez v. Bondi reaffirms that mixed‑motive asylum claims require careful factual and doctrinal navigation. Where private persecutors have plausible nonpolitical reasons to retaliate, courts will look for strong, specific evidence that political opinion occupied a central causal role. For practitioners, the case underscores the need to anticipate mixed‑motive in the record and to marshal concrete indicators—statements, timing, patterns, and corroboration—that tie feared persecution squarely to a protected ground.



# Unable or Unwilling, or Just Unreported? The Tenth Circuit Tightens the Reporting Expectation

## Why this case matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi—denying asylum to a Colombian family who received threats and some physical intimidation—sends a clear signal about how this circuit will treat the “unable or unwilling” prong of asylum law when the applicant did not make robust attempts to involve local authorities. At its core, the court’s analysis reinforces a practical rule: if an applicant does not show that she reported persecution to government authorities (and that reporting would have been futile or dangerous), the BIA’s finding that the home government could have protected the applicant is likely to survive review. That emphasis tightens the evidentiary expectation for many asylum seekers, particularly in contexts of organized crime or quasi-state actors where distrust of police is common.

Practitioners and adjudicators should take note. The opinion foregrounds two pieces of evidence that sufficed to defeat the applicants’ claim: (1) the petitioners’ limited steps to seek protection (essentially a couple of conversations with local officers), and (2) affirmative government action against the criminal enterprise plaintiff’s client encountered—namely prior searches and seizures at the property linked to the persecutors. The combination allowed the BIA and the Tenth Circuit to conclude that the Colombian government was neither unwilling nor unable to control the persecutors, and that internal relocation would be feasible. The decision refines, and in practice constrains, the contours of the futility-and-danger exception in this circuit.

## What the court held and how it reasoned

The family’s claims rested on allegations of threats, an assault at gunpoint, and an incident where a man accosted the petitioner’s young son—conduct they attributed to a narco-trafficking leader (“Don Rafa”) and his associates. The IJ and BIA found the family credible but concluded: (1) the harms did not amount to past persecution; (2) the threats were motivated more by the petitioner’s interference with Don Rafa’s economic interests than by their political activity; (3) the Colombian government was not shown to be unable or unwilling to protect them; and (4) internal relocation was reasonable.

On the government-protection point, the court framed the inquiry around two central facts it deemed dispositive: whether the government tried to stop the persecutors and whether the applicant sought or was offered protection. The Tenth Circuit emphasized precedent that notice to authorities matters because, without notice, the record cannot show how the police would have responded. Although the petitioners spoke with two officers who advised them not to “seek trouble,” the court viewed those isolated interactions as insufficient to establish systemic police failure. The court also pointed to evidence that Colombian authorities had previously attempted “several searches and seizures” at the property linked to Don Rafa, which undercut the claim that the persecutors were aligned with or protected by the state. Because the petitioners did not pursue wider reporting—through other police channels, prosecutors, or leveraging the petitioner’s own family contacts in law enforcement—the court held the BIA’s finding that the government could have protected them was supported by substantial evidence.

## Analysis: a tightening, not a new test

The Tenth Circuit’s opinion does not announce a new legal test: it applies longstanding principles about reporting and futility. But it does sharpen the evidentiary tilt against applicants who provide only limited evidence of attempts to engage the state. Two interpretive points matter.

First, the decision underscores the circuit’s insistence on a fact-specific showing that reporting would have been futile or dangerous. The futility/danger exception remains available, but the petitioner bears a meaningful burden to either (a) produce evidence that reporting would have been ineffective (for example, objective evidence of collusion between persecutors and authorities), or (b) show that reporting itself would have put the applicant at immediate risk. Here, the court found neither: the record included at least some official action against the property, and the petitioners did not show systemic collusion or that the officers they contacted were representative of the government’s response.

Second, the opinion elevates corroborating government activity as a counterweight to the applicant’s narrative. Evidence that officials had investigated the very property connected to the persecutors provided the court a tangible reason to credit the government’s ability to act—a contrast to cases where the state’s absence from the scene is the dominant fact. In other words, affirmative, documented government efforts can be a powerful component of an IJ/BIA credibility and protection analysis.

The Tenth Circuit’s approach is defensible: asylum law contemplates, as a matter of fairness and feasibility, that governments be given an opportunity to protect their citizens unless doing so would have been pointless or perilous. But the decision also raises practical concerns. In many contexts of organized crime or endemic corruption, a petitioner’s reluctance to report stems precisely from reasonable fear that the report will bring retaliation, or from a realistic belief that officers are compromised. Requiring compelling evidence of systemic corruption may be a classic catch-22: applicants who cannot safely document corruption for fear of exposure will struggle to meet the evidentiary bar.

## How this compares across circuits

Circuits vary in how aggressively they treat the reporting expectation and the futility exception. The Tenth Circuit’s emphasis on affirmative governmental action and on the need for more than an isolated, unhelpful officer remark aligns it with the Seventh Circuit’s skepticism of one-off negative interactions (see, e.g., Osorio-Morales), and with decisions that require systemic evidence of state complicity rather than anecdotal police apathy. Other circuits—particularly the Ninth—have sometimes been more receptive to a broader set of indicators of futility or danger, such as demonstrated patterns of corruption in a region, credible reports of collusion between local police and organized crime, or explicit testimony showing that reporting would have risked immediate retaliation.

Jimenez thus situates the Tenth Circuit toward the stricter end of the spectrum: an applicant cannot rely solely on subjective fear or limited, localized refusals from officers to demonstrate that reporting would have been futile. That divergence means asylum strategies that work in, say, Ninth Circuit filings may need supplementation in the Tenth Circuit.

## Practitioner takeaways: documenting the inability or unwillingness to protect

For attorneys representing asylum seekers from countries with organized crime, corruption, or weak rule of law, Jimenez is a practical reminder to build the record on both process and background evidence. Key strategies include:

- Document attempts to notify authorities: contemporaneous police reports, copies of any written complaints, docket numbers, names and ranks of officers contacted, dates, and, where possible, signed receipts. If reporting was done informally, memorialize details as soon as feasible and corroborate with witnesses.

- Preserve contemporaneous evidence of danger in reporting: if an applicant feared reporting because an assailant threatened retaliation for contacting police, get that testimony on the record with specifics (who said what, when, in front of whom). Corroborate with witnesses, medical records, or contemporaneous communications.

- Collect objective country- and region-specific evidence of corruption or collusion: credible NGO or U.S. government reports (Human Rights Watch, Amnesty, State Department country reports), local press accounts documenting police-collusion incidents, or academic studies tying criminal groups to state actors. In Jimenez the court gave weight to evidence of the government’s actions against the property; the converse—evidence of government inaction or complicity—should be developed with equal care.

- Use professional and institutional contacts: where the applicant or family members have ties to law enforcement or prosecutors (as in Jimenez), trace how those contacts were used, whether higher-level reporting channels were pursued, and why those channels were or were not viable. If the applicant sought help from a local officer who shrugged them off, attempt to show whether that officer’s response reflected broader practice (e.g., multiple citizens reporting similar experiences).

- Consider strategic declarations from experts or former officials: affidavits from Latin America country experts, former prosecutors, or police accountability organizations can paint a picture of systematic problems that go beyond the applicant’s immediate neighborhood.

- If reporting is truly futile, show why: affidavits that explain the risk of retaliation tied to reporting (e.g., “in this municipality, those who report are subsequently attacked”) or documentary evidence that others who reported suffered reprisals can be persuasive.

Finally, where feasible, counsel should make a record of internal-relocation feasibility contemporaneously: lay out where the applicant could go, whether they have family there, and what steps would be required to relocate safely, because the government can often rebut a fear-of-return claim through relocation evidence as the court did here.

## Broader implications and likely downstream effects

Jimenez will likely produce three predictable effects. First, asylum applicants in the Tenth Circuit—particularly those fleeing organized criminals or non-state actors—will face a higher hurdle unless they can either show that reporting would have been futile/dangerous or that the government was systemically compromised. Second, immigration courts and the BIA will lean on similar fact patterns (e.g., any documented police activity against perpetrators) to deny claims, framing those exercises as evidence of the state’s capacity to protect. Third, attorneys operating in the Tenth Circuit will increasingly front-load detailed reporting evidence and country-specific corroboration into administrative records and initial asylum applications.

At the doctrinal level, the opinion reinforces the centrality of the “unable or unwilling” inquiry to the asylum claim and underscores the interplay between credibility, corroboration, and standard of review. It also reminds us that the BIA and courts will treat governmental action—or its absence—as a pivotal, often outcome-determinative fact. The potential risk is that applicants who reasonably avoid formal reporting because fear is rational will be asked to produce an evidentiary record they cannot safely create. That tension between rational fear and evidentiary demands will be fertile ground for future litigation and may prompt calls for more nuanced guidelines on what kinds of background evidence suffice to establish futility when direct reporting is dangerous.

## Conclusion

Jimenez v. Bondi does not rewrite asylum law, but it signals a narrower pathway for applicants in the Tenth Circuit who rely on the futility-and-danger exception to explain why they did not seek state protection. The decision places a premium on contemporaneous reporting efforts, corroborating evidence, and objective indicators of state capacity or incapacity. Practitioners should respond by documenting reporting attempts and by assembling robust country- and region-level materials showing why reporting would have been pointless or perilous. For courts, the opinion reflects a familiar judicial impulse to require some demonstrable effort to engage state remedies unless the record convincingly shows that such efforts would have been futile or dangerous. The practical consequence is a heightened evidentiary bar—one that will shape asylum litigation strategy across the Tenth Circuit going forward.



# Internal Relocation as a Decisive Tool: Short-Term Safety, Family Residence, and Regulatory Reasonableness

Why this matters: In Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez v. Bondi, the Tenth Circuit denied asylum and withholding of removal for a Colombian family whose political activity and an ensuing property dispute drew violent threats. After rejecting past persecution, nexus, and government unwillingness to protect, the court also held — briefly but decisively — that internal relocation within Colombia remained a reasonable option. That conclusion rested in part on two relatively thin factual predicates: (1) the family safely stayed for three weeks at a rural residence in the Bogota countryside before fleeing to the United States, and (2) family members still reside in Colombia without apparent harm. The decision illustrates how courts can lean on safe-relocation doctrine to dispose of asylum claims even where persecution is plausible, and it highlights important doctrinal tensions about what makes relocation “reasonable” under 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i).

The court’s treatment of relocation: The Tenth Circuit’s analysis follows the familiar two-part framework by which the government may rebut an asylum applicant’s claim — either by showing a fundamental change in circumstances or that internal relocation would avoid future persecution. After concluding petitioners failed to establish past persecution or government inability/unwillingness to protect, the court addressed relocation only briefly. It emphasized that (a) relatives still live in Colombia unharmed; (b) Jimenez’s father remains employed in the criminal justice system; and (c) the family had already lived safely with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks. The court also noted that the threats the family received had told them to leave their neighborhood, not the country, and thus were geographically limited in reach. On that record the court found relocation reasonable and feasible, and thus confirmed denial of asylum and withholding (and implicitly disposed of any remaining asylum presumption).

Why the concise treatment is consequential: Relocation is often the government’s most practical defense to asylum claims. The Tenth Circuit’s reliance on relatively modest indicia of safety — a three-week stay and the presence of unharmed relatives — signals a willingness to accept short-term and geographically focused evidence of safety as dispositive of “reasonableness.” That approach can be efficient, but it also raises doctrinal questions about how courts should apply the regulatory factors that govern feasibility and reasonableness.

Doctrinal friction: 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i) and the accompanying caselaw require courts to weigh a range of considerations when determining whether internal relocation is reasonable: the nature of the feared harm, the geography and size of the home country, the applicant’s ability to relocate (including age, health, family ties and economic prospects), whether state protection exists in the proposed area, and any foreseeable hardships that could result from relocation. The Jimenez opinion cites several traditional touchstones — family presence and a prior temporary relocation — but does not delve into many of these other factors. Specifically:

- Economic and administrative burdens. The court mentions Petitioners’ cursory briefing that relocation would impose administrative and economic burdens but does not engage those claims substantively. For families uprooted from urban livelihoods (a factory, local political networks, and potential professional licensure issues), relocation may mean loss of income, hurdles to transfer or recognition of credentials, and the need to reestablish housing and schools. These costs can be particularly acute for single-income or low-margin households. The regulation and many circuits look to such burdens when assessing “reasonableness,” but the Tenth Circuit’s opinion treats them as peripheral given the court’s other findings.

- Duration and representativeness of a safe stay. A three-week stay in the countryside is persuasive on one level — it is direct, contemporaneous evidence that the family could avoid immediate harm by moving — but it is limited as proof of sustained safety. The court accepts the three weeks as evidence that relocation is “feasible,” but does not interrogate whether that short interval reliably predicts long-term safety (e.g., whether the threats were truly localized or whether persecutors could easily follow or surveil the family).

- Child-focused concerns and schooling. Petitioners raised stress and panic attacks in a child, yet the opinion does not consider the practical impact of relocation on the children’s healthcare, schooling continuity, or psychological care. Courts increasingly recognize that relocation carries distinct burdens for minors that can factor into the reasonableness calculus.

- Security reach and perpetrator mobility. The opinion relies on threats that appeared neighborhood-specific. But it does not analyze how organized criminal actors or paramilitaries in Colombia move and whether geography alone insulates potential victims. Given the alleged ties between local narco-trafficking elements and political actors, assessing the credible mobility and reach of the perpetrators is often central to a relocation inquiry.

Practical implications for practitioners and future litigants: Because relocation evidence can decide claims, applicants should build a fuller record on feasibility and reasonableness when opposing the government’s safe-relocation defense. A richer record should, at minimum, include:

- Detailed testimony and documentary evidence about employment, licensure, and earnings history; prospects for employment elsewhere in-country; and costs of relocation (housing, transportation, school enrollment). For professionals like provisional lawyers, evidence about whether licensure or law-student status transfers or is recognized elsewhere is important.

- Longitudinal safety indicators. Short stays matter, but applicants should show sustained safety or explain why a brief safe period nonetheless demonstrates durable safety (for instance, by showing the persecutor’s lack of regional reach or local law enforcement’s effectiveness in the new area).

- Child welfare and education impacts. Medical or school records demonstrating ongoing psychological harm or treatment needs, plus evidence about availability of services in the proposed relocation area, can meaningfully inform the relocation reasonableness analysis.

- Country-condition evidence about perpetrator mobility and local government capacity. Local police responsiveness, investigations after similar threats, and reports about the reach of criminal gangs can frame whether a localized threat is likely to migrate with the target.

- Evidence of futile reporting or systemic corruption if applicants cannot or did not report to authorities; conversely, if prior reporting occurred, documentation of the government’s inadequate response can undercut a relocation defense.

Broader doctrinal stakes and downstream effects: Jimenez exemplifies how courts use relocation doctrine to cabin asylum claims without resolving harder questions about nexus or government complicity. That can be appealing for courts seeking efficient resolution, but it risks turning relocation into a near-per se escape hatch where a minimal showing of temporary safety or family presence will extinguish a claim. Over time, that trend could incentivize more fact-intensive, prophylactic government defenses and place a premium on asylum counsel’s ability to litigate nuanced feasibility facts that go beyond headline allegations of threat.

Conclusion: The Tenth Circuit’s use of relocation in Jimenez underscores the doctrine’s practical potency. The opinion is consistent with the governing regulation, but its succinct treatment leaves open important questions about how courts should balance short-term safe stays, economic and child-centered burdens, and the mobility of persecutors. Practitioners should anticipate this battleground and prepare robust relocation records — not only to rebut government assertions of safety but also to show why temporary refuge or family presence does not equate to a reasonable, sustainable option under 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13.



# The Perils of Under‑Developed PSG Claims: What Wasn’t Briefed in Canon Jimenez

Why this case matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Canon Jimenez v. Bondi is an object lesson for practitioners: if you hope to win asylum or withholding of removal on the basis of membership in a particular social group (PSG), you must define and develop that theory fully in the administrative record and on appeal. The petitioners in this Colombia case presented a fact pattern—campaigning for an opposition party, alleged threats and violence from organized criminal actors, and one petitioner’s role as a provisional lawyer and occasional prosecutor’s office intern—that could plausibly fit within several PSG formulations that have traction in Latin American and gang‑related contexts. But the family never pressed a PSG argument in briefing below or on appeal, and the court refused to reach it. The result: the petitioners lost on alternative grounds (no past persecution, no nexus, government protection/relocation feasible), and the opinion serves as a cautionary blueprint on how omission can be dispositive.

What the court did — and did not — decide

The Tenth Circuit’s analysis is straightforward and anchored to the administrative record. The court affirmed the BIA’s conclusion that the petitioners failed to prove past persecution and, in any event, failed to show that any harm was “on account of” a protected ground. Critically, the court limited its review to the political‑opinion nexus because petitioners “did not brief any arguments regarding the existence of their particular social group,” and therefore the PSG avenue was forfeited. That procedural point mattered: without a concrete PSG on the table, the court had no occasion to apply the tripartite PSG test (particularity, social distinction, and nexus) as articulated in Supreme Court and circuit precedents such as Crespin‑Valladares.

The practical upshot for practitioners is stark. PSG theories are often the most viable path in cases involving non‑state or quasi‑state persecutors—gangs, paramilitaries, criminal syndicates—and where political opinion is not a dominant or clearly articulated motivation. But PSG claims do not survive on generalities; they must be pled, developed, and supported with tailored evidence.

Plausible PSG formulations in Colombia cases — and what they require

Looking at Colombia litigation more broadly, several PSG formulations have been recognized (or at least frequently litigated) as potentially viable:

- “Witnesses/informants” or “people who cooperate with law enforcement.” This group is natural where the targeted conduct stems from perceived cooperation with prosecutors or police investigations. To carry this theory a practitioner should show (1) that the applicant actually performed roles or functions that would make them be perceived as a cooperating witness or informant (e.g., assisted prosecutors, reported crimes, initiated complaints, or helped facilitate searches/seizures); (2) that society—particularly the persecutor community and the applicant’s local community—recognizes and treats such persons as a distinct group (social distinction); and (3) that the persecutor targeted them because of that perceived role (nexus).

- “Family members of those who oppose or have opposed gangs/organized crime” (or “family members of those who cooperate with the state”). Family‑based PSGs arise where violence targets relatives as a way to punish, intimidate, or neutralize a perceived opponent. Establishing such a group requires showing that the persecutors understood the family relationship and targeted the family because of it, that the family is seen as a distinct social grouping in the relevant context, and that the family members bear the stigma or threat that the persecutor intended.

- “Social leaders” or “community leaders” — often used in contexts where NGOs, human‑rights defenders, local politicians, or community activists are targeted. Petitioners in Colombia who are political activists or locally prominent volunteers sometimes fit this mold. To succeed, the applicant must show that (i) they occupy a role that distinguishes them from the broader community; (ii) that role is recognized (and feared) by the persecutors and neighbors; and (iii) the targeting is at least in part because of that role.

What Crespin‑Valladares and related doctrine demand

The Supreme Court’s ruling in Pereira/Crespin‑Valladares (which practitioners routinely cite for PSG analysis) and subsequent circuit decisions require careful attention to three elements:

- Particularity: The PSG must be defined with sufficient clarity and discrete boundaries so it is administrable. “All poor people in X region” is too amorphous; “women who report sexual violence to police in Municipality Y” may be sufficient if framed with evidentiary anchors. Particularity asks whether the group is internally cohesive and readily identifiable.

- Social distinction: The group must be perceived as a social grouping within the claimant’s society. This is an empirical question, and courts look for evidence — expert reports, country‑condition literature, testimony from community members, and corroborating documentation — showing that ordinary people in the claimant’s society would recognize the group.

- Nexus: The persecutor’s motive must be at least one central reason for the harm. For PSG claims, that means demonstrating that the persecutor targeted the claimant because of their membership in the group, not merely because of criminality, economic motive, or private vendetta.

Crespin‑Valladares and its progeny show that courts will scrutinize both legal formulations and supporting proof. Where a PSG is thinly defined or unsupported by the record, the risk of rejection increases.

Evidence that practitioners should gather (and marshal)

The Canon Jimenez record illustrates recurring evidentiary weaknesses in PSG cases. Below are practical pointers on building a record that lets a PSG theory live or die on the merits rather than on procedural omission:

- Define the group early and concretely in the written asylum application and in the IJ hearing. Don’t wait until appeal. If a claimant could plausibly be an “informant” or “family of a targeted leader,” say so and explain why based on facts.

- Fact detail matters for particularity: describe the attributes that make membership identifiable (e.g., “persons who have filed formal complaints with the Fiscalía in Municipality X,” “family members of people who have testified against narco‑traffickers,” “provisional lawyers who have assisted in seizures of day‑rental housing used for trafficking”).

- Social‑distinction proof: procure expert declarations (country experts, sociologists, former local officials), contemporaneous news reports, NGO reports describing threats to informants or families, and affidavits from members of the claimant’s community. Courts respond well to multiple corroborating sources showing that the group is recognized and targeted.

- Nexus proof: keep correspondence, screenshots, threats that mention the claimant’s role, witness statements from neighbors or coworkers describing what the persecutors said (e.g., “you work with the prosecutor”), and documentation that connects the persecutor’s stated rationale to the claimant’s group membership. When persecutors explicitly label someone a “snitch” or “informant,” that can be powerful if the record demonstrates that the persecutor understood and acted on that label.

- Government response: because inability or unwillingness to protect is critical, show police reports, attempts to seek protection, evidence that reporting would be futile or dangerous (e.g., complaints to police that produced no action, evidence of collusion), or proof of systemic failure in the relevant locality. If the applicant did not report because it would have been futile, explain why and back the claim with documentation.

Why omission is costly: waiver and missed doctrinal analysis

Canon Jimenez’s procedural posture is a reminder that courts routinely decline to consider unbriefed claims. The Tenth Circuit expressly limited its PSG review because the petitioners “did not brief any arguments regarding the existence of their particular social group.” That procedural forfeiture is consequential: it prevented an analysis that might otherwise have unpacked the facts under the particularity/social‑distinction/nexus rubric and could have yielded a different outcome had the record been developed.

Beyond forfeiture, the opinion underscores another common hazard: trying to shoehorn a dispute into a political‑opinion theory when the facts are more naturally characterized as an informant/witness/PSG problem. The court here concluded the dominant motive was economic/personal (threat to Don Rafa’s revenue/security) tied to a property dispute and alleged interference with illicit operations, not a political opinion central to the harm. When motives are mixed or ambiguous, explicit PSG advocacy can preserve an alternative path.

Downstream effects and strategic takeaways

Canon Jimenez will be cited as a cautionary tale in removal adjudications involving criminal or quasi‑state persecutors. For practitioners, the takeaways are pragmatic:

- Put your PSG theory on record early and develop it fully — both legally and factually. Don’t assume a political‑opinion argument alone will carry the day when non‑state actors are the primary aggressors.

- Build a multi‑layered evidentiary record: contemporaneous documents, police reports, expert declarations, community witness statements, and media/NGO reporting that supports particularity and social distinction.

- Anticipate and rebut the government’s potential repositioning of motive (economic, personal vendetta, or criminally opportunistic) by corroborating evidence that ties the persecutor’s actions to the claimant’s group membership.

- Where reporting to police occurred, document it; where it did not occur, explain and document the futility, fear, or risk of reporting.

- On appeal, don’t sacrifice alternative theories. If you have a viable PSG claim, brief it even if you emphasize political opinion below; appellate courts are less forgiving of new theories not developed in the administrative record.

Conclusion

Canon Jimenez is not a decision that reimagines PSG doctrine. Instead, it reaffirms an enduring lesson: asylum outcomes often turn on the quality of legal framing and the depth of factual development. In countries like Colombia, where organized criminal actors target a range of people—informants, community leaders, family members—the PSG route is often the most doctrinally and factually apt. But PSGs are not self‑executing; they require definition, proof of social recognition, and a clear link between membership and harm. For practitioners, the case is a prompt to be thorough, early, and strategic in both pleadings and evidence collection—because what you fail to brief can determine whether the merits are ever reached.



# Substantial Evidence as the Silent Decider: Deference and Record-Building in Asylum Appeals

## Why this case matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi (Oct. 7, 2025) is not a dramatic new doctrinal pivot. But it is a clear, practical reminder of how often—and how decisively—immigration appeals lose at the petition-for-review stage because of the substantial-evidence standard and related doctrines of deference. For practitioners, the opinion is a compact lesson in what arguments typically flounder on appeal and what kinds of record-building can make the difference between reversal and the court shrugging that “substantial evidence” supports the Board’s view.

The panel denied review of the BIA’s affirmance of an IJ’s asylum and withholding denials for a Colombian family, emphasizing three core points that recur across asylum jurisprudence: (1) threats and emotional distress alone rarely amount to past persecution; (2) mixed-motive claims fail when the record supports a predominant non-protected motive; and (3) applicants who do not meaningfully seek or document state protection undercut an “unable or unwilling” showing. Layered over those substantive holdings is the appellate court’s deference to the BIA—and its willingness to consult the IJ’s fuller reasoning under Uanreroro when a single-member BIA order affirms. For lawyers preparing asylum cases, the decision is a primer on appellate vulnerability and on practical steps to shore up the record.

## What the court did and how it reasoned

The petitioners (a Colombian family) presented a fact pattern familiar in asylum litigation: repeated threats, an assault on a factory employee looking for them, an incident with their child at school, and an alleged nexus to politics because of campaign activity. The IJ and BIA found the family credible but concluded (a) the cumulative harm did not rise to past persecution, (b) any harm was motivated primarily by a private actor’s economic and criminal concerns (not the family’s political opinion), (c) the Colombian government was not shown to be unable or unwilling to protect them, and (d) internal relocation was reasonable.

The Tenth Circuit affirmed on substantial-evidence grounds. Key strands of the court’s reasoning:

- Persecution threshold: The panel reiterated that threats, psychological distress, and precautionary measures, without more, generally do not meet the circuit’s definition of persecution. The court cited its precedents—Karki, Ritonga, Brandy and others—underscoring that, even where credible, phone threats and stress-related symptoms often fall short absent more invasive physical harm or sustained, severe mistreatment.

- Nexus and mixed motives: The court emphasized that an applicant must show a protected ground was “at least one central reason” for the harm. Although mixed motives are permissible, the record here—escalation after a property dispute; statements pointing to economic or law-enforcement-related motives; absence of evidence tying earlier campaign-related threats to the same perpetrators—supported the BIA’s conclusion that the persecutor’s predominant motive was retaliation for interfering with criminal enterprise revenue, not political animus.

- Government protection and reporting: Because the applicant did not meaningfully document attempts to seek help beyond unhelpful individual officers and did not use available contacts, the court found substantial evidence supporting the BIA’s conclusion that the government was not shown to be unable or unwilling to protect them. The opinion relied on the principle that notice and opportunity to respond matter; absent reporting or evidence that policing would have been futile or dangerous, the “unable or unwilling” claim was weak.

- Deference and record consultation: Critically for appellate practitioners, the court made clear it was applying the substantial-evidence standard to the BIA’s factual determinations and, because a single-member BIA affirmed the IJ, it was appropriate under Uanreroro to consult the IJ’s more complete explanation. That consultation reinforced the BIA’s findings and limited the court’s ability to overturn them.

## Analytical perspective: deference is the engine that does the real work

Two doctrinal features dominate the outcome: the substantial-evidence standard and the mixed-motive/nexus burden. Substantial-evidence review is inherently backward-looking—asking whether a reasonable adjudicator could reach the BIA’s factual conclusions based on the record—so the appellate court’s role is narrow. Once the IJ and BIA make a factual determination that sits comfortably within precedent, reversal requires either legal error or record evidence so compelling that no reasonable factfinder could agree. Jimenez demonstrates both limitations.

The panel’s willingness to consult the IJ under Uanreroro is also significant. Single-member affirmances are common because they conserve administrative resources. But they can frustrate petitioners on appeal if the BIA’s terse order hides key factual reasoning. Uanreroro allows courts to consult the IJ’s fuller opinion, which in practice often reinforces the agency’s conclusions rather than undermining them. In Jimenez, the IJ’s detailed findings—about the timing of threats, statements by the perpetrators, the limited steps petitioners took with law enforcement, and the government’s prior efforts against the property—filled out a narrative that made reversal unlikely.

The consequence is structural: appellate courts will frequently be the site where deference does the decisive work. Appeals that rely on reweighing credibility, recharacterizing motive from the same facts, or arguing harm is “severe enough” will stumble unless the record undermines the IJ’s factual foundation or reveals legal mistakes in applying asylum standards.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Jimenez does not shift legal standards, but it will likely reinforce a set of practical inferences in future asylum appeals:

- “Threats plus fear” cases are vulnerable. Circuits often treat threats and psychological effects as insufficient alone; Jimenez continues that pattern. Practitioners should treat threat-only fact patterns as high risk unless accompanied by more acute harm, pervasive violence, or sustained coercion.

- Motive evidence matters. Mixed-motive fact patterns require careful proof that a protected ground was a central reason. Evidence that attackers acted to protect criminal revenue or retaliate for property disputes can be dispositive. Thus, showing contemporaneous statements tying harassment explicitly to political activity—or demonstrating the perpetrators have a history of targeting political actors—will be essential.

- Government-protection claims hinge on reporting and systemic evidence. Casual conversations with an indifferent officer won’t suffice; the record must show meaningful attempts to seek protection, evidence that reporting would be ineffective or dangerous, or proof of systemic police corruption or collusion. Country conditions reports and testimony that the applicant attempted to use official channels (and those channels failed) become vital.

- Single-member BIA decisions are not appellate “black boxes.” Uanreroro permits appellate courts to consult the IJ’s opinions, which in many cases will be the more detailed source of the factual findings that courts ultimately uphold.

Taken together, these doctrinal pressures nudge asylum litigation toward a robust contemporaneous record: police reports, medical and psychological documentation, documentary or testimonial evidence of motive, and corroboration of government response—or lack of it.

## Practical tips for practitioners seeking to avoid the “substantial-evidence” dead end

Jimenez offers several concrete lessons about what to develop pre-hearing and in the hearing record.

1. Contemporaneous reporting and documentation. File police reports, request written incident reports, and obtain copies. If the client reasonably believed reporting would be futile or dangerous, document attempts, communications, or credible reasons (e.g., prior known collusion or prior complaints proving ineffective). Notes of conversations with police officers should include names, dates, and the substance of the officers’ advice.

2. Corroborate psychological harm and physical risk. Medical and mental-health records, evaluations from qualified clinicians, and school records showing a child’s panic attacks or changed attendance can make emotional harms more tangible. For physical threats, photographs, contemporaneous written notes, witness statements, and employer testimony (e.g., a coworker who saw the assault at the factory) strengthen the cumulative-harm argument.

3. Build motive evidence. When nexus is contested, contemporaneous statements by the persecutors are gold. Seek third-party witnesses who heard statements like “death to snitches” or “this comes from Don Rafa,” collect any written threats (brochures, text messages), and obtain evidence about the perpetrator’s affiliations, patterns of targeting political actors, or prior acts against party members. Country reports, local press, and nonprofit documentation showing that a particular criminal group targets political activists can shift the mix toward political motive.

4. Demonstrate futile or dangerous reporting where applicable. If reporting would endanger the applicant, obtain testimony or documentation that shows the police are corrupt, or that victims who reported were retaliated against. Documentation from NGOs or media about police collusion can help, but the applicant should also show attempts to seek help or a reasoned fear about doing so.

5. Preserve the IJ record and seek fuller findings. When the BIA issues a single-member affirmance, the IJ opinion can be dispositive on appeal. Push for detailed findings at the IJ level—on nexus, on chronology, on why the government would or would not have been able to help. Cross-examine government witnesses, and ask the IJ to make explicit credibility and factual conclusions in the record.

6. Use corroboration to address relocation. If government evidence shows alternatives in-country exist, challenge their reasonableness with economic, familial, or safety-based proof—employment prospects, family threats in other locales, or logistical barriers to relocation.

7. Anticipate mixed-motive arguments and address them head-on. If there are non-protected motives, explain why the protected motive was central: show timing (e.g., harassment tied to campaigning), the content of threats referencing political activity, or that the persecutor’s animus toward the protected characteristic follows an identifiable pattern.

## Conclusion

Jimenez is a reminder that appellate victory in asylum cases often depends less on rhetorical flourish than on the record’s texture. Substantial-evidence review will routinely sustain the BIA when the IJ’s findings sit comfortably within precedent and when the record lacks contemporaneous reporting, corroboration, or direct motive evidence. Uanreroro’s permission to consult the IJ’s fuller explanation reinforces the lesson: make the IJ record as robust and explicit as possible. For advocates, the takeaway is practical and blunt—anticipate appellate deference at the outset, and build a record that leaves little room for “a reasonable adjudicator” to disagree.



# Private Violence, Public Alignments: Proving State Complicity in Colombia

## Why this case matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi (Oct. 7, 2025) is a useful primer on how U.S. courts evaluate claims that criminal actors who commit private violence are effectively arms of the state. The case turns on three tightly related questions that arise again and again in asylum and withholding adjudications from countries with strong criminal nonstate actors and fraught state institutions: Was the applicant persecuted; if so, was the persecution on account of a protected ground; and was the state unable or unwilling to protect the applicant? The court denied relief on all three prongs, but the opinion’s treatment of the “unable or unwilling” inquiry is the most consequential for litigants and adjudicators confronting claims that private persecutors are aligned or colluding with government actors.

Why pay attention? Because many asylum seekers from Colombia and similar countries do not allege that a soldier or policeman dragged them out of their house. Rather, they allege that organized criminal groups—paramilitaries, guerrillas, or narco-traffickers—targeted them, and that these groups have connections to, or de facto protection from, the state. How courts assess whether the state’s connection to those groups is sufficient to defeat a claim of government protection is determinative. Jimenez illustrates both the limits of what a single, factbound record can show and the kinds of country-conditions and case-specific proof that will be required to demonstrate state complicity or acquiescence.

## What the court did: a close reading of the record

The family in Jimenez presented a mixed record. They were Liberal Party canvassers and supported a rival presidential candidate, and the husband had worked as a provisional lawyer and intern at the prosecutor’s office. The harassment escalated after he confronted the tenant of a disputed property who turned out to be a local narco-trafficking leader known as “Don Rafa.” Threatening brochures, in-person threats at the factory, an incident at the son’s school, and a colleague’s earlier kidnapping and murder prompted the family to flee to the United States.

The BIA and IJ concluded, and the Tenth Circuit affirmed, that three independent defects doomed the claim. First, the mistreatment—threats and psychological harms without any lasting physical injury—did not rise to persecution as the court has construed that threshold in previous cases. Second, the primary motive for the threats was not political opinion but retaliation for Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s economic interests and property, so the political-opinion nexus was at best tangential. Third, and most relevant here, the record showed affirmative law-enforcement activity against the property in question (several searches and seizures) and did not show systemic government inaction or collusion. The court relied on petitioner testimony that a couple of individual officers were dismissive, but it held that anecdotal unhelpfulness was not enough to prove the Colombian government was “unable or unwilling” to protect the family, especially when the government had in fact sought to police the property. In short, the evidence favored a conclusion that state actors had notice and at least some responsiveness, undermining a claim that filing a police report would have been futile.

The practical effect: Jimenez’s factual sequence—escalating threats that track closely to a discrete property dispute with a local criminal leader, along with evidence that police had acted against that property—was insufficient to show state complicity or effective absence of protection.

## What the record shows — and what it does not

The Tenth Circuit’s dispositive factual finding was that government response to the underlying criminal enterprise existed in the record: the premises were allegedly subject to searches and closures by health and police authorities. That factual finding is important; it directly undercuts a theory of state acquiescence. The court also emphasized the lack of evidence that petitioners sought help beyond a few neighborhood officers or that they reported the prior politically motivated threats. From the court’s perspective, without a showing of systemic police apathy or political collusion—rather than isolated instances of unhelpful officers—the legal standard for “unable or unwilling” was unmet.

But it is equally important to note what the record lacked. There was no documentary evidence that police or prosecution units systematically protected Don Rafa, no proof of investigations that were opened and then intentionally shut down, no forensic linkage between named government officials and the persecutors, and no expert or country-conditions evidence showing a pattern of collusion in that locality. Absence of such evidence is a predictable Achilles’ heel in many asylum cases—courts are reluctant to infer systemic corruption from isolated anecdotes.

The lesson: when government actors have taken some, even imperfect, enforcement steps against the perpetrators or premises tied to the attackers, petitioners need stronger, more systemic proof to show that those actions were superficial, pretextual, or insufficient because of collusion higher up the chain.

## How to prove state complicity or acquiescence — what the record needs

Jimenez is a reminder that proving the state is “unable or unwilling” to protect someone—particularly where the persecutors are private criminal actors—requires more than a few hostile officers and a claimant’s subjective fear. Successful claims typically combine case-specific evidence with country-conditions proof. Key categories of proof that courts and adjudicators often find persuasive include:

- Contemporaneous reports and official records. Police reports, complaints filed with prosecutors, documentation of investigations that were opened and later closed without reasonable explanation, and administrative records showing inaction after notice are powerful. They show notice, opportunity to act, and actual government choices.

- Evidence of non-enforcement patterns. Reliable country-conditions materials (U.S. State Department reports, credible NGO reports, local press investigations) that show chronic non-enforcement, impunity rates, or patterns of law-enforcement refusal to pursue organized-crime actors in the relevant region help transform an anecdote into a pattern.

- Evidence linking perpetrators and officials. Documents, testimony, or investigative reporting showing ties—financial, familial, or political—between named persecutors and identifiable state actors can demonstrate that enforcement was not just weak but compromised.

- Evidence of targeted impunity. Proof that other victims who reported similar crimes were ignored, threatened, or worse, or that complaints were systematically dismissed, supports a finding that the government will not protect the applicant.

- Expert affidavits. Well-sourced expert declarations that situate the particular incidents in the broader context of local collusion or political alliances can bridge gaps between a claimant’s individualized story and country-wide patterns.

- Corroboration of direct threats and incidents. Photos, contemporaneous witness statements, medical or school records (e.g., therapy notes for a child), or statements from coworkers who can attest to threats or to perpetrators’ identifications strengthen credibility and help establish the sequence of events that produced the claim.

Without such layered proof, courts will often credit the fact that some state action occurred—a search, a closure, an arrest—or that the applicant did not take available steps to seek broader protection. That is precisely what happened in Jimenez.

## How asylum’s “unable or unwilling” standard differs from CAT’s “acquiescence” standard

The Jimenez opinion did not reach the Convention Against Torture (CAT) claim on the merits, but the case highlights an important doctrinal point courts and advocates must keep in mind: the “unable or unwilling” test used in asylum and withholding cases and the “government acquiescence” standard under CAT are related but distinct.

For asylum/withholding, petitioners must show persecution on account of a protected ground and that the government is “unable or unwilling” to control the persecutor. Courts assess governmental response and look for notice, opportunity, and demonstrable failure to protect. Evidence of some state action—especially action against the same criminal enterprise—can be fatally persuasive for adjudicators unless petitioners show that enforcement was a sham or that the state tacitly supported the persecutors.

CAT’s standard is different in emphasis. CAT protection turns on whether torture would be inflicted by or with the consent or acquiescence of public officials. Courts have sometimes required evidence of the government’s intent to permit or tolerate torture, or at least that officials were aware and willfully blind to likely torture. While there is overlap—both standards probe governmental engagement—CAT often requires stronger proof of deliberate tolerance or complicity in the particular acts of severe harm. In practice this means that even where an asylum claim fails because the government’s overall response appears at least partially effective, a CAT claim might still succeed if there is convincing evidence that the particular persecutors operate with actual acquiescence from identifiable officials or that the state would be willfully blind to the torture the applicant would face upon return.

The practical implication is that advocates should not abandon CAT claims simply because they cannot establish asylum’s political-nexus prong. But they must be prepared to offer evidence that gets closer to proving state knowledge and consent to the specific torturous conduct.

## Downstream effects and practical takeaways

Jimenez will likely be cited by IJ’s, the BIA, and courts in future cases from Colombia and elsewhere for two propositions. First, where private violence is temporally tied to a discrete, non-political dispute (here, a property confrontation and threats over “revenue and security”), courts will scrutinize nexus and often find political opinion secondary. Second, where the record contains evidence of some government action against the perpetrators or locations in question, adjudicators will demand stronger proof of systemic collusion or targeted impunity to find state “inability or unwillingness.”

For practitioners that means early and aggressive evidence-gathering. Obtain police reports, complaint numbers, filings with prosecutors, and any local administrative records. Assemble reliable country-conditions reports focused on the specific region and the named groups. Secure declarations from investigators, journalists, or NGOs who have direct knowledge of regional patterns of collusion. Corroborate threats and incidents with witnesses, contemporaneous messages, or other documentary material. And do not rely solely on a client’s reported belief that the persecutors are “aligned” with the government—translate that belief into objective, verifiable evidence.

For adjudicators and courts, Jimenez is a cautionary reminder to distinguish isolated municipal failures from systemic state complicity while remaining sensitive to how local power-brokers can render state protections effectively unavailable. The record-based approach the Tenth Circuit applied is defensible, but it should not become a pretext for dismissing hard-to-prove allegations of collusion where credible, corroborated evidence of officials’ ties to perpetrators exists.

## Conclusion

Jimenez offers a factbound lesson in how difficult it can be to win asylum or withholding claims premised on private violence without robust documentary and country-conditions support. The presence of some official enforcement activity against the perpetrators’ property—combined with sparse evidence of systemic non-enforcement or collusion—was fatal to the claim. For future claimants from Colombia and similar contexts, the path to demonstrating state “unable or unwilling” status requires assembling both granular, case-specific proof and broader evidence of patterns of impunity or collusion. Absent that dual showing, courts will be reluctant to infer that private violent actors operate with effective governmental protection.



# From Campaigning to ‘Snitching’: Calibrating Political-Opinion Claims in Narco/Gang Contexts

Why this case matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi (Oct. 7, 2025) is a useful, if familiar, exemplar of how U.S. courts navigate asylum claims that sit at the intersection of political activity, criminal actors, and allegations of cooperation with state institutions. At bottom, the court rejected an asylum and withholding-of-removal claim by a Colombian family who had campaigned for a presidential candidate and later were threatened and harassed after the husband, a provisional lawyer and prosecutor’s intern, confronted a property controlled by a locally powerful narco-trafficking figure. The court found (1) the harm did not rise to past persecution; (2) even taken together, the evidence showed the persecutors were motivated primarily by retaliation against the husband’s interference with their revenue and security—not by the family’s partisan political opinion; (3) there was insufficient evidence that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect the family; and (4) internal relocation was feasible.

The opinion is noteworthy because it highlights two recurring fault lines in asylum practice: when does partisan political activity (or anti-corruption work and “snitching”) translate into a protected political opinion, and how courts distinguish politically motivated persecution from criminally motivated retaliation. The court’s analysis underscores the evidentiary and doctrinal hurdles claimants face in narco/gang contexts, and it gives practitioners a roadmap—both warning signs and strategies—for how to sharpen nexus proofs going forward.

How the court reasoned

The Tenth Circuit applied settled asylum law. To prevail on asylum based on past persecution, an applicant must show (1) persecution (more than episodic threats or non-persistent harassment), (2) that the harm was “on account of” a protected ground (here, political opinion), and (3) that the government was unable or unwilling to control the persecutors. A showing of past persecution generally gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of future persecution; absent that, a claimant must show a well-founded fear of future persecution.

On the first element, the court accepted that the family endured significant threats, a brochure reading “death to snitches from Liberal Party,” intimidating armed confrontations, the assault of a factory employee, and an incident involving the husband’s young son being grabbed at school. But the court emphasized precedent limiting persecution findings based solely on threats and psychological distress, noting petitioners conceded no lasting physical harm. In the court’s view, the cumulative record did not compel a finding of past persecution under Tenth Circuit standards.

On nexus—the centerpiece of the opinion—the court concluded the persecutors’ predominant motive was economic/personal retaliation tied to the husband’s intervention at Don Rafa’s property, not the family’s partisan campaigning. Even though petitioners had been active members of the Liberal Party and had campaigned for a presidential candidate, the escalation coincided with the husband’s property dispute and the men’s explicit references to him as a “snitch” and a prosecutor’s collaborator. The court stressed that a protected ground must be “at least one central reason” (not merely incidental or subordinate) for the harm. The court treated the snitching allegations as instrumental and directly tied to Don Rafa’s asserted loss of revenue and security; that, in the court’s view, made political opinion a tangential rather than central motivation.

On government protection, the court relied on the fact that law enforcement had, at least in part, attempted to address criminal activity at the property, and that the petitioners had limited efforts to seek protection beyond two unhelpful officers. The court pointed to the absence of systemic evidence showing government complicity or a pattern of failure. Finally, on internal relocation, the court saw corroborative facts: family members remained safely in Colombia, the petitioners had temporarily stayed with kin in the countryside, and the threats were neighborhood-specific (“move out of the neighborhood”), not countrywide.

Analytical perspective: doctrines, distinctions, and evidentiary fault lines

This opinion hews closely to established Tenth Circuit precedent and illustrates how courts operationalize two doctrinal distinctions that often decide cases: (1) political opinion versus non-protected motives in mixed-motive contexts, and (2) “snitching” framed as a political stance (e.g., anti-corruption or pro-rule-of-law expression) versus “snitching” as a functional accusation implicating private disputes or criminal enforcement (i.e., economic or personal motives).

First, nexus demands “one central reason,” which puts a heavy thumb on the scale in mixed-motive settings. Practically, courts ask whether the persecutor’s grievance stems from the target’s ideas, status, or messages, or instead from the target’s interference with the persecutor’s economic or criminal interests. Here, the temporal sequence mattered: threats existed early in the campaign, but the escalated, violent incidents followed the husband’s involvement at the property. The persecutors’ contemporaneous statements—calling him a “snitch” and blaming him for searches—proved decisive. That kind of explicit contemporaneous attribution is powerful because it links motive to conduct, but crucially not to partisan views. For courts, “snitch” commonly signifies cooperation with state enforcement rather than a political ideology, so claimants must bridge that gap.

Second, the court’s analysis signals skepticism about reading anti-corruption or pro-law-enforcement activity as political opinion absent evidence that the persecutor understood and targeted the claimant because of that opinion. In many contexts—especially where criminal actors view enforcement as a commercial threat—conduct that looks political to the applicant (or to outside observers) will be perceived by the persecutor as a threat to revenue. This dynamic is particularly acute in places where criminal groups wield local governance power and have ambiguous relationships with official actors.

Third, the opinion reaffirms the evidentiary premium courts place on applicant reporting to authorities, systemic evidence of state collusion, and efforts to seek protection beyond the immediate neighborhood. The Tenth Circuit treated the petitioners’ limited engagement with state institutions and the existing evidence of law-enforcement attempts to police the property as undermining the “unable or unwilling” claim. The court’s reliance on such higher-level indicators—government action at the property, family still resident in Colombia, temporary safe relocation to the countryside—reflects a pragmatic assessment of safety and avoidability.

Practical litigation strategies for claimants in similar narco/gang contexts

Counsel preparing claims that involve narco/gang actors should incorporate strategies that address these doctrinal fault lines directly. The Jimenez opinion highlights several avenues to strengthen a political-opinion nexus and to rebut a court’s inclination to view “snitching” as purely instrumental retaliation:

1. Establish contemporaneous evidence that persecutors tied the harassment to political expression or partisan activity, not merely to interference with criminal revenue. The strongest nexus proof is a persecutor’s statement linking the target’s political message or party affiliation to the attack. So: collect, document, and emphasize contemporaneous threats, flyers, social-media posts, or intercepted communications that connect the violence to political views (e.g., “you support X party” or “you campaigned for Y”).

2. Show that the claimant’s political activities were public, message-driven, and visible to the persecutor. Campaigning that included public rallies, leafleting, public statements, or other public-facing acts is easier to frame as political opinion than private door-to-door canvassing—particularly if the persecutor had knowledge of those activities.

3. Contextualize anti-corruption or law-enforcement cooperation as expressive political conduct when possible. If a claimant intervened publicly to denounce corruption or to advocate for law-and-order reforms—rather than merely conducting a private property dispute—highlight those public elements. Demonstrate that the claimant’s actions conveyed an opinion about governance, rule of law, or public policy rather than being limited to a private contractual or property interest.

4. Capture and emphasize any evidence that the persecutor’s “snitch” label was deployed to suppress political expression. In many settings, criminal actors use the “snitch” epithet to discredit or intimidate civic actors; when evidence shows the label was used to chill political speech (for example, against neighborhood political organizers, journalists, or anti-corruption activists), it strengthens the political-opinion narrative.

5. Use corroborating country conditions and expert testimony to show systemic targeting of political activists, or patterns where criminal groups target a political party or movement. If there is documentary evidence or credible reporting that criminal organizations have targeted a political party, or that state actors have failed to protect party activists, that can rebut an inference that the motive was purely economic.

6. Document attempts to seek protection beyond single officers and establish that doing so would have been futile or dangerous. Exhausting remedies is not always required, but courts give weight to applicants who sought help through multiple channels (local police stations, prosecutors, internal affairs, national-level bodies, or documented complaints) or who can show credible reasons why reporting would have been futile (e.g., history of collusion, threats tied to police, membership of persecutors in paramilitary or political networks).

7. Demonstrate the personal costs of relocation and contrast neighborhood-specific threats with broader persecution. Where relocation is genuinely infeasible—due to economic, familial, or targeted patterns—build a factual record (affidavits, economic assessments, child-care or medical needs) showing that internal relocation would not, in practice, eliminate risk or would impose intolerable hardship.

Where courts will likely push back

Even with careful preparation, some structural tendencies in judicial review make these claims difficult. Courts are rightly wary of treating ordinary criminal retaliation as a protected ground; asylum is not a remedy for victims of generalized criminal violence unless the violence is targeted for a protected reason. Mixed-motive situations are thorny: courts will parse sequencing and statements, and they will credit other plausible motives (revenge, economic harm). The onus is on claimants to convert private disputes or economically-inflected threats into evidence of a politically motivated campaign of persecution. That often requires contemporaneous and documentary proof that may not exist in dangerous, low-recordkeeping settings.

Conclusion

Jimenez v. Bondi is not a novelty—it mirrors many published circuit decisions grappling with the line between political targeting and criminal retaliation. But it is a useful refresher on what courts expect from claimants who say they were targeted for political opinion in contexts dominated by violent nonstate actors. The opinion underscores a simple lesson for practitioners: where “snitch” is alleged, turn the focus away from the label and toward what the persecutor understood the label to mean. Was the conduct and message political, public, and expressive? Did the persecutor act to suppress that expression? Did the state systematically fail to protect those with that expressive profile? Answering those questions through contemporaneous statements, broader patterns of targeting, and evidence of futile or dangerous attempts to seek protection will be essential to prevail in future cases that sit at the fraught intersection of partisan politics, corruption, and organized criminality.
