Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Jimenez v. Bondi

Chapter 12: # Withholding of Removal and CAT: Relationship to Asylum

This chapter explains how withholding of removal and protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT) relate to—and differ from—asylum. Students must understand the doctrinal, evidentiary, and practical distinctions among these forms of protection because the same factual record can yield different outcomes under different standards. The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi, discussed below, provides a useful vehicle to illustrate these differences and to show how, in practice, asylum denial often—but not always—forecloses other forms of relief.

At the outset, recall three basic structural points. First, asylum is a discretionary form of protection rooted in the refugee definition in the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA): an applicant must prove she is a “refugee,” i.e., unable or unwilling to return to her country because of persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. By contrast, withholding of removal (statutory withholding) and CAT relief are mandatory forms of non‑refoulement relief if their statutory or regulatory standards are met. Second, the burdens of proof differ among the three forms of relief. Third, the substantive elements that an applicant must prove are not identical: withholding requires a higher likelihood of future harm and still depends on a protected-ground nexus; CAT requires proof of torture and state involvement or acquiescence but does not require a protected-ground nexus.

The Jimenez opinion exemplifies these themes. The petitioners appealed denial of asylum and withholding; they did not appeal the denial of CAT relief. The Tenth Circuit upheld the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA), first concluding that petitioners failed to show past persecution or a well‑founded fear of future persecution, and then stating that because petitioners failed to satisfy asylum’s standard for a well‑founded fear, they necessarily could not meet withholding’s higher “clear probability” standard. That reasoning reflects a common, but not universal, relationship among the remedies: asylum denial often predicts withholding denial, but withholding and CAT may still succeed in some circumstances even when asylum fails. The sections that follow explain the standards and the circumstances in which one form of relief may succeed where another fails.

## Withholding’s higher probability standard and non‑derivative nature

Doctrinal baseline. Asylum and withholding address related harms, but they are governed by different statutory provisions and different standards of proof. Asylum is discretionary and premised on the INA’s refugee definition; withholding of removal is mandatory relief under 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3) (formerly § 243(h)) for any individual who shows “a clear probability” (or “more likely than not”) that his life or freedom would be threatened on account of a protected ground if returned. The Supreme Court in INS v. Cardoza‑Fonseca made the critical point that the “well‑founded fear” standard relevant to asylum is meaningfully less demanding than the “more likely than not” standard that applies for withholding (and that this difference has consequences for proof and outcomes). Practically, then, meeting the asylum standard does not automatically satisfy withholding’s more demanding likelihood threshold.

What “higher standard” means in practice. The “well‑founded fear” standard for asylum requires a reasonable possibility of persecution. Courts have sometimes quantified this as requiring something less than a 50% chance—an objectively reasonable possibility—while withholding requires the applicant to demonstrate that it is more likely than not (i.e., greater than 50%) that she will be persecuted. Because the withholding standard requires a higher probability, an applicant who persuades an adjudicator that persecution is reasonably possible may obtain asylum (if discretion favors her) but still fail to meet withholding’s mandatory standard. Conversely, in limited circumstances where an adjudicator denies asylum for discretionary reasons despite finding persecution, withholding may nevertheless be granted if the higher likelihood requirement is established.

Non‑discretionary character and consequences. The mandatory nature of withholding is an important difference. If an applicant proves the withholding standard, the government is statutorily required to withhold removal to the persecuting country; the relief is not subject to the same form of favorable discretion that governs asylum. But the protections that flow from withholding are narrower. Withholding does not confer permanent lawful status or the same discretionary benefits (such as eventual adjustment to lawful permanent resident status) that asylum can open the door to; instead, it simply bars removal to the designated country while the noncitizen remains in the United States. It is therefore critical to understand both the legal standard and the real‑world consequences of a grant of withholding versus asylum.

How the Jimenez decision applies the principle. The Tenth Circuit described this relationship succinctly: because the petitioners failed to establish a well‑founded fear of future persecution under asylum standards, they necessarily could not meet the higher standard required for withholding. That is a straightforward application of the higher likelihood requirement. But the opinion is careful to treat the two inquiries as analytically distinct—failure under asylum does not mechanically foreclose withholding in every conceivable factual posture; rather, the higher standard is simply harder to meet on the same facts.

Practical tips for practitioners and analysts. When preparing a withholding claim, counsel should recognize that corroboration, documentary evidence, and clear testimony are especially important because of the higher probability requirement. If the record shows past persecution, that fact can create a rebuttable presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution and materially help on the withholding inquiry, but counsel must be prepared to show why future persecution is more likely than not. Where asylum rests on a thin record of threats or harassment, it will be difficult to meet withholding’s threshold unless more persuasive evidence appears.

## CAT’s separate elements: torture, state action or acquiescence, and non‑refoulement

CAT’s legal structure. Protection under CAT operates under a different legal regime. The United States implements the Convention Against Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment through federal regulation (8 C.F.R. § 1208.16(c)) and by statute that prohibits removal to any country where “it is more likely than not that the alien would be tortured.” CAT relief is independent of the refugee definition; it does not require a nexus to a protected ground. Instead, it requires proof of (1) torture as defined by the Convention—severe physical or mental pain intentionally inflicted for certain purposes; and (2) that the torture would be inflicted by, or at the instigation of, or with the acquiescence of, a public official or other person acting in an official capacity. If these elements are met to the requisite likelihood standard, non‑refoulement under CAT prohibits removal to the country where torture is likely.

Defining “torture.” The Convention defines “torture” as intentional infliction of severe physical or mental pain or suffering for purposes such as obtaining information, punishment, intimidation, coercion, or discrimination, and excludes pain arising only from lawful sanctions or inherent in lawful penalties. In practice, applicants must show both the quality of the harm (severe) and the intent and purpose behind it. Not all persecution or abuse will qualify as torture; the mistreatment must reach the extreme level contemplated by the Convention.

State action or acquiescence. A central and often difficult element of a CAT claim is establishing state involvement. CAT does not require that the government itself physically commit the torture; it is sufficient to show that state actors would be the torturers or that public officials would acquiesce—i.e., know of the torture and consent or remain willfully blind. Courts assess whether the government is the author of the harm, whether it has failed to control private actors, or whether it otherwise would be complicit. The Tenth Circuit in Jimenez discussed similar issues in the asylum context—government inability or unwillingness to control persecutors is relevant there—but CAT’s state‑action analysis is narrower and focused on the specific question of whether government acquiescence would facilitate torture.

Standard of proof. CAT uses the “more likely than not” standard (substantively similar to withholding’s higher likelihood standard). The applicant must show it is more likely than not that she would be tortured if returned to the proposed country of removal. Because CAT does not require a nexus to a protected ground, it can protect individuals whose prospective mistreatment stems from non‑political motives so long as the torture element and government involvement or acquiescence can be proved.

How CAT differs functionally from asylum and withholding. There are several important practical differences. First, CAT’s lack of a protected‑ground requirement means it is available to applicants who suffer grave abuse for reasons outside the five protected grounds. Second, the kind of harm required is more severe than what many asylum claims allege—CAT demands evidence of torture-level treatment. Third, CAT relief, like withholding, does not confer lawful permanent residence; it is an enforceable protection against removal to a specific country. Finally, the evidence and strategies to prove CAT differ: demonstrating police or military involvement, systemic impunity, or documented patterns of torture in the country are often crucial.

Jimenez and CAT. In Jimenez, the petitioners did not press their CAT claim on appeal, and the panel explicitly declined to reach it. The court’s opinion therefore does not speak to the CAT merits. That procedural posture illustrates a practical lesson: CAT claims, although different in substance from asylum and withholding, must be preserved and argued to obtain appellate review; failure to appeal CAT denial waives a potential protection that might succeed even if asylum and withholding fail.

## When withholding or CAT succeed despite asylum denial (e.g., discretion or nexus failures)

It is tempting to conclude that denial of asylum almost always predicts denial of withholding and CAT, but that inference is overbroad. There are important scenarios in which withholding or CAT may be granted even when asylum is denied.

1) Asylum denied on discretionary grounds. Because asylum is discretionary, an applicant might satisfy asylum’s substantive elements (including a well‑founded fear or past persecution) yet be denied because the immigration judge declines to exercise discretion in her favor (for example, because of adverse discretionary factors like a criminal conviction, delay in filing, or other negative equities). Withholding, by contrast, is mandatory: if the applicant proves the statutory standard (clear probability) for withholding, the government must withhold removal even if the IJ would have denied asylum in the exercise of discretion. Thus, if the record supports the higher likelihood standard, the applicant can prevail on withholding even when asylum is denied on discretionary grounds.

2) New or different evidence on appeal or after the IJ. Sometimes, an applicant’s asylum claim fails because of inadequate evidence or an unpersuasive nexus showing, yet, through supplementation of the record or further documentary proof, the applicant later can meet withholding’s likelihood requirement. If the IJ or BIA denies asylum for lack of corroboration or insufficient evidence, timely supplemental evidence can support withholding on remand or at the appellate stage if properly presented.

3) Asylum denial for lack of nexus; CAT success because no nexus is required. The asylum analysis requires nexus to a protected ground; withholding requires the same nexus. But CAT does not require that the harm be on account of a protected ground. Consequently, if asylum fails because the harm stems from criminal targeting, vendetta, economic motives, or a private dispute (i.e., no protected ground), withholding will usually fail for the same reason; however, CAT may still succeed if the applicant can show torture and government acquiescence or participation. A classic hypothetical: an applicant is targeted for kidnapping and torture by security forces or militia because of a private dispute unrelated to political opinion—no asylum or withholding—but CAT may apply if the government is complicit in or will not prevent the torture.

4) Government protection or relocation findings. Denial of asylum sometimes rests on a finding that the applicant could safely relocate within her country or that the government would protect her if notified. Withholding and CAT inquiries consider government protection and internal relocation, but the importance and standards differ. Because withholding requires a higher probability of harm, evidence that internal relocation is practical or that the government would protect the applicant may defeat withholding even more readily than asylum. Conversely, an asylum denial that turns solely on adverse discretion leaves open the possibility of a mandatory withholding grant if the higher likelihood is proved.

How to read Jimenez in this context. Jimenez demonstrates the common trajectory where asylum failure leads to withholding failure because the facts did not show past persecution, nexus, or an inability or unwillingness of the state to protect. The court’s analysis emphasized the insufficiency of the threats, the predominance of non‑political motives in the persecutors’ acts, and the lack of systemic evidence that Colombian authorities were complicit. Those same factual weaknesses killed both asylum and withholding claims. But the opinion also implicitly acknowledges that had the IJ found past persecution and that the persecutors acted with state acquiescence—facts sufficient to show torture or a clear probability— the result on withholding or CAT could have diverged.

## Strategic sequencing and fallback theories in removal defense

Given the doctrinal differences described above, practitioners must plan claims and present evidence strategically. A typical defensive posture coordinates asylum, withholding, and CAT claims and then tailors proof to each remedy’s elements.

1) Plead and preserve all possible claims. Counsel should file applications and pleadings that preserve asylum, withholding, and CAT claims at the earliest stage. Preserving arguments is essential for appeal; failing to raise CAT at the IJ or to appeal its denial may waive a potentially viable non‑refoulement claim. The Jimenez petitioners did not appeal the CAT denial; that foreclosed relief on that ground at the appellate level.

2) Recognize divergent elements and collect different kinds of evidence. Because asylum requires nexus to a protected ground, evidence of political activity, statements by persecutors citing that activity, or country conditions showing targeted groups are vital. For withholding, the same evidence is relevant but must be stronger in quality and quantity to meet the higher likelihood threshold. For CAT, the emphasis shifts: evidence of torture‑level abuse, state complicity or acquiescence, medical or psychological reports documenting severe harm, and country reports showing systemic torture or impunity are key.

3) Address government protection and internal relocation comprehensively. Government protection and internal relocation are common defenses the government uses to rebut asylum or withholding claims. Applicants should document efforts (or the futility of efforts) to seek police help, provide evidence that local authorities are unresponsive or complicit, and explain why relocation within the country is not reasonable (e.g., because perpetrators reach widely, lack of documentation, inability to gain work or subsistence, or family ties that cannot be severed). Counsel should be prepared to distinguish asylum relocation issues from the similar inquiry under withholding and to show why relocation is not a meaningful safety valve.

4) Use past persecution evidence strategically. Evidence of past persecution creates a rebuttable presumption of future persecution and is powerful for both asylum and withholding. If past persecution is established, the government must rebut the presumption. Past persecution is also potentially relevant to CAT if it involves torture or shows a pattern of severe abuse and state acquiescence. Because the adjudicator may view the same acts differently under the different frameworks, emphasize aspects of past harm that meet the highest standards (severe physical injury, evidence of state involvement) to preserve CAT and withholding potential even if asylum is shaky.

5) Prepare for mixed‑motive arguments and nexus disputes. Persecutors often have mixed motives (for example, criminal profit and political intimidation). Under asylum and withholding law, an applicant need only show that a protected ground was “at least one central reason” for persecution—not necessarily the only one. Gather statements, contemporaneous documentation, and corroboration that link the harm to the protected motive. The Jimenez court underscored that where the protected motive is tangential or subordinate to other motives, the nexus requirement is not met. Successful withholding or asylum claims will therefore present evidence showing the protected ground played a central role.

6) Be mindful of relief consequences. Counsel should explain to clients the practical differences among the forms of protection—discretionary relief and the potential for adjustment to lawful permanent residence in the case of asylum, the mandatory but narrower protection of withholding, and CAT’s bar to removal without pathway to permanent status. These distinctions affect long‑term planning and client expectations.

Concluding synthesis. As illustrated by Jimenez, asylum, withholding, and CAT are interrelated but distinct protections. Asylum’s lower substantive standard and discretionary nature mean it can sometimes be granted where withholding is not; yet asylum’s discretionary character can also mean withholding is available even after asylum denial if the applicant meets withholding’s higher probability threshold. CAT operates on a separate axis: it requires torture and state involvement but no protected‑ground nexus, and can therefore protect applicants who are ineligible for asylum or withholding for lack of nexus. Practitioners must therefore plead and develop each claim with its unique elements in mind, collect evidence tailored to each standard, and preserve all arguments for appellate review. The Jimenez decision underscores both the predictable relationship between asylum and withholding outcomes in many cases and the continuing importance of preserving and developing CAT claims as an independent avenue of protection.
