Chapter 11: # Comparative Perspectives and Circuit Variations

This chapter uses the Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi as its touchstone to map how federal appellate courts and the Board of Immigration Appeals (and Attorney General decisional authority) have defined and applied key asylum concepts: when threats become persecution, how to treat persecution by private actors (and when reporting to the state is required), how mixed motives are assessed after the REAL ID Act, the evolution of the “unable or unwilling” government-protection inquiry, and what these doctrinal differences mean for venue and litigation strategy. Jimenez is paradigmatic of a conservative, fact‑specific application of asylum doctrine: the court stressed that threats without physical harm and without strong proof of a protected‑ground motive do not ordinarily constitute persecution, and that an applicant’s limited efforts to seek state protection undercut claims that the government was unable or unwilling to protect them. Placing that approach alongside other circuits and BIA/AG guidance reveals both common law contours and important fault lines.

## Threats-as-persecution: stricter and more permissive approaches across circuits

A central question that arises repeatedly in asylum adjudication is when threats—standing alone or combined with other conduct—cross the line from criminal wrongdoing or harassment into persecution. The Tenth Circuit in Jimenez reiterates a familiar rule in that circuit: threats by themselves are rarely sufficient to amount to persecution. The opinion emphasizes precedent holding that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution” and that the applicant must show suffering or harm that is “offensive” and more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats. The court also focused on the absence of physical injury and the relatively commonplace nature of psychological distress (insomnia, panic attacks) to conclude the cumulative conduct did not meet the persecution threshold.

Across the circuits, the baseline analytic move—look at the cumulative severity, frequency, context, and consequences of threats—is widely shared. But circuits diverge in how readily they convert threats into persecution.

- At the stricter end (as illustrated by the Tenth), courts ask whether threats were concrete, imminent, and caused substantial physical or long‑term psychological harm. They emphasize the need to connect the threats to a pattern of serious interference with fundamental rights or to evidence of actual violence.

- More permissive courts have recognized that sustained, credible threats—especially when backed by the capacity and history of the persecutor to carry them out—can be persecution even without prior physical harm. In those courts, the assessors weigh (a) specificity and credibility of the threats, (b) the persecutor’s demonstrated capacity to act (e.g., organized criminal groups that have committed violence), and (c) credible evidence of significant psychological impairment or forced flight.

The practical takeaway is that identical facts—sustained telephone threats, a leaflet threatening “death to snitches,” a later in‑person confrontation at gunpoint, and a child’s being accosted at school—may produce different legal results depending on how a tribunal weighs the cumulative effect and the ordinary baseline for “persecution.” Jimenez shows the Tenth Circuit’s reluctance to stretch the persecution concept to cover the facts before it: the threats escalated, but lacked past physical injury and were linked, in the court’s view, primarily to a personal dispute involving a criminal actor rather than to a protected ground.

For students and practitioners: when evaluating (or pleading) a threats claim, assemble evidence on three axes—objective risk (capacity and history of the persecutor), concrete acts (dates, locations, witnesses, contemporaneous reports), and medical/psychological documentation of harm. The more each axis is populated, the better the odds of persuading circuits that are willing to recognize non‑physical—but severe—persecution.

## Private-actor persecution and the reporting requirement: how circuits assess futility and danger exceptions

The Immigration and Nationality Act requires that persecution be “by the government or forces the government is either unable or unwilling to control.” Thus many claims about private actors (gangs, criminal enterprises, private militias) turn on whether the state can realistically be expected to provide protection. Jimenez raises the reporting requirement squarely: the Tenth Court emphasized the applicant’s limited efforts to seek state protection and treated the failure to report (or to seek help beyond one or two neighborhood officers) as significant evidence that the record did not demonstrate the government was unable or unwilling to protect the family.

What courts demand of asylum seekers before accepting the contention that reporting would have been futile or dangerous varies.

- The Tenth Circuit’s approach (reflected in Jimenez and earlier cases the court invokes) places substantial weight on whether the applicant actually notified authorities and whether the authorities had notice and an opportunity to respond. If an applicant did not report, the court will normally require a compelling reason—such as credible evidence of systemic collusion between officials and the persecutors, or a clear and immediate danger from reporting—to excuse nonreporting. Single, unhelpful encounters with police do not ordinarily prove state incapacity or unwillingness unless supported by broader evidence.

- Other circuits have been more willing to accept a futility or danger exception on the basis of reasonably understood risks. Some panels have accepted that reporting would have been futile when the persecutor has known ties to state actors, when reporting would have exposed the claimant to immediate retaliation, or when there is substantial country‑condition evidence showing widespread impunity or pervasive corruption. Those decisions often rely on documentary country conditions, reports of the persecutor’s collusion with officials, or credible testimony demonstrating that police inaction is systemic rather than idiosyncratic.

- A third, middle approach treats the reporting requirement as contextual: courts will accept a failure to report when an applicant shows that reporting was impracticable, would have posed an unreasonable risk, or under the circumstances could not be expected. The showing required is nontrivial—the applicant must supply evidence that reporting would have been futile or dangerous—but courts apply the standard flexibly in light of individual circumstances.

Jimenez illustrates the Tenth Circuit’s insistence on more concrete engagement with state actors before excusing nonreporting: the applicants spoke with a couple of police officers who essentially advised them to leave, but they did not otherwise seek help from police beyond their neighborhood or use the legal contacts available to them. The court treated that as insufficient to establish government inability or unwillingness.

For lawyers: when representing clients persecuted by private actors, prioritize contemporaneous reporting where safe to do so, document all contacts with law enforcement, and collect corroborating country‑condition evidence showing systemic failure or collusion if reporting did not occur. If reporting would have been dangerous, build a record to prove that danger—witnesses, contemporaneous messages, and credible expert or NGO reports—because some circuits will not excuse nonreporting on the basis of one or two unhelpful officer statements alone.

## Mixed-motive causation after the REAL ID Act: recurring fact patterns (gang extortion, whistleblowing, political canvassing)

The REAL ID Act requires that an applicant show that a protected ground was “at least one central reason” for persecution. That statutory formulation tightened pre‑existing, somewhat malleable standards and created a concrete test for mixed‑motive cases: the persecutor can have multiple motives, but a protected ground must be a central one.

The Jimenez decision applies that standard in a common fact pattern: political canvassing by a member of a political party who is then threatened not only because of politics but also because of a separate civil dispute with a criminal actor (here, a property dispute with a narco‑group leader). The Tenth Circuit concluded the dominant motive was economic and personal—retaliation for suspected interference with the criminal enterprise—rather than political.

Three recurring patterns illustrate the significance of the “one central reason” test:

- Gang extortion and political activity. Extortion often looks like political persecution when the victim is politically active, but courts will scrutinize whether the gang’s motive is primarily economic. Evidence that the gang targeted multiple people regardless of political views, or that the gang’s statements and actions focused on money, tends to undermine a political nexus. By contrast, threats that explicitly reference political activity, come in the context of targeted, selective attacks against partisan actors, or coincide temporally with political campaigning, help sustain a political‑motive inference.

- Whistleblowing or cooperation with authorities. When a person is threatened after providing information to prosecutors, the persecutor’s animus may be tied to the individual’s role as a “snitch” rather than to a protected political opinion. The nexus is stronger if the government or the persecutor indicates the harassment was because of the applicant’s political expression or opinions; weaker if the persecutor is responding solely to the applicant’s cooperation with law enforcement and economic disruption. Courts will look for statements by persecutors, for the timing and singularity of the targeting, and for patterns of persecution limited to those engaged in the protected activity.

- Political canvassing and localized harassment. Small‑scale political activity—going door‑to‑door for a candidate—frequently precipitates threats from actors who resent any authority or who profit from quiet. The central question is whether the canvassing was a central reason for the harm. Evidence that the persecutor referenced the canvassing in threats, that only those engaged in comparable political activity were targeted, or that the persecutor had a history of politically motivated violence supports a nexus finding. Jimenez is illustrative: the court found that although Petitioners were active in the Liberal Party, the escalation of threats coincided with a property dispute that more plausibly explained the men’s conduct.

The practical implication of REAL ID Act jurisprudence is that litigants must prove that a protected ground was a causal center of the harm—not merely present or a minor pretext. Documentary evidence of persecutor statements, contemporaneous threats referencing political views, selective targeting of politically active individuals, and careful temporal sequencing will matter. Absent such proof, courts will treat competing motives (financial, revenge, criminal control) as dispositive against an asylum nexus.

## BIA and Attorney General guidance: evolution and current status of the ‘unable or unwilling’ test

The Board of Immigration Appeals (and when the Attorney General steps in, the AG) has long shaped the standards for asylum through decisions and, more recently, through high‑profile AG referrals that recalibrated doctrines governing private‑actor persecution.

- Historically, BIA decisions emphasized the need to show either state involvement in persecution or a failure of state protection that is systemic, pervasive, or demonstrated by the government’s repudiation or inability to control the persecutor. The BIA has balanced the need to prevent granting asylum for ordinary crime victims against the protection of individuals genuinely threatened by private actors that the state cannot control.

- In the last decade, Attorney General decisions have sometimes narrowed eligibility for asylum against private actors. The best known is Matter of A‑B‑ (AG decision), in which the Attorney General took a narrow view of asylum claims grounded in private‑actor domestic violence and emphasized state protection and political motive limitations. That decision sparked litigation and divergent circuit responses; many courts and commentators criticized it as overly restrictive and insensitive to the reality that victims of private violence can be politically persecuted or systemically unprotected.

- The cumulative effect is a patchwork: some BIA precedents (and AG decisions) remain influential; yet appellate courts retain authority to interpret the statute and have sometimes rejected the AG’s narrow readings. Jimenez reflects a BIA‑friendly, deferential posture to the immigration court’s factual findings and to the requirement that applicants either seek government help or show why seeking it would have been futile or dangerous.

At present, the “unable or unwilling” inquiry is a mixed question of law and fact. The legal framework is stable: demonstrate government collusion, incapacity, indifference, or futility. But outcomes turn on the quality of evidence and jurisdictional predispositions. The BIA and AG have encouraged careful documentation and conservative factfinding. Appellate courts vary in their willingness to find systemic governmental failure on a thin record.

For practitioners and students: do not rely solely on an applicant’s testimony that they feared the police; compile contemporaneous reports, formal complaints, medical records, and independent country‑condition sources (NGO reports, U.S. State Department reports, media accounts) that show patterns of impunity, police collusion, or state incapacity in the relevant region. If the persecutor has links to the state, prioritize proof of those links—financial, social, or institutional—to rebut the presumption that the state can and will protect.

## Forum selection consequences for asylum lawyering

The doctrinal variations across courts and the BIA’s evolving posture have direct implications for where and how asylum cases are litigated. “Forum shopping” in immigration cases—which circuit hears an appeal—can be determinative. For example, petitioners may face more favorable law in circuits that have recognized broader circumstances under which threats, private‑actor persecution, or futility exceptions suffice; conversely, circuits that require robust evidence of physical harm, state collusion, or actual reporting will be harder venues.

Practical consequences for lawyers:

- Pre‑filing assessment. Evaluate the client’s strongest claims against the doctrinal leanings of the local circuit. If the client’s case rests principally on threats and psychological harm, consider filing in jurisdictions with precedent more favorable to non‑physical persecution claims. If the main problem is private‑actor persecution but reporting did not occur, favor circuits with broader futility doctrine and higher receptivity to country‑condition evidence of systemic corruption.

- Evidence design. Anticipate the element most likely to lose (nexus, persecution, or state protection) and structure discovery and corroboration to target that issue. For nexus, collect persecutor statements and proof of selective targeting. For persecution, compile medical or psychological evaluations, employment or school disruptions, and witness statements. For state protection, document police reports, contact records with officials, and authoritative country‑condition reports.

- Preservation and framing. Courts defer to the IJ and the BIA on factual findings; however, appellate courts vary in reviewing legal errors. Preserve arguments about burden, evidentiary weight, and legal standards at the administrative level. Frame issues in a way that courts in your circuit have found persuasive: in the Tenth, emphasize objective evidence and reporting; in other circuits, emphasize systemic failures and the applicant’s reasonable fear.

- Appellate strategy. If the initial result is unfavorable, assess whether the circuit’s law provides ripeness for a favorable reversal. Consider whether to urge an en banc rehearing or a cert petition where circuits have split. When a case implicates a circuit split, it can be an asset in seeking rehearing or certiorari, but those remedies are difficult and exceptional.

A brief practical checklist for counsel informed by Jimenez and comparative doctrine:
- Immediately document threats with as much contemporaneous evidence as possible (photographs, recordings, witness statements).
- File police reports if doing so is safe; if not, document why—text messages, witness testimony, NGO reports, medical records.
- Evidence the persecutor’s motive: direct threats referencing political views, selective targeting, membership lists, or public statements by the persecutor.
- Collect corroborative country‑condition documentation about impunity, corruption, or collusion.
- Tailor litigation strategy to local circuit precedent on threats, futility of reporting, and mixed motives.

Conclusion

Jimenez v. Bondi is a useful exemplar of a conservative, fact‑intensive application of asylum doctrine: it illustrates the Tenth Circuit’s reluctance to treat threats and non‑physical harms as persecution, its insistence on nexus to a protected ground, and its skepticism toward claims of government inability absent stronger evidence or reporting. When compared with other circuits and the BIA/AG landscape, the decision highlights the doctrinal levers that determine asylum outcomes: the required quantum of harm for persecution, the evidentiary threshold for excusing reporting, and the precise proof needed to satisfy the REAL ID Act’s “at least one central reason” test. For students and practitioners alike, the lesson is that asylum law sits at the intersection of fact‑finding and jurisdictional doctrine—sound lawyering for asylum cases requires both meticulous fact development and strategic forum‑aware legal framing.
