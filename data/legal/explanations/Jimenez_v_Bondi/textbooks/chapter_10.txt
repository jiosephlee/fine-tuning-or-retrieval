Chapter 10: # Evidence, Credibility, and Corroboration: Building a Winning Record

A successful asylum or withholding claim turns on two linked inquiries: whether the applicant suffered (or reasonably fears) persecution, and whether that persecution is connected to a protected ground. Those legal questions are litigated through testimonial narrative and documentary proof. Courts give substantial deference to the Board of Immigration Appeals’ factual findings, so practitioners must assemble a record that not only persuades an adjudicator at the IJ level, but also survives the "substantial evidence" review at the BIA and the courts of appeals. The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi, decided on October 7, 2025, illustrates how gaps in reporting, corroboration, nexus proof, and evidence of failed internal relocation undermine claims even when petitioners are found credible. This chapter converts doctrine into practice: what evidence moves the needle on each legal element, how to develop and present it, and how to anticipate adverse findings.

Effective advocacy begins with understanding the legal standards the adjudicator will apply. As the Tenth Circuit reiterated, asylum applicants must prove refugee status by a preponderance of the evidence—either by establishing past persecution (which raises a rebuttable presumption of future persecution) or by showing a well‑founded fear of future persecution on account of a protected ground such as political opinion. The inquiry has three core components: (1) whether the harm rises to the level of persecution; (2) whether the harm is on account of one of the statutorily protected grounds; and (3) whether the persecutor is an agent of the state or the state is unable or unwilling to control the persecutor. Each element has evidentiary consequences that inform what documents, evaluations, affidavits, and expert testimony will be needed.

## Corroboration expectations post-REAL ID: police reports, medical and psychological evaluations, affidavits

The REAL ID Act reshaped credibility and corroboration analysis by emphasizing that an applicant’s testimony may be sufficient alone unless it is inconsistent, implausible, or contradicted, and by authorizing adjudicators to require corroboration where the applicant’s testimony is lacking. But that does not mean corroboration is optional; rather, adjudicators now make an individualized assessment whether corroborating evidence is reasonably available and whether the applicant’s testimony, standing alone, meets the statutory standard. In practice, adjudicators are more likely to credit testimony supported by contemporaneous and independent documents.

Police reports and complaint receipts are among the most persuasive forms of corroboration when available. They do three critical things: (1) show the applicant gave the government notice (a relevant fact under the "unable or unwilling" analysis), (2) document the timing and content of threats or attacks contemporaneously, and (3) supply official identifiers—dates, complaint numbers, and officer names—that can be used to follow up. In Jimenez, the petitioners’ weaknesses included limited reporting: they had only anecdotal encounters with individual officers and did not present official complaint numbers or a sustained paper trail, a deficiency the court emphasized. When police reports cannot be obtained, counsel should document the reasons—e.g., offices destroyed during violence, officials refusing to accept complaints, credible threats against reporting—so the adjudicator can consider a futility or danger argument.

Medical and psychological evaluations give shape and specificity to claims of physical injury, pain, and psychological trauma—precisely the types of harms courts scrutinize when assessing whether the conduct rises to persecution. A credible clinician’s diagnosis (for example, PTSD, anxiety disorder, or a panic disorder), anchored by standardized instruments and by a clear causal nexus to the incidents at issue, materially increases the odds that an adjudicator will treat emotional and psychological harms as more than "subjective fear." Jimenez highlights the court’s reluctance to treat subjective sleep loss and panic attacks, unsupported by clinical assessment, as sufficient. A strong mental‑health report will describe symptoms, assessments used (e.g., CAPS, CIDI), history of treatment, functional impairment (work or school problems), and an expert opinion tying symptoms to the hostile acts; it should also explain why the symptoms would persist or recur upon return.

Affidavits and witness statements remain indispensable. Employers, neighbors, co‑workers, teachers, and local community leaders can supply contemporaneous detail—who said what, when, and where—that lends credibility to the applicant’s narrative. The best affidavits are specific: dates, locations, verbatim hostile statements when remembered, physical descriptions of assailants, and the affiant’s basis of knowledge. Where possible, affidavits should be notarized and accompanied by documentary corroboration (photos, receipts, text messages). The Tenth Circuit’s treatment of the brochure saying "death to snitches from Liberal Party" suggests that a single piece of documentary evidence, standing alone and not clearly tied to the alleged aggressors’ later conduct, may lack persuasive force; the record should therefore include proof the document came from the persecutor or was distributed in the same context as subsequent confrontations.

Practical steps for assembling corroboration: obtain and certify police complaint numbers and follow‑up letters; secure contemporaneous medical records and arrange mental‑health evaluations promptly; collect and notarize third‑party affidavits explaining personal knowledge; preserve digital evidence (texts, voicemail, social‑media posts) with metadata when possible; and provide translations and, where necessary, expert witness statements authenticating documents.

## Documenting government inability or unwillingness: complaint receipts, follow-ups, ombudsman or prosecutor outreach

The state‑protection inquiry turns heavily on whether the government had notice and an opportunity to intervene, and whether it did so effectively. Singh—cited by the Tenth Circuit in Jimenez—explains that evidence of governmental response (or lack thereof) is key: whether authorities stopped or tried to stop the persecutors, and whether protective measures were offered. The applicant therefore should build a record that addresses those two prongs directly.

Start with proof of reporting: formal complaints, receipts, police report numbers, names and ranks of officers, and copies of any letters requesting help. Follow‑up communications—emails to prosecutors, documented visits to a precinct, certified mail, and contemporaneous notes of conversations—demonstrate persistence and give the adjudicator a timeline to evaluate whether the state had a reasonable opportunity to act. In Jimenez, the court noted that petitioners “spoke to two individual police officers who said that Petitioners should not seek trouble,” but there was no evidence of formal complaints or systemic official inaction; that gap undercut their claim that the state was unwilling or unable to protect them. Where the police give unhelpful oral responses, counsel should memorialize those interactions in writing immediately and seek a written response or refusal.

When ordinary police reporting is fraught, applicants should document outreach to other state institutions: the prosecutor’s office, an ombudsman (procuraduría or defensoría), the Human Rights Unit, or national authorities responsible for protection programs. Certified copies of complaint filings with prosecutors, acknowledgment receipts, and any correspondence from oversight bodies demonstrate that the applicant sought protection through formal channels. If those offices decline jurisdiction or otherwise fail to respond, obtain written refusals or contemporaneous proof of inaction. This creates a firmer record for invoking the futility exception to the reporting requirement.

Expert country‑condition declarations can tie particular local steps—e.g., known impunity in a municipality, evidence of police collusion with a paramilitary leader—to the applicant’s individualized experience. But such evidence must be specific and connected: a national human‑rights report describing generalized corruption is helpful background, but is typically insufficient by itself to prove the government was unable or unwilling to protect a particular applicant absent a showing that the particular officials who could have helped either declined to do so or were complicit. The Jimenez court emphasized this point by distinguishing isolated interactions with unhelpful officers from systemic failures: courts often require proof of systemic, not merely anecdotal, dysfunction to find the government unable or unwilling to protect.

Finally, document any real risks inherent in making reports—threats, retaliation, or evidence that complainants have been targeted after filing. If reporting would have been dangerous, describe the specific reasons, supported by affidavits or expert testimony, rather than relying on generalized assertions of corruption.

## Nexus proof: persecutor statements tying harm to protected grounds; timing and comparator evidence

Nexus is the sine qua non of a successful claim: an applicant must show the persecutor’s motive was at least one central reason for the harm. The Tenth Circuit applies the "at least one central reason" standard and treats motive as a factual question of mixed intent. Evidence of persecutor statements that link the conduct to a protected ground—contemporaneous threats, leaflets, shouted slogans, texts explicitly naming the victim’s party, religion, or social identity—are the strongest proof of nexus.

Direct evidence is best: contemporaneous utterances such as "we will kill you for supporting Candidate X" or written threats like the brochure in Jimenez, when clearly traceable to the perpetrators, provide powerful nexus proof. In Jimenez the brochure read “death to snitches from Liberal Party”—that might have supported nexus to political opinion had the court been convinced the brochure was tied to the later attacks and to the individuals who threatened the family. But the court found the timing and context (the property dispute and the tenant "Don Rafa") showed the primary motive was retaliation for alleged interference in criminal activity; the political wording was, in the court’s view, tangential. That illustrates the need to show not only the content of the message but also the identity of the author and contemporaneity with persecution.

Circumstantial evidence can also establish nexus. Timing is crucial: an escalation of violence that begins only after an applicant takes an overt political role strengthens the inference of a political motive. Comparator evidence—showing a pattern where others who share the protected characteristic were targeted—bolsters an inference of motive. For example, evidence that several local Liberal Party volunteers received similar threats or were attacked during the same campaign season supports the political nexus. Documentation such as police blotters, media articles reporting targeted attacks against a political group, and affidavits from other victims can help build this pattern.

Mixed‑motive cases require careful evidentiary framing. If a persecutor had multiple motives (personal revenge, economic gain, and hostility toward the applicant’s political activities), the applicant must show that a protected ground was at least a central reason. To do so, offer evidence showing the persecutor’s knowledge of the applicant’s political activity (campaign photos, membership cards, witness testimony that the persecutor used political labels during threats), and explain why political animus mattered (e.g., the persecutor targeted only those who campaigned). Equally important is rebutting alternative motivations the government might posit. Where the persecutor alleges a personal dispute (land, debt, criminal retaliation), record evidence that discounts that explanation—show the applicant had no prior animus, explain why a property dispute would not have led to lethal threats except when combined with political activity, or show a contemporaneous pattern that the property dispute alone does not explain.

In short: procure contemporaneous statements, preserve the chain linking the statement to the aggressor, show timing and patterns, and use comparator evidence to demonstrate motive was not incidental.

## Internal relocation rebuttal: expert declarations, relocation failures, persecutor networks’ geographic reach

Whether internal relocation is a reasonable alternative is a question adjudicators often decide early. The government can rebut the presumption of future persecution by showing the applicant could reasonably relocate to another part of the country. To defeat that defense, an applicant must show that relocation is not reasonable—because it is unsafe, impossible, or unduly harsh—or that the persecutor has the reach to find and harm the applicant wherever he or she goes.

The record should include expert declarations about the persecutor’s network and geographic reach. An expert can explain whether criminal groups or corrupt networks operate regionally or nationally, whether they have the capability to track and retaliate, and how they cooperate with or evade state authorities. In Jimenez the court credited evidence that the client safely stayed with family in Bogota’s countryside for three weeks and that relatives continued to live in Colombia unharmed; those facts supported the BIA’s conclusion that internal relocation was reasonable. To counter such a finding, evidence must show either that short‑term relocations are illusory (the persecutors followed the family and continued threats), that the geographic reach of the persecutor is broad and well‑documented, or that the claimant faces special obstacles to relocation (economic impossibility, lack of family or job prospects, medical or educational needs).

Document any prior attempts to relocate and their outcomes. If the applicant previously tried to move to another city and the threats continued, preserve contemporaneous evidence—new threats, surveillance incidents, or local officials’ inability to protect the applicant—that shows relocation failed. Financial and administrative burdens can also be relevant: provide leases showing unaffordability, employer letters documenting loss of income, proof of lack of legal immigration alternatives in the area of relocation, and expert testimony on barriers to safe integration in a new locale.

When arguing relocation is unreasonable because the government cannot protect against a persecutor operating nationally, tie that claim to concrete evidence: prosecutors’ and police reports showing failed enforcement, media accounts of organized crime operating across regions, and expert testimony about the group’s national reach. Avoid speculative assertions that a persecutor might find an applicant anywhere; courts require concrete proof that relocation would not solve the problem.

## Child-specific evidence and UNHCR guidance on child persecution

Children merit special evidentiary care. International guidance, including UNHCR’s country‑specific and child‑protection guidance, emphasizes the distinct ways children experience persecution and the need for age‑appropriate corroboration. Domestic courts recognize children’s vulnerabilities—school threats, abductions near school, forced recruitment, or psychological trauma—and the evidence should be tailored accordingly.

School records are often highly probative. Attendance records that show absences related to fear, notes from teachers documenting behavioral changes, and written reports from school administrators about an incident (for example, a stranger grabbing a child’s mouth as he exits school) are powerful. In Jimenez, a man put his arm around the petitioners’ younger son and covered his mouth as the boy tried to flee; the record contained testimony about the event and reported panic attacks but lacked clinical or school documentation that would have amplified the child‑specific harm. A contemporaneous letter from the school corroborating the incident—or a teacher’s affidavit—is especially persuasive.

Medical and psychological evaluations are crucial for children. Pediatricians, child psychologists, or psychiatrists can diagnose trauma‑related disorders, show functional impairment (sleep problems, school performance decline), and explain long‑term developmental consequences. Because children may not be able to describe experiences with the granularity adults can, clinicians’ explanations linking symptoms to documented incidents help fill the inferential gap. Clinicians should use child‑appropriate instruments and explain why the child’s symptoms stem from the events alleged.

UNHCR guidance recommends child‑sensitive interviewing techniques and emphasizes best‑interest determinations. Practitioners should ensure interviews with children are conducted in a manner that minimizes retraumatization and maximize reliability: short sessions, presence of a trusted adult when appropriate, use of child‑trained interpreters, and corroboration through independent child‑focused professionals. Where possible, identify child‑welfare or social‑service records, foster‑care documents, or reports from child protection agencies that document the risk or harm. These documents do double duty: they corroborate the child’s experience and provide evidence that returning the child would not be in the child’s best interests.

Finally, when the claim principally concerns a child, consider relief avenues that center on child welfare (e.g., withholding or Convention‑based relief where applicable) and be prepared to explain the child's needs in relocation analysis—whether education, specialized therapy, or family support necessary for safety.

Conclusion: translating doctrine into evidence

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez demonstrates the practical consequences of an evidentiary gap: credible testimony alone can be insufficient where key corroboration (police complaints, clinical evaluations, documents tying threats to a persecutor’s motive) is absent or ambiguous. To build a winning record, practitioners must think in advance about how to document each element:

- For past persecution: assemble contemporaneous reports, physical or clinical records, and third‑party affidavits that describe the incidents in detail and show cumulative harm beyond mere threats.
- For nexus: preserve persecutor statements, document timing and patterns, collect comparator evidence, and use experts to explain local dynamics linking the harm to a protected ground.
- For state protection: secure formal complaint receipts, document attempts to follow up with prosecutors or ombudsmen, and, if reporting was dangerous or futile, demonstrate why with concrete evidence.
- For internal relocation: obtain expert analysis of the persecutor’s reach, document any prior relocation failures, and provide evidence of economic or logistical barriers where relevant.
- For child claims: gather school and medical records, child‑sensitive psychological assessments, and use UNHCR guidance to craft child‑appropriate interviews and corroboration.

In every case, the overarching principle is connectivity: the record must connect the dots—who did what, when, and why; how the state responded; and why relocation does or does not solve the problem. Meticulous, contemporaneous documentation and carefully targeted expert evidence turn credible stories into adjudicable proofs.
