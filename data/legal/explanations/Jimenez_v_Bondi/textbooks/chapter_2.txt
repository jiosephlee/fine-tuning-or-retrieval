Chapter 2: # Elements of an Asylum Claim

Asylum doctrine is structured to answer a deceptively simple question: should a noncitizen who fears mistreatment if returned to her country of nationality be allowed to remain in the United States? The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) resolves that question by defining a “refugee” and by setting out the elements an asylum applicant must prove. This chapter explains those elements, the ways they interact, and how courts evaluate claims in practice. We use the Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi to illustrate how the doctrinal tests operate and why certain evidentiary gaps commonly prove fatal.

At the outset, a few framing points. Asylum is a discretionary remedy; an applicant must first show statutory eligibility before the Attorney General may grant relief. The applicant bears the burden of proving eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence. The law recognizes two alternative routes to refugee status: a showing of past persecution (which creates a rebuttable presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution) or a showing of a well‑founded fear of future persecution without past persecution. Both routes require a nexus to one of the statutorily protected grounds. The BIA and immigration judges (IJs) make factual determinations under the substantial‑evidence standard, and courts reviewing the BIA will overturn factual findings only if the record compels a contrary conclusion.

## Defining a refugee under § 1101(a)(42)(A): past persecution or well‑founded fear on account of a protected ground

The INA defines a “refugee” as someone who is outside his country and “unable or unwilling to return” because of “persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution on account of” race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. That language yields several discrete doctrinal components: (1) persecution (past) or a well‑founded fear (future); (2) causal nexus to a protected ground; and (3) that the persecution is inflicted by the government or by private actors whom the government is unable or unwilling to control. An applicant who proves past persecution is entitled to a rebuttable presumption that he also has a well‑founded fear of future persecution. If an applicant cannot show past persecution, she may still prevail by establishing both the subjective and objective components of a well‑founded fear.

Jimenez illustrates the practical application of these components. The family presented evidence of threats, harassment, and one violent incident involving their child. The IJ and BIA found the family credible but concluded their cumulative harm did not rise to past persecution, and that the evidence did not establish a nexus to a protected ground or a lack of governmental protection. Because asylum eligibility hinges on these interrelated elements, failure on any single component is often dispositive.

## The three‑part past persecution showing: harm rising to persecution; nexus to a protected ground; by government or private actors the government is unable or unwilling to control

An applicant asserting past persecution must prove three things: (1) an incident, or cumulative incidents, that rise to the level of persecution; (2) that the harm was inflicted “on account of” (i.e., because of) a protected ground; and (3) the persecutor was either the government or private actors that the government could not or would not control.

Harm rising to persecution. Persecution is not precisely defined by statute, but courts require more than ordinary harassment, economic disadvantage, or sporadic threats. The relevant inquiry asks whether the harm inflicted amounts to suffering or harm that an objective observer would view as serious and offensive. Physical violence commonly meets this bar, but physical injury is not necessary; severe psychological harm, confinement, or prolonged, targeted abuse can also constitute persecution. Importantly, adjudicators assess incidents cumulatively: a series of threats and non‑fatal mistreatment may, when viewed together, reach the threshold even if no single event would suffice.

In Jimenez the court emphasized that threats alone frequently fall short. The family received recurring threats, a brochure calling for “death to snitches,” and a few harassing and violent confrontations, but no lasting physical injury. The Tenth Circuit relied on precedent holding threats and psychological stress, without more, rarely amount to persecution. Thus, even where applicants are credible and understandably frightened, courts often require either physical harm or a pattern of severe conduct to find past persecution.

Nexus to a protected ground. Persecution is actionable only if it is “on account of” one of the protected grounds. That means the protected ground must be at least one central reason for the persecution—not merely incidental or tangential. Showing the requisite nexus can come from direct evidence (persecutors’ statements indicating a political motive, for example) or from circumstantial evidence (timing, patterns of targeting of persons who share the protected trait, or contextual country conditions). Mixed‑motive situations are common: persecutors often have several motives. After the REAL ID Act, the causation standard requires that a protected ground be at least one central reason for the persecution. Put differently, the protected ground must play a central (not minor) causal role in the harm.

In Jimenez, the court concluded that the predominant motive was personal and economic retaliation for the applicant’s interference with a criminal’s business (a property dispute that threatened “Don Rafa’s” revenue and security), not political opinion. Although the family had been politically active, the escalated threats correlated temporally with the property dispute and included references to prosecutorial activity—evidence that the persecutors were motivated principally by Jimenez’s role as a provisional lawyer and his interference with criminal enterprises. The court therefore found the political‑opinion nexus lacking.

Government or government‑unable/unwilling private actors. The third prong asks whether the persecutor is the state, or private actors whom the state cannot or will not control. Where private actors inflict the harm, the applicant must show that a reasonable effort to seek state protection would have been futile, dangerous, or impossible—either because the state lacks the capacity to respond or because it is complicit. The key factual issues are whether the applicant reported the abuse and how the state responded (or would likely respond), and whether there is evidence of systemic failure rather than a one‑off indifferent officer.

Jimenez underscores how critical the reporting/notice inquiry can be. The applicant told a police officer about the threat; the officer replied that “nothing could be done” and suggested they leave. The record also showed government authorities had pursued searches and seizures at the relevant property and that some police activity had occurred. The court viewed the government’s investigative efforts against the applicant’s assertion of state collusion, concluding the record did not support a finding that Colombia was unable or unwilling to protect the family.

## Well‑founded fear: subjective and objective components; role of country conditions

If an applicant has not suffered past persecution, she may still qualify for asylum by showing a well‑founded fear of future persecution. This standard has both subjective and objective components. The subjective component requires that the applicant genuinely fears returning. That is satisfied by credible testimony that the applicant fears persecution. The objective component asks whether a reasonable person in the applicant’s circumstances would fear persecution—that there is a reasonable possibility of suffering persecution on account of a protected ground.

The objective prong is typically satisfied by showing a reasonable possibility of persecution, which courts have variously described as a “reasonable chance,” a “reasonable possibility,” or a nontrivial likelihood. Country conditions evidence—country reports, human‑rights documentation, expert testimony, and corroborating evidence—plays an important role in demonstrating the objective risk. Where an applicant has suffered past persecution, the BIA presumes a well‑founded fear of future persecution unless the government rebuts that presumption by showing a fundamental change in circumstances or that the applicant could relocate safely within the country.

Jimenez illustrates these dynamics. Because the court found no past persecution, the presumption never arose. The family therefore had to establish both subjective fear and the objective likelihood of future persecution. The court found no objective basis: the persecutors’ motives leaned non‑political, the government had taken some action against the criminals’ property, and the family had previously relocated within Colombia without harm—facts that undercut the claim of a reasonable possibility of future persecution.

A related point: withholding of removal requires a higher showing than asylum. An applicant seeking withholding must demonstrate it is “more likely than not” (a clear probability) that she would be persecuted if returned. If an applicant fails to establish a well‑founded fear for asylum, she necessarily fails to meet the withholding standard.

## Protected grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group (PSG), political opinion

The five protected grounds—race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, and political opinion—define the permissible reasons an applicant can offer for persecution. Race, religion, and nationality are typically straightforward categories. Political opinion and membership in a particular social group often raise the most contested legal and factual disputes.

Political opinion. To succeed on a political‑opinion claim, the applicant must show that the persecutor perceived her as holding a political opinion (this can include imputed opinions) or that she actually holds a political opinion that motivated the persecution. Courts look for evidence that persecutors targeted the applicant because of her political activities, affiliations, or expressions. Statements by persecutors referring to the applicant’s politics, signs of targeted attacks against a class of politically active persons, or temporal correlation between political activity and escalated harm can all support the required nexus.

In the Jimenez case, petitioners argued that campaign activity for a presidential candidate and membership in a political party established a political‑opinion nexus. The court acknowledged their political engagement but found the critical threats escalated after and because of Jimenez’s involvement in a property dispute with a criminal leader and his role as a provisional lawyer. Because the persecutors’ statements and the timing of the threats pointed to retaliation for interference with criminal activity rather than political beliefs, the political‑opinion claim failed.

Particular social group (PSG). PSG claims require not only that the group be cognizable under immigration law (generally defined by immutable characteristics, shared past experience, or characteristics fundamental to individual dignity), but also that the applicant be a member of that group and that the group be a central reason for the persecution. PSGs have been a litigation hotspot. As the Jimenez opinion shows, an applicant who settles on political‑opinion theory but fails to brief a PSG will forfeit that argument on appeal. Counsel should plead and develop any PSG theories at the administrative level and explain how the group is defined and why persecutors targeted the group.

Practical implication: when an applicant is targeted for overlapping reasons (political activity, social leadership, or a profession such as lawyering), counsel should identify alternative grounds and develop record evidence for each—by eliciting persecutor statements, showing patterns of attacks on similarly situated individuals, and using country‑condition evidence.

## REAL ID Act causation: “one central reason” and its application to mixed motives

The REAL ID Act clarified the causation standard for asylum claims. Under the Act, an applicant must demonstrate that the protected ground was “at least one central reason” for the persecution. This is a causation standard that recognizes persecutors may have multiple motives but requires that a protected ground be a central—not merely incidental—reason for the harm.

How this standard works in practice can be subtle. Evidence that a persecutor knew of an applicant’s political activity and expressed animus toward it is strong proof of political motive. But where the record shows stronger or alternative motives—economic gain, personal vendetta, criminal retaliation—the protected ground must still be shown to play a central role. The key questions are: what did the persecutor say? What actions were taken and in what context? Is there a temporal or causal link between the applicant’s protected characteristic and the mistreatment? Courts will examine both direct statements from persecutors and circumstantial patterns.

The Jimenez court applied this principle to conclude that political opinion was merely tangential. The persecutors’ references to “snitches” and to prosecutorial activity, the escalation of threats after Jimenez’s direct interference with the criminal’s property and the men’s explicit threat that the order came from Don Rafa strongly suggested that retaliation for legal/professional interference—rather than political activism—was the central motive. Even though the family had engaged in political campaigning earlier (and faced more general threats), the pivotal acts that escalated into targeted threats related to conduct that threatened the criminal enterprise’s business. Under REAL ID Act analysis, that dispositive context meant the political‑opinion claim failed.

Mixed‑motive cases are therefore often resolved on the relative strength of competing motives and the evidence demonstrating causation. Where protected‑ground motive is one of several reasons, an applicant should develop evidence that the protected ground was not subordinate—by eliciting overtly political statements, evidence that only politically active individuals were targeted, or documentary proof showing the persecutors’ political objectives.

## Interplay of burdens, presumptions, and the government’s rebuttal

Two important procedural rules shape asylum adjudication. First, an applicant bears the initial burden to prove eligibility by a preponderance. A showing of past persecution creates a rebuttable presumption that the applicant has a well‑founded fear of future persecution; the government then can rebut that presumption by proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, either a fundamental change in country conditions or that the applicant could safely relocate to another part of the country. Second, the government bears the burden of demonstrating that internal relocation is reasonable and that the applicant could avoid persecution by moving within the country.

Although these burdens seem straightforward, facts control. The government’s ability to rebut a presumption often depends on country‑conditions evidence, testimony about the applicant’s familial and social ties elsewhere in the country, and evidence of state responses to the persecutors. In Jimenez, the government’s position that internal relocation was reasonable was bolstered by the family’s successful temporary relocation to Jimenez’s mother’s home in the countryside, the continued safety of relatives who remained in Colombia, and evidence of government operations directed at the criminal property. Those facts allowed the BIA and the court to conclude that relocation would be reasonable.

Finally, appellate review matters. The BIA’s factual findings—about whether the harm rises to persecution, whether the government is unable or unwilling, and whether internal relocation is reasonable—are reviewed under the substantial‑evidence standard. Courts will not reweigh the evidence where the record supports the BIA’s conclusions. Jimenez demonstrates this principle: the family’s credibility did not compel a finding of persecution, and the appellate court deferred to the BIA’s factual judgments.

## Practical considerations for building an asylum case

The doctrine distilled above suggests several practical evidentiary rules for counsel and applicants:

- Gather specific evidence of persecutors’ motive: statements, notes, threats that reference political activity or protected status, or contemporaneous witnesses who heard the persecutors’ rationale.
- Document the severity and cumulative pattern of harm: medical records, psychological evaluations, police reports, photographs of injuries or property damage, and affidavits from neighbors or colleagues.
- Preserve evidence showing government notice and response (or lack thereof): police reports, written complaints, correspondence with authorities, and records indicating state investigations or their absence.
- Develop country‑conditions evidence that corroborates the objective risk; expert declarations can explain local politics, the power of criminal actors, or patterns of state collusion.
- If advancing a PSG claim, clearly define the group and develop evidence that persecutors target that group as such; PSGs require careful legal framing and factual development.
- Anticipate mixed‑motive arguments and be prepared to show that the protected ground was at least one central reason for the mistreatment.
- If internal relocation is an issue, document feasibility: family or community ties elsewhere, employment prospects, housing availability, and the geographic scope of the persecutors’ reach.

Conclusion

The asylum statute establishes a nuanced, narrative‑driven inquiry. Applicants must translate personal fear into legal elements: persecution (or a well‑founded fear), nexus to a protected ground, and the role or impotence of state protection. Courts evaluate these components holistically: credible fear alone is insufficient; the applicant must show that the fear rests on a legally cognizable ground and a realistic risk of future harm. Jimenez v. Bondi is a useful example of the interaction among these elements. There, credible testimony did not overcome the evidentiary gaps on persecution, nexus, and government protection; the Tenth Circuit therefore denied relief. Understanding the doctrinal tests and how they play out in practice is essential for effective advocacy in asylum proceedings.
