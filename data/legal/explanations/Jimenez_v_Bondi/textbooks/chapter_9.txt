Chapter 9: # Particular Social Group and Political Activism: Framing Alternative Theories

Asylum law requires careful legal framing and factual development to win relief. When a client has been targeted for civic or political activity—in communities riven by gangs, cartels, or corrupt officials—counsel must decide how to conceptualize the claim: as persecution on account of political opinion, as persecution on account of membership in a particular social group (PSG), or both. The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi (the opinion reproduced above) illustrates the risks of underdeveloped alternative theories. The court denied relief in part because the petitioners both failed to establish the required nexus to a protected ground and failed to develop or brief an alternative PSG theory. This chapter explains the PSG doctrinal tests, how they commonly arise in civic/political contexts, why alternative grounds must be preserved and developed, how imputed political opinion interacts with PSG claims against gangs and cartels, and how to plead and preserve mixed-motive cases strategically.

## PSG requirements: immutability, particularity, and social distinction

A “particular social group” is a statutory category of protected ground under the refugee definition. Courts have developed three central features that a claimed social group must satisfy: (1) immutability or fundamental characteristic; (2) particularity; and (3) social distinction. Each element is legal and factual; understanding what they require and why they matter is essential to drafting winners.

Immutability or fundamentality refers to a characteristic that a person either cannot change, should not be required to change, or that is so fundamental to human dignity that it is akin to an immutable trait. Sexual orientation is a paradigmatic example. But immutability also encompasses characteristics the person cannot or should not be required to alter: political belief is mutable as a matter of fact, but courts sometimes accept a PSG that is defined by conduct that demonstrates a protected characteristic (e.g., “former police officers”) when the conduct reflects a trait fundamental to identity or conscience. When asserting a PSG built on activism or civic leadership, counsel must show why the characteristic at issue meets the immutability/fundamentality prong—why the persecutor targets the claimant for something the claimant cannot or should not be forced to abandon.

Particularity requires that the group be discrete and definable as a group. The group must have clear boundaries so that members can be identified with reasonable certainty. Vague, amorphous descriptions—“people who oppose criminal organizations” or “social leaders” without more—risk being rejected as lacking particularity because they could encompass nearly anyone and do not identify an intelligible group. A successful PSG in a political context is often defined narrowly with reference to geography, specific political party or activity, or a distinct role (for example, “Liberal Party campaign volunteers in [named municipality] who engaged in door-to-door campaigning in 2022”). Narrow definitions help ensure the group is particular.

Social distinction is an empirical inquiry into whether the society in question perceives the group as a discrete class. This is not purely aspirational; adjudicators look to country conditions, media reports, community testimony, or other evidence showing that the relevant population recognizes the group as distinct. Evidence can be direct—testimony that perpetrators referred to the claimant by a label—or indirect—reports showing gangs target “community leaders,” news articles identifying municipal campaigners as targets, or expert evidence that civic activists are singled out in certain regions. Courts repeatedly emphasize that social distinction is a factual question and that a bare assertion without supporting evidence is unlikely to satisfy the requirement.

These three components are interrelated. A narrowly drawn PSG that reflects a fundamental characteristic and is recognized by the relevant society has the best chance of surviving adjudication. The Jimenez decision exemplifies the consequences of failing to present or develop a PSG theory: the petitioners “did not brief any arguments regarding the existence of their particular social group,” and the court limited review to their political-opinion theory. That omission proved consequential.

## Common PSG formulations in civic/political contexts (e.g., “social leaders”) and pitfalls

In civic and political contexts, lawyers commonly propose PSGs such as “social leaders,” “community organizers,” “local political activists,” or “members of [named party]’s grassroots campaign in [municipality].” These labels may capture the lived reality of vulnerability, but they also present pitfalls.

First, labels like “social leaders” are often too broad and indeterminate to meet particularity and social-distinction requirements. Who counts as a “social leader”? Does the label include anyone who speaks at community meetings, anyone who organizes a charity drive, or only those with a formal leadership title? Without a clear definition and supporting evidence that the society perceives and treats “social leaders” as a distinct class, adjudicators are likely to dismiss the group as amorphous. The Jimenez court explicitly noted that the petitioners “did not brief any arguments regarding the existence of their particular social group,” and the absence of such briefing allowed the court to avoid resolving a poorly defined PSG.

Second, PSGs tied to political activity can run into immutability problems: political opinions are mutable and voluntary. To overcome that, the PSG must be framed in terms of an immutable or fundamental characteristic or as a discrete role that members cannot or should not be compelled to abandon (for example, “Liberal Party campaign volunteers who engaged in door-to-door canvassing in 2022 in [neighborhood],” accompanied by evidence that the persecutors singled out such canvassers). Narrowing by geography, time, specific activities, or affiliation helps establish particularity and shows why members cannot feasibly dissociate to avoid harm.

Third, evidence must show social distinction. In gang/corrupt environments, persecutors may not use civic labels but may brand targets with epithets (“snitches,” “government collaborators”). Documentation that perpetrators labeled the claimant as a member of a specific group, or that community members recognized a discrete class of targets, strengthens the social-distinction showing.

Finally, watch out for groups defined by the persecutor’s motive alone—“people the cartel deems problematic” is circular and unconstitutionally broad. Instead, define the group in objective terms tied to immutable or fundamental traits or to discrete, recognizable roles.

## Preserving alternative protected grounds: briefing and record development

Jimenez is a cautionary tale about preservation. The petitioners primarily argued political opinion; they did not brief PSG theories and thus forfeited them. Federal courts will not consider an unbriefed theory on appeal, and adjudicators at the BIA and IJ level expect claimants to present and develop alternative grounds. Preservation requires more than a passing mention—it requires legal argument and factual support in the administrative record.

Record development must be active and intentional. If counsel intends to pursue a PSG theory, they should:

- Frame the PSG narrowly and precisely in written filings and at hearing. Avoid generic labels; define the group by specific traits (party affiliation, type of activism, geography, time period).
- Elicit and submit evidence showing why the group is immutable/fundamental, particular, and socially distinct: testimony about how neighbors, perpetrators, or local authorities identify the claimant; affidavits from community members; news articles; NGO reports; and country-condition evidence.
- Secure corroboration of key events. Police reports, contemporaneous complaints, witness statements, and documentation of threats or acts against similarly situated people are crucial.
- Use expert testimony where appropriate to show social recognition of the group, patterns of targeting, and the role of criminal organizations in political violence.

Failing to brief or document the PSG theory invites the result in Jimenez: the court declined to entertain a PSG claim that the petitioners had not argued or developed.

## Imputed political opinion versus PSG theories in gang and cartel environments

In contexts dominated by non-state actors—gangs, cartels, guerrillas—two doctrinal paths often compete: (1) the persecutor imputed a political opinion to the claimant (or perceived the claimant as cooperating with the government), or (2) the claimant belongs to a PSG (for example, “people perceived to oppose [criminal group]” or “people who cooperate with the state”).

An imputed political opinion claim focuses on the persecutor’s perception of the claimant’s beliefs. If a cartel or gang targets someone because it believes the person supports or cooperates with the state or a rival political movement, that targeting may be “on account of” political opinion even if the claimant never openly expressed such an opinion. The key is evidence that the persecutor’s actions were motivated—at least in part—by that perception. Statements by attackers, notes, pamphlets, or patterns of targeting campaign workers can show motive. The Tenth Circuit requires that a protected ground be “at least one central reason” for the persecution; in mixed-motive situations the protected ground must be a central, not incidental, reason.

A PSG theory in this setting might attempt to capture the same reality by defining the group as “persons perceived to be cooperating with the government” or “people who refuse to cooperate with the cartel.” These formulations can succeed, but they face particularity and social-distinction hurdles. “Persons perceived to be cooperating with the government” may be sufficiently particular if defined by geography or specific acts (e.g., “residents of [town] who reported kidnappings to local police in 2022”), and if evidence shows community recognition. But a PSG that is merely “people the cartel dislikes” is circular and unlikely to survive.

Which path to pursue depends on the facts and evidence. If attackers explicitly framed the victim as a “snitch” or a government collaborator, an imputed-political-opinion theory is natural and often more straightforward. If the claimant and others share a discrete, socially recognized status (e.g., “local election monitors”), a PSG claim may be preferable. Where possible, plead both and develop evidence for both: an imputation can often be shown with attacker statements, and a PSG with corroboration of social recognition.

## Strategic pleading in mixed-motive settings

Mixed-motive cases—where persecutors have both protected and unprotected reasons—are common. The governing rule is familiar: the protected ground need only be “at least one central reason” for harm. Strategically, counsel should aim to demonstrate protected grounds are central rather than incidental.

Begin pleadings by asserting all plausible protected grounds: political opinion (actual and imputed), PSG, and in appropriate cases race, religion, or nationality. Carefully define any PSG alternatives to satisfy immutability, particularity, and social distinction. Use a layered approach to the record:

- Collect contemporaneous statements by perpetrators (threat letters, pamphlets, recorded threats) that reference political alignment or label victims (e.g., “death to snitches from Liberal Party,” as in Jimenez). Such statements are powerful on both political-opinion and imputation theories.
- Document patterns of targeting: others affiliated with the same party, campaign, or role who were threatened, attacked, kidnapped, or murdered.
- Submit police reports and correspondence demonstrating reporting and the government’s response (or lack thereof). If the government discouraged reporting or law enforcement is linked to persecutors, collect evidence demonstrating systemic failure.
- Use affidavits and expert reports describing local dynamics and social perceptions to satisfy social-distinction and demonstrate the persecutor’s motives.

Finally, anticipate and counter obvious defenses. If the persecutor will argue the motive was private (a personal vendetta or property dispute), gather evidence tying the dispute to political activity or showing the property dispute was a pretext for targeting a political actor. In Jimenez, the court credited evidence that the escalation followed a property dispute with a local narco leader and found the predominant motive economic and personal; had counsel developed a tighter PSG or adducing stronger evidence that the threats were politically motivated (for example, contemporaneous statements tying the threats to campaign activity or a pattern of targeting campaigners), the result might have differed.

Conclusion — Practical checklist

- From the outset, decide whether to pursue political-opinion claims, PSG claims, or both. Preserve each theory in pleadings and at hearing.
- If asserting a PSG, define it narrowly and tie it to immutability/fundamentality, particularity, and social distinction. Avoid labels without definition.
- Develop the record: contemporaneous documentation, witness statements, police reports, country conditions, and expert testimony are often determinative.
- Treat imputed political opinion as a distinct and often powerful route in gang/cartel settings; plead it alongside PSG theories where appropriate.
- In mixed-motive cases, assemble evidence that the protected ground was “at least one central reason” for the harm. Perpetrator statements and pattern evidence are key.
- Preserve arguments and develop factual support at the administrative level; appellate courts will not rescue unbriefed or underdeveloped theories.

The Jimenez opinion underscores a recurring lesson: asylum outcomes turn on doctrine and on disciplined advocacy. Clear legal framing, narrow PSG definitions, robust factual development, and preservation of alternative grounds can mean the difference between relief and denial.
