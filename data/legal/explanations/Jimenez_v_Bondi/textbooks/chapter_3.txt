Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Jimenez v. Bondi

Chapter 3: # What Counts as Persecution?

This chapter examines the central but elusive question that arises in virtually every asylum claim: when does harassment, threat, or other mistreatment cross the line into persecution? The United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi (the opinion excerpted above) supplies the immediate doctrinal scaffolding for this inquiry. Jimenez illustrates how courts apply settled Tenth Circuit tests—drawing on Wiransane, Ritonga, Rivera-Barrientos, Karki and related precedents—to distinguish persecution from lesser wrongs, how adjudicators aggregate multiple harms, and what kinds of proof are necessary when physical injury is absent but psychological injury or threats are alleged.

The chapter is organized to explain the term of art “persecution,” the importance of cumulative assessment, the limited circumstances in which threats alone will suffice, the evidentiary challenges when reliance is placed on psychological injury rather than physical harm, the special weight given to family- and child-targeted incidents, and the contours of inter-circuit variation. Throughout, we use the Jimenez facts as a running example: repeated telephone and in-person threats, a threatening brochure, an assault on an employee seeking the family’s whereabouts, an attempted interference with the family’s child, and the murder of a fellow campaigner—but no physical injury inflicted on the petitioning family themselves.

## Persecution as a term of art: more than restrictions or sporadic threats; “offensive” harm

Persecution is a term of art in asylum law, not a synonym for any mistreatment or inconvenience. In the Tenth Circuit it requires "the infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ (in race, religion, or political opinion) in a way regarded as offensive," and must "entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty." That language, adopted from Wiransane and repeatedly applied in later decisions, emphasizes two linked points. First, the harm must be meaningfully serious—something beyond ordinary criminality, neighborhood disputes, or attenuated harassment. Second, the harm must be of a kind that an adjudicator would regard as offensive in the context of the protected characteristic alleged: it is harm targeted at the applicant because of who they are or what they believe.

Jimenez illustrates the application of that standard. The family received repeated threats, a brochure reading “death to snitches from Liberal Party,” and at least one violent in-person encounter in which men pointed guns and told them to leave the neighborhood. Nevertheless, the Tenth Circuit concluded that the cumulative harm—absent actual physical injury to the petitioners—did not meet the court’s definition of persecution. The panel emphasized the distinction between threatening conduct and persecution: threats may be alarming and destabilizing, but the key inquiry is whether they rise to the level of suffering offensive enough to meet the statutory standard. In Jimenez the court found the threats and associated incidents fell short.

For practice, keep in mind that persecution should be evaluated against the surrounding facts. The same verbal threat may be persecution where delivered by state forces to a marginalized political dissident with prior history of targeted violence, but merely criminal intimidation in local gang-controlled neighborhoods where the harms are not connected to a protected ground. The "offensive" component therefore is contextual: identity or opinion-based targeting, repeated or escalatory conduct, and the severity of consequences are all relevant.

## Assessing harm cumulatively rather than incident-by-incident

Adjudicators must analyze patterns of abuse holistically. Tenth Circuit precedent requires consideration of incidents in the aggregate because the cumulative effect of multiple smaller harms may constitute persecution even if any single incident would not. Ritonga emphasizes that adjudicators should not parse incidents in isolation; rather, they must examine frequency, escalation, and the totality of consequences to the claimant’s life.

Jimenez demonstrates the cumulative approach at work. The family experienced a string of events over a period of months—phone threats, the men’s first armed visit, a later armed search for the family, an employee assault, and an attempted interference with the child. The IJ and BIA evaluated these events jointly and so did the Tenth Circuit on review. Nonetheless, the panel concluded that even considered together the harms lacked the requisite gravity or offensive character: the threats had not culminated in violence against the petitioners, and the psychological effects, while real, were not shown to be sufficiently severe by the record presented.

When you analyze a new fact pattern, assemble incidents chronologically and assess whether they show a pattern of sustained, targeted coercion that materially altered the applicant’s life—loss of liberty, forced displacement, severe injury, forcible expropriation, or sustained attempts to terrorize. A checklist to apply in cumulative analysis: number and frequency of incidents; escalation from threats to physical acts; corroboration (witnesses, contemporaneous reports); adverse consequences (job loss, forced relocation, functional impairment); and the nexus to a protected ground. Cumulative harms that combine to deprive the person of basic security or liberty are more likely to be treated as persecution.

## Threats alone: when are they insufficient, and what aggravating factors matter (specificity, imminence, repetition, capability, accompanying actions)?

A core, recurring rule in the Tenth Circuit is that threats alone—without more—generally do not constitute persecution. Karki and other decisions reflect the view that while threats are a relevant part of the calculus, threats unaccompanied by further objective indicators of serious mistreatment usually fall short. But threats are not categorically irrelevant: their character and context determine their legal significance.

Several aggravating features can elevate threats into persecution-sufficient harm:

- Specificity: Vague or generic warnings are weaker than threats that name the target, specify a timeframe, or declare an explicit intention to kill or harm.
- Imminence: A threat that is both specific and immediately actionable—that is, signaling an impending attack—carries more weight than speculative or conditional threats.
- Repetition and duration: Threats repeated over time, showing persistence and an inability to escape them, are more threatening in the persecution sense than one-off utterances.
- Capability: The sender’s demonstrated capacity to carry out the threat—possession of weapons, control of territory, prior successful attacks—makes the threat credible and dangerous.
- Accompanying actions: Threats followed by acts such as assaults on property, kidnapping or murder of associates, or attempted abductions of relatives are more likely to be treated as persecution because they show willingness to act.
- Targeting of vulnerability: Threats directed at children, the elderly, or that leverage personal details (addresses, routines) amplify risk.

Applying these factors to Jimenez: some aggravators were present—the threats were repeated; men pointed guns; a colleague was kidnapped and murdered; assailants assaulted an employee and searched for the family; a man intercepted the younger son at school and put his hand over the child’s mouth. These accompanying acts and the murder of a nearby campaigner are significant aggravating facts. Still, the Tenth Circuit found that, as recorded in the administrative record, those facts did not cross the line into persecution for the petitioners themselves. The court stressed the absence of physical injury to the petitioners, the limited scope of the incidents, and the lack of certain corroboration (for example, evidence that the threatening group had already harmed or attempted to harm the petitioners) as reasons for finding the threats insufficient.

For practitioners, arguable persecution cases based principally on threats should emphasize specificity, implicated actors’ demonstrated capability, contemporaneous reporting, and any follow-through acts. Evidence that the persecutors have controlled territory, conducted prior kidnappings or murders, or made credible attempts to seize or harm the claimant increases the chance the threats will be treated as persecution.

## Physical harm not required: the evidentiary challenges of proving severe psychological harm

It is settled that physical injury is not a necessary component of persecution; psychological and emotional harm can suffice. But the Tenth Circuit’s jurisprudence imposes a demanding evidentiary standard when claimants rest their case primarily on psychological injury rather than on physical abuse. Cases such as Karki and Chhetri show that courts require persuasive, objective evidence demonstrating severity and functional impairment, not mere assertions of fear, insomnia, or stress.

In Jimenez, Petitioners described anxiety, panic attacks in a child, and disrupted sleep. The family endured stress and altered behavior (monitoring children’s routes, moving temporarily to a relative’s house). Despite that, the court characterized these psychological effects as insufficiently severe and insufficiently documented to constitute persecution. The decision recognized the reality of psychological injury, but emphasized that psychological harms are rarely, if ever, the sole basis for an asylum grant in the Tenth Circuit absent robust evidence.

What does “robust evidence” look like? Effective proof of severe psychological harm typically includes:

- Clinical documentation from qualified mental-health professionals (diagnoses such as PTSD, major depressive disorder), contemporaneous treatment records, and treatment plans;
- Objective measures of impairment: hospitalization for psychiatric care, prescriptions and compliance records, documented inability to work or care for self or family;
- Corroborating testimony that links psychological symptoms temporally and causally to the persecutory acts;
- Expert affidavits that explain the severity of the condition, its typical causes, and its expected persistence if the claimant were forced to return.

Absent this type of corroboration, courts will often treat complaints of fear, anxiety, and sleeplessness as insufficient unless they are extreme and clearly disabling. Practitioners relying on psychological harm should therefore secure early psychiatric or psychological evaluations and contemporaneous treatment documentation, and explain how the symptoms meet standard diagnostic thresholds and materially impair the claimant’s life.

## Family-targeted threats and child-focused incidents: how they factor into the aggregate assessment

Threats or acts that implicate a claimant’s family, especially children, can intensify the assessment of persecution because they bear on the severity and scope of harm and can demonstrate the persecutor’s willingness to attack noncombatant targets. Courts have recognized that an attack on a family member or a child can reflect a broader campaign of intimidation and may make otherwise equivocal threats more credible and severe.

In Jimenez, the attempted interference with the younger son—where a man put an arm around the child, covered his mouth, and told him to “run to your dad”—was a salient incident. The family reasonably perceived this as a threat to a vulnerable child, prompting them to relocate temporarily and ultimately to depart the country. Nevertheless, the Tenth Circuit found that, when combined with the other incidents, this child-focused incident did not tip the balance toward persecution. The court noted that the assailant did not abduct or physically injure the child and that the family had not been physically harmed despite other neighborhood violence.

When analyzing child- and family-targeted incidents in other cases, focus on two elements. First, the nature and severity of the action directed at family members: attempted abduction, injury, or murder of a relative is plainly more weighty than verbal intimidation near a school. Second, the effect on the family’s overall security and ability to remain: did the family have to flee their home or relocate long-term? Did a child suffer lasting psychological trauma or physical injury? Courts are more likely to find persecution where children are explicitly targeted in acts that demonstrate the persecutor’s operational capacity and intent to harm noncombatant family members.

Finally, evidence that a persecutor intentionally seeks to exploit a claimant’s emotional ties (for instance, threatening children to coerce political silence) will be treated as more aggravated than threats that merely happen to affect family members tangentially.

## Comparative notes: varying circuit approaches to threats and psychological injury

Although the Tenth Circuit’s approach—requirement of more than mere threats, close scrutiny of psychological-only claims, and an emphasis on corroboration—reflects mainstream federal adjudication, there is inter-circuit variation in both emphasis and threshold. Some circuits are stricter in requiring physical injury or clear evidence of an intention to carry out threats before treating them as persecution. Others are more willing to treat sustained, targeted threats—especially when combined with demonstrated capability and a credible history of violence against similarly situated individuals—as persecution without actual physical injury to the claimant.

The Tenth Circuit, as shown in Jimenez, falls toward the cautious side: threats are significant but normally insufficient standing alone; psychological harm must be well-documented; and the cumulative assessment must show that the applicant was subject to offensive suffering. The Seventh Circuit has likewise cautioned that a single unhelpful police response does not automatically prove systemic government failure to protect, undercutting claims based on threats without reporting or corroboration. Other circuits have sometimes accepted asylum where the record demonstrated that a persecutor had the capacity and propensity to carry out threats and where the threats were specific and imminent—especially where the claimant’s social or political visibility made them an obvious target.

For a practitioner or student, the practical lesson is to study the controlling circuit’s precedents and to tailor evidence-gathering accordingly: in the Tenth Circuit, prioritize concrete proof of violent follow-through or clinical corroboration of severe psychological harm; in courts that give greater weight to the context of threats, emphasize the persecutor’s demonstrated capacity and community-level consequences.

## Practical implications and a litigation checklist

Jimenez confirms that credible fear and even genuine psychological distress will not automatically equal persecution. To present the strongest possible claim where persecution is alleged but physical injury is absent, counsel should assemble a record that attends to both the objective and subjective elements of persecution:

1. Corroborate threats and confrontations: contemporaneous police reports, factory logs, witness affidavits, medical or school records documenting incidents involving children, photographs, or communications (texts, brochures).
2. Demonstrate persecutor capability and propensity: evidence that the group has engaged in kidnappings, murders, or control of territory; local press accounts or country reports showing similar attacks on political activists.
3. Document psychological harm: evaluations and diagnoses from licensed mental-health professionals, records of counseling or medication, evidence of functional impairment (inability to work, attend school, or care for children).
4. Show pattern and escalation: establish chronology demonstrating repetition, escalation in severity, and lack of effective remedy.
5. Report to authorities and document attempts to secure protection: police reports, record of contacts with prosecutors, and evidence that reporting was futile or dangerous if reporting did not occur.
6. Demonstrate nexus where required: connect the persecutor’s statements or conduct to the applicant’s protected characteristic (e.g., political activity), and counter alternative motives that might explain the mistreatment.

Conclusion

Persecution is a high-threshold claim with important remedial consequences. Jimenez demonstrates that even an assortment of threats, disturbing incidents, and attendant psychological harms may not be enough in the Tenth Circuit absent stronger corroboration of severity, demonstrable capability to carry out threats, or convincing clinical evidence of debilitating psychological injury. Adjudicators assess harms cumulatively, but the aggregated misconduct must amount to suffering "regarded as offensive" in light of the statutory purposes of asylum. For law students and practitioners the central tasks are doctrinally familiar—focus on severity, nexus, and corroboration—and factually exacting: assemble credible, contemporaneous, and clinical evidence to bridge the gap between alarming harassment and legally cognizable persecution.
