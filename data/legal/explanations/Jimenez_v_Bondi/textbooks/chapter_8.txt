Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Jimenez v. Bondi

Chapter 8: # Case Study: Canon Jimenez v. Bondi (10th Cir. 2025)

This chapter uses the Tenth Circuit’s decision in Canon Jimenez v. Bondi to examine how federal appellate courts apply the key elements of asylum and withholding law: past persecution, nexus to a protected ground, government unwillingness or inability to protect, and internal relocation. The court denied review of the Board of Immigration Appeals’ (BIA) affirmance of the Immigration Judge (IJ), concluding that substantial evidence supported the agency’s findings that the petitioners had not suffered persecution, that their mistreatment was not on account of a protected ground (political opinion), and that safe internal relocation and state protection were realistically available. The court’s analysis provides a compact example of how courts evaluate cumulative threats and psychological harm, mixed-motive claims, the importance of reporting to authorities, and the interaction between asylum and withholding claims.

## Factual narrative

The petitioners are a Colombian family—Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez, his wife Angelica Parada Duarte, and their two young sons—who entered the United States in late 2022 and applied for asylum and withholding of removal. The facts the IJ and BIA credited, and the Tenth Circuit relied upon, are straightforward: the family had been active in local politics as members of the Colombian Liberal Party and canvassed for a presidential candidate in 2022. Separate from that political activity, Jimenez (a provisional lawyer and law student who had interned at the national prosecutor’s office) became involved in a civil dispute on behalf of a client seeking to reclaim rental property. The tenant turned out to be a man known as “Don Rafa,” described in the record as a leader of a narco-trafficking group with a local power base and with reported ties to prostitution, migrant day‑rental activities, and violent crime.

Beginning in late spring 2022 Jimenez received recurring threatening phone calls about his party activity. The threats escalated after his unsuccessful efforts concerning the property associated with Don Rafa. The family received an anonymous brochure reading “death to snitches from Liberal Party,” unknown assailants twice accosted Jimenez at the factory where the family worked—once holding him at gunpoint and ordering him to leave the neighborhood—and assailants later assaulted a factory employee while seeking the family. In a particularly alarming incident, a stranger put his arm around the petitioners’ younger son as he left school, placed a hand over the boy’s mouth, and told him to “run to your dad.” The family temporarily moved to Jimenez’s mother’s home in the countryside and ultimately fled to the United States after being told by a police officer that “nothing could be done” and that they should leave.

These facts raise a mix of criminal retaliation-type conduct (the property dispute and alleged threats by narco-affiliates) and politically connected activities (campaigning for a presidential candidate and membership in a political party). The legal question posed by the petitioners’ narrative is whether their mistreatment—and the risk of future harm—is persecution on account of a protected ground, whether the state is unable or unwilling to protect them, and whether they could avoid harm by relocating internally.

## Procedural history and posture

Procedurally, the petitioners were charged with removability, admitted the allegations of the Notices to Appear, and sought asylum and withholding of removal (they did not press Convention Against Torture protection on appeal). The IJ found the petitioners credible but denied relief: the IJ concluded that (1) the harm did not rise to past persecution; (2) there was no nexus to a protected ground (political opinion or a claimed particular social group); (3) the Colombian government was not shown to be unwilling or unable to protect them; and (4) internal relocation was feasible. The BIA affirmed on the same grounds, and the petitioners appealed only the asylum and withholding denials. The Tenth Circuit reviewed the BIA’s decision under the substantial-evidence standard and denied the petition for review.

This procedural posture matters: the IJ’s credibility findings were not disturbed, so the dispute turned on legal and mixed-fact issues—how to assess cumulative threats and psychological harm for persecution, how to determine motive (nexus), and how to weigh evidence of state response and relocation feasibility. Because the IJ and BIA are factfinders, the appellate court’s role was limited: it must affirm unless no reasonable adjudicator could have reached the agency’s conclusions.

## Persecution analysis: threats and psychological distress assessed cumulatively yet found insufficient

The court began with the threshold question for asylum through a past‑persecution route: did the petitioners suffer persecution? Per the statute and precedent, a refugee must be unable or unwilling to return because of persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution on account of a protected ground. A showing of past persecution gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of future persecution; conversely, a failure to show past persecution means the applicant must demonstrate a well‑founded fear of future persecution without the benefit of that presumption.

The term “persecution” is not statutorily defined, and the Tenth Circuit follows precedents requiring an “infliction of suffering or harm . . . in a way regarded as offensive” and more than mere “restrictions or threats to life and liberty.” The court treats incidents cumulatively—multiple non‑severe harms can, in combination, amount to persecution—but also recognizes that threats standing alone rarely meet that threshold. In Canon Jimenez, the court accepted that the petitioners experienced serious and frightening conduct (continuing threatening calls, a brochure threatening “death to snitches,” two armed episodes, an assault of an employee seeking the petitioners, and a child‑targeted intimidation). The IJ found the petitioners credible and the court accepted that finding.

Despite this accumulation, the Tenth Circuit concluded that substantial evidence supported the agency’s determination that the conduct did not amount to persecution. The critical points were that the petitioners conceded they suffered no physical injury and that the traumatic effects—sleep disturbance, panic attacks—while real, were not the type of severe, lasting physical or psychological harm that the court’s precedents treat as sufficient in the absence of physical harm. The court relied on its prior decisions refusing to equate threats (even repeated, serious ones) and consequent emotional distress with persecution when the record lacks more invasive or sustained physical harm or other indicia of systematic, severe mistreatment. Thus, this case illustrates how courts evaluate psychological harm: documentation of diagnosis, treatment, or functional impairment can matter, but generalized fear and stress following threats are frequently held insufficient unless accompanied by additional severe acts or systemic targeting.

Two pedagogical points follow. First, cumulative analysis matters: a single threat may not be persecutory, but repeated, escalating actions can be. Second, applicants who rely on psychological injury should support their claims with contemporaneous evidence—medical or psychological records, expert testimony, or objective corroboration—that shows the harm’s severity and lasting impact. Absent that, appellate courts are likely to defer to the agency’s finding that threats plus stress do not meet the persecution standard.

## Nexus analysis: criminal/professional conflict as the primary motive; political opinion deemed tangential

Nexus—the requirement that persecution be “on account of” a protected ground—is often litigated when persecutors may have multiple motives. The statutory and precedential test requires an applicant to show that a protected ground was “at least one central reason” for the harm. The court’s approach in Canon Jimenez demonstrates how motive is inferred from the totality of evidence: threats’ content, timing relative to political activity, any statements by the persecutors linking the attacks to a political stance, and whether the persecution escalated after non‑political conduct.

Here, the court emphasized several facts that undercut a political nexus. The petitioners had been politically active earlier and had received some early threats, but the more serious, escalated threats and episodes occurred after Jimenez became involved in the property dispute with the tenant identified as Don Rafa. The assailants explicitly accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and blamed him for searches of the property. One of the brochures referred to “snitches from Liberal Party,” but the timing and the specific accusations by the attackers focused on alleged interference with Don Rafa’s business and security. The IJ and BIA concluded, and the Tenth Circuit agreed, that these facts show the primary motive was retaliation for Jimenez’s professional/personal conflict with a criminal actor, not political opinion. The political dimension—party membership and campaigning—was therefore “tangential and subordinate.”

The court also reiterated the mixed‑motive doctrine: an attacker can have multiple motives, but an applicant must show the protected ground was a central reason. Evidence that proves mixed motives tends to include direct statements by perpetrators (e.g., “we hate you for your support of X”), contemporaneous documents or threats linking the harm to the protected ground, or a context in which only those holding the protected attribute are targeted. In mixed‑motive cases, the analysis turns on which motive predominates, and the agency’s fact determination will be upheld if reasonable. In Canon Jimenez, the timing, the attackers’ statements, and the property‑specific escalation pointed toward private retaliation as the central motive.

For practitioners, this component of the case underscores the importance of eliciting and preserving evidence of the persecutors’ statements, threat language, and contemporaneous documents tying conduct to political activity. If a persecutor mentions politics, that strengthens nexus; if a persecutor’s statements relate to criminal retaliation, nexus to a protected ground becomes harder to establish.

## Government protection: minimal reporting; evidence of police action undermines claims of state complicity

For asylum based on persecution by private actors, an applicant must show the harm is committed by the government or that the government is unwilling or unable to control the persecutors. The Tenth Circuit in Canon Jimenez applied the familiar two‑part inquiry: (1) whether the government attempted to stop the persecutors or otherwise responded to complaints, and (2) whether the applicant sought and received protection or whether reporting would have been futile or dangerous.

The petitioners argued the state was complicit with persecutors or at least unwilling/unable to protect them, pointing to an officer’s discouraging response when Jimenez reported the incident and to political ties allegedly connecting Don Rafa to national actors. The agency concluded, and the court agreed, that the petitioners’ reports to authorities were minimal: they did not pursue complaints beyond a couple of officer interactions, and they did not produce police reports or evidence of systemic state complicity. Crucially, the record actually contained evidence that law enforcement had conducted searches and seizures at the property associated with Don Rafa, undermining the claim that the state was powerless or complicit. The court emphasized that a single discouraging conversation with an officer is not necessarily proof of systemic failure, and absent a showing that reporting would have been futile, applicants are generally expected to seek state protection.

This part of the decision highlights a central evidentiary rule: reporting to authorities matters. While courts recognize a futility exception—where applicants show that reporting would be ineffectual or dangerous because of state collusion—establishing futility requires evidence beyond the applicant’s subjective belief. Contemporaneous complaints, police reports showing refusal to investigate, affidavits showing threats by officials, country‑condition evidence of pervasive collusion, or prior unsuccessful complaints by similarly situated persons can all be persuasive.

In Canon Jimenez, the evidence of police investigative efforts against the property and the lack of comprehensive reporting by the petitioners left the agency free to conclude the government was neither unwilling nor unable to protect them. The court gave weight to the agency’s inference that, had petitioners sought help more forcefully or at higher levels, they might have obtained protection.

## Internal relocation: temporary move and family in-country support weighed against fear of persecution

The government may rebut a well-founded fear of future persecution by showing that internal relocation within the applicant’s home country is reasonable and safe. The two-prong test requires the government to establish either a fundamental change in circumstances or that the applicant could safely relocate.

The Tenth Circuit found substantial evidence supporting the agency’s determination that the petitioners could relocate within Colombia. The factual bases included the petitioners’ ability to move temporarily to Jimenez’s mother’s home in the Bogota countryside without incident, the presence of unharmed relatives in Colombia (including Jimenez’s father who continued to work with the criminal justice system), and the localized nature of the threats (the men told them to move out of the neighborhood rather than threatening them anywhere in the country). These facts suggested relocation was a realistic option and that the risk of harm was not nationwide or inescapable.

Relocation reasonableness depends on multiple considerations: the applicant’s age and health, financial resources, familial ties, evidence of the persecutor’s geographic reach, and country conditions. The Canon Jimenez decision illustrates that courts will consider whether the applicant actually attempted to relocate domestically or whether the record shows that others in similar circumstances live safely in other parts of the country. Here, the petitioners’ successful short‑term relocation bolstered the agency’s finding that relocation could mitigate risk.

For practitioners, when litigating relocation, it is important to document reasons why relocation would be dangerous or unworkable (e.g., pervasive national reach of perpetrators, family or economic ties making resettlement impossible, lack of access to livelihoods, or discriminatory practices in other regions). Conversely, if an applicant can show members of the same social or political group have been targeted nationwide, relocation is less likely to be considered feasible.

## Withholding claim’s dependency on asylum findings (higher standard)

Withholding of removal requires the applicant to prove a “clear probability” of persecution upon return—a higher standard than the “well‑founded fear” required for asylum. The Tenth Circuit reaffirmed the settled rule that failure to meet the asylum standard necessarily undermines withholding relief because an applicant who cannot satisfy the lower standard ordinarily cannot satisfy the higher burden. Thus, the court denied withholding as a corollary of its asylum ruling.

This point is doctrinally straightforward but practically important: asylum is both discretionary (if the statutory elements are met, relief is possible but not guaranteed) and uses the lower standard of proof. Because withholding provides mandatory protection only on a showing of a high likelihood of persecution, successful withholding claims typically require stronger factual records (more direct evidence of physical harm, systemic targeting, or unrebuttable country‑condition proof).

## What additional evidence might have altered the outcome

The outcome in Canon Jimenez turned on factual determinations—degree of harm, motive, government responsiveness, and feasibility of relocation—so different or stronger evidence could have produced a different result. The kinds of evidence that might have changed the adjudication include, but are not limited to, the following:

- Contemporaneous police reports or written complaints showing the petitioners reported threats and detailing official responses (or official refusals to act). Formal documentation of a complaint and a demonstrable refusal to investigate would bolster a claim of state unwillingness or inability to protect.

- Medical and mental‑health records diagnosing post‑traumatic stress disorder or other conditions directly linked to the incidents, together with expert testimony establishing the severity and expected persistence of psychological harm. Demonstrable, clinically significant psychological injury can convert threats-plus-stress into persecution in the right factual mix.

- Evidence of physical harm or more invasive attacks (assault with injury, abduction attempts, prolonged forced detention, torture, or forced disappearance) rather than threats and intimidation alone. Physical injuries or egregious mistreatment are more likely to cross the persecution threshold.

- Direct statements, messages, or documentation showing that the attackers targeted petitioners explicitly because of their political activity (e.g., threats that named the specific political activity and linked it to the reason for harm). Clear contemporaneous evidence that persecutors referred to political views or party membership as reasons for threats would strengthen nexus.

- Affidavits or testimony from witnesses (neighbors, employees, other political activists) that corroborate targeted political harassment or show a pattern of political actors being singled out, or evidence that other known Liberal Party members were attacked under similar circumstances.

- Country‑condition evidence showing systematic collusion between the government and the particular criminal group, or a pattern of police refusal to investigate crimes associated with that group. Reports from reputable NGOs, human-rights organizations, or governmental sources documenting state complicity in the persecutor’s region could support a futility claim.

- Documentation showing the perpetrator’s national reach or evidence that moving to another part of Colombia would not avoid the risk (e.g., affidavits that the perpetrators have connections throughout the country, incidents of cross‑regional attacks, or proof that the threats were not geographically limited).

- Proof that petitioners sought protection at higher levels (prosecutor’s office, national police headquarters) and were rebuffed, or that prior complaints by other victims were ignored or resulted in reprisals, demonstrating systemic inability to protect.

Collectively, this sort of corroborative, contemporaneous, and expert evidence reduces judicial reliance on demeanor and subjective testimony and makes it harder for an adjudicator to conclude that threats are merely localized criminal retaliation or that state protection was available.

## Practical takeaways for advocates and students

Canon Jimenez is a compact demonstration of familiar asylum law patterns and evidentiary weaknesses that frequently determine outcomes:

- Credibility is necessary but not sufficient. Even fully credible testimony will fail if the record lacks the particular legal elements—persecution, nexus, and government failure.

- Threats without physical injury can be persecutory, but bare threats + generalized fear often fail. Document and corroborate psychological injury and any physical incidents.

- Nexus is a fact‑intensive, motive inquiry; contemporaneous statements by persecutors and timing of escalation are crucial. Where criminal retaliation and political activity coexist, focus on evidence tying the harm to the protected ground.

- Report to authorities whenever safe and feasible; contemporaneous police reports and documentation of official inaction are powerful evidence of state inability or unwillingness to protect.

- Collect country‑condition materials and witness statements about state‑perpetrator relationships and the reach of criminal actors; these can support futility claims and rebut relocation arguments.

- Internal relocation arguments are fact sensitive. Show why relocation is not reasonable if you want to rebut the government’s relocation defense: economic barriers, lack of family or community support, occupational licensing barriers, or pervasive reach of persecutors can matter.

Finally, students should note how appellate deference to the agency’s factual findings operates here. The Tenth Circuit repeatedly emphasized the substantial‑evidence standard: so long as reasonable adjudicators could reach the IJ’s/BIA’s conclusions on persecution, nexus, and protection, the court will not substitute its view. That underscores the importance at the administrative stage of building a comprehensive, well‑documented record that anticipates and answers the agency’s questions about motive, harm severity, reporting, and relocation.
