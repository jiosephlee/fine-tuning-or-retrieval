Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Jimenez v. Bondi

Chapter 13: # Significance and Doctrinal Trajectory of Canon Jimenez

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Canon Jimenez v. Bondi reiterates core asylum doctrines while sharpening practical rules for litigants whose claims rest on threats, mixed motives, and government protection. The panel denied asylum and withholding of removal to a Colombian family whose leader, a provisional lawyer and political canvasser, received repeated threats and experienced a series of menacing encounters after confronting the tenant of a client’s property—an individual tied to a local narco-trafficking boss. The court affirmed the Board of Immigration Appeals’ conclusions that (1) the conduct alleged did not rise to past persecution; (2) the petitioners failed to prove that political opinion was at least one central reason for the mistreatment; (3) they did not show the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect them; and (4) internal relocation in Colombia was reasonable. That framework embodies familiar Tenth Circuit rules, but the opinion also supplies practical guidance on evidentiary priorities and highlights several contested doctrinal contours likely to shape future litigation.

Below we analyze the opinion’s doctrinal holdings, their fit in circuit precedent, and the takeaways for advocates and adjudicators.

## Reaffirmation of a High Bar for Threats-Only Persecution in the Tenth Circuit

Canon Jimenez reaffirms the Tenth Circuit’s longstanding principle that threats, even when repeated and terrifying, rarely suffice on their own to establish “past persecution.” The court reiterated that persecution requires “the infliction of suffering or harm … that must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty,” and that claims grounded only in threats or in transient emotional harms generally fail the persecution prong. The opinion follows prior Tenth Circuit decisions—Karki, Chhetri, Brandy, and others—that decline asylum relief where the record shows threats, harassment, or short-term detention without enduring physical injury, permanent disability, or comparable severity.

The practical consequences are twofold. First, litigants should treat “threats-only” fact patterns as an uphill battle in this circuit. Counsel must present rich, concrete evidence to show that the threats produced harm of the kind historically understood as persecution: severe physical harm, prolonged detention with mistreatment, forced displacement, or demonstrable long-term disabling psychological injury corroborated by expert evidence. Second, the court’s emphasis on cumulative analysis (recognizing that multiple incidents may combine to amount to persecution) does not displace the underlying requirement of objectively severe harm; repeated threats must be shown to have produced substantial, enduring harm or been part of a pattern of objectively violent, coercive conduct to clear the threshold.

For practitioners that retain these claims, the lesson is clear: do not rely on the rhetorical force of threats alone. Augment threats with corroboration of physical incidents, injuries, credible medical/psychological evaluations showing diagnosis and causation, or evidence that threats were coupled with extortion, forced exile, or similar harms that collectively create an objectively intolerable environment.

## Clarifying Mixed-Motive Analysis Where Criminal/Economic Motives Predominate

A central legal issue in Canon Jimenez was whether political opinion was “at least one central reason” for the targeting—a statutory requirement for asylum. The Tenth Circuit reaffirmed the “one central reason” standard and applied it in a mixed-motive context where criminal or economic motives were plausibly primary. The court accepted that persecutors often have multiple motives, but emphasized that in mixed-motive cases the protected ground must be a central, not incidental, reason for the mistreatment.

The facts were determinative: Petitioners faced escalated threats only after the principal (Jimenez) intervened in a property dispute that threatened a narco-trafficker’s economic interests and the security of his illicit operations. Threats linked to the property dispute, explicit references to “snitching” and involvement with prosecutors, and the timing of escalating harassment supported the BIA’s finding that economic and security motives predominated over political animus. The court therefore credited a non-political explanation as the principal driver of harm.

Doctrinally, Canon Jimenez clarifies that where criminal actors can point to proximate, non-political triggers (for example, direct interference with revenue streams, eviction conflicts, or allegations of informing on criminal operations), adjudicators will generally accept criminal/economic motives as the central reason unless the applicant adduces specific evidence tying the persecutor’s conduct to political opposition. For applicants, the practical inference is straightforward: when criminal or pecuniary motives exist, counsel must develop affirmative, case-specific evidence showing that the persecutor was responding to political activity—statements tying the harassment to campaign work, contemporaneous documentation of threats referencing political labels, reliable witness testimony that the persecutor targeted the applicant for political reasons, or pattern evidence showing the persecutor targeted political actors beyond mere economic complaints.

## Emphasis on Reporting and Evidentiary Development for the ‘Unable or Unwilling’ Prong

A critical portion of the court’s analysis addressed whether Colombia was “unable or unwilling” to control the persecutors. Canon Jimenez underscores two evidentiary touchstones that often decide this inquiry in the Tenth Circuit: (1) whether the government had notice and an opportunity to act (normally demonstrated by formal reporting to law enforcement), and (2) whether the government actually took steps to stop the persecutors or offer protection.

The opinion criticized Petitioners for scant efforts to seek redress beyond isolated conversations with individual officers who expressed apathy. The court observed that one-off, uncorroborated encounters with unhelpful officers do not necessarily indicate systemic inability or unwillingness. Conversely, the record showed that authorities had investigated the contested property and undertaken searches and seizures—facts that undercut a claim that the state was complicit or powerless. The court thus treated reporting (and the related documentation) as a pivotal piece of the puzzle.

For litigators, Canon Jimenez sends several practical instructions. Counsel should aggressively document any contact with authorities: obtain police reports, complaints, reference or case numbers; preserve records of calls, emails, or official correspondence; secure statements from government officials if available; and, if reporting would have been futile, develop contemporaneous evidence to support futility—examples include threats of retaliation for reporting, prior complaints that led to retaliatory harm, or credible evidence that law enforcement is collusive with persecutors. Country-condition materials and expert testimony become important when asserting systemic corruption or complicity: detailed reports showing pervasive impunity, patterns of collusion between criminal groups and officials, or credible accounts of retaliation against those who report can help transform an individual officer’s apathy into persuasive evidence of systemic failure.

The opinion also demonstrates that alleging government inability or unwillingness without concrete, corroborative evidence will likely fail. Attorneys should therefore treat the government-protection prong as an evidentiary exercise: identify the specific governmental actors who could have protected the applicant, document the applicant’s attempts to inform those actors (or explain why attempts would have been futile), and supply independent corroboration of governmental dysfunction when necessary.

## Internal Relocation as a Practical Safety Valve in Neighborhood-Confined Threats

Canon Jimenez reiterates the government’s ability to rebut a well-founded fear of persecution by showing internal relocation within the country would be reasonable and safe. The court observed that the threats were localized—confined to the neighborhood around the factory and the contested property—and that the family had safely lived with relatives in the Bogota countryside for several weeks prior to departure. The presence of family ties, a history of safe temporary relocation, and no evidence that the persecutors sought to remove the family from the country supported the BIA’s finding that internal relocation was a feasible alternative.

This aspect of the opinion highlights the practical contours of the relocation analysis. Adjudicators assess the reasonableness of relocation by considering safety, access to livelihood, the applicant’s age and health, family ties, and whether the persecutor has reach beyond a particular locale. Localized threats—especially those where persecutors threaten only that the applicant should leave the neighborhood, not the country—weaken asylum claims if the state provides reasonably safe options elsewhere.

For practitioners: when forced to confront a relocation defense, build a record showing why relocation is not reasonable—evidence of national-level reach of persecutors, inability to secure housing or employment elsewhere, threats that explicitly apply nationally or are linked to mobile forms of persecution, or country reports showing pervasive targeting that undermines the plausibility of safe relocation. Conversely, if an applicant has already relocated within the country and remained safe, admit that as strong evidence that relocation is reasonable.

## Implications for Political Canvassers and Community Advocates Entangled with Criminal Groups

Canon Jimenez has immediate implications for political canvassers, community organizers, and lawyers who perform advocacy in environments where criminal actors exert control. The opinion demonstrates that so long as a persecutor can show a proximate, non-political reason for targeting (for example, protecting illicit revenue, retaliating for a property challenge, or preventing law enforcement action), an asylum claim premised on political persecution will face significant headwinds.

Advocates working in such contexts should pursue two parallel litigation strategies. First, if the persecution arises from political activity (campaigning, opposition organizing, public denunciation of criminal groups tied to political actors), develop clear, contemporaneous evidence that persecutors explicitly targeted the applicant for the political conduct: threats that reference the candidate, statements linking the harassment to campaign activity, contemporaneous witness statements, and documentation that the persecutor has targeted others specifically because of political activities. Second, if the danger is more plausibly economic or criminal, consider alternate asylum theories—particular social group (family, community leaders, etc.)—or CAT claims where the state’s complicity rises to the level of torture risk. Be mindful that the Tenth Circuit scrutinizes social-group claims and will require that the group be cognizable under prevailing test elements (immutability, social visibility, and particularity).

Canon Jimenez also signals caution for those whose political work overlaps with law-enforcement functions (e.g., being a prosecutor’s intern). When persecutors view an individual as an agent of law enforcement, persecutors may couch threats as retaliation for “snitching” or prosecutorial work—motivations that courts may characterize as criminal rather than overtly political. Counsel should therefore anticipate the mixed-motive inquiry and proactively develop evidence tying the persecutor’s animus to protected political viewpoints rather than merely to perceived law enforcement activity.

## Open Questions and Areas for Further Development

Although Canon Jimenez clarifies several issues, it leaves important open questions that will be litigated in subsequent cases.

- Imputed Political Opinion. The opinion did not fully explore whether the persecutors imputed a political opinion to the applicants based on their campaign involvement or association with the prosecutor’s office. Courts have recognized imputed political opinion claims where persecutors punish persons because they are perceived to hold a political view. Future cases will need to delineate when mere association (campaigning in a contested neighborhood, working briefly with a prosecutor) suffices for an imputation finding—particularly when the persecutor’s stated reasons reference anti-police or anti-snitch sentiments rather than explicit political labels.

- Proof of Systemic Corruption or State Complicity. Canon Jimenez demonstrates the Tenth Circuit’s demand for concrete evidence when applicants assert state inability or unwillingness. But what quantum and type of proof suffices to show systemic corruption? The court suggested that isolated encounters with unhelpful officers are insufficient; at the same time, it did not set a bright-line rule for when reports and country-conditions evidence overcome that burden. Future decisions will need to clarify thresholds for expert testimony, independent investigations, or detailed country reports to meaningfully prove systemic failure or complicity.

- Child-Focused Claims and Psychological Harm. Petitioners invoked children’s panic attacks and parental sleep deprivation, but the court found psychological harms insufficient without more. The opinion leaves open how courts should evaluate children’s psychological injuries in asylum claims—what kind of mental-health documentation, longitudinal therapy records, school reports, or expert testimony would convincingly demonstrate long-term, debilitating consequences rising to persecution. This area is particularly salient because threats directed at children or acts that impede a child’s safe education may warrant different treatment in future cases.

- Particular Social Group Theories. Petitioners did not press a particular social group argument here, and the court declined to analyze that theory. Given the evolving jurisprudence on family- and community-based PSGs—especially where crime groups target “social leaders” or “community advocates”—future litigation will likely revisit whether community leaders or canvassers constitute cognizable PSGs and how those groups overlap with political-opinion claims.

- Government Actors and Criminal-Political Alliances. The canonical tension between criminal groups’ independence and their political alignments remains unsettled. Canon Jimenez weighed evidence that the government executed searches and was not complicit—but many other cases will involve less clear-cut evidence and allegations of closer ties between criminals and state officials. Courts will continue to sort when criminal actors’ political alignments convert a private actor’s harms into state-action or when such alignments suffice to show state unwillingness.

## Practical Checklist for Litigators (What to Develop in the Record)

Canon Jimenez highlights several concrete pieces of evidence that frequently decide asylum claims in similar circumstances. Litigators should, early and deliberately, seek to develop the following:

- Documented reports to police or prosecutors (official complaint numbers, copies of reports, follow-up correspondence). If reporting did not occur, contemporaneous reasons (threats asserting retaliation for reporting, prior failed reports) and corroboration.

- Medical and psychological records that (a) diagnose specific conditions and (b) link causally the applicant’s symptoms to the alleged persecution. For children, school or counseling records indicating persistent functional impairment.

- Witness statements and affidavits from coworkers, neighbors, or family who observed threats, assaults, or the persecutor’s statements tying the mistreatment to political activity.

- Contemporaneous documentary evidence connecting the persecutor to the alleged criminal or political organization (news articles, police incident reports, municipal records showing enforcement actions at the property).

- Country-condition and expert evidence on systemic corruption, police performance, and the reach of criminal groups—tailored to the applicant’s locality and the persecutor’s network.

- Evidence of internal relocation attempts or success (temporary stays with relatives or moves to other parts of the country without incident).

- Strategic articulation of nexus: frame whether the claim rests on the applicant’s own political opinion, an imputed opinion, or membership in a particular social group—and develop evidence specific to that theory.

Canon Jimenez teaches that litigation is won or lost in evidentiary development. Courts will credit well-documented, specific showings over generalized or speculative assertions.

## Conclusion

Canon Jimenez should be read as an exemplar of the Tenth Circuit’s careful, evidence-driven approach to asylum law. The decision confirms that (1) threats alone rarely constitute past persecution in the Tenth Circuit; (2) mixed motives require the protected ground to be a central reason for targeting; (3) applicants must develop a factual record showing the government had notice and failed to act, or that reporting would have been futile; and (4) internal relocation remains an important, and often decisive, consideration where threats are localized. For practitioners, the case underscores the premium on early, thorough evidentiary development—police reports, medical and psychological documentation, corroborating witness statements, and persuasive country-condition proof. For the doctrine, Canon Jimenez narrows certain avenues for relief while leaving pivotal questions—about imputed political opinion, the thresholds for demonstrating systemic corruption, and the evidentiary proof needed for child-focused psychological claims—ripe for future development.
