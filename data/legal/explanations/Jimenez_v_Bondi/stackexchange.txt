Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Jimenez v. Bondi

### How does § 1252 give the Tenth Circuit jurisdiction here, and what exactly is encompassed by “final orders of removal” for consolidated review?
Question:
The panel says it exercises jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252 and cites § 1252(a)(1) and (b)(9) for consolidated review of asylum claims. I'm confused about the scope of § 1252(b)(9). Why is this petition characterized as a review of a “final order of removal,” and how does that allow the court to consider the asylum denial alongside it? Also, are there any jurisdictional limits (like the criminal alien bar or exhaustion requirements) that the court implicitly found satisfied, and how does that analysis typically get done when it isn’t spelled out?
Answer:
Short answer
- The Tenth Circuit exercised jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252 because the Board of Immigration Appeals affirmed an IJ’s removal order, producing a “final order of removal,” and Petitioners challenged the BIA’s denial of asylum/withholding as part of that final order. The panel relied on § 1252(a)(1) and the “consolidated review” provision, § 1252(b)(9), which channels judicial review of claims arising from final removal orders to the court of appeals. (See Opinion I–II.A, citing Singh and § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9).)

- “Final orders of removal” for consolidated review include the BIA’s affirmance (or dismissal) of an IJ’s removal order together with the underlying decisions on asylum/withholding that formed the basis for removal. The opinion makes clear the court’s review is limited to the administrative record on which that final order is based (§ 1252(b)(4)(A)), and that factual findings are reviewed under the substantial‑evidence standard. (See II.A and citation to § 1252(b)(4)(A); II.A last paragraph.)

Why § 1252 applies here (what the opinion does)
- The procedural posture: DHS issued Notices to Appear, Petitioners admitted the allegations, the IJ ordered removal and denied asylum/withholding, the BIA affirmed, and Petitioners sought review in this court. The BIA’s affirmance converted the IJ decision into a “final order of removal.” The panel therefore had jurisdiction under § 1252 to review the BIA’s decision denying asylum and withholding because those denial decisions were part of the final order. (See Opinion, Procedural Background; II. Discussion opening.)

What “consolidated review” means in practice (from the opinion)
- Section 1252(b)(9) channels review of claims that are “inextricably intertwined” with a final order of removal into the court of appeals, so the court reviews the BIA’s (and IJ’s) determinations that produced the removal order. The opinion explicitly states: “We generally have jurisdiction to review final orders of removal, which can include a consolidated review of asylum claims.” It then explains the limits of that review: (1) the review is confined to the administrative record (§ 1252(b)(4)(A)); and (2) factual findings are tested under the substantial‑evidence standard. (See II.A and citations to Singh and § 1252(b)(4)(A); Rivera‑Barrientos citation.)

How that allowed the court to consider the asylum denial
- Because the BIA’s affirmance of the IJ’s denial was part of the final order of removal, § 1252(b)(9) permitted the court to review those merits determinations as part of the consolidated review of the final order. The panel thus reviewed whether the BIA/IJ erred on the asylum elements (past persecution, nexus, government protection, internal relocation), applying the usual administrative‑law constraints (administrative record, substantial‑evidence review). (See II.A; II.B–F.)

Jurisdictional limits (criminal‑alien bar, exhaustion, discretionary review) — what the opinion shows and what it doesn’t
- The opinion applies the ordinary limits that follow from § 1252 and the administrative record rule: review is of the BIA’s decision and the administrative record, with factual findings upheld unless no reasonable adjudicator could agree. (See II.A and citations.)
- The opinion does not discuss a criminal‑alien bar or statutory exhaustion in depth because those issues were not in the posture of this case. Relevant indicators in the opinion:
  - Petitioners admitted removability and sought asylum/withholding as forms of relief; they appealed the IJ’s denial to the BIA and then to this court. That procedural path shows the typical exhaustion requirement (appeal to BIA) was satisfied here — the panel reviews what the BIA actually decided. (See Procedural Background; II.A.)
  - The panel never invoked any statutory bar (e.g., criminal‑alien bars) to jurisdiction or relief, nor did it state any jurisdictional defect. Instead, it addressed the merits elements under the substantial‑evidence standard. That implies the court treated jurisdictional prerequisites as met or not at issue in this case.
- In short: when the court does not explicitly discuss jurisdictional bars, you should read the opinion as indicating the matter was not raised or was resolved below (e.g., removability admitted, BIA appealed), so the court proceeded to consolidated review of the merits. The opinion’s focus on substantial‑evidence review of the BIA’s factual findings (II.A, II.C–F) shows the review framework it applied.

How courts typically handle these issues when not spelled out
- Courts usually follow a short checklist before reaching merits:
  1. Is there a final order of removal? (BIA affirmance/dismissal of IJ order → yes.)
  2. Were the issues presented to and decided by the agency (BIA/IJ)? (If yes, the court will review those issues; if not, jurisdiction may be limited by § 1252(d) or exhaustion doctrines.) Here Petitioners did appeal asylum/withholding to the BIA, so the court could review them. (See Procedural Background; II.A.)
  3. Are there statutory limits on review for the particular claims? (If a statutory bar applies, the court will either dismiss for lack of jurisdiction or limit review. The panel did not identify such a bar here.)
  4. Apply the appropriate standard (e.g., questions of law reviewed de novo; factual findings under substantial evidence; discretionary denials typically not disturbed absent abuse). The opinion follows that pattern: it treats asylum as discretionary but reviews factual findings for substantial evidence and legal questions for correctness. (See II.B and II.A.)
- If a party asserts a jurisdictional bar (criminal alien, terrorism bar, etc.), courts typically address that threshold question explicitly before reaching merits. Absence of such discussion normally signals the issue was not presented or the court viewed it as resolved below.

Takeaway for this case
- The Tenth Circuit had jurisdiction under § 1252 because the BIA’s affirmance created a final order of removal and Petitioners’ asylum/withholding claims were part of what made the order “final.” The panel therefore conducted the consolidated review permitted by § 1252(b)(9), limited to the administrative record and under the substantial‑evidence standard for factual findings. Because the opinion shows Petitioners exhausted their administrative remedies (appeal to the BIA) and admitted removability, the court did not need to resolve any separate jurisdictional bars, and it addressed the merits elements directly (II.A, II.C–F).

### Why doesn’t the court discuss Article III standing in petitions for review of removal orders?
Question:
There’s no standing section. Is it because being subject to a final order of removal obviously constitutes an injury-in-fact traceable to the agency and redressable by a court order? In immigration petitions for review, is standing analysis usually omitted as a matter of course, and if so, what are the doctrinal reasons for that practice?
Answer:
Short answer: Because jurisdiction under the Immigration and Nationality Act is statutory and the record shows a concrete, redressable injury (a final removal order), the court treatsthis as a straightforward statutory petition for review under 8 U.S.C. § 1252 and proceeds to the merits. When Article III standing is not in dispute (and the petitioner is plainly subject to a final removal order and seeks reversal), courts routinely assume or subsume standing into the statutoryjurisdictional analysis and do not separately spell out an Article III standing section.

Why that is correct here (doctrinally and on the face of the opinion)

- Statutory jurisdiction is invoked and the injury is obvious. The opinion opens “Exercising jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, we DENY the petition for review.” A petition for review under § 1252 is the statutorily prescribed method for challenging a final order of removal. The petitioners are respondents to removal proceedings (they admitted the DHS allegations and the IJ ordered removal), so they have a concrete interest — the final order of removal — that is directly at stake.

- The three Article III standing elements are satisfied on the record. Standing requires (1) an injury‑in‑fact (concrete, particularized, and actual or imminent), (2) causation (traceable to the defendant), and (3) redressability (a favorable ruling likely to relieve the injury). Here:
  - Injury-in-fact — a final removal order and denial of asylum/withholding is a concrete, particularized injury to the petitioner’s interest in remaining in the U.S. The opinion treats the BIA’s denial as the adjudicated injury to be reviewed.
  - Traceability — the injury is traceable to the agency decision (BIA/IJ) that ordered removal; the petition asks this court to set aside that decision.
  - Redressability — reversal or remand could vacate the removal order or reconsider asylum, which would redress the injury. The court explicitly frames its review as one “to review final orders of removal” (citing § 1252(a)(1), (b)(9)) and evaluates the BIA’s findings under the substantial‑evidence standard.

- Practice in immigration cases. Courts of appeals commonly do not include a freestanding Article III standing section in routine petitions for review where:
  - The petitioner is the person subject to the final removal order (so injury is not contested); and
  - The statutory grant of review and the relief sought (vacatur/reversal of the BIA/IJ decision) plainly meet redressability and traceability. The opinion follows that pattern: after invoking § 1252 jurisdiction, it goes straight to standard of review and the merits (see Parts II.A–II.F). The court would raise standing if there were a doubt (for example, third‑party suits, collateral challengers, or when petitioner lacks a legally protected interest), but no such doubt appears here.

- The court’s treatment in this opinion. The opinion’s structure confirms the approach: it begins “Exercising jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. § 1252” and then discusses the standard of review and substantive asylum elements (see II.A–II.F). Nothing in the opinion suggests the court had a jurisdictional or Article III doubt to resolve; instead it proceeds to apply the statutory framework (asylum, nexus, government protection, relocation) and the substantial‑evidence standard. That is the ordinary course when standing is satisfied on the face of the record.

Conclusion: The court did not need a separate Article III standing discussion because the petitioners — as people subject to a final order of removal seeking review under § 1252 — plainly met Article III’s requirements. In such routine petitions for review, appellate opinions commonly proceed directly to statutory jurisdiction and the merits without a standalone standing section unless standing is contested or unclear.

### Why does the court treat “past persecution” as a factual question reviewed for substantial evidence rather than a mixed question of law and fact?
Question:
The opinion states that whether a noncitizen proved past private persecution is a fact question reviewed under the substantial-evidence standard. But 'persecution' is a legally defined concept (albeit undefined in the statute and fleshed out by case law). Why is the Tenth Circuit reviewing this as a factual finding rather than applying de novo review to at least the legal component? How does this fit with other circuits that characterize persecution or 'nexus' as mixed questions?
Answer:
Short answer: Because the Tenth Circuit (following its own precedent) treats whether an applicant has suffered “past persecution” as the application of a fact‑intensive, context‑dependent legal standard to the specific record of incidents — i.e., a finding that the BIA/IJ is best suited to make — it reviews that determination under the substantial‑evidence standard. The opinion explicitly relies on Tenth Circuit precedent saying the issue is factual and therefore conclusive unless the record would compel a contrary result.

Why the court does this (with textual support from the opinion)

- The opinion states the governing review rule: “We review the BIA’s findings of fact under a substantial‑evidence standard.” (A. Standard of Review.) That is the baseline.
- It then frames the persecution inquiry as a fact problem: “Whether a noncitizen has proved past private persecution in their home country—the overarching issue here—is a fact question.” (C. Past Persecution (quoting Singh, 130 F.4th at 859–60).) The court therefore applies substantial‑evidence review to the persecution determination.
- The opinion explains why persecution determinations are fact‑intensive: persecution is an undefined statutory term fleshed out by caselaw and requires looking at the “cumulative effects of multiple incidents” and whether the harm “entail[s] more than just restrictions or threats” in the particular factual context. (C. Past Persecution; citing Ritonga and Wiransane.) Those are classic fact‑finding inquiries — weighing incidents, their character, and their cumulative impact — not pure legal questions.
- On nexus the court similarly treats motive and the “central reason” inquiry as a factual determination tied to the record: it asks whether the persecutors’ predominant motive was political or instead personal/professional, relies on the sequence of events, who threatened whom and when, and concludes there is “substantial evidence supporting the BIA and IJ’s findings that Petitioners were primarily targeted because Jimenez threatened Don Rafa’s revenue and security, not because of their political opinion.” (D. Nexus.) That shows the court is applying legal standards (e.g., “at least one central reason”) to disputed facts and thus deferring under substantial evidence.

How that fits with other circuits’ characterizations

- The opinion does not re‑write the general law that some aspects of asylum law can be mixed questions (e.g., legal standard vs. factual application). Instead, it follows Tenth Circuit precedent that the ultimate question whether the specific incidents amount to persecution is treated as a factual finding. (The opinion repeatedly cites Tenth Circuit authorities — Singh; Rivera‑Barrientos; Vicente‑Elias — to support that approach.)
- In practice, circuits differ on labeling. Some circuits emphasize that elements like “nexus” or the legal definition of “persecution” involve mixed questions (legal standard + factual application) and review the legal component de novo. The Tenth Circuit here has chosen — consistently, per its precedent cited in the opinion — to treat the contested application (did these incidents rise to persecution? were these actions “on account of” a protected ground?) as findings of fact when made by the IJ/BIA, and so to apply substantial‑evidence review.

Practical consequence (as reflected in the opinion)

- Because the question is reviewed for substantial evidence, petitioners must show the record compels a different factual conclusion — not just that a different reasonable view of the facts is possible. The court applies that standard throughout: it examines the record, credits the IJ’s credibility findings, considers the cumulative incidents, and concludes substantial evidence supported the BIA’s conclusion that the events did not amount to persecution and that the persecutors’ motive was not primarily political. (See C. Past Persecution; D. Nexus.)

Bottom line: The opinion treats “past persecution” as a factual question because Tenth Circuit precedent characterizes the application of the persecution standard to the concrete record as a fact‑finding task (fact‑intensive, cumulative, and judgmental). That is why the court applies the substantial‑evidence standard rather than de novo review of the ultimate persecution determination.

### How does the substantial-evidence standard work when the record supports multiple plausible inferences?
Question:
The panel repeatedly says there is 'substantial evidence' for the BIA’s findings. I struggle with how to evaluate this: if the record would allow both the petitioners’ inference (political motive) and the agency’s inference (retaliation for a property dispute), is the court’s role simply to ask whether a reasonable adjudicator could choose the agency’s view, even if the court might pick the other? What does it mean for evidence to be so strong that 'any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled' to reach the opposite conclusion?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — under the substantial‑evidence standard the court’s role is limited: it asks whether the administrative record permits the BIA’s factual findings, and the BIA’s findings must be upheld unless the record is such that “any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.” That is a demanding test: where the record supports two plausible inferences, the court does not substitute its preferred inference for the agency’s. Only if the opposite inference is so clearly required by the evidence that no reasonable factfinder could accept the agency’s view will the court overturn the BIA.

How the doctrine works (with citations to the opinion)
- The governing test. The opinion states the well‑known standard: “We review the BIA’s findings of fact under a substantial‑evidence standard. Under this standard, the BIA’s findings of fact are conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.” (A. Standard of Review (quoting Rivera‑Barrientos), page referencing that section.)
- Court’s limited role. The opinion repeats the principle that the court “is not our prerogative to reweigh the evidence, but only to decide if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s [or the BIA’s] decision.” (Nexus section, quoting Yuk v. Ashcroft.) That reflects the point that when the record allows multiple plausible inferences, deference mandates upholding the agency so long as its inference is reasonable.
- What “compelled” means in practice. The opinion illustrates the practical boundary of the test in two ways:
  - On persecution severity: the court upheld the BIA where the record could show threats and psychological harm but not the level of harm the court’s precedents treat as persecution — i.e., although harms were “physically stressful and psychologically unsettling,” prior cases and the record supported the agency’s conclusion that threats alone (and the particular emotional harms here) did not constitute persecution. (C. Past Persecution.)
  - On nexus/mixed motives: the opinion explains that even when a persecutor can have mixed motives, the applicant must show a protected ground was “at least one central reason” for harm. The BIA’s inference that the predominant motive was retaliation for Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s property (rather than political opinion) was supported by timing and the content of threats: the threats escalated after the property dispute, men explicitly accused him of being a “snitch” tied to searches of the property, and the record lacked evidence that Don Rafa had been behind earlier politically‑tinged threats. Because that chain of facts permissibly supports the BIA’s mixed‑motive conclusion, the court refused to reweigh. (D. Nexus.)
- When courts reverse. Implicit in the opinion is that reversal would follow only where the record overwhelmingly pointed the other way — e.g., where the evidence establishes, as a matter of common sense and documentary support, that the protected ground was necessarily central, or where no reasonable factfinder could credit the agency’s contrary explanation. The opinion’s repeated citations to precedent (e.g., Rivera‑Barrientos; O.C.V.; Yuk) show that the burden to overturn is high.

Applying that to the panel’s statements here
- The panel repeatedly says there is “substantial evidence” for the BIA’s findings because the record contains factual predicates that reasonably support the BIA’s inferences: the escalation of threats after the property dispute, threats referring to searches and “snitching,” lack of reporting to higher police or evidence of systemic government failure, and evidence that authorities had tried to address the property. The panel thus concludes the agency’s explanation is a reasonable reading of the record and not one that a court may replace with a different plausible reading.
- The panel’s “any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled” language signals that only if the record so strongly favored the petitioners’ political‑motive inference that no reasonable adjudicator could adopt the BIA’s property‑dispute motive would the court overturn. The panel found no such compelling record here.

Bottom line for future cases (how to evaluate)
- If the record allows two plausible inferences, the court will normally defer to the agency’s choice among them.
- To overturn, the challenger must show the record so clearly favors the opposite inference that the agency’s conclusion is untenable — not merely that the court would have preferred a different inference.
- In this opinion, the record supported the BIA’s inferences (on persecution severity, motive, and government protection), so the substantial‑evidence standard required denial of review.

### Why are threats and psychological harm deemed insufficient to constitute persecution in the Tenth Circuit?
Question:
The court says 'threats alone are insufficient,' and it downplays psychological harms like insomnia and panic attacks. Some circuits have recognized death threats as persecution in certain contexts. What is the Tenth Circuit’s doctrinal basis for requiring more than threats and psychological harm, and are there circumstances in this circuit where threats alone can qualify (e.g., especially menacing death threats, accompanied by specific targeting or evidence of capability)?
Answer:
Short answer
The Tenth Circuit treats threats and attendant psychological harms as insufficient for “persecution” unless they are part of a larger pattern of objectively offensive harm. Its doctrinal basis is settled precedent requiring “the infliction of suffering or harm … more than just restrictions or threats,” and the court will only find persecution from threats when the threats are sufficiently severe, credible, and accompanied by other indicia (e.g., physical harm, detention, a pattern that in the aggregate amounts to severe mistreatment, lack of state protection, or an unavoidable risk on return). In this opinion the court denied asylum because Petitioners (1) suffered no physical harm, (2) conceded psychological harms only, and (3) only had threats that, even taken together, did not reach the level of persecution under Tenth Circuit cases (Karki, Wiransane, Brandy, Chhetri, etc.).

What the opinion actually says and why
- Legal standard: The court repeats the Tenth Circuit rule that “a finding of persecution requires the infliction of suffering or harm … and must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty.” (Opinion § II.C, quoting Wiransane v. Ashcroft.) The court also emphasizes that courts consider incidents collectively, but the cumulative effect must still amount to persecution (Ritonga cited).
- Precedent cited: The opinion cites Karki v. Holder for the principle that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution” (Opinion § II.C). It also cites Brandy and Chhetri for the proposition that psychological stress, fear, insomnia, or a few telephone threats typically do not meet the persecution threshold in the Tenth Circuit (Opinion § II.C; see also discussion of Carias-Mejia and Chhetri).
- Application to this case: The court notes key factual points that drive its conclusion:
  - Petitioners “conceded … they were not physically harmed” and reported “no lasting physical harm.” (II.C.)
  - Duarte’s insomnia and her son’s panic attacks are acknowledged but described as “fears stemming from their received threats” that “are insufficient to rise to the level of persecution.” (II.C.)
  - The court observes that the Tenth Circuit has “rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone.” (II.C.)
  - Because the threats here were not accompanied by physical injury, detention, torture, or other objectively severe mistreatment, the BIA’s finding—that the harm did not amount to past persecution—is supported by substantial evidence. (II.C.)

When can threats/psychological harm be enough (within the Tenth Circuit)?
The opinion does not draw a bright line that threats can never be persecution. From its reasoning and cited cases you can extract the court’s approach:
- Threats may contribute to a persecution finding when they are part of a pattern of conduct that, taken together, amount to the infliction of unlawful suffering (the court expressly considers cumulative effects; Ritonga). The difference is that the aggregate must cross the “more than threats” threshold.
- The court’s discussion implies that “extreme fact patterns” (which the opinion acknowledges exist in prior Tenth Circuit rulings) could support persecution even in the absence of permanent physical injury — but the opinion also stresses that the instant facts fall short. The court says it has “rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone,” suggesting that only unusually menacing, credible, and effective threats (often coupled with other objective harms or clear inability/unwillingness of the state to protect) would suffice. (II.C.)
- Other relevant considerations the court uses (and that would matter if threats were to qualify): the credibility/severity of the threat, whether the persecutor has the means and intent to carry it out, whether there is a pattern of escalation or actual violence against others, nexus to a protected ground, and demonstrable government inability or unwillingness to protect (II.C, II.D, II.E).

Practical summary for litigation
- Don’t rely on subjective fear or isolated insomnia/panic claims alone. The Tenth Circuit expects objective indicia of harm or a clear, continuous pattern of menacing conduct.
- Bolster threat-based claims with evidence showing (as available): actual physical attacks against the applicant or close associates, credible evidence the persecutor has the capability and intent to carry out death threats, a pattern of escalating acts, government refusal or systemic inability to protect, and a clear nexus to a protected ground. Absent such corroboration, the court will likely treat threats and consequent psychological harms as insufficient (as it did here).

Bottom line
The Tenth Circuit requires more than threats and attendant psychological distress because its precedent demands “the infliction of suffering or harm … more than just … threats” (Wiransane; Karki). Threats can be part of a successful persecution claim, but only if they are sufficiently severe, credible, and tied to other objective indicators (or an inability of the state to protect) so that the cumulative effect amounts to persecution. This opinion applied those principles and found the threats and psychological harms here fell short.

### How did the court analyze the 'nexus' requirement and decide political opinion was only tangential?
Question:
The opinion emphasizes that the escalated threats began after Jimenez intervened in a property dispute with 'Don Rafa,' and it treats that non-political conflict as the central motive. But petitioners had earlier received threats referencing the Liberal Party and campaigning. Why did the court discount those earlier threats as establishing or corroborating a political motive? What principles govern how the agency and court weigh temporally separated incidents and infer the primary motive in mixed-motive cases under the 'one central reason' standard?
Answer:
Short answer
The court treated the early, politically‑worded warnings as insufficient to show that political opinion was a central reason for the persecutors’ conduct because (1) there was no evidence tying those early threats to Don Rafa, (2) Petitioners continued political activity despite those early threats (undercutting their weight as motive evidence), and (3) the threats and violence escalated only after Jimenez’s direct, non‑political intervention in Don Rafa’s property—facts the BIA and IJ reasonably found made personal/professional motives primary. Under the governing “one central reason” test, mixed motives are possible, but a protected ground must be at least one central reason; temporally separated incidents are weighed for causal connection, identity of persecutor, and relative explanatory power, and the agency’s factual weighing is entitled to substantial‑evidence deference.

Why the court discounted the earlier political threats
1) No linkage to the actual persecutor. The opinion stresses that Petitioners “do not point to any evidence in the record that suggests Don Rafa was behind those initial politically affiliated threats.” (Opinion, Nexus section.) Because the later, escalated threats were explicitly tied to Don Rafa (the assailants said the order came from Don Rafa and accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” responsible for searches of Don Rafa’s property), the BIA reasonably concluded the later conduct revealed who was actually driving the campaign of threats. The absence of evidence that Don Rafa connected the earlier Liberal‑Party threats to Petitioners meant those early threats could not reliably be used to show Don Rafa was motivated by politics.

2) Petitioners’ own conduct undercuts the political‑motive inference. The court notes Petitioners “were undeterred by threatening phone calls and a fellow campaigner’s death, and continued their political activities.” (Nexus section.) If political opinion were the central reason, the court found it less persuasive that Petitioners kept campaigning despite the early threats; that fact made the later property dispute a more plausible proximate cause of the escalated, targeted conduct.

3) Temporal and factual sequencing pointed to a non‑political catalyst. The panel emphasized that the threats “escalated after Jimenez got involved in a property dispute with Don Rafa” and after Don Rafa learned Jimenez was investigating the property’s criminal use. The assailants’ contemporaneous statements blamed Jimenez’s intervention (snitching/prosecutor ties) rather than political campaigning. The court therefore treated the property conflict as the predominant motive. (See Nexus section: “Petitioners only began receiving escalated threats after Jimenez got involved in a property dispute with Don Rafa…”)

The governing legal standard and how it controls the analysis
- “One central reason” standard: To show persecution “on account of” political opinion, an applicant must prove the protected ground was “at least one central reason” for the persecutor’s actions; it cannot be merely “incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate.” (Quoting Rivera‑Barrientos; Dallakoti language cited in the opinion.)
- Mixed‑motive cases: The court recognizes persecutors may have multiple motives, but the protected motive must be central. The panel cited O.C.V. v. Bondi to confirm a persecutor can have multiple motives, yet reiterated the statutory “central reason” requirement applies.
- Temporal incidents are assessed for causal connection and probative value: The agency and court weigh temporally separated incidents by asking (a) whether the same persecutor is implicated in both sets of incidents, (b) whether later conduct is plausibly caused by the earlier incidents, and (c) which explanation (protected‑ground motive vs. nonprotected motive) better fits the totality of contemporaneous statements and timing. Here, the contemporaneous statements at the time of the escalated threats tied the conduct to the property dispute and “snitch” activity—so the later incidents were better explained by the nonpolitical dispute.
- Deference to the agency’s factual weighing: The court repeatedly notes it will not reweigh evidence where “substantial evidence” supports the BIA/IJ. The opinion invokes Yuk and the substantial‑evidence standard to decline reweighing conflicting motive inferences when the record supports the BIA’s conclusion that the property dispute was the predominant motive.

How those principles applied here (concise synthesis)
- The early threats mentioning the Liberal Party were temporally separated and not tied to Don Rafa; Petitioners continued political activity after them, so they carried less probative force for attributing motive to Don Rafa.
- The decisive evidence—the timing of escalation, the assailants’ statements blaming Jimenez for searches/seizures and calling him a “snitch,” and the assailants’ claim the orders came from Don Rafa—pointed to a property‑related, nonpolitical motive.
- Because the protected ground (political opinion) was found only tangential in light of the contemporaneous and escalated conduct, the BIA’s finding that political opinion was not “at least one central reason” is supported by substantial evidence and was upheld.

Bottom line
The court discounted the earlier party‑related threats because they lacked an evidentiary tie to the actual perpetrators and were less persuasive than the contemporaneous, escalated threats that expressly identified the property dispute and “snitch”/prosecutor allegations as the cause. Under the “one central reason” test, that factual sequence permitted the BIA and IJ to conclude political opinion was only tangential, and that factual conclusion was entitled to and sustained by substantial‑evidence review.

### What is the legal source for the 'government unable or unwilling to control' requirement when the persecutor is a private actor?
Question:
The definition of 'refugee' in § 1101(a)(42)(A) doesn’t expressly mention private actors, yet the court applies the familiar unable-or-unwilling test. What precedents or interpretive principles in the Tenth Circuit ground importing this requirement into the statutory text, and how does the court justify it as a matter of statutory interpretation?
Answer:
Short answer
The “government unable or unwilling to control” requirement when the persecutor is a private actor is not newly created by this opinion; it is an element long read into the INA by Tenth Circuit precedent and applied here. The opinion relies on (and cites) controlling Tenth Circuit decisions — principally Niang, Rivera‑Barrientos, and Singh — which interpret § 1101(a)(42)(A) and the asylum framework to require proof that private persecution cannot be remedied by the applicant’s government. The court treats that rule as part of the statutory inquiry (and as a factual element subject to substantial‑evidence review), and it explains how the government‑protection inquiry is performed (notice, opportunity to respond, reporting, futility).

Legal source and the court’s interpretive justification
1) Statutory anchors the opinion cites
- The opinion begins from the INA’s refugee definition, 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A), which the court quotes (B. Legal Framework). It then explains the three elements the applicant must prove, including that the harm “is committed by the government or forces the government is either unable or unwilling to control” (quoting Rivera‑Barrientos and Niang). Thus the court roots the control inquiry in the statutory definition as interpreted by prior Tenth Circuit caselaw.

2) Precedent the court relies on
- Niang v. Gonzales, 422 F.3d 1187, 1194–95 (10th Cir. 2005): quoted in the opinion as supplying the multi‑part refugee/asylum test including the government control element.
- Rivera‑Barrientos v. Holder, 666 F.3d 641, 646 (10th Cir. 2012): cited for the three elements (persecution, protected ground, and government control) and for the proposition that past persecution gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of future fear.
- Singh v. Bondi, 130 F.4th 848, 861 (10th Cir. 2025): relied on in the Government Protection section for articulating how to evaluate whether a government was able or willing to protect (focus on government response to past persecution, notice/opportunity to respond, and whether reporting would be futile or dangerous).
- The opinion also cites related Tenth Circuit authority (e.g., Uanreroro, Ritonga) to explain standards of review and how facts are assessed.

3) Interpretive principles applied by the court
- The court treats the government‑control inquiry as part of what it means to be a “refugee” under the statutory definition: a person “unable or unwilling to avail himself or herself of the protection of” her country. The court reads that statutory language together with precedent to impose an element that distinguishes private violence the state controls from private violence the state cannot or will not remedy.
- The court emphasizes evidence‑driven, fact‑specific analysis: whether the government had notice and an opportunity to act; whether the applicant reported the persecution (and if not, whether reporting would have been futile or dangerous); and whether there is evidence of systematic government failure rather than isolated official apathy (see Government Protection section, citing Singh and Aviles‑Gonzalez).
- The opinion thus imports the “unable or unwilling” requirement by treating the statutory phrase “unable or unwilling to avail himself or herself of the protection of” as imposing on applicants a duty to show the state would not or could not provide protection, and by relying on Tenth Circuit precedents that framed that showing as a discrete element.

How the opinion applies those principles (specific text)
- The court restates the three elements: “(1) an incident…that rise[s] to the level of persecution; (2) that is on account of one of the statutorily protected grounds; and (3) is committed by the government or forces the government is either unable or unwilling to control.” (B. Legal Framework, quoting Rivera‑Barrientos and Niang.)
- In the Government Protection section the court explains the evidentiary focus: “Two types of information weigh heavily…: (1) whether the government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors…and (2) whether the government offered protection to the applicant…” and emphasizes the importance of notice: “a key fact … is whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government.” (citing Singh, 130 F.4th at 861–62).
- The court then applies those principles to the facts, finding insufficient evidence that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect Petitioners (noting minimal efforts to seek help, lack of reporting beyond conversations with two officers, evidence that searches had been attempted at the problematic property, and no proof of systemic collusion with the persecutors).

Bottom line for practice
If the persecutor is a private actor, the Tenth Circuit requires the applicant to show the government is unable or unwilling to control those actors as part of proving refugee/asylum eligibility. That requirement is grounded in the INA’s refugee definition as read by Tenth Circuit precedent (Niang, Rivera‑Barrientos, Singh), and the court evaluates it by looking for evidence the government knew of the danger and either failed to act or would have been unable to protect — or that reporting would have been futile or dangerous.

### Why did the court rely on a Seventh Circuit articulation of 'systemic' government failure, and is that standard binding in the Tenth Circuit?
Question:
The opinion cites Osorio-Morales (7th Cir.) for the idea that evidence of 'systemic, rather than individual, failures' is often required to show a government is unwilling or unable to protect. Does the Tenth Circuit endorse that 'systemic' framing as its own rule, or is it using it persuasively? How does this square with Tenth Circuit cases that seem to accept evidence of localized police inaction when combined with futility/danger in reporting?

Short answer: The Tenth Circuit cited the Seventh Circuit’s Osorio‑Morales language as persuasive support for a commonsense point (that an isolated unhelpful officer is weak evidence the government is “unable or unwilling” to protect), not as a binding change to Tenth Circuit law. The controlling Tenth Circuit framework remains the tests and cases the panel repeatedly cites (e.g., Singh, Aviles‑Gonzalez, Chhetri): the court looks to whether the government had notice and an opportunity to respond, whether the applicant reported the threats, and whether reporting would have been futile or dangerous. Localized police inaction can be enough, but only when the record as a whole shows futility/danger or other evidence that the government was unable or unwilling to protect.

- That use is clearly persuasive: the panel invoked a Seventh Circuit articulation to illustrate and bolster its factual inference that the two encounters Petitioners had with individual officers did not, standing alone, establish governmental inability or unwillingness.

- No. Federal circuits are not binding on one another. The Tenth Circuit panel did not say it was adopting a new, circuit‑wide “systemic failures only” rule. Instead it applied the Tenth Circuit’s own doctrinal touchstones (see the opinion’s repeated citations to Singh, Aviles‑Gonzalez, Chhetri, Rivera‑Barrientos) and used Osorio‑Morales as persuasive authority for one factual point about the probative weight of isolated officer comments. (See Op. § E, pp. 12–13; § A (standard of review) and § B (legal framework) for the governing Tenth Circuit law the panel applies.)

How this fits with Tenth Circuit decisions that accept localized police inaction
- The opinion itself recognizes the settled Tenth Circuit approach: the inability/unwillingness inquiry “considers evidence about a government’s response,” focusing on whether the applicant reported persecution and whether reporting would have been futile or dangerous (citing Singh, Aviles‑Gonzalez). (Op. § E, p. 12.)
- Under Tenth Circuit precedent, evidence of localized police inaction can support an inability/unwillingness finding when tied to futility/danger or other facts showing that reporting would not have produced protection (see the court’s discussion and citations to Aviles‑Gonzalez and Chhetri). The panel here concluded the record lacked those additional features: Petitioners spoke only to two officers (who told them not to seek trouble), did not seek help beyond their neighborhood or through their contacts, and there was evidence the government had attempted searches of the implicated property—so the IJ/BIA reasonably inferred the government could and would act. (Op. § E, pp. 12–13.)
- In short: Tenth Circuit law permits localized inaction to carry the day, but only when the whole record supports the inference that reporting would have been futile or that the government more generally was unable/unwilling to protect. - The Osorio‑Morales quote is persuasive, not binding, authority in this opinion. The controlling test is Tenth Circuit precedent (notice/opportunity to respond; whether the applicant reported; whether reporting would have been futile/dangerous). Localized police inaction can satisfy the “unable or unwilling” element in the Tenth Circuit, but only when accompanied by other record evidence that justifies inferring systemic inability or futility of reporting — which the panel found lacking here.

### How much reporting to authorities is required before concluding a government is unwilling or unable to protect?
Question:
The court faults petitioners for 'minimal' outreach and emphasizes they didn’t pursue channels beyond conversations with two officers, even though one officer suggested police were contributing to the problem. Why did the court view that as insufficient to establish futility or danger in reporting? What kinds of evidence typically satisfy the futility/danger exception in the Tenth Circuit, especially when there is some evidence of law-enforcement corruption or alignment with persecutors?
Answer:
Short answer
The court rejected Petitioners’ “minimal” outreach because Tenth Circuit precedent treats government notice and opportunity to respond as a central fact in the unable-or-unwilling analysis. A single or isolated conversation with one or two officers—without reports to other authorities, evidence that those officers’ statements reflected systemic policy, or independent proof of government collusion or impunity—does not normally establish futility or danger in reporting. To trigger the futility/danger exception the applicant typically must show more: either formal complaints that the state ignored, contemporaneous evidence of systemic police/state corruption or control by persecutors, or other objective indicia that reporting would be useless or expose the applicant to greater risk.

Why the court found Petitioners’ outreach insufficient
The opinion explains the legal framework: “a key fact … is whether an applicant reported past persecution to the government” because without notice there is no basis to know how authorities would have reacted (opinion § II.E, citing Singh, 130 F.4th at 861–62). The court relied on those principles in concluding Petitioners’ efforts were “minimal.” The record shows Petitioners spoke with two individual police officers (one allegedly told them police were contributing to the problem and advised them to leave), but there was no evidence those conversations reflected a broader government policy or that Petitioners tried other reporting channels. The court cited Aviles-Gonzalez and noted that “without such reporting, we have no way of knowing how police would respond” (opinion § II.E). It also quoted a Seventh Circuit decision that “a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect” (opinion § II.E, citing Osorio‑Morales). Given Petitioners’ failure to report more broadly (e.g., to other police units, prosecutors, or through Jimenez’s government contacts) and the existence of evidence that the government had taken steps against the property (searches/seizures), the court found the record contradicted a claim that reporting would have been futile or dangerous.

The legal standard the court applied
- Notice/opportunity to respond matters: the inquiry “considers evidence about a government’s response to an applicant’s past persecution,” with two important factors being whether the government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors and whether it offered protection (opinion § II.E, citing Singh).  
- Failure to report is not automatically fatal, but it weakens an unable-or-unwilling claim unless the applicant can show reporting would have been futile or dangerous. To show futility/danger, an applicant must produce evidence that reporting would not have elicited protection or would have made things worse. The court applied this standard and held Petitioners did not meet it.

Types of evidence that typically satisfy the futility/danger exception (as shown by the opinion and cited precedent)
The opinion itself and the Tenth Circuit cases it cites illustrate the sorts of evidence that can carry an applicant past the default rule that reporting is required:

- Formal reports/complaints that were ignored or met with ineffective responses: prosecutions, protective steps requested and denied, or documented refusals to act provide direct evidence the government would not protect the applicant (the opinion treats absence of such reporting as important; Singh/Aviles‑Gonzalez reasoning is cited).

- Multiple attempts to notify different government channels: contacting local police plus higher-level authorities (municipal/state police, prosecutors, interior ministry) or using available institutional channels (formal complaints, protective custody requests). The court emphasized Petitioners did not seek help “outside their immediate neighborhood or through Jimenez’s law enforcement contacts” (opinion § II.E).

- Objective, corroborating evidence of systemic failure or collusion: country‑ or region‑wide evidence, reports, or adjudicative findings showing police corruption, impunity, or that persecutors are embedded in the state (the opinion notes courts “often require evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures” and cites Osorio‑Morales).

- Prior complaints by others and patterns of impunity: evidence that others in the applicant’s situation reported crimes and the state failed to act (patterns of complaints with no investigations/prosecutions).

- Threats or evidence that reporting would expose the applicant to more harm: specific evidence that authorities would disclose the complaint to persecutors or that government actors themselves are persecutors (Chhetri is cited where an applicant’s belief that persecutors were “part of the government” was insufficient without supporting evidence).

- Documentary country‑evidence or official records: police files showing no action, public reports documenting collusion, or affidavits from NGOs or officials corroborating that reporting is futile or dangerous in the applicant’s locale.

How those principles apply to the opinion’s facts
- Petitioners only spoke to two officers; one officer’s statement that “police officers were contributing to the problem” was not backed by evidence showing that statement reflected official or systemic policy. The court held that single, isolated conversation did not substitute for formal reports or broader evidence of state incapacity or unwillingness (opinion § II.E).  
- The record showed the government had in fact attempted searches and seizures at the property, which undermined the claim that authorities were wholly aligned with the persecutor. That concrete evidence of government action weighed against finding reporting would have been futile (opinion § II.E).

Practical takeaways for future claimants (based on the court’s reasoning)
- If you intend to argue futility/danger, try to produce contemporaneous evidence: reports you made and the government’s nonresponse, multiple attempts to notify different authorities, or objective documentation (country reports, police files, NGO affidavits) showing systemic corruption or collusion.  
- Isolated conversations with a single officer, without corroborating evidence that those conversations reflect a systemic governmental failure or collusion, are unlikely to establish futility in the Tenth Circuit.

Conclusion
The court required more than two informal conversations because Tenth Circuit law treats government notice and opportunity to act as central to the unable-or-unwilling inquiry. To avoid the reporting requirement, applicants must present stronger, usually documentary or systemic, evidence that reporting would have been futile or dangerous—evidence the Petitioners here failed to provide.

### Why did the court discuss internal relocation burdens using the government’s rebuttal standard when no presumption applied?
Question:
The opinion quotes the regulation about the government rebutting the presumption of future persecution via internal relocation, but it also says petitioners never established past persecution (so no presumption). In that posture, isn’t the burden on the applicant to show that relocation isn’t reasonable under § 1208.13(b)(2)–(3)? Is the court offering an alternative holding ('even if they had a presumption, the government could rebut via relocation')? How should we read the burden-shifting discussion here?
Answer:
Short answer: The court first applied the correct legal framework — it held petitioners did not prove past persecution (so no statutory presumption arose) — and then, as an alternative ground, addressed internal relocation under the government’s rebuttal standard. That discussion is an alternative-holding / “even if” analysis, not a misallocation of the burdens that would govern the case as actually decided.

Why: two steps in the opinion

1) The primary disposition (no presumption). In Section C the court concludes there is substantial evidence that petitioners’ harms do not rise to past persecution. Because the past‑persecution element fails, the opinion correctly states later (F, Safe Relocation) that petitioners “have not demonstrated a fear of future persecution” and “are not entitled to a rebuttable presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution.” That shows the court did not treat a presumption as having arisen.

2) The relocation discussion is an alternative holding. In Section F the court says, explicitly, “Petitioners have not demonstrated a fear of future persecution, but even if they had, the record supports the finding that they could reasonably relocate.” The court then cites the regulation and cases describing the government’s burden to rebut a presumption (8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i); Rivera‑Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646), and applies the relocation facts (family in Colombia, three weeks safely at mother’s home, threats limited to neighborhood) to conclude relocation is feasible. That is a classic alternative‑holding: the court disposes of the claim on the primary ground (no past persecution, no presumption) and then states that, even under the different burden‑shifting framework that would apply if a presumption existed, the result would be the same.

How burden‑shifting fits in (doctrine recap)
- If the applicant proves past persecution, a rebuttable presumption of future persecution arises; at that point the government must rebut the presumption by a preponderance of the evidence, either by showing a fundamental change or that internal relocation is reasonable (8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i); Rivera‑Barrientos).  
- If no past persecution is proved, no presumption arises and the applicant bears the burden to show a well‑founded fear of future persecution and that relocation is unreasonable (the applicant’s general burden of proof for asylum).  

How the opinion uses those rules here
- The court first finds petitioners failed to meet the applicant’s initial burden on past persecution and nexus (Sections C & D).  
- It then applies the government‑rebuttal framework only as an alternative rationale (Section F): “even if they had” a presumption, the evidence would show relocation is reasonable. Therefore the government would have met its rebuttal burden and the asylum claim would still fail.

Bottom line for reading the opinion
Read the relocation discussion as an alternative/backup holding rather than as the court applying the government’s rebuttal standard to a situation where a presumption actually existed. The court resolved the case on the primary factual/legal failures (no past persecution, no nexus, and inadequate evidence of government inability to protect) and added that, had the presumption arisen, the government would have rebutted it by showing internal relocation was reasonable.

### How probative is the continued safety of family members in assessing internal relocation?
Question:
The court leans on the fact that petitioners’ relatives remain unharmed in Colombia and that petitioners briefly lived safely with Jimenez’s mother. Petitioners argued family presence doesn’t always establish safety. Why did the court view family safety as strong evidence favoring reasonable relocation in this case? Are there Tenth Circuit or BIA principles that limit how much weight adjudicators should assign to family safety, especially where persecutors allegedly have national reach?
Answer:
Short answer
The court treated the continued safety of petitioners’ relatives—and the fact the family had briefly lived safely with Jimenez’s mother—as persuasive evidence that internal relocation in Colombia was reasonable because those facts (1) bear directly on whether the petitioners could avoid future persecution inside the country and (2) were supported by other record facts showing the threats were localized and the government had taken some investigative steps. The opinion also makes clear limits: family presence is not dispositive, and where the record affirmatively shows national reach, government collusion, or systemic inability/unwillingness to protect, family safety will carry less weight. Under the Tenth Circuit’s substantial‑evidence review the BIA’s weighing of these facts stands unless the record compels a contrary conclusion.

Why family safety mattered here (what the court relied on)
- Internal relocation doctrine: The government can rebut a well‑founded‑fear presumption by showing the applicant could reasonably relocate within the country. The court applied Rivera‑Barrientos/8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i) principles and asked whether Petitioners “could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” (Opinion, F. Safe Relocation.)
- Direct, record‑based indicators: The court pointed to two concrete record facts that undercut Petitioners’ claim that relocation was unreasonable: (i) “Petitioners’ family and relatives continue to live in Colombia unharmed,” and (ii) “Petitioners safely lived with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before deciding to relocate to the United States.” (F. Safe Relocation.) Those facts bear on the foreseeability of future harm outside the petitioners’ neighborhood and therefore support the BIA’s conclusion that relocation was feasible.
- Contextual corroboration: The court viewed those family‑safety facts together with other record indications that the threats were localized—e.g., the men “told Petitioners to move out of the neighborhood; they did not threaten the family to move out of the country”—and with evidence that law enforcement had at least attempted searches of the criminal property tied to the perpetrators. (Compare F. Safe Relocation and E. Government Protection.) That cumulative picture made family safety evidence probative of reasonable internal relocation.

Doctrinal limits the opinion recognizes (when family safety is less persuasive)
- Not dispositive; hypothetical safety insufficient: The opinion expressly notes Petitioners’ counterargument that “the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety” and that relatives might share political opinions. But the court rejects granting asylum on “such hypothetical hardships.” (F. Safe Relocation.) In other words, mere assertion that family could be unsafe or that relocation would impose economic/administrative burdens is insufficient without record support.
- Government inability/unwillingness and national reach: The court tied the weight of family‑safety evidence to its findings on government protection and the persecutors’ reach. In the unable/unwilling analysis the court emphasizes two core considerations: whether the government tried to stop the persecutors and whether the applicant reported persecution (notice/opportunity to respond). (E. Government Protection.) Because the record showed police had attempted searches of the criminal property and there was no solid evidence of systemic collusion, the court was unwilling to accept a claim that the persecutors had national reach or effective governmental protection. Where the record instead showed government incapacity or collusion, family presence would be much less probative.
- Need for affirmative record support to rebut relocation: The court applied substantial‑evidence review and required more than speculative assertions. It held that Petitioners “haphazardly briefed” arguments about national reach, judicial infrastructure, and economic burdens, and that the record did not show the persecutors were allied with the government or that contacting authorities would have been futile. (E. Government Protection; F. Safe Relocation.) Thus, family safety will be undermined if the record affirmatively shows: (a) the persecutors have nationwide reach or state protection, (b) family members have been targeted, or (c) contacting or relying on government protection would be futile or dangerous.

How this fits the court’s overall analysis
- The court did not treat family safety in isolation. It used family safety as one piece of record evidence consistent with (i) threats that appeared localized, (ii) the government’s having taken some action against the criminal property, and (iii) the petitioners’ minimal use of available channels to seek broader protection (their failure to seek help beyond local officers or through law‑enforcement contacts). Under the substantial‑evidence standard the BIA’s conclusion that internal relocation was reasonable stands because the record, as described above, supports that finding. (See A. Standard of Review; E. Government Protection; F. Safe Relocation.)

Bottom line for analysis on similar cases
- Family members living safely at home and a prior safe stay with relatives are persuasive evidence that internal relocation is reasonable, but only when the record lacks affirmative proof of national reach, systemic government collusion, or other conditions that make relocation futile. Adjudicators should weigh family safety together with evidence about the geographic scope of threats, government efforts (or failures) to control perpetrators, and whether the applicant actually sought help or reasonably could have sought help beyond the immediate neighborhood.

### Under what circumstances may the court 'consult the IJ’s more complete explanation' when reviewing a single-member BIA affirmance?
Question:
The panel cites Uanreroro to say it primarily reviews the BIA decision but can consult the IJ’s fuller reasoning. What are the boundaries of that practice? If the BIA’s reasoning is thin or ambiguous, can the court rely on IJ rationales the BIA didn’t expressly adopt without running afoul of the Chenery principle (no post hoc rationalizations)?
Answer:
Short answer
- When a single BIA member affirms an IJ’s decision without a full separate opinion, the court’s review remains of the BIA’s ruling. But the court may consult the IJ’s fuller opinion to determine the basis for the BIA’s action and to see the reasoning the BIA purportedly adopted. Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006) (quoted in the panel’s Standard of Review).
- The boundary is that the court may rely on the IJ’s explanation only where it actually explains the same grounds the BIA affirmed (or where the BIA’s terse affirmance makes clear it adopted the IJ’s reasoning). The court may not accept post‑hoc rationalizations or new grounds raised for the first time on appeal (the Chenery rule).

Legal doctrine and how it applies to the opinion
1) Who you review: the BIA, not the IJ
- The opinion correctly states the baseline: a single‑member BIA affirmance is the Board’s decision, and the court “generally” reviews the BIA’s decision. (A. Standard of Review, quoting Uanreroro.) That means the legal standard and factual sufficiency analysis are applied to what the BIA decided.

2) When and why the court may consult the IJ
- The court may consult the IJ’s more complete explanation when the BIA’s single‑member affirmance does not contain a full reasoning but clearly rests on (or adopts) the IJ’s analyses. The Tenth Circuit’s Uanreroro formulation—“we are not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds”—permits using the IJ opinion to understand the factual findings and reasoning the BIA affirmed. The panel used that approach repeatedly: it framed the review as substantial‑evidence review of the BIA’s findings but looked to the IJ’s findings (credibility, nexus, government protection, relocation) to assess whether substantial evidence supports the BIA’s conclusions.

3) Limits imposed by Chenery (no post‑hoc rationalizations)
- The Chenery principle forbids courts from upholding an agency action on a ground the agency itself never relied upon. That rule applies here: the court may not accept or invent a new rationale in the first instance on judicial review. Thus:
  - If the BIA’s affirmance is explicit or sufficiently clear that it adopted the IJ’s specific findings and reasoning, the court may rely on the IJ’s explanation as the operative administrative rationale.
  - If the BIA’s affirmance is ambiguous or silent as to particular issues, the court may consult the IJ to discern what the agency considered, but the court cannot treat new rationales (arguments or factual predicates) advanced for the first time by the government on appeal as the Board’s reasoning. In short, consulting the IJ is for clarification of what the agency did, not for supplying a retroactive justification.

How the panel applied these principles in this opinion
- The panel states it “review[s] the BIA’s decision, but ‘we are not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds.’” (A. Standard of Review.) The court then assesses whether “substantial evidence” supports the BIA’s findings, citing the IJ’s findings (e.g., that threats did not constitute persecution, that the predominant motive was economic/professional rather than political, that governmental protection and internal relocation were reasonable). That use stays within Uanreroro: the IJ’s reasoning was consulted to illuminate the Board’s affirmed conclusions rather than to invent new grounds.
- The opinion does not rely on any rationale that the IJ rejected or that the BIA did not at least implicitly endorse; it uses the IJ’s detailed findings to evaluate the BIA’s conclusions under the substantial‑evidence standard. That is consistent with the Chenery limitation.

Practical checklist for application (brief)
- Before relying on IJ reasoning, confirm the BIA’s affirmance (explicitly or implicitly) adopted the IJ’s findings on the point.
- If the BIA’s order is silent or cryptic, consult the IJ to determine likely grounds the BIA relied upon, but do not accept brand‑new rationales raised for the first time on appeal by the government.
- Treat the IJ opinion as part of the administrative record to the extent it explains the same grounds the BIA affirmed; do not use it to create new factual findings or legal bases not present in the Board’s decision.

Conclusion
The court may consult the IJ’s fuller explanation when a single‑member BIA affirmance leaves the operative reasoning thin or where the BIA plainly adopted the IJ’s analysis. But the Chenery rule prevents the court from upholding the Board by embracing IJ rationales or government arguments that the BIA did not actually adopt. The panel’s use of Uanreroro in this opinion follows that boundary: it reviews the Board’s decision under substantial evidence while consulting the IJ only to illuminate the Board’s affirmed grounds.

### Why does the court limit review to the administrative record and not consider developments after removal proceedings?
Question:
The court says it’s limited to the administrative record under § 1252(b)(4)(A). If new evidence arose (e.g., fresh threats), why can’t the court consider it on petition for review? Is the appropriate avenue a motion to reopen before the BIA, and how does that procedural posture interact with pending petitions for review?
Answer:
Short answer
The court is bound to review only the “administrative record on which the order of removal is based” under 8 U.S.C. § 1252(b)(4)(A). That means new evidence developed after the IJ/BIA issued the removal order normally cannot be considered by the court on a petition for review. The proper avenue to present new facts—fresh threats, changed country conditions, etc.—is a motion to reopen (or a motion to reconsider) to the agency (the BIA or IJ). How that interacts with a pending petition for review depends on timing and the relief sought: the agency can be asked to reopen the proceedings first; if the agency denies reopening, that denial is itself reviewable by petition for review.

Why the court limited review to the administrative record
The opinion states the governing limitation up front: “Our review of the Board’s denial of asylum relief is limited to ‘the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.’” (Section II.A, quoting § 1252(b)(4)(A)). The court therefore confines itself to the evidence before the IJ and the BIA because Congress expressly tied judicial review of removal orders to the administrative record. The court’s role on review is further framed by the substantial-evidence standard: it asks whether the administrative record compels a different factual conclusion (see Section II.A and the substantial-evidence discussion). Those two points explain why the panel did not accept after‑the‑fact evidence proffered for the first time to the court.

Why a motion to reopen is the appropriate procedure
When applicants develop new, material evidence after the agency decision (for example, new threats or changed country conditions), the agency mechanism for presenting that evidence is a motion to reopen (or, less commonly, a motion to reconsider). The opinion’s structure emphasizes that facts in the administrative record control the court’s review; it follows that evidence not in that record must be lodged with the agency so the agency can consider it and create a new administrative record if it reopens the case. Absent reopening, the court has no statutory basis to supplement the record on petition for review.

How motions to reopen interact with a pending petition for review
The opinion does not narrate a districting rule about timing, but the underpinning is clear:

- If a petitioner wants the agency to consider new evidence, the petitioner should file a motion to reopen with the BIA (or the IJ if jurisdiction permits). If the BIA grants reopening, it will take new evidence, and any later petition for review would be of the new final order — with the new facts in the administrative record.

- If a petitioner files a petition for review while (or before) seeking reopening, the court is still limited to the administrative record that existed when the agency decided the case. If the agency later reopens and changes the order, the petitioner can ask the court to stay or dismiss the pending petition as moot or seek voluntary remand/withdrawal depending on circumstances.

- If the agency denies the motion to reopen, that denial becomes an administrative determination that the federal courts can review (subject to applicable jurisdictional constraints). Then the petitioner may challenge the denial by pursuing or continuing a petition for review.

These practical interactions are implicit in the opinion’s repeated framing of review as confined to the administrative record (see Section II.A) and the court’s deference to the BIA/IJ factual findings under the substantial-evidence standard. Because the court’s task here was to test whether the record compelled a different result, introducing new evidence at the court stage would bypass the agency fact-finding and the statutorily-mandated record-based review.

Takeaway
If you have new threats or other post-decision evidence, present them first to the agency via a motion to reopen so the administrative record can be expanded. Only after the agency acts on reopening (or denies it) will the federal courts have a proper administrative record to review or a separate agency denial to challenge on petition for review. The Tenth Circuit’s opinion enforces that division of labor by denying relief based on the existing administrative record and the substantial-evidence standard (see Section II.A and the court’s factual analysis in Sections II.C–F).

### How does the court treat unbriefed theories (like the 'particular social group' of 'social leaders')—waiver, forfeiture, or failure to exhaust?

In this opinion the panel did not remand or say the BIA had failed to consider a PSG claim such that remand would be required; instead it resolved the case on the ground the petitioners actually argued and supported.

What to watch for (if you litigate this issue)
- If petitioners want the court to consider a theory (e.g., a particular social group), they must either (a) develop and brief it on appeal, or (b) ensure the BIA clearly addressed it in its decision so there is a developed administrative ruling for the court to review. Absent that, the appellate court may treat the theory as forfeited and decline to consider it—precisely what happened here.
- If the BIA has expressly decided a theory below but the petitioner fails to brief it on appeal, some courts have nonetheless reviewed the BIA’s reasoning sua sponte in limited circumstances. This opinion, though, shows the panel chose not to do so and limited review to the briefed ground.

Bottom line: The Tenth Circuit treated the unbriefed “social leaders” PSG as abandoned on appeal (forfeiture/waiver) and limited its review accordingly; it did not treat the omission as an exhaustion defect requiring remand.

### What weight do unpublished Tenth Circuit decisions carry in asylum reviews, and why did the court cite several?
Question:
The opinion cites unpublished Tenth Circuit cases (e.g., Chhetri, Carias-Mejia). Are these cited purely for persuasive value under Tenth Circuit Rule 32.1, and how does the court decide when unpublished cases sufficiently state or apply circuit law, especially on recurring issues like threats-as-persecution or the asylum/withholding standard interplay?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — unpublished Tenth Circuit decisions cited in this opinion are cited for persuasive value under Tenth Circuit Rule 32.1, not as binding precedent. The court uses them pragmatically to illustrate how the circuit has applied its controlling legal standards to similar factual patterns (e.g., threats-as-persecution, the relationship between asylum and withholding standards) where published precedent is either on point only in principle or where multiple unpublished decisions have repeatedly applied the same legal rule.

Explanation and how the opinion uses them

1) Rule 32.1 and precedential effect
- The Tenth Circuit permits citation of unpublished opinions under Rule 32.1, but those opinions are not precedential in the same way published opinions are. The opinion here cites Chhetri v. Rosen, 844 F. App’x 23 (10th Cir. 2021) and Carias‑Mejia v. Garland, 860 F. App’x 121 (10th Cir. 2021) as non‑precedential authorities applying circuit law. That is the ordinary posture: unpublished decisions are persuasive evidence of how the court has applied controlling law, not new binding rules.

2) Two distinct roles the opinion gives unpublished decisions
- Factual analogies for the persecution inquiry. The court cites Chhetri and Carias‑Mejia when explaining how the court has treated threats and related harms in past asylum fact patterns. For example, after summarizing the persecution standard, the opinion states that the court has “rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone,” and then cites Chhetri and Carias‑Mejia as illustrations (opinion pp. 10–12). That use shows the court leaning on unpublished decisions to demonstrate how the substantial‑evidence/persecution test has been applied to comparable facts.
- Reinforcing doctrinal propositions where controlling precedent exists. On the asylum/withholding relationship the opinion relies on the settled proposition that withholding of removal requires a higher showing and therefore flows from the asylum analysis; it cites Carias‑Mejia at the end of the opinion to support the point that failing to meet the asylum standard means the withholding standard is necessarily unmet (opinion p. 15). Here the unpublished decision is cited to illustrate application of a controlling rule (Uanreroro and Rivera‑Barrientos are also cited for the governing standards).

3) How the court decides when to rely on unpublished decisions
- The court will cite unpublished decisions when they (a) apply the same legal standard to materially similar facts, (b) resolve recurring factual issues under controlling law, or (c) fill practical gaps where there is no directly on‑point published decision. That is visible in this opinion: the court repeatedly cites prior unpublished decisions about “threats alone” not constituting persecution (Chhetri, Carias‑Mejia) when evaluating whether this family’s threats and psychological harms, taken together, amount to persecution (opinion pp. 10–12).
- Unpublished decisions are especially useful on recurring, fact‑intensive issues (e.g., what degree or combination of threats, harassment, and non‑physical harm constitutes persecution; when police apathy shows government unwillingness to protect). The opinion follows that pattern when it compares this record to Chhetri and similar unpublished cases (opinion pp. 11–13 regarding government protection and futility of reporting).

4) Practical takeaway for future asylum reviews
- Treat unpublished Tenth Circuit opinions as persuasive, evidence‑of-practice authorities that the court will use to show consistency in applying the governing rules (persecution definition, nexus, government protection, relocation, and asylum/withholding interplay).
- But remember they do not supplant or alter controlling published precedent; they are most useful when they apply published standards to similar facts — precisely how this opinion uses Chhetri and Carias‑Mejia (see the court’s comparisons on threats, government response, and the higher withholding standard; opinion pp. 10–15).

In short: the citations are persuasive applications of established circuit law (per Rule 32.1). The court relies on them when they compellingly illustrate how the governing standards have been applied to fact patterns like the one before it, particularly on recurring, fact‑intensive questions such as whether threats and psychological harms amount to persecution and how asylum findings bear on withholding of removal.

### Why didn’t the court address the due process right-to-counsel claim the BIA rejected?
Question:
The panel notes petitioners did not appeal the due process claim. Is that treated as waiver/abandonment because it wasn’t raised in the opening brief? If so, would the court have had an independent obligation to consider structural due process errors, or is it entirely forfeitable? Also, how does the prejudice standard for right-to-counsel claims interact with the substantial-evidence framework if it had been reviewed?
Answer:
Short answer: Because Petitioners expressly declined to press that issue on appeal, the panel treated it as forfeited and therefore did not reach it. The opinion says so twice: the procedural summary notes that the BIA “also rejected Petitioners’ due process arguments that the IJ violated their right to counsel” (Procedural Background), and the court later states plainly, “They do not appeal the denial of protection under the CAT or their due process claim” (II.D Discussion opening). That omission in their briefing is why the panel did not decide the right‑to‑counsel claim.

Why that matters (legal doctrine/standard)
- Forfeiture/abandonment: Federal courts of appeals ordinarily do not consider issues a party fails to raise in its opening brief. The opinion follows that basic appellate practice: it confines review to the claims the petitioners actually appealed (see the court’s framing: “Petitioners only appeal whether the BIA erred in denying their applications for asylum and withholding of removal” (Intro) and “We review the BIA’s decision” (Standard of Review)). The court therefore had no occasion to resolve a claim the petitioners chose not to press.
- No independent duty shown in the opinion: The panel did not identify any structural or jurisdictional defect that would obligate it to raise the counsel‑claim sua sponte. The opinion limits itself to the issues presented and applies the usual substantial‑evidence review to the BIA’s factual findings (see II.A: “We review the BIA’s findings of fact under a substantial‑evidence standard”). The court did not suggest the due‑process claim was non‑forfeitable or that it had to consider it on its own motion.

Could the court have independently addressed a structural due‑process error?
- The opinion does not say so. It treats the due‑process claim as unappealed and therefore forfeited. While some courts will review certain fundamental, jurisdictional, or plain‑error matters sua sponte, this panel did not invoke any such doctrine here and identified no jurisdictional defect requiring independent review. The relevant passages are the Procedural Background (noting the BIA rejected the due‑process arguments) and the sentence in II.D stating Petitioners “do not appeal” the due‑process claim. Because the panel limited its review to the appealed claims, it had no occasion to reach structural‑error arguments.

How a right‑to‑counsel prejudice standard would interact with substantial‑evidence review (if the claim had been preserved)
- Two separate principles would have governed review:
  1. For a right‑to‑counsel due‑process claim, the claimant must show a violation that caused prejudice — i.e., that the absence or deprivation of counsel meaningfully affected the outcome. The opinion does not set out the exact prejudice test for counsel claims, but it treats Petitioners’ unraised due‑process claim as an issue they abandoned.
  2. For the substantive immigration findings, the court applies the substantial‑evidence standard: BIA factual findings are “conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary” (II.A).
- Interaction: Had Petitioners preserved and won a showing of prejudice from counsel deprivation, that would undermine the factual findings the BIA relied on; but under substantial‑evidence review the court would still reverse only if the record compelled a contrary conclusion. Practically, a successful prejudice showing would have to demonstrate that, but for the counsel error, the record would have supported a different factual result such that no reasonable adjudicator could have reached the BIA’s decision. Put another way, a due‑process prejudice showing and the substantial‑evidence standard converge: the petitioner would need to show both (a) a reasonable probability that counsel (or a fair proceeding) would have produced a different outcome, and (b) that the record, read as it should have been developed, compels reversal under the substantial‑evidence test.
- The opinion’s governing language on review—substantial‑evidence deference to the BIA (II.A)—frames how any preserved prejudice claim would be assessed on appeal.

Bottom line: The panel did not address the BIA’s rejection of the right‑to‑counsel claim because Petitioners waived/forfeited it by not appealing it or briefing it. The court applied ordinary appellate and immigration review limits (substantial‑evidence review of the issues presented) and did not invoke any independent obligation to reach a forfeited due‑process claim.

### How did the court apply the 'one central reason' standard to dismiss a mixed-motive political opinion claim?
Question:
The court recognizes persecutors can have multiple motives but says political opinion must be more than 'incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate.' What analytical steps does the Tenth Circuit use to distinguish central from tangential motives? Here, the record included explicit references to 'snitches from [the] Liberal Party'—why were those not enough to show political opinion was at least one central reason?
Answer:
Short answer
The Tenth Circuit applied a mixed-motives analysis in three clear steps: (1) identify the protected ground and state the “at least one central reason” legal standard (political opinion cannot be “incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate”), (2) examine the record for evidence tying the persecutor’s motive to that protected ground (timing, context, and persecutor statements), and (3) compare that evidence to evidence of other, non‑protected motives to decide which motive was predominant. Applying those steps here, the court concluded the political‑opinion evidence (the “snitches from [the] Liberal Party” brochure and passing accusations) was present but subordinate to a stronger, non‑political motive—punishing Jimenez for interfering with Don Rafa’s property/revenue—so political opinion was not shown to be “at least one central reason.”

Relevant legal standard
The court invoked the statutory and Tenth Circuit rule that to show persecution “on account of” political opinion an applicant must prove that political opinion was “at least one central reason” for the harm and “cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate” (opinion, citing Rivera‑Barrientos and Dallakoti). The court acknowledged mixed motives are possible, but reiterated that in mixed‑motive cases a protected ground must be a central reason (opinion, citing O.C.V.).

How the court analyzed central vs. tangential motive (the analytical steps)
1. Frame the inquiry around the “at least one central reason” test (opinion, Nexus section).  
2. Look to record evidence that ties the persecutor directly to the protected ground: direct statements, timing of threats, content of messages, and whether the persecutor’s words/acts link the harm to the applicant’s political activities (opinion: court reviews brochure, accusations of being “snitches,” and who said what).  
3. Examine competing evidence of other motives and the temporal and causal relationship between those motives and the harm: did the harassment escalate after a non‑political event? Did persecutors refer to non‑political reasons? Were threats tied to the applicant’s professional conduct or economic impact on the persecutor? (opinion: court emphasized escalation after the property dispute and threats referencing searches/seizures and the prosecutor’s office).  
4. Determine which motive was predominant (or whether the protected ground was at least a central reason) by weighing the comparative strength of political‑motive evidence against the non‑political motive evidence under the substantial‑evidence standard (opinion: BIA/IJ findings sustained because record supported that the primary motive was non‑political).

Why the “snitches from [the] Liberal Party” evidence was insufficient
- Presence of political‑language evidence: The court acknowledged the brochure saying “death to snitches from Liberal Party” and the men’s accusations that Jimenez was a “snitch” (opinion, Factual Background). Those items show the persecutors invoked political language.  
- But timing and causal link favored a non‑political motive: The court stressed that the threats escalated only after Jimenez’s direct involvement in a property dispute with Don Rafa and after Don Rafa learned Jimenez was investigating/linked to law‑enforcement actions at the property (opinion, Nexus: “Petitioners only began receiving escalated threats after Jimenez got involved in a property dispute … and after Don Rafa became aware that Jimenez was investigating his rented property”).  
- Persecutors’ statements tied the harm to prosecutorial/financial/security concerns, not to campaign activities: The men accused Jimenez of working for the prosecutor’s office and being responsible for searches and seizures at Don Rafa’s property (opinion: “a man accused Jimenez of working for the prosecutor’s office and for being responsible for searches and seizures”). The BIA/IJ reasonably concluded those statements showed the persecutors were reacting to Jimenez’s interference with criminal operations (revenue/security), not primarily to his party affiliation.  
- Comparative strength of evidence: Petitioners had continued political campaigning despite earlier threats and produced no evidence that Don Rafa was behind the initial politically framed threats; the record tied the most serious harassment to the property/prosecutorial issues. The court therefore found political opinion “merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm” (opinion, Nexus: “We agree that Petitioners’ political opinion was merely tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm.”).

Bottom line for law students
When a persecutor uses political language, that language alone does not automatically make political opinion a central reason. The court looks at the whole record—especially timing, who benefited or was threatened economically or professionally by the applicant’s conduct, and direct statements tying motives to non‑political conduct—and will treat political language as subordinate if the stronger, contemporaneous evidence points to a non‑political motive. Here, although the “snitches from [the] Liberal Party” brochure and accusations existed, the escalation after the property dispute and the persecutors’ focus on prosecutorial/search issues led the court to conclude political opinion was not a central reason.

### Why does failure on asylum necessarily doom withholding of removal in this case?
Question:
The court says petitioners fail asylum and thus necessarily fail withholding because withholding has a higher standard. Is that always true, or could differing burdens (no discretion, different nexus standard under § 1231(b)(3)) ever allow a petitioner to lose asylum but win withholding? What specific elements here foreclosed withholding once asylum failed?
Answer:
Short answer
Not always in the abstract, but in this case yes. The Tenth Circuit said withholding of removal requires a higher showing than asylum, and because Petitioners failed on the substantive elements (persecution, nexus, government unwilling/ unable to protect, and feasibility of internal relocation) they cannot meet the stricter withholding standard. The court relied on its findings that no past persecution was proved, the political opinion was only “tangential and subordinate,” the Colombian government had not been shown to be unable/unwilling to protect them, and internal relocation was feasible — so failure on asylum necessarily foreclosed withholding here.

Why the court treats asylum failure as fatal to withholding
- Different burdens: Asylum is discretionary and requires proof by a preponderance that the applicant is a “refugee” (well‑founded fear or past persecution that gives a rebuttable presumption). Withholding of removal is mandatory relief under 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3) and requires a higher substantive showing — a “clear probability” (sometimes phrased as “more likely than not”) of persecution on a protected ground. The opinion summarizes that withholding has a “higher standard” and cites Carias‑Mejia and Uanreroro for the settled principle that failing to meet asylum’s elements ordinarily means the applicant cannot carry the heavier burden for withholding. (See slip op. pp. 2, 15–16, and conclusion.)

- Elements are essentially the same but the proof required is higher: Both asylum and withholding require persecution (or a well‑founded fear), a nexus to a protected ground, and that the government is unable/unwilling to control the persecutor (and the feasibility of internal relocation affects both analyses). Because the underlying factual showings required for withholding are the same as for asylum but measured against a higher probability standard, a failure to establish the facts supporting asylum usually dooms withholding unless there is some separate factual or legal reason the withholding standard would nevertheless be satisfied.

How that plays out in this opinion — the specific elements that foreclosed withholding
The court identified multiple factual/legal failures that foreclose withholding even under its higher standard:

1) No past persecution (and no rebuttable presumption of future persecution). The IJ and BIA found that the threats, isolated incidents, and psychological harms did not rise to persecution as a matter of law. The Tenth Circuit affirmed: Petitioners conceded no lasting physical harm, threats alone were insufficient, and their emotional distress did not tip the cumulative evidence into persecution (slip op. pp. 9–12). Because Petitioners could not show persecution or a well‑founded fear, they could not meet the higher “clear probability” standard required for withholding.

2) No nexus to a protected ground. The court concluded the dominant motive was retaliation by Don Rafa for Jimenez’s interference in criminal activity and threats to Don Rafa’s revenue/security, not the Petitioners’ political opinion. The opinion states Petitioners’ political activity was “tangential and subordinate” and the escalated threats followed Jimenez’s property dispute with Don Rafa (slip op. pp. 11–12). Without a protected‑ground nexus under the asylum test, Petitioners cannot carry the heightened withholding burden.

3) Government protection / inability or unwillingness not shown. The court held the record lacked evidence that the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect Petitioners: they did not meaningfully report wrongdoing beyond two unhelpful officers; the government had in fact attempted searches of the property; and there was no evidence of systemic government complicity (slip op. pp. 12–14). That factual failure prevents meeting withholding’s higher standard on government inability or unwillingness.

4) Internal relocation feasible. Even assuming some risk, the court found relocation reasonable and feasible (family in Colombia unharmed; they safely stayed with relatives; threats told them to leave the neighborhood, not the country) (slip op. pp. 14–15). If internal relocation is available, withholding relief also fails.

Could a petitioner sometimes lose asylum but win withholding?
Yes, in theory — but only if the particular reasons for denial depart from the sort of evidentiary failures present here. Two typical scenarios where asylum denial does not automatically defeat withholding:

- Discretionary denial of asylum despite meeting refugee status: If an applicant demonstrates refugee status but the IJ denies asylum in the exercise of discretion (e.g., because of negative discretionary factors), withholding could still be required because withholding is mandatory and non‑discretionary once the higher standard is met. That situation is not present here because the court affirmed denial based on failure to prove persecution/nexus/protection issues, not discretionary denial of otherwise established refugee status.

- Different legal standards that could cut opposite ways: Occasionally legal distinctions (e.g., differing nexus formulations, or evidence that meets the “clear probability” standard but was deemed insufficient for a discretionary asylum grant) could produce divergent outcomes. But the opinion makes clear the facts here fail the substantive elements for asylum (and thus cannot satisfy the stricter withholding standard), so no such divergence exists in this case.

Bottom line
The court’s statement that failure on asylum “necessarily” dooms withholding in this case rests on concrete factual findings: lack of persecution, lack of nexus, lack of government inability/unwillingness, and feasible internal relocation. Because withholding requires meeting the same elements to an even higher degree, those failures foreclose withholding here. Theoretically a different record (or a discretionary denial of asylum despite established refugee status) could allow a win on withholding after an asylum loss, but that is not the situation presented or decided in this opinion.
