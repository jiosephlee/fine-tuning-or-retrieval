Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Jimenez v. Bondi

### How does the Tenth Circuit review a single-member BIA affirmance and when can it rely on the IJ’s reasoning?
Question:
The opinion says it reviews the BIA’s decision but can consult the IJ’s “more complete explanation” (citing Uanreroro). Here, a single BIA member affirmed and the panel repeatedly leans on the IJ’s findings. What are the limits on this practice? If the BIA’s short decision didn’t expressly adopt every IJ ground, can the court still rely on those IJ rationales under substantial-evidence review, or must the BIA have clearly incorporated them?
Answer:
Short answer
- When a single BIA member issues a short affirmance of an IJ’s decision, the Tenth Circuit reviews the BIA’s decision but may consult the IJ’s “more complete explanation” of the same grounds the BIA affirmed. (Jimenez v. Bondi, slip op. § A (citing Uanreroro v. Gonzales, 443 F.3d 1197, 1204 (10th Cir. 2006)).)
- The court still applies the substantial‑evidence standard to the BIA’s factual findings: they are “conclusive unless the record demonstrates that any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.” (Jimenez § A (quoting Rivera‑Barrientos).)
- The key limit is that the court may consult the IJ’s reasoning only insofar as it explains “those same grounds” that the BIA actually affirmed. The Tenth Circuit will not import or rely on IJ rationales that are inconsistent with, or not adopted by, the BIA’s affirmance.

Explanation and practical rule of thumb
1. Governing framework in Jimenez
- Jimenez starts from two propositions the panel follows: (1) when the BIA issues a short affirmance by a single member, the court reviews the BIA’s decision; and (2) the court is “not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds.” Jimenez § A (citing Uanreroro). The court then applies the familiar substantial‑evidence standard to the BIA’s factual findings. Jimenez § A (quoting Rivera‑Barrientos).

2. What “consulting the IJ’s explanation” means—and its limits
- Permitted use: If the BIA’s short order plainly affirms the IJ’s conclusions on particular issues (for example, findings on past persecution, nexus, government protection, or relocation), the Tenth Circuit may look to the IJ’s fuller written decision to understand the factual and legal bases of those conclusions. That is what the Jimenez panel did: because a single member affirmed, the court reviewed the BIA’s decision but relied on the IJ’s fuller findings where they explained the “same grounds” the BIA affirmed (see Jimenez §§ C–F).
- Prohibited or inappropriate use: The court should not rely on IJ reasoning that (a) advances grounds the BIA did not affirm, (b) contradicts an express BIA rationale, or (c) supplies a post‑hoc rationale that the BIA never embraced. The limiting phrase from Uanreroro—“those same grounds”—is pivotal: consulting the IJ is proper only to explain or flesh out the grounds actually before the court as affirmed by the BIA.

3. How to analyze a single‑member BIA affirmance (practical checklist)
- Read the BIA order carefully: does it say it affirms the IJ’s decision generally, or does it affirm only particular holdings or reject some IJ findings? If the BIA explicitly adopts or affirms a ground, the court can look to the IJ’s written explanation of that ground.
- Compare the IJ’s explanation with the BIA’s disposition: only rely on IJ passages that plainly correspond to the issues the BIA affirmed. Avoid importing IJ rationales on issues the BIA did not adopt.
- Apply the substantial‑evidence test to the BIA’s findings (using the IJ’s explanation where appropriate): ask whether any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to reach a different factual conclusion on the affirmed grounds (Rivera‑Barrientos, quoted in Jimenez § A).

Illustration from Jimenez
- In Jimenez, the panel explicitly stated the Uanreroro principle and then relied on the IJ’s fuller findings to evaluate past persecution, nexus, government protection, and relocation—because a single BIA member had affirmed the IJ (see Jimenez §§ A, C–F). The court did not treat the IJ’s reasoning as independently creating BIA precedent; it used the IJ’s explanation to determine whether substantial evidence supported the BIA’s affirmed conclusions.

Bottom line
The Tenth Circuit may consult an IJ’s more complete opinion when the BIA issues a single‑member affirmance, but only to elucidate the same grounds the BIA actually affirmed. The court’s role remains to review the BIA’s decision under the substantial‑evidence standard; it should not uphold (or adopt) BIA orders on IJ rationales that the BIA did not adopt or that conflict with the BIA’s express reasoning.

### Why weren’t armed threats and the attempted grabbing of the child enough to constitute past persecution?
Question:
The panel emphasizes that “threats alone are insufficient,” but the facts include two men holding Jimenez at gunpoint, later assaulting an employee while searching for the family, and a man putting his arm around the son and covering his mouth. The court notes petitioners conceded no lasting physical harm. In Tenth Circuit asylum law, how are incidents like being held at gunpoint or an apparent attempted abduction treated—are these still categorized as mere threats absent injury? What factors tip such episodes into “persecution” when aggregated?
Answer:
Short answer: Because under Tenth Circuit law (as articulated in Jimenez), isolated or episodic armed threats, an assault on a third party, and an apparent attempted grab of a child can still be treated as “threats” that do not by themselves amount to past persecution—especially where the applicants conceded no lasting physical injury, the psychological harms are not shown to be severe or enduring, the incidents were not shown to be so extreme or continuous as to be “offensive” suffering, and the record did not show the mistreatment was motivated centrally by a protected ground or that the government could not protect them.

Why the panel ruled this way (legal standard and its application)
- Persecution requires more than “restrictions or threats to life and liberty.” As Jimenez quotes Wiransane, persecution “must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty.” The court therefore treats threats as a distinct (and often insufficient) category of harm. Jimenez expressly repeats the settled rule that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” (citing Karki and the panel’s own discussion).
- Cumulative analysis, but with a threshold. The court does not look only at each incident in isolation; it considers the aggregate impact of multiple incidents (Ritonga). Even so, the cumulative harms must reach the level of suffering or harm that is “offensive” under the statute and more than “sporadic threats” (Karki; Brandy).
- Physical injury and lasting effects matter. Jimenez notes petitioners conceded they suffered no physical harm or lasting physical injury. Although physical harm is not strictly required, the court observes that where there is no lasting physical harm, courts have been reluctant to find persecution based on threats plus psychological stress alone. The opinion says the lived fears and stress were “insufficient to rise to the level of persecution” and that the court has “rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone.” (citing Brandy).
- Context and motive (nexus) matter. Even if the conduct is serious, it must be shown to be “on account of” a protected ground (political opinion, etc.). Jimenez found substantial evidence that the dominant motive was a personal/professional conflict with Don Rafa (threats tied to the property dispute and to interfering with criminal revenue), not principally the family’s political activities. Mixed motives are possible, but a protected ground must be at least one central reason.
- Government protection and relocation are also relevant. Jimenez emphasized that government unwillingness or inability to protect the applicants and the impossibility of internal relocation can push harms toward persecution. The record here, however, contained little showing that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to help.

Putting those points against the facts
- Two men held Mr. Jimenez at gunpoint, later assaulted an employee while searching for the family, and a man briefly grabbed/covered the son’s mouth. Those are serious factual events. But the panel relied on (1) petitioners’ concession of no lasting physical harm, (2) the characterization of much of the conduct as threats rather than completed physical injury, (3) the lack of evidence that the persecution was centrally motivated by a protected ground, and (4) limited evidence that the government could not protect them. Under the court’s precedent, that combination made the incidents insufficient, even in the aggregate, to meet the past-persecution threshold.
- The court also points to prior Tenth Circuit decisions where even more extreme fact patterns were rejected (Chhetri; Carias-Mejia), reinforcing that threats-plus-transient trauma often fall short.

What sorts of facts tip episodes into “persecution” (drawing on Jimenez’s framework)
- Severity and permanence: physical injury, prolonged detention, torture, or other harms causing lasting physical impairment or severe, enduring psychological trauma.
- Frequency and continuity: a sustained campaign of serious abuse rather than isolated or sporadic threats/encounters.
- Forced loss of liberty or forced displacement (e.g., kidnapping, sustained custody, or an effective expulsion from one’s home).
- Nexus: evidence that a protected ground (political opinion, etc.) was at least one central reason for the mistreatment.
- Government inability/unwillingness to protect: credible evidence that reporting would be futile or dangerous, or that state actors were complicit—especially where the applicant actually sought help and was rebuffed systemically (not just by a single officer).
When these factors appear—particularly together—the Tenth Circuit has been more willing to find that an applicant suffered past persecution.

Bottom line
Jimenez treats the armed encounters and the attempted grabbing as serious but, given the applicants’ concessions, the lack of lasting physical injury, the court’s threshold that threats-plus-transient psychological harm usually do not equal persecution, the weaker showing on nexus, and limited proof the state could not protect them, those episodes did not meet the Tenth Circuit’s legal standard for past persecution in that case.

### Did the court properly apply the mixed-motive ‘one central reason’ test given both the political leaflet and the ‘snitch’ accusations?
Question:
Petitioners received a brochure saying “death to snitches from Liberal Party,” and the men later called them snitches and linked them to the prosecutor’s office, while also saying the order came from Don Rafa. The court concludes the predominant motive was Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s criminal enterprise, not politics. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i), how should a court weigh evidence of both political animus and personal/economic motives? Does the leaflet explicitly tying politics to being a “snitch” support an imputed political opinion theory, and if so, why was that not enough to satisfy “at least one central reason”?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — under the court’s framing in Jimenez v. Bondi the panel applied the mixed‑motive “one central reason” test correctly. The opinion explains the governing standard, acknowledges mixed motives are possible, but concludes that the record (timing of escalation, the persecutors’ statements, and the Don Rafa connection) supports the BIA’s factual finding that politics was only a subordinate/tangential reason. The leaflet alone, even if probative of an imputed political opinion, was not enough to make politics “at least one central reason” in light of that other evidence and the substantial‑evidence standard of review.

Why that is the correct application of the law (doctrine and how the court weighed the evidence)

- The legal standard. The court quotes the statutory and circuit standard: to show persecution “on account of” political opinion an applicant must show the political opinion was “at least one central reason” for the persecution (8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i); Rivera‑Barrientos). Political motive cannot be “incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate” or play “a minor role.” The court also recognizes that persecutors can have multiple motives — mixed‑motive claims are cognizable — but the protected ground must still be a central reason. (See D. Nexus discussion.)

- How courts weigh mixed‑motive evidence. The opinion demonstrates the ordinary approach: examine the totality of record evidence (persecutor statements, timing of events, causal sequence, who ordered or benefitted from the harm) to decide whether a protected ground was central. The panel relied on contemporaneous statements by the attackers, the sequence (initial politically‑tinged threats predated—but did not escalate into—violence until after Jimenez’s property dispute with Don Rafa), and the attackers’ specific accusations that Jimenez was “working for the prosecutor’s office” and responsible for searches and seizures at Don Rafa’s property. Those facts supported the BIA’s factual finding that the primary motive was retaliation for interference with Don Rafa’s criminal enterprise. (See Background (leaflet), D. Nexus paragraphs.)

- Substantial‑evidence review matters. The court repeatedly explains that it reviews the BIA’s factual findings under the substantial‑evidence standard and will not reweigh competing inferences if reasonable adjudicators could reach the same conclusion. Because the record contains evidence pointing to a non‑political motive (personal/professional retaliation), the panel concluded the BIA’s finding that politics was subordinate is supported by substantial evidence. (See A. Standard of Review; D. Nexus.)

Why the leaflet and “snitch” accusations were not enough here

- The leaflet is probative but ambiguous in context. The brochure saying “death to snitches from Liberal Party” includes political language and could support a theory that persecutors viewed Petitioners as politically affiliated (an imputed political opinion theory). The opinion, however, treats that leaflet as one piece of evidence to be weighed against other, clearer indicators of motive. The court focused on what produced escalation to threats and near‑violence — namely Jimenez’s confrontation with Don Rafa’s property and the attackers’ framing of Jimenez as a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office connected to searches/seizures.

- The attackers’ own statements pointed to non‑political motive. The men explicitly accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office and tied the threats to Don Rafa and the property searches. Those specific, contemporaneous accusations more directly point to retaliation for Jimenez’s professional/personal interference with criminal activity than to punishment for political campaigning. The opinion treats those statements as decisive on motive. (See D. Nexus.)

- Timing and behavior undercut a politics‑central theory. The panel notes Petitioners continued their political activity despite earlier threats, and that escalation only followed Jimenez’s involvement in the property dispute. The court therefore read the chronology as indicating the property dispute (and its impact on Don Rafa’s revenue/security) was the predominant cause of the attacks. That factual narrative diminishes the probative weight of the leaflet as showing politics was a central reason.

- Imputed opinion alone does not satisfy the “central reason” requirement when other evidence shows a different predominant motive. Even if the leaflet could support an imputed‑opinion claim, the court reasonably concluded that, on the whole record, political opinion was merely “tangential and subordinate,” not a central reason. The panel’s approach is consistent with the mixed‑motive rule: multiple motives can exist, but the protected motive must be central — and the record here supported the BIA’s contrary factual finding.

Bottom line for future claims (how to think about similar facts)
- A political leaflet that labels someone a “snitch from [party]” can be important evidence of imputed political opinion, but it must be read in context. Contemporaneous persecutor statements, the sequence of events, and the persecutor’s apparent interest (e.g., protecting revenue/security) can outweigh the leaflet.
- To prevail on a mixed‑motive “one central reason” claim, an applicant needs stronger connecting evidence that politics was not merely referenced but a central driver of the harm (for example, clearer contemporaneous statements tying the violence to political activity, consistent pattern of politically motivated attacks, or other evidence showing the persecutor targeted the victim primarily because of political views).

Conclusion: The court articulated the correct legal standard for mixed‑motive cases, weighed the leaflet against more direct evidence pointing to retaliation for interference with criminal activity, and — under substantial‑evidence review — reasonably concluded politics was not a central reason for the threats.

### What level of reporting to authorities is required to prove the government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect, and does speaking with two officers suffice?
Question:
The opinion stresses the lack of formal reporting and cites a Seventh Circuit case about avoiding reliance on a single unhelpful officer. Here, petitioners spoke to two officers, one of whom told them police were contributing to the problem and advised them to leave. In the Tenth Circuit, is this kind of informal notice enough to show the state had an opportunity to respond? What evidence is necessary to establish futility or danger from reporting when the persecutor is allegedly tied to political actors, especially where the record also shows prior police search attempts?
Answer:
Short answer
Under the Tenth Circuit’s governing analysis (as applied in Jimenez v. Bondi), informal conversations with one or two local police officers are not, by themselves, enough to prove the government was “unable or unwilling” to protect. The applicant must show the government had notice and an opportunity to respond — or that reporting would have been futile or dangerous — with evidence that goes beyond a one‑off, unhelpful officer. Where the administrative record affirmatively shows police action (e.g., repeated searches/seizures), that undercuts a futility claim.

Why (legal standard and how the opinion applies)
- Legal standard. Jimenez reiterates the established inquiry: to show the government is unable or unwilling to protect, an applicant should show (1) the government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors and (2) the government offered protection to the applicant. Intertwined with that is whether the government had notice and an opportunity to respond. Singh v. Bondi and related authorities (cited in Jimenez) make clear that notice/reporting is a “key fact” and that failure to report may undercut—though not automatically doom—a claim unless the applicant can show reporting would have been futile or dangerous.  
- One‑off officer statements are weak evidence. Jimenez relies on the principle the Seventh Circuit explained in Osorio‑Morales: police apathy by an individual officer can be probative, but a single unhelpful conversation does not ordinarily prove the government is unwilling or unable to protect. Jimenez applies that reasoning to the petitioners’ testimony that they spoke with two officers who said “don’t seek trouble” and advised them to leave; the court held the record contained “nothing to show that this reflects the Colombian government’s position.”  
- The record controls. Jimenez stresses that, here, the record also showed prior police searches and seizures at the relevant property. Those government actions cut against a contention that the government was colluding with the persecutor or would be powerless to act; they undermine a futility inference.

What type of evidence will suffice (practical checklist)
To establish futility/danger or systemic inability/unwillingness in the Tenth Circuit you need more than an informal rebuff by local officers. Useful—and often necessary—evidence includes some combination of:
- proof that complaints were actually made to higher‑level or multiple police officials (written reports, complaint numbers, follow‑up correspondence);  
- evidence that reporting led to reprisals or punishment of complainants (so reporting was dangerous);  
- documentation of sustained police inaction or cover‑ups across the relevant jurisdiction (patterns showing systemic failure rather than isolated apathy);  
- evidence tying the persecutor to state actors (official positions, public records, credible reports) showing collusion or control;  
- independent country‑condition or human‑rights reports that corroborate systemic corruption/impunity in the applicant’s locality; and/or  
- evidence that the applicant attempted formal channels (protective custody, relocation assistance) and those measures failed.  

How Jimenez’s specific holdings affect your facts
- Two informal conversations where officers told the family to “leave” were not enough in Jimenez because (1) the record lacked evidence that those officers’ statements represented official policy or systemic failure, (2) petitioners did not show they sought help beyond their immediate neighborhood or through law‑enforcement contacts, and (3) the record affirmatively showed police had attempted searches/seizures at the implicated property—evidence inconsistent with collusion or government inability.  
- So, in the Tenth Circuit as applied there, speaking with two officers did not demonstrate the government had notice and an opportunity to respond, nor did it establish that reporting would have been futile or dangerous.

Bottom line
Informal conversations with one or two officers can be part of a protection claim, but standing alone they ordinarily will not meet the Tenth Circuit’s burden to show the government was unable or unwilling to protect. To succeed you need affirmative record evidence showing either (a) the government actually knew and had an opportunity to act but failed systemically, or (b) reporting would have been futile or dangerous — supported by documentary or corroborative proof, not just isolated officer comments. Jimenez illustrates that showing prior police efforts to address the problem may rebut a claim of government inability or unwillingness.

### Who bears the burden on internal relocation when there is no finding of past persecution, and did the panel apply the correct standard?
Question:
The court says even if petitioners had a fear of future persecution, they could reasonably relocate within Colombia, citing family safety and a prior three-week stay in the countryside. Under 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(3), when the alleged persecutor is a private actor and there’s no past persecution, the applicant usually bears the burden to show relocation is unreasonable. Did the panel analyze the regulatory reasonableness factors (e.g., ongoing risk, social ties, access to work, government reach), or was it enough to point to family members’ safety and the assailants’ demand to leave only the neighborhood?
Answer:
Short answer
- Who bears the burden: When there is no finding of past persecution (and therefore no presumption of future persecution), the applicant must show that internal relocation is not reasonable. The panel applied the governing Rivera‑Barrientos/8 C.F.R. framework that places the task of defeating relocation on the applicant in that posture.
- Did the panel apply the correct standard: Yes. The panel cited the correct precedent and regulation (Rivera‑Barrientos and 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13) and evaluated record evidence relevant to relocation and government protection. The court did not recite a checklist of every conceivable reasonableness factor, but it relied on the kinds of considerations the caselaw requires (family ties, prior safe relocation, scope of the threat, and government capacity), and its conclusion is supported by substantial evidence.

Explanation and how the opinion supports this
1) Burden of proof
- The opinion frames the relocation question under Rivera‑Barrientos and 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13, explaining that the government can rebut an applicant’s presumption of future persecution by showing either a fundamental change or that “the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country.” Jimenez v. Bondi, slip op. at § F (citing Rivera‑Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646 and 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)).  
- That formulation reflects the settled framework: where there is no past persecution (hence no presumption), the applicant must demonstrate that internal relocation would not reasonably avoid the risk. The panel’s discussion (saying “even if they had [a fear], the record supports the finding that they could reasonably relocate”) treats relocation as the obstacle the petitioners must overcome.

2) Whether the panel analyzed the relevant factors
- The panel expressly considered the central evidentiary points that bear on reasonableness:
  - Family and community ties and their safety: “Petitioners’ family and relatives continue to live in Colombia unharmed, and Jimenez’s father still works for the criminal justice system.” (Safe Relocation ¶.)  
  - A prior safe relocation episode: Petitioners “safely lived with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks.” (Safe Relocation ¶.)  
  - The geographic scope and content of the threats: the assailants “told Petitioners to move out of the neighborhood; they did not threaten the family to move out of the country.” (Safe Relocation ¶.)  
  - Government response and capacity: in an earlier section the court analyzed whether the Colombian government was unwilling or unable to protect petitioners (noting police had attempted searches at the property, that petitioners did not exhaust or fully pursue police remedies, and that isolated unhelpful officers did not establish systemic inability). (Government Protection ¶s.)
- The court therefore touched on the same types of considerations immigration adjudicators and courts have used to evaluate reasonableness of internal relocation (ongoing risk elsewhere, social ties, prior safe relocation, and government ability/response), even though it did not set out a point‑by‑point regulatory checklist in the Safe Relocation paragraph.

3) Is the panel’s analysis legally sufficient?
- Under the Tenth Circuit’s substantial‑evidence review the BIA’s factual findings stand unless the record compels a contrary conclusion. The panel tied its relocation conclusion to concrete record facts (family safe, three‑week countryside stay, the limited geographic character of the threats, and evidence the government had acted against the criminal property). Those factual predicates are the kinds of evidence Rivera‑Barrientos and the regulation require.  
- A petitioner could argue the BIA/IJ should have made a more granular analysis of each regulatory factor (workability, administrative/economic burdens, broader evidence of government reach or collusion). The panel noted petitioners raised such arguments but treated them as haphazardly briefed and unsupported by the record. Given that posture, the panel’s summary reliance on the cited facts was a permissible application of the governing standard.

Bottom line
The panel applied the correct legal standard (Rivera‑Barrientos/8 C.F.R. framework) and placed on the petitioners the practical burden of showing relocation was unreasonable in the absence of past persecution. It analyzed the central relocation‑related facts (family safety, a prior three‑week countryside stay, the limited scope of the threats, and government action), and—under substantial‑evidence review—its conclusion that internal relocation was reasonable is supported by the opinion’s factual findings.

### Was any ‘particular social group’ claim forfeited, and could a PSG like ‘perceived informants to law enforcement’ be viable in the Tenth Circuit?
Question:
The court limits the nexus analysis to political opinion because petitioners did not brief PSG. Does failing to brief a PSG theory to the BIA or on petition for review forfeit it entirely? Substantively, has the Tenth Circuit recognized (or rejected) PSGs based on being informants, witnesses, or perceived “snitches” to law enforcement, provided particularity and social distinction are shown? If such a PSG were pursued, what evidence would be needed to meet those requirements in Colombia?
Answer:
Short answer
- Forfeiture: Yes—under Jimenez the Tenth Circuit treated the petitioners’ failure to brief a particular‑social‑group (PSG) theory as forfeiture and therefore confined its nexus review to political opinion. (Jimenez v. Bondi, No. 24‑9573, slip op. at § II.D (10th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025) (“Petitioners . . . did not brief any arguments regarding the existence of their particular social group . . . Accordingly, we limit our review . . .”).)
- Viability of a “perceived informant/snitch” PSG in Tenth Circuit: Jimenez does not decide that question. The panel declined to reach PSG issues because they were not litigated; it therefore neither recognized nor rejected a PSG based on being (or being perceived as) informants. (Id. § II.D.)
- If you want to press a “perceived informant/snitch” PSG in the Tenth Circuit, you should: (1) preserve and brief the PSG before the IJ and BIA; and (2) develop the record to satisfy the circuit’s PSG elements and the related asylum elements (particularity and social distinction; nexus; past persecution or well‑founded fear; government protection). Jimenez illustrates the kinds of evidence the court expects on nexus and government protection and the weaknesses that persuaded the court to deny relief.

Explanation and what Jimenez shows about the proof you’d need

1) Forfeiture / preservation
- Jimenez treats the absence of a PSG argument as dispositive: because petitioners “did not brief any arguments regarding the existence of their particular social group nor did they establish their membership in such a group,” the court limited its review to the political‑opinion theory. Jimenez, § II.D. That is the practical rule: if you want the BIA or a court of appeals to consider a PSG theory, you must present it to the agency and brief it on review. Failure to do so will likely be treated as forfeiture.

2) What Jimenez does (and does not) decide about “snitch”/informant PSGs
- The panel does not address whether a PSG defined as “perceived informants to law enforcement” is cognizable in the Tenth Circuit. The record contained threats calling Petitioners “snitches,” and the court relied on those threats in its factual assessment of motive (nexus), but it expressly declined to reach a PSG analysis because petitioners never advanced one. Jimenez, § II.D. So the opinion is not precedent for or against the viability of such a PSG; it only reinforces that the theory must be litigated and developed before the agency and on appeal.

3) Evidence you should develop to make a “perceived informant/snitch” PSG viable (guided by Jimenez’s analysis)
- Preserve the theory: assert the PSG at the IJ, before the BIA, and brief it on any petition for review. Jimenez shows the court will refuse to consider an unbriefed theory. (Id.)
- Particularity and social distinction: although Jimenez does not analyze these elements, the court’s refusal to consider PSGs means you must plead and prove the PSG elements the Tenth Circuit expects elsewhere—i.e., show that the group is defined with sufficient particularity and is socially distinct. Concretely, that means drafting a PSG definition that is neither amorphous nor merely a description of a risk and introducing country‑and-society evidence supporting that people in Colombia (or the persecutors) recognize and target a discrete class of “people perceived to be informants.”
- Nexus: develop direct evidence that the persecutors targeted the applicants because they were (or were perceived to be) informants. Jimenez suggests what helps on nexus: contemporaneous statements by persecutors calling the applicant a “snitch,” written threats (e.g., the brochure saying “death to snitches”), testimony that attackers linked the applicant to the prosecutor’s office, and other actions that tie the harm to the informant label. Jimenez relied on a man accusing Jimenez of being a “snitch” and a brochure saying “death to snitches” as record facts—those are the kinds of facts you’ll want, but here they were not developed into a PSG claim. (See Jimenez, Background A; § II.D.)
- Persecution severity: if you are relying on “snitch” status, remember Jimenez reiterates the court’s approach to persecution—threats alone are often insufficient. The opinion denied asylum because threats and psychological harm did not, in the aggregate, meet the circuit’s persecution standard. So show physical harms, pattern evidence of targeted violence against perceived informants, or a cumulative course of conduct that establishes persecution under Tenth Circuit precedent. (Jimenez, § II.C.)
- Government protection / inability or unwillingness to protect: Jimenez stresses the importance of reporting to authorities and showing the government’s response. Petitioners’ limited reporting and the record that Colombian authorities had attempted searches on the property undercut their protection claim. To show the state is unable or unwilling to protect perceived informants, adduce evidence such as police complaints and their outcomes; documentation of police inaction, collusion, or threats against informants; country reports showing systemic failure to protect informants; testimony that reporting would be futile or dangerous; and records showing the government knew about the threats but failed to act. Jimenez emphasizes that a single unhelpful officer is weak evidence of systemic failure unless you can show broader governmental inability or unwillingness. (See Jimenez, § II.E.)
- Relocation and future fear: develop proof that internal relocation is not reasonable—evidence that threats follow perceived informants, that family/relatives are not safe, or that the persecutors operate nationally. Jimenez found relocation feasible because family remained in Colombia and petitioners had relocated domestically for a time without harm; you must counter that kind of record if you claim relocation is impossible. (Jimenez, § II.F.)

Bottom line
- Procedurally: failure to brief a PSG at the agency and on review will, as Jimenez demonstrates, foreclose appellate consideration of that theory.
- Substantively: Jimenez does not accept or reject a “perceived informant/snitch” PSG for the Tenth Circuit. If you want to pursue that PSG, preserve and brief it and build a record that (1) defines the group with particularity and shows social distinction, (2) ties the harm to the informant label (statements, threats, documents), (3) demonstrates persecution severity beyond mere threats (or cumulative severe treatment), and (4) proves the government cannot or will not protect the claimant (police complaints and their outcomes, country‑condition evidence, or evidence that reporting would be futile/dangerous). Jimenez highlights each of these practical evidentiary weaknesses when they are not present.

### When does opposition to criminal organizations or corruption count as a ‘political opinion’ for asylum purposes?
Question:
The persecutor here is described as a guerrilla/narco leader allegedly aligned with the sitting president. The court characterized the motive as economic retaliation for interfering with a criminal enterprise, not political opinion. In Tenth Circuit doctrine, when can anti-corruption activity, cooperation with prosecutors, or opposition to criminal groups be treated as an actual or imputed political opinion, rather than a personal or economic dispute? What additional facts would typically be necessary to tilt this into the political-opinion category?
Answer:
Short answer
Under Tenth Circuit law (as applied in Jimenez v. Bondi), opposition to corruption or criminal groups will qualify as a “political opinion” for asylum only when the record shows that the persecutor targeted the applicant at least in part because of that opinion (or the persecutor’s perception of it), and that motive was a central — not merely incidental or subordinate — reason for the mistreatment. Jimenez denied relief because the threats escalated only after the applicant interfered with the group’s revenue/security (a property dispute) and the record supported the BIA’s finding that economic retaliation, not politics, was the predominant motive.

Legal standard (what the court applied)
- Nexus: To prevail on a politics-based asylum claim an applicant must show persecution “on account of” political opinion — the political opinion must be “at least one central reason” for the persecution. (Jimenez v. Bondi citing Rivera‑Barrientos, 666 F.3d at 646; Dallakoti.)  
- Mixed motives allowed, but the protected ground cannot be “incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate” to other motives. (Jimenez citing Rivera‑Barrientos and Dallakoti; O.C.V. referenced for mixed‑motive principle.)  
- Fact‑intensive inquiry: courts look to timing, the persecutor’s statements, who was targeted and why, and the overall context to determine whether political opinion was a central motive. (Jimenez discusses timing and the content of threats and finds substantial evidence supporting the BIA’s conclusion.)

How Jimenez illustrates the boundary
- Timing and causal sequence mattered: Petitioners had received politically‑tinged threats earlier but the threats escalated only after Jimenez intervened in the rented property associated with the narco leader. The court relied on that sequence to infer economic/operational motive rather than political retribution.  
- Persecutor statements were ambiguous/consistent with nonpolitical motive: although assailants called Jimenez a “snitch for the prosecutor’s office,” the BIA and Tenth Circuit read that as blaming him for interfering with searches/seizures (i.e., harming the group’s revenue and security), not as punishment for political campaigning or party activity. The court concluded Petitioners’ political activity was “merely tangential and subordinate” to the principal motive.  
- Result: because the record supported a predominant economic/personal motive, the political‑opinion nexus failed.

What kinds of facts typically push a case into the political‑opinion category
To make opposition to criminal groups or cooperation with prosecutors look like an actual or imputed political opinion (rather than a personal/economic dispute), applicants generally need stronger, clearer evidence linking the mistreatment to politics. Typical helpful facts include one or more of the following (all illustrated by the doctrine the court applied in Jimenez and its cited authorities):

1. Clear contemporaneous statements by persecutors tying the attack to political belief or affiliation
   - explicit threats such as “we’ll kill you for supporting [opposition party/candidate]” or “for opposing our political project,” or other language indicating the target is being punished for political views rather than for disrupting revenue.

2. Temporal and causal linkage to political activity (not just to a private dispute)
   - escalation of threats that directly follows public political acts (e.g., campaigning, public anti‑corruption complaints, public denouncements) rather than an isolated private dispute over property or money.

3. Evidence that the persecutor treats opposition/cooperation as political
   - e.g., the persecutor is acting to suppress political opposition or to protect a political faction; the criminal group functions as a political actor or paramilitary that targets people for their political stance.

4. Pattern or breadth of targeting
   - other members of the same political party, activists, or public anti‑corruption figures are targeted in similar ways; the applicant is not a unique target because of a private quarrel over money.

5. Documented public anti‑corruption acts or formal cooperation
   - filing formal corruption complaints, providing testimony to prosecutors, public legal work showing a political or civic posture, especially when the persecutor later singles out the applicant for those acts.

6. Imputed opinion evidence framed as political
   - evidence that the persecutor perceived the applicant as supporting/working for the government or political opposition (and persecutor’s statements reflect that perception), not merely that the persecutor viewed the applicant as interfering with a business transaction. (Jimenez shows that a “snitch” accusation can cut either way; context decides which.)

7. Corroborating documentary or country‑context evidence
   - reports showing the criminal group’s political role, political alignments of actors, or a pattern of politically‑motivated violence in the area that links the group’s actions to politics rather than only criminal profit.

Practical application to Jimenez‑type facts
- In Jimenez the decisive traits were: (a) the real escalation followed the property dispute; (b) statements about being a “snitch” were tied in the record to searches/seizures (harm to the group’s operations) rather than to campaign activity; and (c) Petitioners continued political campaigning earlier without similar escalation. Those features supported an economic/retaliatory reading and doomed the political‑opinion nexus. To prevail, Petitioners would have needed clearer evidence that the narco leader targeted them because of their Liberal‑Party activity or because he perceived them as political opponents—e.g., contemporaneous threats explicitly referencing their party or campaign, evidence that other campaigners were targeted for the same political reasons, or proof the persecutor treated cooperation with prosecutors as a political act to be punished.

Bottom line
Under Tenth Circuit precedent as applied in Jimenez, opposition to corruption or cooperation with prosecutors can qualify as a political opinion, but only when the persecutor’s motive is shown to be political in character and to be “at least one central reason” for the harm. Mere interference with a criminal enterprise (especially where the harm follows a private dispute over property or revenue) will more likely be treated as nonpolitical retaliation unless the applicant can introduce concrete facts tying the mistreatment to political beliefs, party activity, public anti‑corruption acts, or explicit persecutor statements showing a political motive.

### Why did withholding of removal ‘necessarily’ fail once asylum failed, and are there scenarios where withholding could still succeed?
Question:
The panel concludes that because petitioners failed to show a well-founded fear for asylum, they necessarily fail the higher standard for withholding. I understand withholding has a higher likelihood-of-persecution standard but different bars and no discretion. In what circumstances could a petitioner be denied asylum yet still prevail on withholding (e.g., one-year bar, discretionary denial, firm resettlement), and why were those not in play here? Also, since the children were derivative on asylum, does the analysis change for them on withholding, which does not allow derivatives?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel’s “necessarily fails” statement rests on two things the opinion actually shows: (1) asylum is a lower, discretionary form of relief that requires a well‑founded fear (the refugee standard), while withholding of removal imposes a higher, mandatory burden (a “more‑likely‑than‑not” likelihood of persecution); and (2) the IJ/BIA rejected the petitioners’ claims on the core factual and legal merits—no past persecution, no nexus to a protected ground, government protection available, and internal relocation feasible. Because those same factual findings make it impossible, on the record here, for petitioners to carry the higher withholding burden, the court concluded that withholding necessarily fails. Jimenez v. Bondi, slip op. §§ II.B, II.C–F, III.

When asylum denial can be compatible with a possible withholding grant
The opinion’s logic does not mean withholding can never survive a denial of asylum. There are recurring factual and legal situations (not present in Jimenez) in which an asylum denial and a successful withholding claim can coexist:

- Procedural or asylum‑only bars or discretionary denials: asylum is discretionary (the opinion cites that asylum “may be granted” and requires proof by a preponderance). An applicant can be found ineligible for asylum on procedural grounds or denied in the exercise of discretion while still proving the higher, non‑discretionary likelihood of persecution required for withholding. The opinion itself recognizes asylum’s discretionary character (II.B).  
- Different legal thresholds: because withholding requires a higher probability of future persecution, an applicant who barely fails to meet the well‑founded‑fear standard might still meet the “more‑likely‑than‑not” standard in a different record (again, a possibility the court does not foreclose as a general matter; but Jimenez’s record contained affirmative findings that made that impossible).  
- Separate factual findings: if an asylum denial rests on evidence of discretionary factors or on a bar unrelated to the factual likelihood of future persecution (for example, firm resettlement or asylum‑specific statutory bars), those grounds may not preclude withholding if the factual record independently shows a clear probability of persecution.

Why those scenarios did not apply in Jimenez
Jimenez is different. The IJ found—crediting testimony—that the petitioners nonetheless failed on the core elements: their mistreatment did not rise to past persecution, the predominant motive was not political (no nexus), they did not show the government was unwilling or unable to protect them, and internal relocation was reasonable. Those are the very factual predicates that both asylum and withholding require (see II.C–F). Because the BIA/IJ decided the petitioners lacked the underlying factual showing that would support a prediction of persecution, there was no separate procedural or discretionary ground in the record that could explain an asylum denial while leaving open a withholding claim. The panel therefore concluded the higher withholding standard could not be met on this record (III).

What about the children who were derivatives?
Two short points from the opinion:

- As the opinion notes, the two minor sons proceeded as derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s asylum application (I.B). Withholding of removal, unlike asylum, cannot be obtained simply as a derivative status; it is a separate, non‑discretionary protection tied to an individual’s own likelihood of persecution.  
- Practically, that means the children would need independent evidence that they personally meet the withholding standard. Because the BIA/IJ rejected the family’s core factual showing (no persecution, no nexus, government protection/relocation feasible), there was no record basis for treating the children separately as meeting the higher withholding probability. Thus the court’s denial as to withholding applies to the family on the record before it.

Bottom line
The court’s “necessarily fails” line flows from the opinion’s substantive, adverse factual findings that defeat both asylum (the lower, discretionary well‑founded‑fear inquiry) and withholding (the higher, mandatory “more‑likely‑than‑not” inquiry). A different procedural posture (asylum denied on an asylum‑only bar or for discretionary reasons) or a different record could allow withholding to survive an asylum denial, but those circumstances plainly were not present in Jimenez.
