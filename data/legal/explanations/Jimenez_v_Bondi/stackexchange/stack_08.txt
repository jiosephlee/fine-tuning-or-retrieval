Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Jimenez v. Bondi

### Why did withholding of removal ‘necessarily’ fail once asylum failed, and are there scenarios where withholding could still succeed?
Question:
The panel concludes that because petitioners failed to show a well-founded fear for asylum, they necessarily fail the higher standard for withholding. I understand withholding has a higher likelihood-of-persecution standard but different bars and no discretion. In what circumstances could a petitioner be denied asylum yet still prevail on withholding (e.g., one-year bar, discretionary denial, firm resettlement), and why were those not in play here? Also, since the children were derivative on asylum, does the analysis change for them on withholding, which does not allow derivatives?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel’s “necessarily fails” statement rests on two things the opinion actually shows: (1) asylum is a lower, discretionary form of relief that requires a well‑founded fear (the refugee standard), while withholding of removal imposes a higher, mandatory burden (a “more‑likely‑than‑not” likelihood of persecution); and (2) the IJ/BIA rejected the petitioners’ claims on the core factual and legal merits—no past persecution, no nexus to a protected ground, government protection available, and internal relocation feasible. Because those same factual findings make it impossible, on the record here, for petitioners to carry the higher withholding burden, the court concluded that withholding necessarily fails. Jimenez v. Bondi, slip op. §§ II.B, II.C–F, III.

When asylum denial can be compatible with a possible withholding grant
The opinion’s logic does not mean withholding can never survive a denial of asylum. There are recurring factual and legal situations (not present in Jimenez) in which an asylum denial and a successful withholding claim can coexist:

- Procedural or asylum‑only bars or discretionary denials: asylum is discretionary (the opinion cites that asylum “may be granted” and requires proof by a preponderance). An applicant can be found ineligible for asylum on procedural grounds or denied in the exercise of discretion while still proving the higher, non‑discretionary likelihood of persecution required for withholding. The opinion itself recognizes asylum’s discretionary character (II.B).  
- Different legal thresholds: because withholding requires a higher probability of future persecution, an applicant who barely fails to meet the well‑founded‑fear standard might still meet the “more‑likely‑than‑not” standard in a different record (again, a possibility the court does not foreclose as a general matter; but Jimenez’s record contained affirmative findings that made that impossible).  
- Separate factual findings: if an asylum denial rests on evidence of discretionary factors or on a bar unrelated to the factual likelihood of future persecution (for example, firm resettlement or asylum‑specific statutory bars), those grounds may not preclude withholding if the factual record independently shows a clear probability of persecution.

Why those scenarios did not apply in Jimenez
Jimenez is different. The IJ found—crediting testimony—that the petitioners nonetheless failed on the core elements: their mistreatment did not rise to past persecution, the predominant motive was not political (no nexus), they did not show the government was unwilling or unable to protect them, and internal relocation was reasonable. Those are the very factual predicates that both asylum and withholding require (see II.C–F). Because the BIA/IJ decided the petitioners lacked the underlying factual showing that would support a prediction of persecution, there was no separate procedural or discretionary ground in the record that could explain an asylum denial while leaving open a withholding claim. The panel therefore concluded the higher withholding standard could not be met on this record (III).

What about the children who were derivatives?
Two short points from the opinion:

- As the opinion notes, the two minor sons proceeded as derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s asylum application (I.B). Withholding of removal, unlike asylum, cannot be obtained simply as a derivative status; it is a separate, non‑discretionary protection tied to an individual’s own likelihood of persecution.  
- Practically, that means the children would need independent evidence that they personally meet the withholding standard. Because the BIA/IJ rejected the family’s core factual showing (no persecution, no nexus, government protection/relocation feasible), there was no record basis for treating the children separately as meeting the higher withholding probability. Thus the court’s denial as to withholding applies to the family on the record before it.

Bottom line
The court’s “necessarily fails” line flows from the opinion’s substantive, adverse factual findings that defeat both asylum (the lower, discretionary well‑founded‑fear inquiry) and withholding (the higher, mandatory “more‑likely‑than‑not” inquiry). A different procedural posture (asylum denied on an asylum‑only bar or for discretionary reasons) or a different record could allow withholding to survive an asylum denial, but those circumstances plainly were not present in Jimenez.
