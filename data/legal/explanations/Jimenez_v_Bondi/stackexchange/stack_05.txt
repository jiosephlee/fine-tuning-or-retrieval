### Why are threats and psychological harm deemed insufficient to constitute persecution in the Tenth Circuit?
Question:
The court says 'threats alone are insufficient,' and it downplays psychological harms like insomnia and panic attacks. Some circuits have recognized death threats as persecution in certain contexts. What is the Tenth Circuit’s doctrinal basis for requiring more than threats and psychological harm, and are there circumstances in this circuit where threats alone can qualify (e.g., especially menacing death threats, accompanied by specific targeting or evidence of capability)?
Answer:
Short answer
The Tenth Circuit treats threats and attendant psychological harms as insufficient for “persecution” unless they are part of a larger pattern of objectively offensive harm. Its doctrinal basis is settled precedent requiring “the infliction of suffering or harm … more than just restrictions or threats,” and the court will only find persecution from threats when the threats are sufficiently severe, credible, and accompanied by other indicia (e.g., physical harm, detention, a pattern that in the aggregate amounts to severe mistreatment, lack of state protection, or an unavoidable risk on return). In this opinion the court denied asylum because Petitioners (1) suffered no physical harm, (2) conceded psychological harms only, and (3) only had threats that, even taken together, did not reach the level of persecution under Tenth Circuit cases (Karki, Wiransane, Brandy, Chhetri, etc.).

What the opinion actually says and why
- Legal standard: The court repeats the Tenth Circuit rule that “a finding of persecution requires the infliction of suffering or harm … and must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty.” (Opinion § II.C, quoting Wiransane v. Ashcroft.) The court also emphasizes that courts consider incidents collectively, but the cumulative effect must still amount to persecution (Ritonga cited).
- Precedent cited: The opinion cites Karki v. Holder for the principle that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution” (Opinion § II.C). It also cites Brandy and Chhetri for the proposition that psychological stress, fear, insomnia, or a few telephone threats typically do not meet the persecution threshold in the Tenth Circuit (Opinion § II.C; see also discussion of Carias-Mejia and Chhetri).
- Application to this case: The court notes key factual points that drive its conclusion:
  - Petitioners “conceded … they were not physically harmed” and reported “no lasting physical harm.” (II.C.)
  - Duarte’s insomnia and her son’s panic attacks are acknowledged but described as “fears stemming from their received threats” that “are insufficient to rise to the level of persecution.” (II.C.)
  - The court observes that the Tenth Circuit has “rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone.” (II.C.)
  - Because the threats here were not accompanied by physical injury, detention, torture, or other objectively severe mistreatment, the BIA’s finding—that the harm did not amount to past persecution—is supported by substantial evidence. (II.C.)

When can threats/psychological harm be enough (within the Tenth Circuit)?
The opinion does not draw a bright line that threats can never be persecution. From its reasoning and cited cases you can extract the court’s approach:
- Threats may contribute to a persecution finding when they are part of a pattern of conduct that, taken together, amount to the infliction of unlawful suffering (the court expressly considers cumulative effects; Ritonga). The difference is that the aggregate must cross the “more than threats” threshold.
- The court’s discussion implies that “extreme fact patterns” (which the opinion acknowledges exist in prior Tenth Circuit rulings) could support persecution even in the absence of permanent physical injury — but the opinion also stresses that the instant facts fall short. The court says it has “rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone,” suggesting that only unusually menacing, credible, and effective threats (often coupled with other objective harms or clear inability/unwillingness of the state to protect) would suffice. (II.C.)
- Other relevant considerations the court uses (and that would matter if threats were to qualify): the credibility/severity of the threat, whether the persecutor has the means and intent to carry it out, whether there is a pattern of escalation or actual violence against others, nexus to a protected ground, and demonstrable government inability or unwillingness to protect (II.C, II.D, II.E).

Practical summary for litigation
- Don’t rely on subjective fear or isolated insomnia/panic claims alone. The Tenth Circuit expects objective indicia of harm or a clear, continuous pattern of menacing conduct.
- Bolster threat-based claims with evidence showing (as available): actual physical attacks against the applicant or close associates, credible evidence the persecutor has the capability and intent to carry out death threats, a pattern of escalating acts, government refusal or systemic inability to protect, and a clear nexus to a protected ground. Absent such corroboration, the court will likely treat threats and consequent psychological harms as insufficient (as it did here).

Bottom line
The Tenth Circuit requires more than threats and attendant psychological distress because its precedent demands “the infliction of suffering or harm … more than just … threats” (Wiransane; Karki). Threats can be part of a successful persecution claim, but only if they are sufficiently severe, credible, and tied to other objective indicators (or an inability of the state to protect) so that the cumulative effect amounts to persecution. This opinion applied those principles and found the threats and psychological harms here fell short.
