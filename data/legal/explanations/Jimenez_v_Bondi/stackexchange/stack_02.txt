Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Jimenez v. Bondi

### Why weren’t armed threats and the attempted grabbing of the child enough to constitute past persecution?
Question:
The panel emphasizes that “threats alone are insufficient,” but the facts include two men holding Jimenez at gunpoint, later assaulting an employee while searching for the family, and a man putting his arm around the son and covering his mouth. The court notes petitioners conceded no lasting physical harm. In Tenth Circuit asylum law, how are incidents like being held at gunpoint or an apparent attempted abduction treated—are these still categorized as mere threats absent injury? What factors tip such episodes into “persecution” when aggregated?
Answer:
Short answer: Because under Tenth Circuit law (as articulated in Jimenez), isolated or episodic armed threats, an assault on a third party, and an apparent attempted grab of a child can still be treated as “threats” that do not by themselves amount to past persecution—especially where the applicants conceded no lasting physical injury, the psychological harms are not shown to be severe or enduring, the incidents were not shown to be so extreme or continuous as to be “offensive” suffering, and the record did not show the mistreatment was motivated centrally by a protected ground or that the government could not protect them.

Why the panel ruled this way (legal standard and its application)
- Persecution requires more than “restrictions or threats to life and liberty.” As Jimenez quotes Wiransane, persecution “must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty.” The court therefore treats threats as a distinct (and often insufficient) category of harm. Jimenez expressly repeats the settled rule that “threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution.” (citing Karki and the panel’s own discussion).
- Cumulative analysis, but with a threshold. The court does not look only at each incident in isolation; it considers the aggregate impact of multiple incidents (Ritonga). Even so, the cumulative harms must reach the level of suffering or harm that is “offensive” under the statute and more than “sporadic threats” (Karki; Brandy).
- Physical injury and lasting effects matter. Jimenez notes petitioners conceded they suffered no physical harm or lasting physical injury. Although physical harm is not strictly required, the court observes that where there is no lasting physical harm, courts have been reluctant to find persecution based on threats plus psychological stress alone. The opinion says the lived fears and stress were “insufficient to rise to the level of persecution” and that the court has “rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone.” (citing Brandy).
- Context and motive (nexus) matter. Even if the conduct is serious, it must be shown to be “on account of” a protected ground (political opinion, etc.). Jimenez found substantial evidence that the dominant motive was a personal/professional conflict with Don Rafa (threats tied to the property dispute and to interfering with criminal revenue), not principally the family’s political activities. Mixed motives are possible, but a protected ground must be at least one central reason.
- Government protection and relocation are also relevant. Jimenez emphasized that government unwillingness or inability to protect the applicants and the impossibility of internal relocation can push harms toward persecution. The record here, however, contained little showing that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to help.

Putting those points against the facts
- Two men held Mr. Jimenez at gunpoint, later assaulted an employee while searching for the family, and a man briefly grabbed/covered the son’s mouth. Those are serious factual events. But the panel relied on (1) petitioners’ concession of no lasting physical harm, (2) the characterization of much of the conduct as threats rather than completed physical injury, (3) the lack of evidence that the persecution was centrally motivated by a protected ground, and (4) limited evidence that the government could not protect them. Under the court’s precedent, that combination made the incidents insufficient, even in the aggregate, to meet the past-persecution threshold.
- The court also points to prior Tenth Circuit decisions where even more extreme fact patterns were rejected (Chhetri; Carias-Mejia), reinforcing that threats-plus-transient trauma often fall short.

What sorts of facts tip episodes into “persecution” (drawing on Jimenez’s framework)
- Severity and permanence: physical injury, prolonged detention, torture, or other harms causing lasting physical impairment or severe, enduring psychological trauma.
- Frequency and continuity: a sustained campaign of serious abuse rather than isolated or sporadic threats/encounters.
- Forced loss of liberty or forced displacement (e.g., kidnapping, sustained custody, or an effective expulsion from one’s home).
- Nexus: evidence that a protected ground (political opinion, etc.) was at least one central reason for the mistreatment.
- Government inability/unwillingness to protect: credible evidence that reporting would be futile or dangerous, or that state actors were complicit—especially where the applicant actually sought help and was rebuffed systemically (not just by a single officer).
When these factors appear—particularly together—the Tenth Circuit has been more willing to find that an applicant suffered past persecution.

Bottom line
Jimenez treats the armed encounters and the attempted grabbing as serious but, given the applicants’ concessions, the lack of lasting physical injury, the court’s threshold that threats-plus-transient psychological harm usually do not equal persecution, the weaker showing on nexus, and limited proof the state could not protect them, those episodes did not meet the Tenth Circuit’s legal standard for past persecution in that case.
