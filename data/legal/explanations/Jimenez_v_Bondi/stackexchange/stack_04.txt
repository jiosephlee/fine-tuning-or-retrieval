Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Jimenez v. Bondi

### What level of reporting to authorities is required to prove the government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect, and does speaking with two officers suffice?
Question:
The opinion stresses the lack of formal reporting and cites a Seventh Circuit case about avoiding reliance on a single unhelpful officer. Here, petitioners spoke to two officers, one of whom told them police were contributing to the problem and advised them to leave. In the Tenth Circuit, is this kind of informal notice enough to show the state had an opportunity to respond? What evidence is necessary to establish futility or danger from reporting when the persecutor is allegedly tied to political actors, especially where the record also shows prior police search attempts?
Answer:
Short answer
Under the Tenth Circuit’s governing analysis (as applied in Jimenez v. Bondi), informal conversations with one or two local police officers are not, by themselves, enough to prove the government was “unable or unwilling” to protect. The applicant must show the government had notice and an opportunity to respond — or that reporting would have been futile or dangerous — with evidence that goes beyond a one‑off, unhelpful officer. Where the administrative record affirmatively shows police action (e.g., repeated searches/seizures), that undercuts a futility claim.

Why (legal standard and how the opinion applies)
- Legal standard. Jimenez reiterates the established inquiry: to show the government is unable or unwilling to protect, an applicant should show (1) the government stopped or tried to stop the persecutors and (2) the government offered protection to the applicant. Intertwined with that is whether the government had notice and an opportunity to respond. Singh v. Bondi and related authorities (cited in Jimenez) make clear that notice/reporting is a “key fact” and that failure to report may undercut—though not automatically doom—a claim unless the applicant can show reporting would have been futile or dangerous.  
- One‑off officer statements are weak evidence. Jimenez relies on the principle the Seventh Circuit explained in Osorio‑Morales: police apathy by an individual officer can be probative, but a single unhelpful conversation does not ordinarily prove the government is unwilling or unable to protect. Jimenez applies that reasoning to the petitioners’ testimony that they spoke with two officers who said “don’t seek trouble” and advised them to leave; the court held the record contained “nothing to show that this reflects the Colombian government’s position.”  
- The record controls. Jimenez stresses that, here, the record also showed prior police searches and seizures at the relevant property. Those government actions cut against a contention that the government was colluding with the persecutor or would be powerless to act; they undermine a futility inference.

What type of evidence will suffice (practical checklist)
To establish futility/danger or systemic inability/unwillingness in the Tenth Circuit you need more than an informal rebuff by local officers. Useful—and often necessary—evidence includes some combination of:
- proof that complaints were actually made to higher‑level or multiple police officials (written reports, complaint numbers, follow‑up correspondence);  
- evidence that reporting led to reprisals or punishment of complainants (so reporting was dangerous);  
- documentation of sustained police inaction or cover‑ups across the relevant jurisdiction (patterns showing systemic failure rather than isolated apathy);  
- evidence tying the persecutor to state actors (official positions, public records, credible reports) showing collusion or control;  
- independent country‑condition or human‑rights reports that corroborate systemic corruption/impunity in the applicant’s locality; and/or  
- evidence that the applicant attempted formal channels (protective custody, relocation assistance) and those measures failed.  

How Jimenez’s specific holdings affect your facts
- Two informal conversations where officers told the family to “leave” were not enough in Jimenez because (1) the record lacked evidence that those officers’ statements represented official policy or systemic failure, (2) petitioners did not show they sought help beyond their immediate neighborhood or through law‑enforcement contacts, and (3) the record affirmatively showed police had attempted searches/seizures at the implicated property—evidence inconsistent with collusion or government inability.  
- So, in the Tenth Circuit as applied there, speaking with two officers did not demonstrate the government had notice and an opportunity to respond, nor did it establish that reporting would have been futile or dangerous.

Bottom line
Informal conversations with one or two officers can be part of a protection claim, but standing alone they ordinarily will not meet the Tenth Circuit’s burden to show the government was unable or unwilling to protect. To succeed you need affirmative record evidence showing either (a) the government actually knew and had an opportunity to act but failed systemically, or (b) reporting would have been futile or dangerous — supported by documentary or corroborative proof, not just isolated officer comments. Jimenez illustrates that showing prior police efforts to address the problem may rebut a claim of government inability or unwillingness.
