Law Stack Exchange Q&A: Bruce_Cohen_v_Consilio_LLC

### Why did the court read MPWA § 181.101 to allow only the Commissioner—not employees—to seek average daily wage penalties?
Question:
I'm struggling with the interplay between § 181.101(a) and § 181.171, subd. 1. Section 181.171 says an employee can sue for violations and recover 'civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated,' plus 'other appropriate relief.' Yet the panel holds only the Commissioner can 'charge and collect' the average daily wage penalty in § 181.101(a). Why doesn't § 181.171's broad remedial language let a private plaintiff recover those penalties directly? Is the court applying a specific-controls-the-general canon, or is there some other rule of statutory construction doing the work?
Answer:
Short answer: The panel treated § 181.101 as a statutory scheme that plainly and unambiguously vests the power to demand, charge, and collect the MPWA “average daily wage” penalty in the Commissioner alone; § 181.171’s general authorization for private suits does not override that specific procedural/collection mechanism. The court relied on the plain text of § 181.101, the principle that a specific statutory procedure governs over a general remedial provision, and legislative drafting choices (expressio unius/“legislature knows how” reasoning) to reach that result.

Why — step by step (with the opinion’s specific points)

1) Text controls. The court starts from the statute’s plain language. Section 181.101(a) expressly contemplates the Commissioner’s role: the Commissioner “may serve a demand for payment on behalf of an employee,” and if the employer does not pay within ten days, “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty in the amount of the employee’s average daily earnings.” The opinion quotes this language and treats it as dispositive: because “penalties are only available if the Commissioner first ‘serve[s] a demand for payment’” and thereafter “the commissioner may charge and collect . . . a penalty,” only the Commissioner may pursue that penalty. (Op. Part II.A, quoting Minn. Stat. §§ 181.101(a), 181.171.)

2) The general-remedy provision does not rewrite a specific enforcement scheme. Section 181.171( subd. 1) authorizes a person to bring a civil action for violations listed in the MPWA and provides that an employer “shall also be liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief including but not limited to injunctive relief,” and that an employer “is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.” The court read that language as recognizing private enforcement of the substantive rights and any penalties that a violated section itself authorizes to be “provided for in the section violated.” But because § 181.101 itself sets out a Commissioner-centered enforcement/collection procedure for its penalty, § 181.171 cannot be read to let private plaintiffs skip the Commissioner step. The court therefore treated § 181.101 as the specific provision that controls the availability and mechanism for the average-daily-wage penalty. (Op. Part II.A.)

3) Canons and legislative drafting choices: the court relied on two related ideas.
   - Plain-meaning / specific-controls-the-general: the opinion applies the ordinary rule that if a statute’s language is plain, the court gives it effect (citing Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.). It also applies the practical point that a specific statutory enforcement mechanism governs over a more general remedial authorization. The court’s analysis is framed as giving effect to the specific wording of § 181.101 rather than allowing § 181.171’s broad remedial language to displace that scheme. (Op. Part II.A, citing Allan.)
   - Legislative “knows how” inference / expressio unius: the court observes that the Minnesota legislature knows how to give employees the express power to “charge and collect” penalties (see Minn. Stat. §§ 181.13(a), 181.14, subd. 2), and the absence of similar language in § 181.101 supports the conclusion that the legislature did not intend private plaintiffs to have that power. The opinion cites Lagasse v. Horton and In re Hubbard for the proposition that legislative action in one statute but not another is instructive. (Op. Part II.A, citing Minn. Stat. §§ 181.13, 181.14 and Lagasse v. Horton.)

4) Supporting precedent/context. The court also cites Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch. to show that past cases recognized the Commissioner’s role in collecting penalties under § 181.101 and that, although employees remain entitled to the funds once collected, the statutory collection power belongs to the Commissioner. The panel treats Milner as consistent with the conclusion that only the Commissioner collects the penalty. (Op. Part II.A.)

Putting it together (the doctrinal rule the court applied)
- The court used a plain‑meaning reading of § 181.101 and a “specific controls the general” approach to statutory construction: where a provision prescribes a particular enforcement/collection procedure (the Commissioner’s demand and collection power), a separate, more general remedial provision (authorizing private suits and “any civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated”) does not sweep away that specific scheme. The court supplemented that textual reading with the legislative-knows-how/expressio-unicus observation to reinforce that the absence of employee collection language in § 181.101 was deliberate.

Bottom line for your question: § 181.171’s general remedial language lets employees sue to enforce rights and obtain remedies that the violated provision itself makes available to private plaintiffs, but it does not convert a provision that delegates the assessment and collection of a penalty to an administrative officer into a private-collection right. The panel concluded § 181.101 does just that—it assigns the demand/charge/collection role to the Commissioner—so private plaintiffs cannot bypass that mechanism to recover the average-daily-wage penalty directly.

### How did Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exchange inform the MPWA penalty analysis here?
Question:
The opinion cites Milner for the idea that employees can enforce § 181.101 violations but that penalties collected under § 181.101(a) are to be collected by the Commissioner and paid to employees. If Milner acknowledges employees receive the penalty proceeds, why isn't that enough to let employees seek the penalties themselves under § 181.171? What exactly did Milner decide (or not decide) about a private right to demand penalties?
Answer:
Short answer: Milner confirmed two separate points — (1) that employees may sue to enforce wage rights under the MPWA and (2) that when § 181.101 penalties are collected they ultimately must be paid to the employee — but Milner did not hold that an individual employee may bypass the Commissioner and bring a standalone lawsuit to obtain the § 181.101 “average daily wage” penalties. The Eighth Circuit here reads Milner the same way: Milner recognizes employees get the money if penalties are collected, but it does not convert the Commissioner’s statutory collection role into a private cause of action to recover those penalties.

Why, legally, that matters (doctrine/reading of the statute)
- Statutory text controls. Section 181.101(a) spells out the procedure for penalties: the Commissioner “may serve a demand for payment … on behalf of an employee” and “if payment … is not made within ten days … the commissioner may charge and collect … a penalty.” The statute then provides that “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” The Eighth Circuit emphasizes this text (Op. ¶ A): the penalty-collection mechanism is tied to the Commissioner’s demand and collection power.
- Enforcement mechanism vs. entitlement. Milner (and the court here) separates two things: (i) the employee’s substantive entitlement to be paid penalties (i.e., if a penalty is collected it goes to the employee), and (ii) the procedural mechanism for obtaining the penalty (i.e., the statute vests collection authority in the Commissioner). Milner acknowledged both (employee entitled to proceeds; Commissioner collects), but did not say the statute gives employees their own independent means to demand/collect the penalty in court.
- How the Eighth Circuit applied that. The opinion quotes § 181.101(a) and stresses the statutory language limiting the ability to enforce the penalty to the Commissioner (“penalties are only available if the Commissioner first ‘serve[s] a demand for payment’” and “the commissioner may charge and collect … a penalty”) and then cites Milner for the point that the money, once collected, goes to the employee (Op. ¶ A, citing Milner, 748 N.W.2d at 617). The court also points to other MPWA provisions (e.g., §§ 181.13, 181.14) where the Legislature expressly authorized employees to collect penalties to show it knew how to do that if it wanted to (Op. ¶ A).
- Consequence: no private penalty suit under § 181.101. Because § 181.101 conditions availability of the penalty on the Commissioner’s demand/collection power, the court concluded “only the Commissioner may seek daily average wage penalties under § 181.101” (Op. ¶ A) and affirmed dismissal of the employee’s claim for those penalties.

What Milner did and did not decide
- Milner did decide: employees are entitled to receive § 181.101 penalty proceeds when those penalties are collected by the Commissioner, and employees can enforce wage provisions of the MPWA (so employees have substantive rights under the statute). The Eighth Circuit quotes Milner to that effect (Op. ¶ A: “employees may enforce violations of § 181.101(a) and are also entitled to be paid the penalties ‘even when the Commissioner collects the money.’ Milner … at 617”).
- Milner did not decide: that employees have an independent, private cause of action to demand or recover § 181.101 penalties apart from the Commissioner’s statutory demand-and-collection mechanism. Milner did not reinterpret the statutory text to allow private enforcement of the specific penalty-collection procedure in § 181.101(a).

Bottom line for this case
- Milner supports the proposition that employees ultimately receive any § 181.101 penalties collected, but it does not provide textual or precedential support for a private suit under § 181.171 to obtain those penalties directly. The Eighth Circuit relied on the § 181.101 text (and Milner’s narrower holding) to hold that only the Commissioner may pursue the § 181.101 penalty collection.

### Does Article III allow a private plaintiff to pursue MFLSA civil penalties that are payable only to the Commissioner?
Question:
The majority remands to consider mootness and mentions standing must persist, while the concurrence questions whether Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale even applies. What are the key differences between Laidlaw’s federal environmental penalties and Minnesota's MFLSA penalties that might defeat injury-in-fact or redressability for a private plaintiff? Is it constitutionally sufficient that a penalty payable to the state might deter future wage violations against the plaintiff class?
Answer:
Short answer
Article III does not automatically allow a private plaintiff to pursue MFLSA penalties payable only to the Commissioner. Laidlaw’s deterrence-based standing theory can sometimes justify private suits seeking penalties paid to government, but key structural and factual differences identified in the opinion may defeat both injury‑in‑fact and redressability here. In particular: (1) the MFLSA/Minnesota scheme assigns the penalty‑collection role (and initial enforcement steps) to the Commissioner, not the private plaintiff; (2) any money collected is earmarked to be paid by the Commissioner to employees only after the Commissioner acts; and (3) Cohen has already been paid the wages and liquidated damages he sought, so he must show a real likelihood that the employer’s violative policy persists and that an award of penalties payable to the state would meaningfully reduce the risk of future injury to him or his class. The concurrence expresses these same concerns.

Relevant doctrinal framework (from the opinion)
- Laidlaw: the Supreme Court held that citizen plaintiffs can have Article III standing to seek civil penalties that are payable to the government where those penalties “afford redress” by deterring ongoing or future violations that harm the plaintiffs. See Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw, 528 U.S. 167, 186 (2000) (quoted at slip op. § II.B).
- Standing/mootness rules: a plaintiff must establish standing “for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief” and that standing must continue during the entire litigation. Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017); Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021) (quoted at slip op. § II.B).
- Mootness: a claim may be moot if events make it “absolutely clear” that the wrongful behavior cannot reasonably be expected to recur; voluntary cessation does not automatically moot a case. Friends of the Earth, 528 U.S. at 189, 192 (cited at slip op. § II.B).

Specific differences the opinion highlights that may defeat standing/redressability
1. Statutory allocation of enforcement and collection to the Commissioner
- The opinion stresses that §181.101 authorizes the Commissioner to “serve a demand for payment on behalf of an employee” and to “charge and collect . . . wages . . . and a penalty” if unpaid within ten days (slip op. § II.A, quoting §181.101(a)). The statute thus contemplates a regulatory enforcement role for the Commissioner rather than a private‑attorney‑general role for employees seeking the penalty directly (slip op. § II.A).
- Because the statutory mechanism gives the Commissioner the primary enforcement function, a private plaintiff’s ability to obtain penalties is attenuated: the private plaintiff cannot simply force the statutory penalty collection in the same way Laidlaw’s plaintiffs sought civil penalties under federal environmental law.

2. Where the collected money goes and the plaintiff’s lack of a direct entitlement
- The opinion repeats the statutory text that “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned” (slip op. § II.A). The court reads Minnesota precedent as showing the Commissioner collects the penalties (Milner), not that the private plaintiff has an independent statutory right to recover them directly (slip op. § II.A & n.3).
- That allocation matters for redressability: if penalties are payable to the Commissioner (who controls whether to act and how to distribute), an award in the plaintiff’s favor may not directly or certainly redress the plaintiff’s claimed injury unless the plaintiff can show a realistic prospect that the Commissioner’s collection or the court‑ordered penalty will reduce the risk of future harm to the plaintiff/class.

3. Procedural prerequisites and the Commissioner’s gatekeeping role
- The statute requires a Commissioner demand and a ten‑day period for payment before the Commissioner “may charge and collect” penalties (slip op. § II.A). That prerequisite can make a private plaintiff’s requested relief contingent on independent agency action and therefore less likely to be “capable of redress” by a judicial award to the private plaintiff alone.

4. Factual posture: wages and liquidated damages already paid
- The opinion states Cohen stipulated that he “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed” and that he “does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages” (slip op. § I & § II.B). That concession undercuts injury‑in‑fact: absent a continuing policy or realistic threat of recurrence, the payment of money owed eliminates the concrete injury Laidlaw relied on (slip op. § II.B).
- The court therefore vacated and remanded on the MFLSA claim specifically so the district court can determine whether the alleged wrongful policy remains and whether the claim is moot (slip op. § II.B).

Is deterrence of future violations payable to the state constitutionally sufficient?
- Not necessarily. The opinion sets out the Laidlaw principle that a civil penalty payable to the government may still “afford redress” by deterring future violations (slip op. § II.B). But it also makes clear the plaintiff must still show: (1) an ongoing or imminent injury (a real likelihood that the violation will recur), and (2) that the requested relief (here, penalties payable to the Commissioner) will likely alleviate that injury by discouraging recurrence (slip op. § II.B).
- The concurrence explicitly flags the problem: there are “differences between the penalties in [Laidlaw] and those available under the MFLSA,” and the panel was not briefed on whether Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale applies here (Concurring op., slip op. § III). That expresses skepticism that deterrence alone, absent a clear statutory private right to collect penalties and a realistic risk of recurrence, will satisfy Article III.

Practical implications for Cohen on remand (grounded in the opinion)
- On remand the district court should first decide whether Consilio’s allegedly violative overtime policy remains or whether the risk of recurrence is “absolutely clear[ly]” eliminated (citing Friends of the Earth, slip op. § II.B).
- If the policy persists (or there is a credible risk it will recur), the court must evaluate whether awarding penalties payable to the Commissioner will meaningfully reduce the risk of future injury to Cohen and his putative class—i.e., whether Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale can bridge the statutory allocation to the Commissioner and satisfy redressability.
- If the policy has been abandoned and there is no realistic threat of recurrence, the MFLSA penalty claim is likely moot and Article III jurisdiction lacking (slip op. § II.B).

Conclusion
Laidlaw provides a route to standing for citizen plaintiffs when government‑payable penalties deter ongoing violations, but the opinion identifies statutory and factual differences that make that route far from automatic here: the MFLSA/Minnesota statutory scheme vests the penalty process and collection in the Commissioner, the plaintiff has already been paid the core monetary relief he sought, and the plaintiff must show a real risk of recurrence and a plausible causal link between the court’s award and reduced risk of future injury. Absent those things, deterrence of future violations payable to the state will not by itself satisfy Article III.

### What would make the MFLSA penalties claim moot under the voluntary cessation doctrine in this context?
Question:
The court says the penalties claim may be moot if it's absolutely clear Consilio's overtime policy won't recur. What kind of evidence would satisfy that burden (formal policy rescission, sworn declarations, compliance history)? Conversely, what evidence would keep the case live (current policy documents, ongoing projects billed without overtime)? How does the burden of proof operate on remand?
Answer:
Short answer
- To render the MFLSA penalties claim moot under the voluntary‑cessation line of cases (Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw, cited in the opinion), Consilio must show with concrete, objective evidence that it has stopped the challenged overtime practice and that it is “absolutely clear” the practice will not recur.
- Strong evidence that would likely satisfy that heavy showing: formal, unambiguous rescission or replacement of the 2019 overtime policy coupled with objective, verifiable indicia of compliance (companywide written policy documents, dated rescission notice to affected employees, contemporaneous payroll records showing no overtime paid at base rate after rescission, training materials, supervisory directives, monitoring/audit results, and sworn, specific declarations from responsible managers attesting to the change and enforcement mechanisms).
- Evidence that would keep the claim alive: any contemporaneous policy or communications showing the 2019 policy (or an equivalent) remains in force; recent pay practices that mirror the challenged policy (projects billed/paid without overtime premium); inconsistent or vague declarations; absence of enforcement or monitoring; history of reverting to old practices.
- On remand the legal burden rests primarily on the defendant to convince the court the controversy is moot (i.e., that recurrence is not reasonably possible). The plaintiff may defeat mootness by showing a reasonable expectation or demonstrated pattern that the wrongful conduct could recur.

Reasoning and authorities from the opinion
- The panel recognizes that civil penalties can confer Article III standing only where the plaintiff shows an injury or a threat of ongoing unlawful conduct, and that a claim becomes moot if “subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.” (Opinion quoting Friends of the Earth, 528 U.S. at 186, 189.)
- The court specifically tied mootness here to whether “Consilio’s violative overtime policy is still in place” and noted that if the policy remains the penalties claim may be viable, but if the policy is no longer in place and “it is ‘absolutely clear’ that its wrongful conduct will not recur, Cohen’s MFLSA claim may in fact be moot.” (Op. § II.B.)
- The opinion also reiterates the well‑settled rule that voluntary cessation does not ordinarily defeat jurisdiction unless the defendant carries the heavy burden of showing the wrongful conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur. (Op. quoting City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle and Friends of the Earth.)

What concrete proof will help the district court on remand
- Evidence favoring mootness (what the court will look for as convincing):
  - A clear, contemporaneous, companywide rescission or replacement of the 2019 policy (dated, distributed to the class of affected employees, and unambiguous that overtime premium has been restored or that pay practices comply with MFLSA).
  - Documentation that the rescission is implemented and enforced: revised employee handbook, supervisory instructions, training records, internal memos showing implementation steps.
  - Objective payroll data after the rescission showing employees were paid overtime premiums (or otherwise paid in full compliance) rather than base rate for overtime hours.
  - Independent monitoring or audit reports confirming compliance, or companywide compliance certification by a responsible official in sworn declaration.
  - Evidence of structural changes that remove the financial or operational incentive to revert to the old practice (e.g., new compensation system, corporate directive signed by senior management).
- Evidence undermining mootness (what will keep the claim alive):
  - Current policy documents, emails, or other communications that repeat or preserve the August 2019 policy language (pay “at base rate” for overtime).
  - Recent payrolls or project billing showing employees still being paid on the challenged basis.
  - Sworn declarations that are vague, conclusory, or contradicted by documentary evidence.
  - A corporate history of changing positions only after litigation or settling disputes and then reverting or refusing to adopt meaningful safeguards.
  - Lack of any meaningful enforcement mechanism, monitoring, or remedial structure that would prevent recurrence.

How the burden operates on remand
- The opinion directs the district court to determine jurisdiction on remand and frames mootness as the threshold issue. It relies on Friends of the Earth and the standard that voluntary cessation must be shown to be permanent — the defendant bears the initial, heavy burden to prove it is “absolutely clear” the conduct will not recur. (Op. § II.B.)
- Practically, the defendant should present affirmative, documentary evidence of repeal/implementation and monitoring; mere assertions or litigation‑only concessions are weak.
- The plaintiff need not prove the violation again at the mootness stage; rather, to resist mootness the plaintiff must point to adequate reasons to believe the practice could recur (current policy documents, recent pay practices, gaps or inconsistencies in the defendant’s proof).
- The district court may — and as the concurrence suggests, should — resolve the mootness (jurisdiction) question before reaching the merits on remand. (Op. § III; KOBES, J. concurrence noting remand for mootness question.)

Practical checklist for the parties on remand
- For Consilio (to establish mootness): supply the rescission/replacement policy, distribution proof (emails/acknowledgements), payroll showing compliant payments since rescission, training and audit records, and a specific sworn declaration from a responsible corporate officer explaining enforcement and why recurrence is implausible.
- For Cohen (to oppose mootness): point to any existing policy text that matches the 2019 policy, recent payrolls/projects consistent with the challenged practice, inconsistencies in Consilio’s declarations, lack of monitoring/enforcement, or evidence that the change was litigation‑driven and not permanent.

Conclusion
The court’s controlling principle is the Friends of the Earth “absolutely clear” standard quoted in the opinion. Documentary, objective evidence of a formal, implemented, and monitored policy change is what will most likely satisfy that standard; contemporaneous policy documents or payroll practices showing the old practice persists (or fragile, litigation‑only promises) will keep the penalties claim live. The defendant bears the difficult burden of proving non‑recurrence on remand; the plaintiff may defeat mootness by showing a reasonable prospect that the challenged practice could recur.

### Did the panel need to resolve jurisdiction before deciding the MPWA merits under Steel Co.?
Question:
The court reached the MPWA merits but vacated and remanded the MFLSA penalties claim for a jurisdictional determination. Is that sequencing consistent with the prohibition on 'hypothetical jurisdiction'? Is the explanation that the MPWA claim indisputably presented a live controversy (because the penalties, if available, would go to the plaintiff) while the MFLSA penalties claim uniquely implicated Article III, so they could decide one without the other?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes. The panel’s sequencing is consistent with Steel Co. The court first resolved whether Cohen could obtain the MPWA relief he actually sought (statutory penalties under §181.101 and the remedies available under §181.171). That question presented an indisputably live Article III controversy as to Cohen because the MPWA, properly read, gives employees a distinct private enforcement mechanism for certain remedies (and the statute’s text shows the daily‑wage penalties in §181.101 are directed to the Commissioner). By contrast, the MFLSA penalties claim uniquely implicated Article III standing/mootness (because any penalties would go to the Commissioner and the parties’ stipulation changed the factual posture), so the court reasonably vacated and remanded that claim for a jurisdictional determination.

Why this fits Steel Co. (and related standing doctrine)
- Steel Co. bars a federal court from reaching the merits of a case if jurisdiction is genuinely lacking (it prohibits deciding merits by assuming jurisdiction that is in doubt). But Steel Co. does not require a rigid universal sequencing rule where jurisdiction over one claim is plain and the other claim raises a bona fide Article III question. The controlling rule is that a plaintiff must have standing and that standing must continue for each claim and each form of relief (Town of Chester; Sisney; Friends of the Earth, all cited and applied in the opinion).
- The panel applied that framework: it treated the MPWA question as a merits-and-statutory‑interpretation issue that did not depend on a debatable jurisdictional predicate, while treating the MFLSA penalties as a claim whose justiciability was in doubt given the changed facts and the fact that any penalty would be payable to the Commissioner rather than Cohen.

How the opinion supports that conclusion
- MPWA: The court analyzed the MPWA statutory text (Minn. Stat. §§181.171 and 181.101) and concluded that §181.171 authorizes employees to bring a civil action for violations and awards “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief,” while §181.101’s average‑daily‑wage penalty is a mechanism by which the Commissioner may collect a penalty after serving a demand and is payable to the employee only insofar as the Commissioner collects money and pays it out (opinion §§ II.A, quoting Minn. Stat. Ann. §181.171, subd. 1 and §181.101(a)). The court therefore held that the district court did not err in concluding only the Commissioner may seek the §181.101 average‑daily‑wage penalty. That resolution was a straightforward statutory‑interpretation disposition of the MPWA claim and rested on whether the statute permits Cohen to obtain the particular penalty he sought—not on any assumed Article III hook.
- MFLSA: By contrast, the panel explained that the MFLSA‑penalties question implicated standing/mootness because (1) Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages he claimed; (2) any statutory penalties under the MFLSA would be payable to the Commissioner rather than Cohen; and (3) whether Consilio’s violative policy remains in place (and thus whether the controversy is live or moot) was disputed. The court therefore concluded it had “a serious concern regarding our jurisdiction to decide this issue,” noted Friends of the Earth and the general rule that standing must persist for each form of relief, and vacated and remanded the MFLSA claim so the district court could determine jurisdiction (opinion § II.B).

Putting it together
- The panel did not treat MPWA as a “hypothetical jurisdiction” exercise. It addressed whether Cohen could obtain particular MPWA relief on the merits because the statutory text and the parties’ stipulations left that question properly before the court and because the relief implicated Cohen (the opinion explains employees may enforce violations under §181.171 and describes how §181.101 operates). The MFLSA penalties claim, by contrast, raised classic Article III justiciability doubts (possible mootness and penalties payable to the state actor), so the court appropriately refrained from deciding it and remanded for the district court to resolve jurisdiction.

So the sequencing is consistent with the controlling principles: decide the claim that actually presents a live, non‑hypothetical controversy for the plaintiff; defer claims where Article III standing/mootness is genuinely in doubt. The opinion’s statutory analysis of the MPWA and its separate standing/mootness concerns about the MFLSA illustrate that distinction.

### What is the precise source of a private right to ask a court to impose MFLSA civil penalties payable to the Commissioner?
Question:
The opinion says the parties agree MFLSA statutory penalties are payable to the Commissioner, not Cohen. Under which MFLSA provision does a private plaintiff even have a cause of action to seek a judicial order imposing those penalties (e.g., Minn. Stat. § 177.27, subds. 7–8)? Or are penalties available only in enforcement actions by the Commissioner? How have Minnesota courts characterized a private litigant's role in obtaining these penalties?
Answer:
Short answer
The opinion does not identify a settled, independent statutory source that lets a private MFLSA plaintiff obtain civil penalties that are payable to the Commissioner. The panel assumes (correctly, for purposes of the appeal) that any MFLSA penalties would be payable to the Commissioner, but it expressly vacated the district court’s judgment on the MFLSA penalty claim and remanded for the district court to decide jurisdiction (standing/mootness). Thus, the question whether a private plaintiff may obtain a judicial order imposing MFLSA civil penalties payable to the Commissioner is left unresolved by this opinion.

Why — what the opinion actually says (and does not say)
- The complaint invoked Minn. Stat. §§ 177.25 and 177.27, subd. 8, among other statutes. The opinion acknowledges those filings (Op. I) but does not restate or interpret a provision that gives a private plaintiff the power to compel civil penalties payable to the Commissioner.
- The panel expressly states that “the parties agree that any statutory penalties imposed—unlike overtime wages and liquidated damages—are payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen.” (Op. II.B.) The opinion therefore treats penalties as governmental receipts, not as compensatory relief to the employee.
- Rather than deciding whether the MFLSA creates a private cause of action to impose those penalties, the court vacated the district court’s summary judgment on the MFLSA claim and remanded for the district court to determine jurisdictional questions (standing/mootness), observing that civil penalties payable to the government can nonetheless give plaintiffs Article III standing in some circumstances (citing Friends of the Earth), but that standing must be shown for each form of relief. (Op. II.B.)
- The panel does not hold that penalties are available only in Commissioner enforcement actions; it simply does not resolve whether a private litigant can obtain such penalties under the MFLSA. The court expressly left that issue for the remand. (Id.)

How Minnesota law is treated in the opinion (limited)
- The opinion contrasts the MPWA (Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a)), where the statute explicitly contemplates Commissioner-initiated demands and collection of average daily wage penalties, with other statutes where the legislature expressly authorized employee-initiated penalties. The panel relied on this contrast to conclude only the Commissioner can seek average daily wage penalties under § 181.101. (Op. II.A.)
- For the MFLSA, however, the panel did not reach an analogous statutory construction holding. It neither adopts a rule that only the Commissioner may obtain MFLSA penalties nor declares that a private litigant has a separate statutory right to seek penalties payable to the Commissioner. Instead, the court focused on standing and mootness and remanded for the district court to decide those matters. (Op. II.B.)

Practical implications for a litigant or a law student
- If you are looking for a clear textual source in this opinion that authorizes private plaintiffs to obtain MFLSA penalties payable to the Commissioner, you will not find one — the court left the matter open.
- The opinion points to two necessary inquiries going forward: (1) structural/statutory: whether the MFLSA’s text (e.g., § 177.27 or related provisions) actually creates a private cause of action to seek penalties payable to the Commissioner; and (2) constitutional/prudential: whether the plaintiff has standing to seek those penalties (and whether the claim is moot if the employer has ceased the challenged practice). The Eighth Circuit remanded so the district court can address jurisdiction first. (Op. II.B.)
- Until the district court resolves the remand issues (or another controlling decision is issued), the availability of MFLSA penalties to private plaintiffs remains unresolved by this opinion.

Key passages in the opinion to cite
- Parties’ concession about payee: “The parties agree that any statutory penalties imposed—unlike overtime wages and liquidated damages—are payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen.” (Op. II.B.)
- Remand for jurisdiction: “Accordingly, we vacate the district court’s grant of summary judgment as to Cohen’s MFLSA claim and remand for the district court to determine its jurisdiction.” (Op. III.)
- Standing/mootness discussion: the court’s citation to Friends of the Earth and Town of Chester in its analysis of whether governmental penalties can confer standing and whether the claim is moot. (Op. II.B.)

Bottom line
This opinion assumes MFLSA penalties would go to the Commissioner but does not decide (and therefore does not give you) a statutory holding that a private plaintiff may obtain a judicial order imposing those Commissioner-payable penalties. The issue is expressly left open and sent back to the district court to resolve jurisdictional questions and, if appropriate, statutory interpretation on remand.

### How did the court determine the standard of review and does de novo apply to these mixed statutory and summary judgment issues?
Question:
The panel states it reviews summary judgment de novo, viewing evidence in the non-movant’s favor. Given the heavy statutory-interpretation component and the parties’ stipulations (including payment of wages and liquidated damages), is there any portion of the district court’s decision that would have received deference (e.g., discretionary rulings), or is it all de novo because it’s purely legal?
Answer:
Short answer
- The panel applied the ordinary summary-judgment standard and reviewed the district court’s grant of summary judgment de novo, viewing the record in the non‑movant’s (Cohen’s) favor. (Op. § II, first paragraph; Karlen v. Jones Lang LaSalle citation.)
- That de novo review governs the district court’s legal rulings—statutory interpretation and the related question whether the statutes permit penalties—as well as backstop jurisdictional questions that are themselves legal (standing/mootness). The opinion treats those issues as legal questions appropriate for de novo review (see Op. § II.B on standing/mootness).
- However, purely discretionary or procedural rulings by the district court (e.g., decisions about leave to amend, whether to consider new relief first raised on summary judgment, awards of fees/costs/interest) are not governed by summary‑judgment de novo review and would be reviewed deferentially (abuse of discretion). The panel’s treatment of the injunctive‑relief issue and the concurrence’s comments about fees illustrate that distinction. (Op. § II.D; Concurring op. ¶¶ 1–2.)

Why (and where the opinion says so)
1) The baseline: summary judgment = de novo
- The panel opens Part II by expressly stating it “review[s] the district court’s grant of summary judgment . . . de novo, ‘viewing the evidence and the inferences . . . in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party.’” That language frames the entire review of the district court’s resolution of the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims on summary judgment (Op. § II, first para; quoting Karlen and Placzek).

2) Legal/statutory questions are resolved under that de novo standard
- The heart of the dispute—whether particular statutes authorize penalties—is a question of statutory interpretation. The panel resolves those questions as legal issues (for example, concluding only the Commissioner may seek average‑daily‑wage penalties under Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a) because the statute “only references the Commissioner” and relying on ordinary rules of statutory construction). That analysis appears in Part II.A and is treated as a legal conclusion that the district court did not err in reaching (Op. § II.A, discussing § 181.101(a) and citing Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co. on plain‑meaning rules).

3) Jurisdictional questions (standing/mootness) are also for de novo review
- The panel explicitly frames the MFLSA‑penalties dispute as a jurisdictional concern and remands because it is “serious” whether the court has jurisdiction now that wages and liquidated damages were paid. The standing/mootness analysis is legal and the panel analyzes it under settled Supreme Court precedent (Friends of the Earth; Town of Chester), treating the question as one the appellate court may decide and remand to the district court to address (Op. § II.B). That shows de novo review applies to whether jurisdiction continues.

4) What is not reviewed de novo: discretionary and procedural rulings
- The opinion treats certain matters as procedural/default issues that the district court did not consider or that were waived. For example, Cohen changed his requested form of injunctive relief in summary‑judgment briefing, and the panel says the district court “understandably—did not consider it,” citing Rule 15 and InfoDeli; the court concludes “the issue is not properly before us.” That is a procedural default/waiver analysis, which appellate courts typically review for abuse of discretion or forfeiture rather than de novo (Op. § II.D).
- The concurrence separately notes uncertainty about standing for penalties and also mentions vacating the district court’s rulings as to attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest—those are classic discretionary determinations (concurring ¶¶ 1–2). The panel’s disposition (affirm in part; vacate/remand in part) and these comments show that while statutory interpretation and jurisdictional law get de novo treatment, discretionary determinations (fees, whether to consider new claims/relief, leave to amend) typically receive deferential review on appeal.

Practical rule of thumb for this case (and like cases)
- Pure legal questions resolved on summary judgment (statutory interpretation, standing/mootness) = de novo review; facts and inferences = viewed in non‑movant’s favor.
- Discretionary/procedural rulings (failure to consider a new remedy raised only in briefing, decisions on leave to amend, fee awards, evidentiary rulings) = deferential review (abuse of discretion), and may be treated as waived if not properly preserved.

Relevant citations from the opinion
- “We review the district court’s grant of summary judgment . . . de novo, ‘viewing the evidence . . . in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party.’” (Op. § II, first para.)
- Statutory interpretation holding re: Commissioner-only penalties: Part II.A (discussing Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a) and Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.).
- Jurisdiction/standing/mootness discussion: Part II.B (Friends of the Earth; Town of Chester; Sisney).
- Procedural/default treatment of injunctive relief and citation to Rule 15/InfoDeli: Part II.D.
- Concurrence noting discretionary aspects (fees/costs/prejudgment interest): Concurring ¶¶ 1–2.

Conclusion
Most of the dispositive questions in this appeal (whether the statutes authorize penalties; whether the court has jurisdiction over the penalties claim now) are legal questions appropriately reviewed de novo on summary judgment. By contrast, discrete procedural or discretionary rulings (e.g., declining to consider a new injunction theory raised only in briefing; fee awards) are reviewed for abuse of discretion and may be treated as waived if not properly presented below. The opinion follows exactly that split.

### Why doesn’t § 181.171’s phrase 'other appropriate relief' include penalties available in § 181.101 when the employer violates that very section?
Question:
Section 181.171 says an employer 'is liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief,' and also that an employer 'is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.' The court nonetheless limits penalties under § 181.101 to those the Commissioner 'may charge and collect.' Do courts read § 181.171’s 'other appropriate relief' as subject to the specific enforcement allocation of the violated section, and if so, what interpretive principle requires that reading?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes. The court treats § 181.171’s catch‑all “other appropriate relief” as constrained by the enforcement scheme spelled out in the particular provision that was violated (here § 181.101). The opinion grounds that result in ordinary rules of statutory interpretation: give effect to the statute’s plain text, give effect to a specific enforcement clause (lex specialis/expressio unius), and avoid rewriting the legislature’s chosen remedial allocation.

Why the court so reads the text (steps the opinion takes)

- § 181.171 creates a private cause of action for violations of several MPWA provisions and then separately provides that “An employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief including but not limited to injunctive relief.” That language is broad, but § 181.171 goes on to say employees are liable “to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.” The court treats that second clause as a limiting instruction: penalties are available only if the specific violated section itself provides them. (Op. § A, first paragraph.)

- Looking to the particular provision § 181.101, the court observes that the statute itself assigns the procedure and remedial power to the Commissioner: the Commissioner “may charge and collect the wages . . . and a penalty” after serving a demand, and any money collected “must be paid to the employee concerned.” Because § 181.101 expressly vests authority to pursue penalties in the Commissioner (using “may charge and collect”), the court concludes that private plaintiffs cannot independently obtain the § 181.101 average‑daily‑wage penalties in district court. (Op. at §§ A, quoting Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a) and citing Milner.)

- The court reinforces that interpretation with related canon examples: the legislature knows how to give employees direct authority to “charge and collect a penalty” in other statutes (citing § 181.13(a) and § 181.14(2)). The contrast (expressio unius) supports the view that when the statute assigns the power to the Commissioner, Congress did not intend a private right to collect that penalty. (Op. § A, citing Lagasse and In re Hubbard.)

Doctrinal labels the opinion relies on (implicit and explicit)
- Plain‑meaning/textualism: the court starts from the statute’s unambiguous language and gives effect to what it says about who may “charge and collect” penalties. (Op. citing Allan.)
- Lex specialis / expressio unius (practical effect): when a specific subsection creates a particular enforcement mechanism or allocates who may collect penalties, that specific scheme governs and limits any broader remedial phrase (“other appropriate relief”) in the general private‑action provision. The opinion uses the contrast between statutes that authorize employee collection and § 181.101’s Commissioner‑centered scheme to reach this result.
- Avoidance of judicial rewriting: the court refuses to turn § 181.171’s general remedial grant into a vehicle for providing remedies the legislature assigned elsewhere to an administrative officer.

Key quotes from the opinion supporting this answer
- § 181.171 allows a private civil action and “shall also be liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief,” but “[a]n employer who is found to have violated the above sections is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.” (Op. § A, quoting § 181.171.)
- “[T]he statute reserves only the ability to ‘prosecute a claim for wages.’” And § 181.101 “only references the Commissioner regarding penalties” and provides that “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages . . . and a penalty.” The court therefore concludes “only the Commissioner may seek daily average wage penalties under § 181.101.” (Op. § A.)
- The court relies on Milner (that penalties under § 181.101 are collected by the Commissioner) and points out the legislature’s contrasting language in other statutes that does empower employees directly to collect penalties. (Op. § A, citing Milner and § 181.13(a).)

Bottom line for a student
When a general remedial clause (like “other appropriate relief” in § 181.171) sits beside or refers back to specific substantive provisions that themselves define who may seek penalties, courts read the general clause in light of the specific provisions. The opinion applies basic textualist canons—give effect to the statute’s plain words and the legislature’s specific remedial allocations (and draw the negative inference when the legislature uses different language elsewhere)—to limit private recovery of § 181.101 penalties to the Commissioner, despite the broad language in § 181.171.

### What exactly did Cohen waive on the MWTA claim, and what’s the Eighth Circuit’s threshold for abandonment on appeal?
Question:
The court affirms dismissal of the MWTA claim, citing failure to develop argument or identify statutory authority for civil penalties. What did Cohen need to do to preserve this issue—identify a particular penalty provision in § 181.032 or elsewhere, or develop a theory for 'other appropriate relief'? How strictly does the Eighth Circuit apply forfeiture/waiver rules under Fed. R. App. P. 28(a)(9)(A)?
Answer:
Short answer
- The Eighth Circuit held Cohen effectively abandoned (waived) his MWTA penalty argument by failing to develop any legal argument or point to statutory authority showing he could recover civil penalties under the MWTA. The court expected Cohen to identify a specific statutory basis for civil penalties (whether in § 181.032 or another provision) or to develop a cognizable theory for “other appropriate relief,” and he did neither.
- The circuit applies a fairly strict forfeiture/abandonment rule: perfunctory assertions without developed argument or citation to authority are treated as waived under Fed. R. App. P. 28(a)(9)(A) (and Eighth Circuit precedent). If you do not adequately develop an argument in your brief or opposing filings, the court will not address it.

What the opinion says, and what Cohen needed to do
- The court’s treatment is concentrated in Part II.C. It notes Cohen “stipulated that he received all pay Consilio owes him, and he points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute.” Because he “offer[ed] no meaningful argument in support of reversal,” the court declined to disturb the dismissal. (Op. II.C.)
- The opinion ties that outcome to the standard described in InfoDeli, LLC v. W. Robidoux, Inc., 136 F.4th 792, 802 n.8 (8th Cir. 2025): “fail[ure] to adequately develop an argument in support of alleged error amounts to abandonment” and waiver arises where a party does not develop an argument in accordance with Fed. R. App. P. 28(a)(9)(A). The panel also cites Milligan for the same principle that an argument without supporting authority is waived. (Op. II.C.)

Concretely, to preserve the MWTA issue Cohen needed to do one (or more) of the following:
1) Identify the statutory provision that authorizes civil penalties (or other monetary relief) under the MWTA (for example, point to § 181.032(f) or another specific provision) and explain how that provision creates a private right to the penalty he seeks; or
2) If relying on § 181.171’s authorization of “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief,” develop a legal theory explaining how that provision permits the particular civil penalties he seeks under the MWTA (including citation to analogous case law or statutory interpretation supporting that remedy); and
3) Respond to the stipulation that he has been paid all wages and liquidated damages — show why, despite the payments, he still has a cognizable claim for the penalties (standing/mootness/more-than-speculative risk of recurrence), with authority and argument. 

Why the court treated it as waived
- The court plainly faulted Cohen for “point[ing] to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute” and for offering “no meaningful argument in support of reversal.” (Op. II.C.) Under the Eighth Circuit’s practice, merely asserting error or listing an issue without citation/analysis does not preserve it on appeal.

How strictly the Eighth Circuit enforces this rule
- The opinion and its citations show the court enforces the rule fairly strictly: an undeveloped assertion or failure to cite statutory authority or develop legal reasoning is treated as abandonment/waiver. The court referenced Fed. R. App. P. 28(a)(9)(A) and InfoDeli to explain that inadequate development of an argument means the appellate court will not address it. (Op. II.C.)
- In short: don’t assume the court will infer your statutory basis or legal theory. To avoid waiver you must brief the point with statute or case law and reasoned application to the facts (both in district-court briefing and in your appellate brief).

Practical tip for preserving similar claims
- In the district court and on appeal: (1) cite the exact statutory subsection that you say authorizes the relief; (2) explain how the statutory text creates a private cause of action or remedy; (3) distinguish contrary authorities; and (4) square the requested remedy with stipulations in the record (e.g., that wages were paid). Absent that, the Eighth Circuit will likely treat the issue as abandoned.

### Could Cohen have obtained the injunctive relief he sought (rescission of the policy) without amending the complaint?
Question:
The panel says new claims must be raised by amended complaint, not opposition briefs. But is changing the form of requested injunctive relief (from one set of injunction terms to 'withdraw the 2019 policy') a new claim or just a different remedy? Are there circumstances under Rule 15(b) (issues tried by consent) or Rule 54(c) (grant relief to which a party is entitled) that would have allowed the district court to consider the revised injunction request at summary judgment?
Answer:
Short answer: Probably not. Under the posture and reasoning in the opinion, Cohen could not obtain the newly‑requested relief—“rescind the 2019 policy”—by springing it in his summary‑judgment opposition; he needed to put that claim/remedy into the complaint (or otherwise obtain the parties’ consent or a proper basis under the rules). The panel treated the revised injunction as a new claim/remedy that the district court was not asked to decide, and it relied on the settled principle that “new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs.” Op. at Part II.D (citing InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 and Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a)).

Why the change in requested injunction mattered
- The opinion explains that Cohen originally sought particular injunctive relief in the complaint but “later change[d] course and request[ed] different relief—withdrawing the 2019 policy—in his summary judgment briefing,” and that “the district court—understandably—did not consider it.” Op. Part II.D. That language treats the shift as more than stylistic: it was a discrete request the court had not been asked to adjudicate at summary judgment and therefore was not properly before the court on appeal.

Rule 15(b) (issues tried by consent) is not an effective route here
- Rule 15(b) permits a court to treat an issue as raised at trial if the opposing party has tried the issue by express or implied consent. It is a trial‑stage doctrine about amendment based on issues actually litigated with the parties’ notice and opportunity to address them. Nothing in the opinion suggests that Consilio consented to litigate an equitable rescission remedy at summary judgment; on the contrary, the panel emphasizes the absence of a properly pleaded request and the district court’s understandable refusal to decide a remedy not presented. - Rule 54(c) allows a final judgment to grant the relief to which the prevailing party is entitled even if not precisely demanded in the pleadings, but courts use it to avoid forfeiting relief proven at trial and to conform the judgment to the proofs. It does not permit a party to press a new claim or a fundamentally different form of equitable relief for the first time in an opposition brief without notice to the other side. The panel’s citation and reliance on the basic pleading rule (Rule 15(a) and InfoDeli) implies the district court needed proper pleading or at least notice and opportunity to respond before deciding a rescission remedy. - The panel also flagged jurisdiction/standing and mootness issues as to penalties and prospective relief (MFLSA claims and injunctive relief depend on whether the policy remains in force). Op. Parts II.B–D. Even if Cohen had properly pleaded rescission, the court would have to resolve whether he had standing to seek an affirmative injunction (and whether the claim was moot if the policy were already withdrawn). The opinion vacated/remanded MFLSA issues for the district court to consider jurisdiction; it rejected attempting to press new injunctive relief in the summary‑judgment papers without amendment. Op. Parts II.B, II.D.

Bottom line
- Changing the requested injunction to “rescission” in an opposition brief amounted to a new request that the court could not (and did not) decide. Under the opinion’s reasoning, Cohen needed to amend his complaint (or obtain the defendant’s clear consent to litigate the rescission remedy) so the district court would have notice, the parties could develop the record and briefing on that remedy, and the court could address standing/mootness and equitable factors. ### Does styling oneself as a 'private attorney general' affect standing or remedies in federal court for state-law wage claims?
Question:
Cohen’s caption calls him a 'private attorney general.' Does that label have any legal effect on his ability to pursue civil penalties or injunctive relief in federal court, especially when state law reserves certain enforcement tools to the Commissioner? Are there Article III or prudential standing limits on private-attorney-general style claims when the requested remedy benefits the state rather than the plaintiff?
Answer:
Short answer: No. Calling himself a “private attorney general” does not change the standing analysis or create a statutory right to enforcement remedies that the statute reserves to the Commissioner. The opinion treats the label as irrelevant to Article III and statutory limits: what matters is the plaintiff’s individualized Article III injury (and the statute’s text about who may recover what).

Why — legal doctrine and how the opinion applied it

- Article III standing and the form of relief: The court reiterates the basic rule that “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press and for each form of relief that is sought,” citing Town of Chester and Davis (Opinion § II.B). The opinion then applies Friends of the Earth to explain that civil penalties payable to the government can nonetheless provide Article III redress if the penalty serves to stop or deter ongoing unlawful conduct that harms the plaintiff (Friends of the Earth, 528 U.S. at 186 (quoted in Opinion § II.B)). But the court also makes clear the separate mootness/continuing-injury inquiry: if the employer has ceased the challenged policy and it is “absolutely clear” the conduct will not recur, the claim for penalties can be moot (Friends of the Earth language quoted in Opinion § II.B). The MFLSA penalties issue was therefore vacated and remanded for the district court to decide jurisdiction/mootness because the record was unclear whether the allegedly unlawful policy remains.

- Prudential limits / statutory allocation of enforcement power: The court grounds its disposition of the MPWA penalties claim in the statute’s text. Section 181.101(a) expressly empowers the Commissioner to serve a demand and to “charge and collect” average-daily-wage penalties; “money collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” The opinion treats that statutory scheme as demonstrating the Legislature’s allocation of enforcement tools to the Commissioner and concludes “only the Commissioner may seek daily average wage penalties under § 181.101” (Opinion § II.A (statutory text discussion and conclusion)). The court contrasts other statutes where the Legislature did give private employees an explicit penalty remedy (e.g., §§ 181.13(a), 181.14(subd.2)) to show that silence here matters (Opinion § II.A).

- Labeling does not create substantive rights: The court’s analysis repeatedly treats what Cohen called himself as irrelevant to the legal questions. The relevant inquiry was whether the statute authorizes an employee to recover the particular penalty (MPWA § 181.101), and whether an Article III case-or-controversy exists for penalties payable to the government (MFLSA) — not whether the plaintiff styled himself a “private attorney general.” The court therefore affirmed dismissal of the MPWA penalty claim on statutory grounds and vacated/remanded the MFLSA penalty claim only because of the standing/mootness uncertainty, not because of the “private attorney general” caption (see holdings in §§ II.A and II.B).

- Injunctive relief: The opinion explains that injunctive relief must be properly pleaded and that a plaintiff cannot switch to a different form of injunctive relief in opposition papers; the district court did not consider Cohen’s later-requested injunction (rescission of the 2019 policy) because it was raised too late (Opinion § II.D; citing Fed. R. Civ. P. 15 and InfoDeli). Substantively, the same Article III principles apply: to obtain prospective relief the plaintiff must show an ongoing or imminent injury (Friends of the Earth / Town of Chester principles quoted in § II.B).

What this means, practically

- You cannot bootstrap standing or statutory authority by self-styling as a “private attorney general.” The caption doesn’t create a new cause of action or reach around a statutory allocation of enforcement authority to an administrative official.

- If the statute expressly reserves a penalty or collection power to the Commissioner (as the court reads MPWA §181.101), a private plaintiff cannot obtain that penalty in federal court; the MPWA penalty claim was decided on that statutory ground (Opinion § II.A).

- Penalties payable to the government under a federal statute (here, MFLSA) can, in principle, be pursued by a private plaintiff if (1) the plaintiff has a continuing or threatened Article III injury and (2) the requested relief (including penalties) can redress or deter the defendant’s conduct (Friends of the Earth framework cited in Opinion § II.B). But the court here remanded because it was unclear whether the defendant’s policy remains and thus whether a live controversy exists.

- For injunctive relief, you still must plead and prove a prospective or ongoing injury and follow procedure (amend complaint to seek new forms of relief). The court refused to consider a new injunction request raised only in briefing (Opinion § II.D).

Concise takeaways for a law student

- “Private attorney general” is a label, not a substantive hook. Article III standing and statutory text control.

- If the statute gives the Commissioner exclusive authority to demand/collect penalties (MPWA §181.101), a private plaintiff cannot recover those penalties.

- If penalties are payable to the government under a federal statute (MFLSA), a private plaintiff may have standing to seek them only if she shows an ongoing or threatened injury that the relief will redress or deter; the court remanded to resolve that factual/jurisdictional question.

- Don’t rely on a caption to expand remedies; plead any request for injunctive relief in the complaint (or amend properly) and be prepared to show likelihood of recurrence for prospective relief.

References in the opinion: see II.A (MPWA statutory-text analysis and holding that only the Commissioner may seek average daily wage penalties under §181.101); II.B (standing/mootness discussion, Friends of the Earth quotes, and remand of MFLSA penalties claim); II.D (injunctive relief not properly raised and Rule 15/InfoDeli citations).

### What showing is required to demonstrate a continuing personal stake for penalties based on deterrence?
Question:
Assuming Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale could apply, what facts would establish that civil penalties under the MFLSA would redress Cohen’s injury through deterrence (e.g., ongoing employment, likelihood of being subject to the policy again, class-wide exposure)? Does it matter that he has already been paid his individual overtime and liquidated damages?
Answer:
Short answer
To establish a continuing personal stake to seek MFLSA civil penalties paid to the State (under the Laidlaw deterrence theory), Cohen must show a reasonable likelihood that Consilio’s unlawful policy will recur in a way that threatens him personally. Concretely, the district court on remand will need evidence that (at least) one or more of the following is true: Cohen remains (or is likely to be) employed under the same policy; Consilio still operates the same (or an effectively identical) overtime policy; similarly situated employees remain exposed company‑ or class‑wide such that deterrence of future violations would redress Cohen’s interest; or the employer has not taken effective, permanent remedial steps. The fact that Cohen has been paid all back overtime and liquidated damages does not automatically defeat standing — but it does make him required to show some continued or imminently threatened injury (not merely a past wrong) that civil penalties would redress by deterring recurrence.

Legal doctrine and how it applies here
- Governing precedent. The opinion relies on Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw, 528 U.S. 167 (2000), which permits a plaintiff to seek governmental civil penalties when those penalties redress the plaintiff’s injury by deterring ongoing or future wrongful conduct (opinion at II.B, citing Laidlaw, 528 U.S. at 186). But standing and mootness require a continuing personal interest: “standing for each claim . . . and for each form of relief,” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017), and the interest that existed at filing must “continue throughout” the case (Sisney v. Kaemingk, quoted in the opinion at II.B).
- What must Cohen show. Because his MFLSA wages and liquidated damages have been paid and penalties (if awarded) will go to the Commissioner rather than to him, the only basis for redress is deterrence. As the panel explained, that requires evidence that “the allegedly wrongful behavior could reasonably be expected to recur” — i.e., that voluntary cessation is not “absolutely clear” (Friends of the Earth language quoted at II.B). The remand is to let the district court determine whether Cohen has produced such evidence.
- Types of facts that will suffice. Drawing from the opinion’s framing, facts that would support a non‑moot Laidlaw theory include:
  - Current or ongoing application of the challenged policy: evidence that the 2019 policy (or an equivalent one eliminating overtime premium pay) remains in place or was reinstated. The opinion specifically notes that the parties dispute whether the policy “remains” and ties that directly to whether the claim may be viable (II.B).
  - Personal exposure to recurrence: that Cohen still works (or is likely to work again) under Consilio and therefore faces a real prospect of being paid under the unlawful policy in the future.
  - Class‑ or workplace‑wide exposure: that many similarly situated employees remain subject to the same policy, so penalties that deter the employer would prevent repeated harms to Cohen (and to the workforce) — the opinion recognizes class‑wide exposure as a relevant way penalties can “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones” (II.B).
  - Lack of effective remediation: employer conduct (or admissions) showing the policy was not rescinded, or no assurances, monitoring, or structural changes to prevent recurrence. The court contrasted mere voluntary cessation (which does not necessarily moot the case) with situations where it is “absolutely clear” the conduct won’t recur (II.B, quoting Friends of the Earth).
  - Pattern or history of repeated violations: evidence that the unlawful practice is part of a continuing business practice rather than an isolated past mistake.
- Why being paid already matters, but is not necessarily dispositive. The opinion stresses that Cohen “stipulated that Consilio has compensated him for all overtime wages and liquidated damages” and that statutory penalties “are payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen” (II.B). Payment of past monetary relief eliminates the need for compensatory relief, but under Laidlaw a plaintiff can still have standing to pursue penalties if deterrence would remedy a threatened future injury. Thus receipt of backpay/liquidated damages narrows the plaintiff’s burden: he must now show a continuing or imminent threat of the same unlawful conduct such that penalties would produce deterrence and thereby redress his interest. If the record shows the policy no longer exists and cannot reasonably be expected to recur, the MFLSA penalties claim will be moot (II.B).

What the remand will likely focus on
The district court should decide (as the panel instructed) whether the record establishes a reasonable expectation of recurrence. Practically, the court will look for contemporaneous evidence (not speculation) such as:
- employer statements, policy documents, or practice evidence showing the policy remains or was re‑adopted;
- proof Cohen remains employed or has a realistic prospect of future employment under Consilio;
- evidence of companywide treatment or continued class exposure;
- absence of corrective measures (rescission, training, monitoring, or restitution to the workforce) making recurrence unlikely.

If the district court finds such evidence, Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale can support jurisdiction even though Cohen was paid his individual remedies. If the court finds no reasonable expectation of recurrence — i.e., the wrongful behavior has been effectively and permanently ceased — the penalties claim is likely moot.

Key citations from the opinion
- “Civil penalties . . . may ‘afford redress . . . because such penalties may “encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones.”’” (Op. II.B, quoting Laidlaw, 528 U.S. at 186.)
- “a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim . . . and for each form of relief” and the personal interest “must continue throughout [the litigation]” (Op. II.B, quoting Town of Chester and Sisney).
- The panel vacated and remanded because “whether Consilio’s violative overtime policy is still in place remains in dispute,” and thus “Cohen’s claim for civil penalties may still be viable” if the policy remains (Op. II.B).

Bottom line
Being paid back wages and liquidated damages does not automatically bar a Laidlaw deterrence claim for penalties. But Cohen must produce concrete evidence showing a real, reasonable likelihood the unlawful policy will recur and that civil penalties would plausibly deter future violations affecting him (or the class). If he cannot make that showing, the penalties claim is moot. The Eighth Circuit vacated and remanded precisely so the district court can make that factual/legal determination.

### Should the court have certified the MPWA penalty question to the Minnesota Supreme Court?
Question:
Given the tension between § 181.171’s remedial language and § 181.101’s Commissioner-centric penalty mechanism, why didn’t the Eighth Circuit certify the question to the Minnesota Supreme Court? What are the standards for certification from the Eighth Circuit or Minnesota federal district courts, and do Minnesota cases (like Milner or Allan) make the answer sufficiently clear to avoid certification?
Answer:
Short answer: The Eighth Circuit did not certify the MPWA penalty question because it concluded Minnesota law was sufficiently clear on the point — § 181.101 vests the penalty mechanism in the Commissioner, not in private employees — so there was no unsettled question of state law that required Minnesota Supreme Court resolution. The opinion relies on the plain statutory text and Minnesota precedent (Allan, Milner, Lagasse) to reach that conclusion, and certification is discretionary and ordinarily reserved for genuinely uncertain or determinative state-law questions.

Why the court thought certification unnecessary (doctrinally and as applied here)

- The certification device. Federal appellate courts (and district courts) may certify questions to a state supreme court when a determinative issue of state law is unclear and state-court guidance could control the federal case. Certification is discretionary; courts generally certify only where (a) the answer will be outcome-determinative, (b) there is genuine uncertainty or an absence of controlling state precedent, and (c) doing so would materially aid correct decisionmaking. The Eighth Circuit has the same practical discretion to decline certification if it concludes the state law is clear.

- The Eighth Circuit’s view of the statute. The panel read § 181.101(a) as facially limiting the penalty process to the Commissioner: the statute authorizes the Commissioner (or the Commissioner’s representative) to serve a demand and to “charge and collect . . . a penalty in the amount of the employee’s average daily earnings” if wages are not paid within ten days. The opinion emphasizes that collected money “must be paid to the employee concerned,” but the collection power and the enforcement mechanism are given to the Commissioner (Opinion ¶ A, quoting Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a)). Because the statutory language is plain, the court applied Minnesota’s ordinary rule of statutory interpretation: when the statute is unambiguous, it “must be given effect” (citing Allan). The panel therefore treated the statute as resolving the dispute.

- Precedent the court relied on. The opinion cites Minnesota decisions that reinforce the Commissioner-centered reading:
  - Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.: the court invoked Allan’s canon that plain statutory language should be given effect, which supports resolving the question from the text rather than looking for ambiguity (Opinion ¶ A).
  - Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch.: the panel notes Milner’s clarification that penalties under § 181.101 are collected by the Commissioner and that employees are entitled to be paid the penalties when collected (Opinion ¶ A, discussing Milner at 616–18). The opinion treats Milner as confirming that the enforcement/collection mechanism is through the Commissioner.
  - Lagasse and related citations were used to show the Legislature knows how to give a private right to collect penalties where it wants to (Opinion ¶ A: comparing §§ 181.13 and 181.14). That point helped the panel infer that silence in § 181.101 as to employee-initiated penalties is deliberate.

- Resulting judgment about clarity. Given the plain statutory language and the supporting Minnesota authorities, the panel concluded the relevant question was not a close call of state law requiring certification — only the Commissioner may seek (and collect) the average-daily-wage penalty under § 181.101. Because the court deemed state law clear, certification was unnecessary.

Other practical points reflected in the opinion

- Certification is typically asked for by the parties or considered where the court recognizes a genuine state-law ambiguity. The opinion does not indicate that either party sought certification; instead, the parties litigated the merits and the panel resolved the statutory interpretation on the text and authorities. That procedural posture undercuts the need for certification.

- The panel did certify no federal question on this point; it simply resolved the statutory issue itself and affirmed the district court on the MPWA claim. The court did vacate and remand other parts of the case (MFLSA mootness), but those issues turned on standing/mootness and factual posture rather than an unsettled Minnesota statutory construction.

Conclusion

Certification was unnecessary because the court found the statute’s text and Minnesota precedent (Allan’s plain-meaning rule, Milner’s treatment of the Commissioner’s role, and the Legislature’s demonstrated approach in related statutes) made the answer clear: § 181.101 authorizes penalty enforcement through the Commissioner, not by private suit for the average-daily-wage penalty. Under the usual certification standards, there was no unresolved, outcome-determinative question of Minnesota law that compelled sending the issue to the Minnesota Supreme Court.

### What happens to attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest if the MFLSA penalties claim is moot?
Question:
The concurrence would also vacate the district court’s rulings on fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. If the MFLSA penalties claim is dismissed as moot on remand, does that affect Cohen’s 'prevailing party' status under fee-shifting provisions of the MPWA/MFLSA? Are fees considered collateral and still reviewable if jurisdiction fails on part of the case?
Answer:
Short answer: If the MFLSA penalties claim is held moot on remand, that will likely defeat Cohen’s status as a “prevailing party” with respect to relief under the MFLSA (and thus undermine fee-shifting tied to that claim). The panel treated the fee question as dependent on the court’s retained jurisdiction over the penalty claim — the concurrence expressly vacated the district court’s awards for attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest — which signals that the fees are not a collateral, independently reviewable entitlement when the underlying claim falls out of the case for lack of jurisdiction.

Why (legal doctrine and what the opinion says)
- Standing/mootness must be shown for “each claim … and for each form of relief that is sought.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., Inc., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017), quoted in the opinion at slip op. § II.B. The panel relied on that rule in explaining that Cohen must have a continuing interest in the penalty claim (and in the particular relief sought) for a federal court to decide it. Slip op. § II.B.
- The panel concluded it could not resolve the MFLSA-penalty question without first resolving a jusrisdictional/mootness issue and therefore vacated the district court’s judgment on the MFLSA claim and remanded for a jurisdictional determination. Slip op. § III; see also slip op. § II.B (discussing Friends of the Earth and voluntary cessation doctrine).
- Judge Kobes’s special concurrence goes a step further: he would also vacate the district court’s rulings on fees, costs, and prejudgment interest because he is “not convinced that Cohen has standing to seek statutory penalties” and therefore would not allow the fee rulings to stand while the underlying penalties claim may be moot. Concurring op. (last paragraph).

Putting those pieces together:
- Fee awards under fee-shifting statutes are tied to whether a litigant is a prevailing party on the underlying statutory claims. The opinion emphasizes that standing/mootness questions must be resolved for each form of relief. If Cohen obtains no judicial relief on the MFLSA penalties (because the claim is moot or jurisdiction is lacking), then he has not prevailed as to that relief and cannot be said to have “prevailed” for purposes of fee-shifting tied to it.
- The panel’s vacatur/remand of the MFLSA claim and the concurrence’s express vacatur of fee/cost/interest rulings show the court’s view that fee awards are not merely “collateral” to be reviewed even if the underlying claim evaporates for lack of jurisdiction. The court remanded so the district court can (1) decide whether the MFLSA penalty claim is moot and (2) then adjudicate fee issues in light of that jurisdictional determination.

Practical consequence on remand
- The district court should (and per the panel may) consider mootness/standing as a threshold matter. If the MFLSA penalty claim is moot (because Consilio’s allegedly wrongful policy is no longer in place or otherwise cannot reasonably be expected to recur), then there will be no judicial relief on that claim and any fee award premised on prevailing on the MFLSA penalty claim would (and should) be re-examined or vacated.
- If, by contrast, the district court concludes the MFLSA penalty claim is live and Cohen obtains relief on it, prevailing-party status and any fee-shifting analysis would proceed in the ordinary way.

Bottom line: the appellate opinion requires the district court to resolve mootness/standing first. If the MFLSA penalties claim is in fact moot, Cohen will lack a basis to be deemed a prevailing party with respect to that relief, and the appellate concurrence’s approach indicates that fee/cost/prejudgment-interest awards tied to the now-moot claim should not survive as “collateral” — they will be subject to vacatur or reconsideration on remand.

### Does Consilio’s 'effective concession' of MPWA/MFLSA violations at oral argument change the Article III analysis?
Question:
The panel notes Consilio effectively conceded statutory violations but says jurisdiction is still a concern for penalties. Can such concessions affect standing or mootness, or do they only remove the merits dispute while leaving the court to decide whether there’s still a live case or controversy over the form of relief (penalties, injunction)?
Answer:
Short answer: No — Consilio’s concession at oral argument can eliminate disputes on the merits (whether a statutory violation occurred) but it does not automatically resolve the Article III inquiry. Standing and mootness are separate requirements that the court must assess for each claim and each form of relief (here, statutory penalties and injunctive relief), and the panel treated them that way.

Why — doctrinal points tied to the opinion
- Article III requires a live case or controversy “for each claim … and for each form of relief.” Town of Chester v. Laroe Ests., 581 U.S. 433, 439 (2017) (quoted at Op. ¶ B). The panel repeats this rule and applies it to the penalties issue (Op. ¶ B).
- A concession that removes dispute about liability does not necessarily supply the continuing personal interest (standing) or prevent mootness for prospective or non-compensatory relief. The court relied on Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw, 528 U.S. 167 (2000), to explain that civil penalties may still “afford redress” (through deterrence) but that a plaintiff must still show a concrete, continuing interest — and that voluntary cessation (or past payment) can moot a claim if it is “absolutely clear” the wrongful conduct will not recur (Op. ¶ B, quoting Friends of the Earth at 189).
- The panel therefore distinguished the parties’ stipulation and payments (which disposed of unpaid wages and liquidated damages) from the separate jurisdictional question about penalties (which, under the MFLSA as the parties agree, are payable to the Commissioner). The court expressly vacated the MFLSA summary judgment and remanded for the district court to determine its jurisdiction over the penalties claim (Op. ¶ B).

How the opinion applied these rules to the concession here
- The opinion records that Consilio “effectively conceded violations of the MPWA and MFLSA” at oral argument (Op. ¶ A opening paragraph and text). That concession and the parties’ stipulation resolved the unpaid-wages/liquidated-damages issues (the merits as to those remedies).
- But because penalties are payable to the Commissioner (not directly to Cohen) and because the record left open whether Consilio’s allegedly violative policy remains in place, the panel concluded the Article III question as to penalties remained unresolved and remanded for the district court to decide jurisdiction (Op. ¶¶ A–B). The court also quoted Friends of the Earth and City of Mesquite to explain why voluntary cessation or repayment can moot claims unless recurrence is reasonably expected (Op. ¶ B).

Practical takeaway for litigation
- A concession on liability reduces the issues the court must decide, but it does not automatically create Article III standing for every form of relief the plaintiff seeks. Courts still ask whether the plaintiff has a continuing concrete injury or whether the requested relief (including penalties paid to a government) can redress that injury.
- On remand the district court must determine (1) whether the allegedly unlawful overtime policy remains in effect (if it does, the penalty claim is more likely to be live), (2) whether the defendant’s cessation or repayment is truly voluntary and unlikely to recur (Friends of the Earth/City of Mesquite analysis), and (3) whether the penalties sought can furnish the plaintiff with the required redress (Op. ¶ B; see also KOBES, J., concurring specially expressing skepticism about standing for penalties).

Bottom line: Consilio’s oral concession narrowed the merits dispute but did not — and under the panel’s controlling reasoning could not — resolve the separate Article III issues as to statutory penalties; the court vacated the MFLSA disposition on that ground and remanded the jurisdictional question.
