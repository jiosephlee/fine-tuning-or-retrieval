Title: Blog about the opinion: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC

# The Commissioner’s Penalty Monopoly: Eighth Circuit Narrows Private Remedies Under Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act

## Why this case matters
In Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC, the Eighth Circuit confronted a deceptively simple question with outsized practical consequences: who may pursue the MPWA’s “average daily wage” penalty for unpaid wages under Minn. Stat. § 181.101? The court answered that question plainly — only the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry’s Commissioner may seek those penalties. The decision, grounded in textual analysis and statutory structure, removes a key enforcement lever from private plaintiffs litigating wage claims in Minnesota. That matters because penalty exposure is often the principal source of leverage in wage-and-hour litigation; its absence changes settlement dynamics, enforcement strategies, and the practical remedies available to aggrieved employees.

## What the court held
The Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s dismissal of Cohen’s MPWA penalty claim. The panel concluded § 181.101’s penalty scheme is commissioner-centric: penalties are collectible only after the Commissioner serves a demand for payment and pursues collection, and any money collected by the Commissioner must be paid to the employee. Reading the statute against neighboring provisions such as §§ 181.13 and 181.14 — which expressly authorize employees to recover penalties in certain contexts — the court relied on the familiar canon that legislative silence across provisions is significant. Because the MPWA’s text reserves the penalty enforcement mechanism to the Commissioner, private plaintiffs cannot independently seek the average daily wage penalty under § 181.101 through their civil suits.

## How the court reasoned
The opinion performs a straightforward textualist inquiry. It starts from § 181.171, which authorizes civil actions for MPWA violations and provides that an employer “is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.” But the panel did not treat that language as a grant of a private mechanism to impose all penalties the statute mentions. Instead, it turned to § 181.101 itself, which ties the availability of penalties to a Commissioner-initiated demand and collection process: the Commissioner serves a demand, waits ten days, and only then “may charge and collect” wages and a penalty, with collected funds distributed to the employee. That provision, the court reasoned, plainly contemplates administrative enforcement by the Commissioner rather than private civil actions for the same penalty.

The court reinforced its reading by comparing § 181.101 to separate MPWA provisions that explicitly authorize employee-initiated penalties when the Legislature intended that result — notably §§ 181.13 and 181.14. Citing Minnesota precedent (Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co.), the panel invoked the canon of expressio unius: when the Legislature knows how to grant employees a private right to recover penalties, its failure to do so in § 181.101 suggests it did not intend a private penalty remedy there. The opinion also leaned on lower-court authority applying the same textual reading (Hull v. ConvergeOne, Inc., D. Minn. 2021).

## Analytical perspective
The court’s conclusion is hardly surprising if you accept a plain-text approach to statutory interpretation. The statutory scheme reads like a deliberate design to channel penalty enforcement through the administrative apparatus of the Department of Labor and Industry. That design has two sensible internal logics: it centralizes investigations and penalty determinations in an agency with expertise and statewide perspective, and it lets the agency manage the distribution of recovered funds. But it also produces a more pro-employer litigation posture in private suits: absent an active commissioner-led enforcement action, plaintiffs in private litigation cannot extract the extra-damages penalty Congress or the Minnesota legislature built into the statute.

Two features of the opinion are worth underscoring. First, the court did not deny that employees ultimately receive penalty funds collected by the Commissioner — indeed, it highlighted that any money the Commissioner collects must be paid to the employee. The court’s point is procedural and institutional rather than substantive: the MPWA envisions administrative initiation of penalties even if the economic beneficiary is the private employee. Second, the decision rests heavily on the Legislature’s contrasting treatment of other statutes. That reliance is a double-edged sword: it is persuasive in a state-law context where the bench applies state canons, but it leaves open the policy question whether administrative channeling results in adequate enforcement in practice.

## Practical consequences for litigants and enforcement
For plaintiffs’ counsel, the ruling shifts the calculus in Minnesota wage cases. A private suit for unpaid overtime under § 181.101 now cannot — standing alone — yield the average daily wage penalty absent parallel administrative action by the Commissioner. That reduces the maximum downside exposure for employers in private litigation, weakening plaintiffs’ bargaining position at mediation and during settlement negotiations.

Practically, the decision means plaintiff-side strategies will likely bifurcate. One track is litigation focused on wage recovery and liquidated damages (where available), accepting that § 181.101 penalties are off the table in private court. The other is administrative: counsel must proactively engage the Department of Labor and Industry by filing administrative complaints and urging the Commissioner to serve the statutory demand that unlocks penalties. Because the Commissioner’s resources, priorities, and enforcement cadence may vary, this places significant weight on whether the agency will pursue the case energetically — and it introduces a layer of uncertainty and delay for workers seeking full statutory redress.

For defendants, the ruling offers an avenue to limit exposure: timely payment of wages may forestall commissioner-initiated penalties, and the absence of a private penalty remedy reduces leverage for class-wide treble-like exposure (the “average daily wage” penalty can be significant). That might tilt incentives toward paying what is due rather than litigating liability, but it could also encourage more aggressive policy changes by employers who view the administrative bottleneck as a protective buffer.

## Coordination with MFLSA, MWTA, and mootness concerns
Cohen’s case also touched on other statutes. The Eighth Circuit declined to resolve the parallel MFLSA statutory-penalty issue on the merits, vacating and remanding because the record leaves open whether Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy remains in effect and thus whether Cohen retains a live interest (the court rightly flagged Article III mootness/standing issues). The MWTA claim was dismissed primarily for lack of developed argument on appeal. Those rulings remind practitioners that statutory choice and procedural posture matter: state-law limitations on private penalties do not necessarily foreclose federal FLSA-based strategies (though the MFLSA remand shows that Article III considerations can complicate recovery of penalties that are payable to the state).

## Strategic options for plaintiffs and counsel
Given Cohen, plaintiffs should consider at least three concurrent steps. First, file administrative complaints with the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry promptly and demand that the Commissioner serve the § 181.101 demand for payment; the statute itself requires commissioner initiation for penalties to become available. Second, structure civil suits to preserve claims that do not depend on the commissioner’s action (for example, wage recovery and liquidated damages under MFLSA where available), but be mindful of mootness — defendants can attempt to moot claims by tendering owed wages. Third, develop an advocacy strategy aimed at the agency: briefs, evidence packages, and media or stakeholder pressure may increase the odds the Commissioner acts.

Class-action counsel will need to adjust expectations about the remedies likely to be secured in state-court MPWA class suits versus federal FLSA collective actions. If penalties are a critical component of class recovery or attorney-fee calculations, plaintiffs may need to emphasize coordination with the Commissioner or seek alternative statutory vehicles that permit private penalties.

## Legislative fixes and broader lessons
If Minnesota’s Legislature thinks the Commissioner-only mechanism creates an enforcement gap — for example, if agency resource constraints leave many meritorious claims unaddressed — there are clear legislative fixes. The Legislature could amend § 181.101 to add an express private right to the average daily wage penalty, or revise § 181.171 to clarify that civil actions may seek both compensatory damages and the statutory penalties set out elsewhere in the MPWA. Alternatively, lawmakers could expand the Commissioner’s authority to delegate or accelerate enforcement, or provide targeted funding to the Department of Labor and Industry to ensure victimized employees receive timely penalty recovery.

The opinion also offers a broader doctrinal reminder: statutory structure and the presence or absence of specific employee-directed language matter. Courts will not lightly infer a private enforcement mechanism where the statute’s text centralizes enforcement authority in an administrative actor and where nearby provisions show the Legislature knows how to empower private suits when it wants to.

## Conclusion
Cohen v. Consilio is a practical decision with predictable doctrinal logic: the MPWA’s penalty machinery belongs to the Commissioner unless the Legislature says otherwise. For employees and their counsel, the ruling means rethinking enforcement strategies in Minnesota wage cases — pairing private suits for wages with administrative complaints and advocacy before the Commissioner will now be the path to seek statutory penalties under § 181.101. For policymakers, the decision highlights the tradeoff inherent in channeling enforcement through an agency: it can bring expertise and uniformity, but it can also limit private enforcement and weaken deterrence if the agency lacks the capacity or will to act.



# Civil Penalties and Article III in Wage-and-Hour Cases: Laidlaw’s Shadow over MFLSA Enforcement

## Why Cohen matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Cohen v. Consilio, LLC highlights a recurring fault line in wage-and-hour litigation: what happens to a plaintiff’s Article III standing when an employer pays the wages demanded mid‑case but leaves statutory penalties on the table — penalties that, under Minnesota law as the court reads it, would be paid to the state or its commissioner rather than to the employee. The panel affirmed dismissal of some claims, but it vacated and remanded the district court’s judgment on Cohen’s Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) penalties, instructing the district court to decide whether Cohen’s remaining claim is moot under the framework set out in Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc. The opinion — and Judge Kobes’s skeptical concurrence — underscores how Laidlaw and the Supreme Court’s recent Article III jurisprudence (including Town of Chester’s point that standing and relief are claim‑specific) shape the practical ability of individual plaintiffs to pursue public‑facing penalties in private suits.

## How the Eighth Circuit framed the issue

The decision proceeds in familiar stages. Cohen admitted he’d been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages he claimed under the MFLSA. He nonetheless pressed for statutory penalties, which the parties agreed, unlike back pay, would be payable to the Commissioner. The court recognized that civil penalties payable to government actors do not categorically defeat standing: Laidlaw holds that a plaintiff can have a cognizable interest in penalties even when collected by the government because the penalties can redress an injury through deterrence and by encouraging discontinuance of ongoing illegal conduct. But standing is claim‑ and relief‑specific under Town of Chester, and the court correctly flagged that the case may be moot if the allegedly unlawful policy has been abandoned and is not reasonably likely to recur.

Because the record did not establish whether Consilio’s overtime policy remains in place, the panel concluded the jurisdictional question was live and remanded for the district court to determine whether there is a continuing controversy. The court emphasized the well‑settled rule that voluntary cessation does not automatically moot a case; a defendant cannot defeat jurisdiction merely by ending the challenged conduct unless it is “absolutely clear” the behavior will not recur. Judge Kobes’s concurrence adds a helpful caution: although Laidlaw can support a private plaintiff’s interest in public penalties, the doctrinal fit is not automatic — MFLSA penalties differ from the penalties in Laidlaw in ways the court did not fully brief — so the district court should consider those differences on remand.

## What the district court needs to decide (and what record it should develop)

On remand, the district court’s threshold task is factual: determine whether there remains a live controversy over the MFLSA penalties. That inquiry requires more than the stipulation that Cohen received his back pay. The court should develop a record answering whether Consilio’s allegedly violative overtime policy is still in force, and if it is not, whether there is a reasonable expectation that Consilio could or would resume the policy.

Useful facts and documentary proof the district court should seek include contemporaneous written policies and any subsequent revisions; company communications to employees about overtime pay practices (including the 2019 email and any later emails or memos); payroll records showing how overtime has been calculated and paid after the 2019 change; testimony from corporate representatives about whether the policy remains, the business rationale for past or present policies, and whether the employer reserves the right to revert to the old policy; and evidence of systemic practice (for example, whether offices outside Minnesota continue or resumed the challenged practice). The court should also consider whether Consilio has taken binding steps that would make recurrence unlikely — for instance, a court‑ordered injunction, a binding settlement providing for injunctive relief, or widespread, permanent policy changes reflected in formal corporate governance documents.

The district court should then assess whether, under Laidlaw, Cohen can plausibly show that future violations are reasonably likely such that civil penalties would provide the deterrent and prospective redress Laidlaw describes. If the record shows the policy persists or that the employer has a concrete ability and incentive to revert to the policy, the court should find the controversy live. If the record shows the policy has truly been abandoned and the employer’s cessation is both genuine and unlikely to be reversed, the claim as to penalties may be moot.

## The doctrinal tension: Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale vs. public penalties

The Eighth Circuit fairly summarizes the tension that underlies Laidlaw: civil penalties that inure to the public can still redress a private plaintiff’s interest by preventing future harm. But Judge Kobes’s concurrence raises an important question: do penalties payable to a state actor under the MFLSA map neatly onto Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale? The answer is not obvious and may depend on statutory structure and remedy design. Laidlaw involved environmental penalties and a defendant whose ongoing conduct plausibly put the plaintiff at risk; the Supreme Court found that the possibility of future violations — and the prospect that civil penalties would constrain them — meant the plaintiff had a cognizable interest even though it could not pocket penalty proceeds.

MFLSA statutory penalties that go to the Commissioner are similar in that penalties are public inurements. But there are differences that could matter: statutory allocation of recovered penalties, administrative enforcement mechanisms, and the remedial role of the Commissioner (including whether the Commissioner has independent prosecutorial authority) may influence the Article III analysis. The Eighth Circuit correctly refrained from resolving those finer points without a developed record and briefing; Judge Kobes’s concurrence signals that future panels or the Supreme Court may be asked to decide whether Laidlaw’s logic covers every statutory penalty regime the same way.

## Practical implications for litigants and courts

Cohen’s remand is a reminder of several practical lessons for litigants in wage‑and‑hour cases.

For plaintiffs, the decision underscores the importance of preserving a live controversy on the penalty question. If a defendant tenders back pay or liquidated damages, plaintiffs should seek to preserve claims for penalties and injunctive relief — and seek an evidentiary record on whether the offending policy remains. Early steps that help avoid mootness include promptly moving for class certification if appropriate (class claims raise separate mootness issues but can change the landscape), seeking a declaratory judgment or injunction as part of the complaint, and pressing for admissions or stipulated facts about whether the policy has been changed and whether the employer will continue the practice. If an employer pays back wages during litigation, plaintiffs should consider seeking a consent decree resolving both monetary and injunctive claims rather than accepting a unilateral tender that leaves only public penalties and thus raises standing issues.

For defendants, the case highlights a defensible tactic: paying wages demanded may reduce or remove a plaintiff’s personal injury and thereby complicate a penalty claim that is payable to the state. But voluntary payment is not a guaranteed jurisdictional escape hatch: courts will probe for continuing practices and for the reality of cessation. Employers should be prepared to show concrete and binding changes if they are to argue that a case is moot.

For courts, Cohen v. Consilio provides a useful roadmap for handling mid‑case tenders and voluntary cessation claims. The district court should make findings on whether challenged policies remain and whether cessation is genuine and permanent; those factual findings will decide the jurisdictional question and will help avoid piecemeal appeals.

## Broader doctrinal consequences

This remand may portend further doctrinal development. Different circuits have approached Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale and voluntary cessation doctrine with varying emphases. Cohen flags that Article III’s demand for a live case-or-controversy can create substantial practical limits on private enforcement of public penalties. If defendants can routinely moot cases by paying back pay while leaving statutes’ public penalties in play but without clear prospects of enforcement, private enforcement as a practical matter could be weakened — unless plaintiffs are able to secure injunctive relief, class certification, or otherwise obtain binding commitments from employers.

Judge Kobes’s invitation to scrutinize whether Laidlaw’s rationale uniformly supports claims for public penalties is likely to attract further attention. Lower courts will have to decide how much difference it makes that a penalty is payable to a public official rather than to the aggrieved individual. That question sits at the intersection of standing doctrine, remedial law, and statutory design — and it may push litigants to seek more robust injunctive or declaratory claims to avoid Article III problems.

## Bottom line

Cohen v. Consilio is a practical and doctrinally interesting decision: it affirms that paying a plaintiff’s back pay does not automatically render penalty claims moot, but it requires a concrete factual showing that wrongful conduct is likely to recur before a court will allow a private plaintiff to pursue penalties that inure to the state. The Eighth Circuit’s remand is a sensible, case‑specific application of Laidlaw and Town of Chester, and it invites district courts to develop the factual record needed to decide whether Article III permits private suits for public‑facing penalties to proceed after defendants tender full wages. Litigants on both sides should treat mid‑case payments as the start of a jurisdictional inquiry rather than its end.



# Waiver by Under‑briefing: The Demise of the MWTA Claim and Lessons for Plaintiffs

## Why this decision matters

The Eighth Circuit’s disposition of Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC is a reminder that in wage‑theft litigation, victory on the merits is not the only hurdle: a plaintiff who fails to develop statutory arguments or to link requested relief to a statute’s remedial scheme risks losing claims regardless of factual support. Cohen’s Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) count—premised on an allegedly deficient notice of an overtime policy change—did not fail because the court parsed complicated text about notice obligations; it failed because the plaintiff offered “little to no” briefing and “pointed to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties” under the MWTA, and therefore waived meaningful appellate review. The opinion reinforces two basic lessons for practitioners: preserve issues by briefing them fully at summary judgment and plead and prove entitlement to the specific remedies you seek.

## What the court did

Cohen sued his employer under three Minnesota wage statutes (the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA) alleging unpaid overtime and related penalties and injunctive relief. After the employer paid the overtime and liquidated damages under the MFLSA and the parties stipulated that Cohen had received all wages he claimed, the district court granted summary judgment to the employer on all claims. On appeal the Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s disposition of the MPWA and MWTA claims, vacated and remanded the MFLSA penalties question for the district court to consider mootness/standing issues, and left injunctive relief unaddressed as not properly presented.

On the MWTA claim specifically, the appellate court’s analysis was brief but decisive. Cohen had alleged that Consilio failed to provide the statutorily required notice of a change in its overtime policy under Minn. Stat. § 181.032(f), (d)(4). But the Eighth Circuit observed that Cohen “stipulated that he received all pay Consilio owes him, and he points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute.” Given Cohen’s scant briefing and lack of statutory grounding for a private civil penalty remedy, the court invoked the well‑worn principle that issues not meaningfully briefed on appeal (and in this instance, not meaningfully developed below) are forfeited. The court cited Milligan v. City of Red Oak and declined to disturb the district court’s dismissal.

## The doctrinal takeaways

There are three doctrinal strands woven through this short passage of the opinion that litigators should take to heart.

First, remedial entitlement matters. The Eighth Circuit did not rule on whether the MWTA’s notice provisions were violated on the facts; it ruled that Cohen had not shown that those provisions create a private civil‑penalty remedy payable to him. When statutes allocate penalties or enforcement authority to a government official (as the court earlier concluded the MPWA does for certain “average daily wage penalties”), private plaintiffs must show that the statutory design permits the specific relief they seek. It is not enough to show an alleged statutory violation; counsel must also show where the statute grants a private remedy.

Second, forfeiture by under‑briefing remains a live procedural weapon. Courts routinely refuse to address under‑developed arguments. The Eighth Circuit’s citation of Milligan underscores that a party cannot salvage a thinly briefed claim on appeal; the district court’s reasoning—if any—may stand unchallenged. Practitioners should treat appellate briefing obligations as jurisdictional in practice: the failure to brief an issue adequately can be outcome‑determinative.

Third, stipulations that eliminate damages can put the remaining claim (penalties payable to government or injunctive relief) at risk of dismissal for lack of a concrete, continuing injury. The opinion’s treatment of the MFLSA penalties (vacated and remanded because of potential mootness and standing issues) contrasts with the MWTA result; in the MWTA context, Cohen neither marshaled remedial authority nor preserved argumentation that a non‑monetary or public‑interest remedy remained viable.

## Practical lessons for plaintiffs and counsel

Successful MWTA litigation depends as much on statutory craftsmanship as on facts. A few concrete practices should reduce the risk of a Cohen‑style loss:

- Identify the remedy in the complaint. Plead not only the statutory violation but also the specific relief authorized by the statute—or explain why equitable relief or a public‑interest theory permits a plaintiff to seek penalties otherwise payable to a government official. If the statute channels penalties to the Commissioner, explain how that channeling does not foreclose private relief (or show alternative grounds for relief).

- Preserve and develop arguments below. Don’t rely on appellate rescue. Fully brief statutory interpretation and remedial issues at summary judgment and offer the district court supporting authorities; an under‑developed theory below is likely waived on appeal.

- Avoid stipulating away core injuries without reservation. If you accept payment of back wages to resolve one discrete remedy (e.g., overtime), consider preserving the right to litigate non‑monetary relief, penalties, or injunctive claims. If you must stipulate, make explicit reservations about remaining claims and the rationale for why those claims remain justiciable.

- Think administratively where the statute does. If a statute expressly empowers the state commissioner to demand wages and assess penalties, coordinate with the agency or at least plead how private enforcement can proceed in parallel or why equitable relief is available.

- When in doubt, plead alternative remedies. Pair MWTA theories with MPWA or MFLSA claims (when appropriate) and with requests for declaratory or injunctive relief that do not depend on a private civil‑penalty theory payable to the Commissioner.

## Broader implications and where this might lead

Cohen is unlikely to be read as a broad attack on MWTA claims; instead, it will be cited as a cautionary tale about the mechanics of statutory enforcement. Practically speaking, plaintiffs’ firms will likely sharpen pleadings and summary‑judgment briefs to spell out remedial bases; courts will continue to hold litigants to ordinary briefing standards. Legislatively, the decision highlights a potential gap: where the Legislature intends a private remedy for notice or other technical violations, it should say so clearly if it wants private enforcement to be the norm rather than the exception.

For defense counsel, the ruling reinforces two attractive case‑management strategies: press for stipulations that resolve wages and liquidated damages early, and exploit careful statutory reading (or lack of plaintiff briefing) to move for summary judgment on remedies. For plaintiffs’ counsel, the lesson is simple and unforgiving: don’t let a strong factual record be undone by weak briefing of statutory remedies.

Cohen demonstrates that in wage litigation, as in so much of litigation generally, the merits are necessary but not sufficient. The ability to identify and defend a path to the relief sought — and to preserve that path through the district court and on appeal — can mean the difference between a recoverable wrong and an enforceable victory.



# Pleadings Matter: Why Cohen’s Late-Breaking Injunctive Theory Went Nowhere

## Why this case matters
The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Cohen v. Consilio, LLC is a reminder that in federal practice, how you plead your case matters as much as the merits. Cohen tried to pivot at summary judgment from a monetary overtime claim to seeking an affirmative injunction ordering his employer to rescind a contested overtime policy. The court refused to entertain that new remedial theory because it was never pleaded—relying on the familiar principle that new claims belong in an amended complaint, not in opposition briefs. That procedural posture proved decisive: plaintiffs in wage-and-hour litigation who hope to preserve forward-looking relief (and thus avoid mootness when defendants tender back pay) take heed.

## What happened (short version)
Cohen sued Consilio for unpaid overtime and related state-law penalties after Consilio announced a change to its overtime premium policy. Consilio paid the overtime and liquidated damages Cohen sought under the Minnesota FLSA (and sent stipulated payments to the putative class), and the parties asked the district court to resolve remaining statutory-penalty and injunction issues on summary judgment. In briefing, Cohen abandoned some earlier requests and, for the first time, sought an injunction ordering Consilio to rescind the 2019 policy. The district court did not consider that newly pressed remedy, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed that omission: “[n]ew claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs” (citing InfoDeli, LLC). The court left open the MFLSA penalties claim (vacating that portion of the judgment) because tendered back pay can moot monetary claims, but it rejected Cohen’s belated injunctive demand as improperly raised.

## The court’s reasoning
The Eighth Circuit’s rejection of Cohen’s late theory rests on two interconnected principles. First, Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15 and the case law embody a norm that parties should not spring entirely new causes of action or remedies on an opponent at the summary-judgment stage. A summary-judgment response is not a vehicle to plead a new claim because the opposing party then lacks fair notice and the district court lacks a developed record tailored to that theory. Second, the panel relied on its own recent precedent (InfoDeli) to emphasize that amendments to the substantive scope of relief are properly effected through an amended complaint, not through argument introduced in opposition briefs. The district court’s omission, therefore, was not an abuse of discretion.

The opinion also signals another procedural and substantive concern that pushed against Cohen’s strategy: the risk of mootness. Consilio’s tender of overtime pay and liquidated damages meant the tangible monetary injury was addressed. To preserve a live federal case in those circumstances, plaintiffs often need prospective or injunctive relief to show continuing or imminent injury; but you cannot rely on a theory of forward-looking relief that the complaint never put at issue.

## Why shifting remedial theories at summary judgment is risky
Beyond the formal Rule 15 wrinkle, there are concrete reasons courts resist remedial pivots at summary judgment. The defendant has not had discovery directed at the new remedy; factual development on whether the policy is ongoing or whether rescission is necessary may be absent; the defendant lacks adequate notice to address the new relief; and allowing such pivots invites gamesmanship—plaintiffs could hold back requests until they see how defendant responds and then seek substantive relief without the normal pleading and amendment procedures.

From a strategic perspective, wage-and-hour plaintiffs who wait to request injunctive relief until after defendants tender back pay often find themselves too late. Tender may remove the plaintiff’s live monetary stake, leaving only forward-looking remedies as the basis for Article III jurisdiction. If those remedies were never pleaded, the court may dismiss or refuse to consider them. The Eighth Circuit’s remand on mootness of statutory penalties underscores the stakes: had Cohen pleaded an injunction at the outset, the court could have evaluated whether the policy posed a real and continuing threat and thus whether declaratory or injunctive relief kept the case alive despite the tender.

## How to preserve injunctive relief—practical lessons
Plaintiffs and counsel who want to avoid forfeiting prospective relief should heed three practical points.

First, plead forward-looking remedies from the outset. Include, in the complaint, clear requests for declaratory and injunctive relief tailored to the conduct you want the court to stop (e.g., rescission of an unlawful policy, a requirement to pay overtime going forward at a particular rate, or modification of notice protocols). If you believe a policy change is at issue, allege the policy, how it is unlawful, and why an injunction is necessary to prevent recurrence.

Second, be precise about the scope of injunctive relief. Vague requests for “all appropriate relief” are less helpful than specific, concrete requests (for example, a request that the employer reclassify certain positions, reinstate an overtime-premium practice, or provide specific employee notice). Specificity gives the defendant fair notice and helps justify expedited discovery on the question of ongoing violation.

Third, if a need to pivot arises mid-litigation, move to amend the complaint under Rule 15(a) promptly—do not merely argue a new theory in an opposition brief. Rule 15(a) directs that leave to amend be “freely given when justice so requires,” but courts may deny amendments that are untimely, prejudicial to the defendant, or futile. To improve the odds of a late amendment: explain the reason for delay, propose only narrow, tailored amendments, and show the amendment will not prejudice the defendant (for example, by proposing targeted expedited discovery or by explaining prior discovery already developed the relevant facts).

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects
Cohen reinforces a structural lesson: pleading shapes procedure and relief. For wage-and-hour litigation in particular, the case sharpens a familiar dynamic—defendants can neutralize monetary claims by tendering back pay, and plaintiffs must have contemporaneous claims for prospective relief if they wish to preserve a live controversy. The Eighth Circuit’s hesitance to entertain a new injunction theory at summary judgment also dovetails with separation-of-powers and notice principles: courts should adjudicate claims parties actually bring, after appropriate procedural steps, not theories first raised in response papers.

The opinion may also affect class litigation. Class plaintiffs who seek systemic relief to stop an employer’s policy must plead injunctive or declaratory relief clearly in the complaint. If they rely only on monetary damages, a defendant’s tender could scuttle the litigation by mooting the named plaintiff’s claim before class certification—leaving individual class members without a named representative with a live claim.

Finally, the concurrence’s skepticism about the standing consequences of statutory penalties (and the court’s vacatur of MFLSA summary judgment on mootness grounds) highlights another wrinkle: even when penalties are payable to the government rather than a plaintiff, Article III standing and mootness doctrines still govern. Plaintiffs should not assume that statutory penalties alone will preserve a live controversy where monetary relief has been satisfied. Injunctive relief remains the safest path to maintain jurisdiction when defendants attempt to end litigation through tender.

## Conclusion
Cohen v. Consilio is not a dramatic new rule of law, but it is a useful, concrete lesson in federal practice: if you want forward-looking relief, plead it timely and specifically. Don’t try to sneak new claims or remedies into a summary judgment response; courts expect parties to use the amendment process to enlarge the scope of relief. In wage-and-hour suits where defendants can blunt monetary claims by tendering back pay, failing to plead injunctive or declaratory relief can be fatal to the case’s survival. Counsel should plead forward and plead precisely—or be prepared to move early and explain why a late amendment is warranted.
