Title: Blog about the opinion: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC

# No Private Right to “Average Daily Wage” Penalties Under MPWA § 181.101

## Why this matters

In Cohen v. Consilio, LLC, the Eighth Circuit clarified a question of practical and procedural importance under Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (MPWA): who may seek the statute’s “average daily wage” penalties? The court held that § 181.101 reserves the power to obtain those penalties to the Commissioner of Labor and Industry, not to private employees bringing district‑court actions under § 181.171. That holding narrows one of the most potent levers employees might have used to extract recovery beyond unpaid wages and liquidated damages — and it reshapes litigation strategy and administrative enforcement dynamics in Minnesota wage cases.

The decision is also a tidy example of classic tools of statutory interpretation: careful attention to text, structural context, and comparison with nearby statutes that make different remedial arrangements. The court’s reading rests on the explicit mechanics of § 181.101, the statutory allocation of remedial authority in § 181.171, and the legislature’s demonstrated ability to grant private plaintiffs penalty remedies elsewhere in the MPWA framework. The case leaves intact employees’ ability to sue for unpaid wages, but it channels penalty enforcement through the Commissioner — a change that will affect how plaintiffs, their counsel, and employers approach wage disputes in Minnesota.

## The court’s reasoning, in plain terms

Cohen sued his employer on behalf of himself and similarly situated licensed‑attorney document reviewers, alleging unpaid overtime under a suite of Minnesota statutes: the MPWA (§§ 181.101, 181.171), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA). After Consilio paid the unpaid overtime and stipulated that Cohen had been paid all wages and liquidated damages owed under the MFLSA, the parties asked the district court to resolve whether statutory penalties were available. The district court dismissed the claims for penalties; Cohen appealed.

On the MPWA point, the Eighth Circuit affirmed. The court’s core observation is straightforward: Section 181.101 sets out a process by which the Commissioner (not an individual employee) may serve a demand for unpaid wages and, if the employer fails to pay within ten days, may “charge and collect” the unpaid wages and “a penalty in the amount of the employee’s average daily earnings . . . for each day beyond the ten‑day limit.” The statute expressly contemplates the Commissioner as the actor who “serve[s] a demand” and “may charge and collect” the penalty, and it directs that “money collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee.” The Eighth Circuit reads that language to mean: penalties under § 181.101 are obtained by — and through — the Commissioner, not by private suit.

The court reinforced that conclusion by looking at statutory structure and legislative drafting choices. It observed that other MPWA provisions explicitly authorize private plaintiffs to “charge and collect a penalty” (for example, §§ 181.13(a) and 181.14(2)), so the legislature knows how to confer penalty authority on private litigants when it intends to do so. The absence of comparable language in § 181.101, combined with the Commissioner‑centered mechanics in that provision, signals that penalties under § 181.101 are to be pursued administratively through the Commissioner.

The opinion also discussed Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exchange, a Minnesota Supreme Court decision that the parties had cited. Milner recognized that employees may enforce violations of § 181.101 and are entitled to payment of monies collected, but the Eighth Circuit read Milner as consistent with the proposition that the enforcement vehicle for the average daily wage penalty is the Commissioner. In short, Milner does not authorize a private‑party right to obtain the § 181.101 daily‑wage penalty in district court separate from the Commissioner’s collection role.

Finally, although Cohen’s MFLSA and MWTA claims were addressed in the same litigation, the Eighth Circuit vacated and remanded with respect to the MFLSA penalties because of potential Article III jurisdictional issues — notably, whether the claim had become moot once Consilio paid the substantive overtime and liquidated damages and whether the allegedly wrongful policy remained in place. The MWTA claim was affirmed as dismissed because Cohen neither developed a persuasive statutory argument nor pointed to authority providing for civil penalties under that statute.

## Analytical perspective: clear textual signals, predictable consequences

The court’s approach is conservative and text‑oriented, and that is appropriate here. The language of § 181.101 is strikingly administrative: it describes what the Commissioner “may” do after serving a demand and directs the Commissioner how to distribute collected funds. When a statute prescribes an administrative collection mechanism and names the administrative actor, courts routinely treat that design as limiting private remedies unless the statute elsewhere confers private penalty powers. The Eighth Circuit follows that well‑trodden path.

Comparative statutory analysis was decisive: the legislature’s inclusion of explicit private penalty authority in nearby provisions (e.g., §§ 181.13 and 181.14) acts as a negative indicator that the absence of such language in § 181.101 was purposeful. That is textbook statutory construction — the “knows‑how” argument the court relied on — and it is hard to fault.

Two possible tensions are worth flagging. First, § 181.171 expressly allows an “aggrieved” employee to bring a civil action “seeking redress for violations” of several MPWA provisions, and it says an employer “is liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” At first blush that language could be read to support a private civil claim for all relief “appropriate” for violations of the listed sections. The Eighth Circuit sidestepped overbroad readings of “other appropriate relief” by anchoring relief to what the specific violated section authorizes. Where the violated provision (like § 181.101) ties penalties to the Commissioner, an employee’s private suit cannot bootstrap a separate penalty remedy. That reconciliation avoids a clash between § 181.171’s general grant and § 181.101’s specific machinery.

Second, the court’s reading narrows the practical leverage a private plaintiff has in settlement negotiations. Average‑daily‑wage penalties can be quite large and can provide immediate pressure on employers to settle. By reserving the penalties to the Commissioner, the opinion pushes plaintiffs toward either (1) pursuing the Commissioner’s administrative process (i.e., seeking the Commissioner’s demand and collection authority), (2) staking relief on other statutory remedies that do confer private penalties, or (3) focusing on the MFLSA (federal or state analog) liquidated damages and injunctive relief — though, as the opinion underscores, standing and mootness can complicate injunctive relief once the employer has paid wages.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

For plaintiffs and practitioners in Minnesota wage litigation, Cohen has several important practical implications.

First, it clarifies that when a private plaintiff’s primary hope is to obtain § 181.101’s average‑daily‑wage penalties, the proper avenue is administrative: the Commissioner must be involved (or at least the Commissioner’s statutory process must be triggered). Plaintiffs’ counsel should engage the Department of Labor and Industry early, and consider administrative demand procedures as either a standalone strategy or a complement to private litigation. The Commissioner’s involvement may be necessary to unlock the specific penalty at issue.

Second, the decision will likely affect settlement dynamics. Employers facing private MPWA suits that demand the § 181.101 penalty now face a potentially different calculus. Without the threat of a privately enforceable, large per‑day statutory penalty in district court, defendants may be less pressured to settle early — unless the plaintiff can secure an administrative demand or point to other statutes that allow private penalties. Conversely, plaintiffs who do obtain a Commissioner action may gain the same leverage, but that requires a path through the agency rather than immediate district‑court leverage.

Third, the case highlights the continued importance of choosing statutory predicates carefully. Where a statute explicitly grants private plaintiffs penalty authority, the private suit can press that remedy. Where the statute assigns collection to an administrative official, the private suit may be limited to unpaid wages and other remedies the statute actually affords to private litigants. Practitioners should review the MPWA’s provisions with an eye to which remedial tools each section actually provides.

Fourth, the Eighth Circuit’s remand on MFLSA penalties and its discussion of standing and mootness is a reminder that payment of the substantive relief can strip a plaintiff of Article III standing to pursue non‑monetary or punitive remedies — particularly when the only remaining relief is payable to the state (like MFLSA statutory penalties paid to the Commissioner). Plaintiffs should think early about preserving live controversies (for example, by seeking injunctive relief before a complete mooting payment or by ensuring the challenged policy remains extant) if they intend to pursue penalties or prospective relief.

Finally, employers should take from Cohen a durable compliance lesson. Even though private plaintiffs may not be able to extract § 181.101’s average‑daily‑wage penalty directly in district court, the agency retains authority to demand payment and collect the penalty. From a compliance perspective, employers who ignore a Commissioner demand do so at peril: the administrative route can still produce the same statutory penalty, and the Commissioner may be motivated to act where systemic violations are alleged. In short, the decision channels enforcement rather than eliminating it.

## Bottom line

Cohen v. Consilio is a clear, text‑based ruling that narrows private enforcement of the MPWA by holding that the average‑daily‑wage penalty in § 181.101 is a Commissioner‑driven remedy, not a private statutory penalty available in a district‑court action under § 181.171. The decision emphasizes statutory text and structure and is unlikely to be controversial as an interpretive matter. The practical fallout will be to redirect certain penalty claims into the administrative arena, alter settlement dynamics, and require plaintiffs to plan litigation strategy around the role of the Commissioner — while leaving intact employees’ ability to sue for unpaid wages and other remedies the statutes expressly provide to private litigants.

For Minnesota practitioners, the key takeaway is procedural as much as substantive: if you want the § 181.101 average‑daily‑wage penalty, involve the Commissioner.



# Penalties Without Payout: Standing and Mootness for MFLSA Civil Penalties Payable Only to the State

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s opinion in Cohen v. Consilio, LLC puts squarely before the courts a recurring, practical question that turns on Article III standing and the mootness doctrine: When an employer has paid an employee all back wages and liquidated damages, but the employer’s policy change leaves only statutory civil penalties that—under state law—must be paid to the state commissioner rather than to the employee, does a private plaintiff still have a live case or controversy to sustain a claim for those penalties? That question matters not just for adjudicatory economics (who gets to finish a case when the money has changed hands) but for the enforcement architecture of wage laws across many states, where some statutory penalties are structured to be collected by or through state agencies.

The Eighth Circuit’s decision affirms the district court’s dismissal of some claims, vacates summary judgment on the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) claim, and remands for the district court to decide whether it has jurisdiction. The court relied on Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale for standing, but also recognized the risk that the case is moot if the employer’s wrongful conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur. Judge Kobes—concurring specially—expressed skepticism about whether Laidlaw’s environmental-enforcement reasoning neatly translates to MFLSA civil penalties that flow to the state, rather than to an injured private plaintiff.

The decision is important because it highlights (1) the interplay between standing and statutory designs that direct penalties to government coffers, (2) limits and openings of the voluntary-cessation doctrine, and (3) how pleading and litigation posture (stipulations that wages and liquidated damages were paid) can force courts to confront jurisdictional questions before merits resolution.

## What the Eighth Circuit did

Bruce Cohen sued his employer, Consilio, alleging unpaid overtime under Minnesota law (MPWA, MFLSA) and failure to provide required notice under the Minnesota Wage Theft Act. After suit, Consilio paid the overtime wages and liquidated damages Cohen sought under the MFLSA, and the parties stipulated that he had been paid all wages and liquidated damages claimed. The remaining contested issue was statutory penalties—both whether the statute authorizes them and whether Cohen can pursue them.

On the MPWA claim, the court concluded that the statute’s text confines the power to collect the “average daily wage” penalty to the Minnesota Commissioner of Labor and Industry. Because §181.101 sets up a commissioner-initiated demand-and-collection mechanism, the Eighth Circuit held that the employee cannot independently recover that specific penalty in district court. That part of the opinion is straightforward statutory interpretation—examining the allocation of enforcement authority and noting that Minnesota elsewhere knows how to authorize private collection when it intends to do so.

The more novel doctrinal work comes on the MFLSA claim. Although Cohen had been paid all wages and liquidated damages, he pressed for statutory penalties that, under the MFLSA scheme, would be payable to the Commissioner rather than to him. The court recognized that civil penalties paid to the government can sometimes confer standing for private plaintiffs under the Supreme Court’s decision in Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw: the deterrent effect of an injunction or a penalty can redress an injury by removing an ongoing source of harm. But the court also acknowledged the converse: if the defendant’s voluntary payment or policy change makes it “absolutely clear” that the wrongful conduct will not recur, the claim may be moot. Because the factual record did not clearly resolve whether Consilio’s problematic overtime policy remained in place, the Eighth Circuit vacated the grant of summary judgment and remanded for the district court to determine whether a live controversy remains. Judge Kobes wrote separately noting doubts about the fit of Laidlaw’s rationale outside its environmental context but concurred in the result.

## The court’s doctrinal reasoning — strengths and tension points

The opinion balances two settled principles that can pull in different directions. On the one hand, Town of Chester and other recent Supreme Court decisions require plaintiffs to establish standing “for each form of relief that is sought.” The Eighth Circuit correctly recognized that a plaintiff’s entitlement to injunctive or deterrent relief must be tied to a continuing injury or risk of future injury. On the other hand, Laidlaw stands for the proposition that civil penalties payable to the government can still provide Article III redress when they serve a deterrent function and hence alleviate a plaintiff’s concern about ongoing violations.

Applying Laidlaw here is sensible in the abstract: if a defendant’s continued policy or practice threatens future underpayment, a court-ordered penalty or injunction could deter future violations and thus vindicate the private plaintiff’s interest in compliance. The Eighth Circuit properly flags that standing and mootness turn on whether the allegedly wrongful policy persists and whether voluntary cessation by the defendant “makes it absolutely clear” the conduct won’t recur.

That said, the fit is not seamless. Laidlaw emerged from environmental citizen-suit doctrines where public-interest enforcement and deterrence have a recognized causal link to private plaintiffs’ interests. Wage statutes that house penalties in the coffers of a state commissioner create a different enforcement architecture: the private plaintiff is not the intended recipient of the statute’s monetary remedy. Judge Kobes’s concurrence points to that distinction and suggests the parties’ briefs did not squarely confront whether Laidlaw’s rationale, rooted in environmental enforcement, should be adopted wholesale for the MFLSA context.

There are at least two doctrinal tension points to watch:

1. Redressability when the plaintiff cannot receive the monetary remedy. Standing requires that a favorable decision be likely to redress the plaintiff’s injury. If the only remaining remedy is a penalty payable to the state, courts must ask whether a penalty paid to the commissioner would meaningfully redress the plaintiff’s cognizable injury (the risk of future underpayment). Laidlaw suggests it might, but the link is more attenuated when the statutory design channels the money away from the injured party.

2. Voluntary cessation and the practical effect of payment. A defendant’s payment of back wages and liquidated damages might remove the plaintiff’s personal stake. But the risk of recurrence depends on whether the employer has abandoned the offending policy or merely paid up in this case. The “absolutely clear” standard is high; defendants who return to the same policy soon after litigation are at risk of being subject to later suits. The court’s remand invites the district court to analyze both the existence of the challenged policy now and any evidence suggesting a reasonable likelihood of recurrence.

## Possible outcomes on remand

On remand, the district court has several paths—each with significant implications.

1. The court finds the employer’s policy has been rescinded (or modified), and it is “absolutely clear” the conduct will not recur. The MFLSA penalty claim becomes moot for want of a live controversy, and the district court should dismiss for lack of jurisdiction. That result would treat voluntary cessation and payment as sufficient to strip the plaintiff of Article III standing when no continuing injury remains.

2. The court finds the employer’s policy remains—or there is credible evidence it could recur. Under Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale, the plaintiff could have standing to pursue statutory penalties payable to the Commissioner because a favorable ruling (civil penalties, injunctive relief) could deter future violations and thus remove the threatened injury. The case proceeds to consider the statutory availability of penalties and, if warranted, merits.

3. The court questions Laidlaw’s applicability in this statutory context and seeks briefing on whether the MFLSA’s statutory scheme contemplates private enforcement of commissioner-payable penalties. The court might conclude that, even if a penalty would deter future violations, the statute’s allocation of the monetary remedy to the commissioner means the private plaintiff lacks standing to seek that relief. That would leave a statutory gap in private enforcement—penalties exist, but private litigants may not be the vehicle to obtain them—potentially shifting enforcement to the Commissioner unless state law provides alternative private remedies.

Which path the court chooses will hinge on the factual record (does a policy still exist?) and legal choices about Laidlaw’s reach. The Eighth Circuit left both lines open.

## Broader implications

The decision (and the remand) matters beyond Minnesota. Many states mix private wage claims with administrative enforcement roles for labor commissioners. Where statutes direct penalties to a state agency, the availability of private enforcement becomes uncertain if private plaintiffs cannot obtain the penalty themselves. The Eighth Circuit’s analysis suggests that a plaintiff can sometimes proceed even when the only monetary prize would go to the state, but only if the deterrent effect meaningfully redresses the plaintiff’s injury.

Practically, employers can limit private suits by paying back wages and liquidated damages and representing that they’ve changed conduct—but courts will scrutinize whether the conduct change is genuine and durable. Plaintiffs’ lawyers should be prepared to develop the factual record about whether the defendant’s policy remains and to brief squarely whether Laidlaw’s deterrence logic applies when penalties flow to the state. Conversely, state commissioners may see an influx of cases where private plaintiffs, rather than the commissioner, press for penalties—they may wish to clarify enforcement priorities or seek statutes that either explicitly allow private recovery of penalties or otherwise vest enforcement more fully in the agency.

The decision also has constitutional overtones: if private plaintiffs routinely lack standing to seek state-direct penalties, states may find themselves with under-deterrence unless agencies step in. That would create a de facto enforcement gap if agencies are under-resourced or unmotivated to pursue every employer transgression.

## A note on statutory interpretation and separation of powers

The Eighth Circuit’s MPWA analysis is a reminder that statutory text matters. Where Minnesota’s legislature expressly empowered the commissioner to collect certain penalties, the court enforced that allocation and declined to create a private right of recovery by implication. That approach hews to separation-of-powers and administrative-design principles: if the legislature intends private plaintiffs to recover penalties, it knows how to say so. Lower courts will likely follow that textual cue when similar statutes exist.

But on the MFLSA side, the question is less purely textual and more about Article III doctrine. The court did the right thing procedurally by vacating and remanding rather than issuing a broad ruling about standing in the abstract. That restraint keeps a potentially far-reaching doctrinal rule from being made on an incomplete record.

## Conclusion

Cohen v. Consilio forces a junction between the mechanics of statutory enforcement and the constitutional limits of Article III. The Eighth Circuit’s vacatur and remand emphasize that standing and mootness analyses remain live, fact-sensitive inquiries even where statutory penalties exist but are payable to a state agency. The opinion preserves two central possibilities on remand—either the case will be dismissed as moot if the employer’s policy has been abandoned, or the plaintiff may proceed under Laidlaw-style deterrence reasoning if the threat of recurrence persists. Judge Kobes’s concurrence is a useful caution: the doctrinal gatekeeping that served Laidlaw in environmental cases may not be a perfect fit everywhere. The district court’s next steps—and any subsequent appellate decision—will help define how courts reconcile private enforcement interests with statutory designs that route penalties through public agencies.



# When Paying Up Isn’t the End: Voluntary Cessation, “Pick‑Offs,” and Injunctive Relief in Wage Cases

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Cohen v. Consilio, LLC is a useful reminder that defendants who “pay up” after a suit is filed do not always succeed in foreclosing all relief. Employers can extinguish a plaintiff’s claims for past money damages by tendering payment, but the doctrine of voluntary cessation and Laidlaw’s standing principles mean plaintiffs may still have live claims for civil penalties payable to the state and for injunctive relief designed to stop ongoing or future violations. At the same time, Cohen underscores a cautionary lesson for plaintiffs: if you want injunctive relief or to preserve class/representative claims aimed at deterrence, you must plead them clearly and build the factual record early. And for defendants, the decision highlights both the utility and limits of tactical tenders — especially in the post‑Campbell‑Ewald era where “pick‑offs” can raise thorny mootness and jurisdictional questions.

The case arose after Consilio adopted a new overtime policy eliminating premium overtime pay for certain Minnesota‑based hourly licensed‑attorney document reviewers. Plaintiff Bruce Cohen sued under several Minnesota wage statutes, seeking overtime pay, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief. Consilio paid the overtime and liquidated damages. The district court granted summary judgment for the employer; the Eighth Circuit affirmed in part, vacated in part, and remanded—most notably vacating the summary judgment dismissal of Cohen’s Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) penalties claim and sending that issue back for the district court to assess whether it retained jurisdiction.

The opinion is compact, but it ties together several recurring themes in modern wage‑and‑hour litigation: the difference between relief that benefits a private plaintiff directly (wages, liquidated damages) and relief that benefits the public (statutory penalties payable to an agency), the continuing vitality of Laidlaw’s voluntary‑cessation inquiry in assessing mootness, and the procedural importance of preserving injunction claims. Those themes are worth unpacking for practitioners and courts alike.

## How the court reasoned

The Eighth Circuit addressed three discrete buckets of relief: (1) penalties under Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), (2) penalties under the MFLSA (state analogue), and (3) penalties under the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), plus injunctive relief.

On the MPWA claim (Minn. Stat. § 181.101), the court applied ordinary statutory interpretation. While the MPWA allows employees to bring a civil action under § 181.171, the penalty machinery in § 181.101—an “average daily wage” penalty—can be triggered only after the Commissioner of Labor serves a demand and then collects penalties if the employer does not pay within ten days. The court concluded that the statute’s text ties the collection of that particular penalty to the Commissioner’s office, not to employees directly. The result: the district court did not err in denying Cohen an “average daily wage” award under § 181.101 because the statutory text reserves that remedy to the Commissioner.

By contrast, the MFLSA claim presented a different question. Cohen had been paid (and stipulated he had been paid) all overtime wages and liquidated damages, but he continued to press for statutory penalties. The Eighth Circuit recognized that civil penalties—even those payable to the government—can sometimes cure the plaintiff’s Article III injury by deterring ongoing or future unlawful conduct (citing Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw). But the court also emphasized that standing and mootness must be evaluated for each form of relief sought and throughout the case. Because the parties had not developed the factual record about whether Consilio’s unlawful overtime policy remained in place (and because that factual question is pivotal to whether the case is moot under Laidlaw/City of Mesquite principles), the court vacated the district court’s dismissal of the MFLSA penalty claim and remanded for the district court to determine jurisdiction—i.e., whether the voluntary cessation was permanent or whether the plaintiff retained a credible threat of future injury.

The MWTA claim, by contrast, was dismissed without extended discussion because Cohen failed to develop a statutory or factual argument showing a right to civil penalties under the MWTA, and he had stipulated that he received all compensation owed. Finally, Cohen’s request for an injunction to rescind the 2019 policy was not considered on appeal because he had altered his requested injunctive relief in briefing without amending the complaint or preserving the argument properly; the court treated that as forfeited.

Judge Kobes’ concurrence added a useful caveat: he was skeptical (but not definitive) about plaintiff standing to seek statutory penalties under Laidlaw, noting that the parties had not briefed that question, and he agreed remand to resolve mootness was the appropriate course.

## Putting the decision in context: Laidlaw, Campbell‑Ewald, and “pick‑offs”

Cohen sits at the intersection of two modern doctrinal currents. First is Laidlaw’s teaching about voluntary cessation and citizen‑plaintiff standing: even when a defendant stops an alleged wrongdoing, a plaintiff can retain standing to seek relief that has deterrent effect if the defendant’s cessation is not “absolutely clear” and if the challenged conduct could reasonably be expected to recur. That principle preserves courts’ ability to issue injunctions or awards serving public or prospective interests even after a monetary remedy for past harm has been offered.

Second is the more recent concern about defendants’ strategic tenders or “pick‑offs.” After the Supreme Court’s treatment of tenders in Campbell‑Ewald and subsequent cases, defendants sometimes try to extinguish representative or class claims by paying an individual plaintiff the full individual relief before class certification or before the plaintiff can expand relief to a class. Courts have split on whether such tenders moot the plaintiff’s claim and on how to treat the residual interests of the class or in deterrence. Cohen makes plain that tendering wages and liquidated damages will often not be the endgame: if statutory penalties are payable to the government or if injunctive relief is sought to stop an ongoing policy, defendants’ tender may not moot the whole case.

But Cohen also shows the limits of this protection for plaintiffs. If a plaintiff does not preserve an injunction claim in the complaint or fails to develop facts showing an ongoing policy (the “credible threat” needed under Laidlaw), a defendant’s tender may effectively neuter the case. That is precisely why the Eighth Circuit remanded the MFLSA penalty claim for a focused jurisdictional inquiry instead of accepting a premature summary‑judgment dismissal.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

For plaintiffs: Cohen underscores two important doctrinal points. One, differentiate the kinds of relief you seek and explain why each form of relief remains necessary even after monetary payments: if you seek statutory penalties payable to the state or injunctive relief, explain how those remedies will redress ongoing or future injuries and how enforcement would work. Two, plead injunctive relief clearly and early; don’t try to introduce a new form of equitable relief for the first time in opposition briefings or summary‑judgment papers. Failing to do so risks forfeiture.

For courts: the opinion is a reminder that mootness and standing questions require a fact‑specific inquiry. When defendants voluntarily cease conduct or tender individual relief, courts should not reflexively dismiss remaining claims without evaluating whether the cessation is permanent and whether the plaintiff has a continuing interest in deterrent remedies. This dovetails with Laidlaw’s caution against treating voluntary cessation as inherently rendering a case moot.

For defendants: the decision tempers the apparent allure of pick‑offs. Paying past wages and liquidated damages may resolve the individual plaintiff’s direct monetary claims, but it may not foreclose statutory penalties or injunctive remedies, particularly where those remedies serve the public interest or are payable to a state agency. Tactical tenders thus carry a risk: they buy a partial peace, but they may leave open the possibility of agency‑level penalties or a renewed plaintiff challenge if the policy persists.

For class litigation: Cohen is another reminder that representative litigation incentives (deterrence, systemic change) are often not fully satisfied by single‑plaintiff tenders. Plaintiffs seeking to preserve class or representative claims should seek class certification early where appropriate or ensure their complaints and discovery show ongoing, class‑wide practices that justify injunctive or statutory relief beyond individual make‑whole payments.

## Practical guidance for litigants and counsel

1. Plead and preserve injunctive relief from the outset. If your theory depends on stopping an ongoing policy (e.g., an illegal overtime policy), the complaint should say so and identify the relief sought: rescission of the policy, injunctive prohibition of its enforcement, and prospective monitoring or reporting if needed. Do not rely on introducing new equitable requests only at summary judgment or on appeal.

2. Build a factual record about current policies early. Discovery should target whether the defendant’s policy is still in force, who implements it, and whether pay practice changes were formal and durable. The stronger the record that a policy persists, the less likely a court will find a voluntary cessation to be “absolutely clear.”

3. Distinguish remedies in your pleadings and briefs. Explain why statutory penalties payable to a government agency can still provide non‑speculative deterrent benefits that remedy an Article III injury, and show how injunctive relief would redress a continuing or future injury.

4. Anticipate and litigate “pick‑off” strategies. If a defendant tenders individual relief, move quickly to preserve classwide or representative relief (seek class certification if fit), and file clear arguments on why the tender does not moot penalties or equitable claims. Be prepared to show the cessation is not permanent.

5. Consider coordinating with state agencies. When statutory penalties are administered by a state Commissioner or agency (as under Minnesota’s § 181.101), consider whether parallel administrative complaints or coordination with the agency are prudent. The statute’s allocation of collection authority to the Commissioner can materially affect remedies available in private litigation.

## Conclusion

Cohen v. Consilio is not a blockbuster ruling, but it is a practical and doctrinally instructive one. It reiterates that voluntary payment of past wages does not inevitably end litigation, especially when penalties payable to the public and injunctive relief remain in play. At the same time, it reminds plaintiffs that preserving those forms of relief takes care: plead them expressly, build the record about current practices, and resist the temptation to rely on last‑minute equitable pivots. For defendants, the decision tempers the promise of quick tolling through tactical tenders: paying the plaintiff may win one battle but leave other, institutional battles — and the courts’ jurisdictional scrutiny — very much intact.



# Preservation Matters: The MWTA Claim and the Perils of Changing Remedies Midstream

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Cohen v. Consilio, LLC offers a compact but instructive lesson about two oft-overlooked features of modern litigation: the limits of appellate review when a party reframes its relief late in the case, and the practical obligation to align claims, remedies, and procedural posture before pressing an appeal. At first blush, this is an overtime-pay case; on closer inspection, it is a preservation case. The court affirmed dismissal of the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) claim and declined to entertain a newly framed request for injunctive relief that Cohen raised in opposition to summary judgment. The opinion underscores (1) the risk of waiver and forfeiture when a party fails to develop arguments or to seek leave to amend the complaint, and (2) the distinct procedures—Rule 15 and appellate briefing rules—that govern introducing new theories of relief in district courts and on appeal.

Practitioners who litigate wage-and-hour claims (or any statutory claim for statutory and equitable relief) should take note: late changes in the remedy you seek, or a failure to tie a requested remedy to statutory authority, can be fatal to appellate review.

## What the court did

The Eighth Circuit affirmed summary judgment dismissal as to Cohen’s MWTA claim and declined to consider his request, made for the first time in opposition to summary judgment, that the court order Consilio to rescind its 2019 overtime policy. The panel relied on two principles.

First, the court applied ordinary forfeiture and waiver doctrine: Cohen had not developed a sustained, statutory- or precedent-based argument that the MWTA furnished a damages or penalty remedy, and he did not adequately present or preserve an argument in the district court or on appeal. The opinion relies on the familiar (and recently cited) line of Eighth Circuit decisions holding that failure to develop an argument in briefing amounts to abandonment and forfeiture — the opinion cites InfoDeli, LLC v. W. Robidoux, Inc., and earlier cases such as Milligan v. City of Red Oak. That jurisprudence places the burden on litigants to present legal authority and argumentation in district court and on appeal in order to preserve issues for review.

Second, the court emphasized pleading and amendment rules. Cohen sought different specific forms of injunctive relief in his complaint but then shifted course in his summary-judgment briefing to seek rescission of a policy he alleged was unlawful. The panel explained that a litigant who wishes to advance "new claims" or materially different remedies should do so through an amended complaint under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15, not by springing them in an opposition brief. Because Cohen did not seek leave to amend, and because the district court reasonably declined to consider remedies that were not properly pleaded, the appellate court treated the newly urged relief as not properly before it.

## Why the court’s reasoning makes sense — and its limits

The court’s reasoning is pragmatic and steeped in institutional comity. District courts adjudicate cases on the claims and remedies the parties present, and they depend on adversarial presentation to identify the legal contours of relief. Allowing litigants to transform their requested relief later in the case—without amendment and without giving the district court and the opposing party a chance to respond—would undermine notice and could unfairly sandbag defendants. The Eighth Circuit’s reliance on InfoDeli and Rule 28(a)(9)(A) (the rule requiring appellate briefs to include “appellant’s contentions and the reasons for them, with citations”) simply enforces these bedrock procedural norms.

There are nevertheless normative tensions. Modern summary-judgment practice often features fluid claims and iterative briefing; parties learn facts through discovery and may reasonably adjust the relief they seek. Courts must balance procedural regularity against the equitable imperative of resolving statutory rights. If a litigant frames a theoretically available but unpleaded equitable remedy late in the game, a strict bar on consideration can feel formalistic—particularly where the defendant’s policy is conceded to be unlawful (a circumstance the Eighth Circuit acknowledges elsewhere in the opinion). The court tried to cabin those concerns by noting that Cohen had previously sought other forms of injunctive relief, but that did not save his new, specific request from waiver.

Finally, the opinion correctly reminds practitioners that different remedies can implicate different pleading and standing issues; rescission or affirmative injunctive relief often requires distinct factual showings and may implicate mootness or redressability in ways that monetary claims do not. Thus, the procedural requirement to amend isn’t mere formality—it helps ensure courts and parties address whether equitable relief is appropriate given the available facts and statutory framework.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Several doctrinal takeaways flow from this disposition.

First, the decision reinforces the line between mere advocacy in briefs and formal pleading. Parties seeking new forms of relief should not expect appellate courts to entertain them if they were not pleaded and litigated below. That has particular salience in wage-and-hour litigation, where plaintiffs may seek a mix of statutory damages, penalties payable to state agencies, liquidated damages, and injunctive relief. Each remedy can raise distinct procedural and substantive hurdles; litigants must choose their path deliberately and, if they pivot, must use Rule 15 to do so.

Second, the opinion signals that appellate courts will not loosen forfeiture rules simply because statutory authorities are unsettled or because a defect in the district court’s analysis might otherwise exist. Cohen criticized the district court for providing “little to no discussion” of the MWTA claim, but the panel treated the omission as politically unremarkable given the failure to preserve the issue. That may discourage plaintiffs from “throwing in” alternative remedies in opposition briefing and expecting a later do-over on appeal.

Third, the decision may incentivize earlier engagement with statutory text and administrative procedures. The Eighth Circuit’s broader opinion (which, in other parts, discusses whether certain penalties are collectable only by the Commissioner) shows how statutory design can shape both remedies and process. Plaintiffs and their counsel must map statutory enforcement schemes before selecting remedies—penalties reserved for administrative collection cannot necessarily be commandeered as private causes of action without careful briefing and litigation strategy.

Fourth, lower courts will likely cite this decision when trimming untimely or unpleaded requests for equitable relief. The combined reliance on Rule 15 and appellate forfeiture doctrine gives trial judges solid footing to deny last-minute remedial pivots as lacking proper procedural foundation.

## Practical lessons for practitioners

The opinion offers concrete lessons about crafting litigation strategy and preserving issues for appeal.

First, plead with your remedies in mind. If you might ultimately seek rescission, an order requiring policy changes, or other equitable relief, put those requests in your complaint (or move to amend early). That gives you the best claim to appellate review if the district court declines to grant your requested relief.

Second, if you decide during the case that a different form of relief is necessary, move to amend under Rule 15 promptly. Rule 15 favors amendment, but courts are within their discretion to deny late amendments that prejudice the opposing party or disrupt the schedule. If you seek leave, explain the factual or legal development that justifies the change and be prepared to explain why the motion is timely.

Third, develop statutory arguments in the record. The Eighth Circuit’s reliance on waiver and forfeiture is rooted in the absence of developed argumentation regarding statutory authority for particular penalties or remedies. Don’t assume the panel will fill gaps for you; marshal statutory text, precedent, and administrative schemes in district court briefing.

Fourth, remember that appellate rules matter. Appellate counsel should ensure that briefs comply with Rule 28 and related local rules; failure to identify and brief an issue sufficiently can lead to waiver on appeal.

Finally, consider strategic sequencing. Where statutory penalties are payable to a state agency rather than to the employee, or where the employer has already paid monetary damages, consider whether injunctive relief is your only meaningful remedy — and if so, articulate that clearly from the outset so that the district court can evaluate standing and the availability of equitable relief.

## Conclusion

Cohen v. Consilio is not a sweeping pronouncement about the substantive scope of the MWTA or about when rescission is appropriate. Instead, it is a reminder of litigation fundamentals: you must present and preserve the remedies you seek, and you must tie those remedies to statutory authority and proper procedural practice. For litigators, the takeaway is straightforward: preserve early, brief clearly, and amend formally when your theory of relief changes. Courts will enforce those boundaries, and failure to do so risks losing review on appeal.



# Agency-Centered Penalties and Private Enforcement in Minnesota Wage Law After Cohen

The Eighth Circuit's decision in Cohen v. Consilio, LLC reaffirms a distinctive feature of Minnesota wage law: the state’s enforcement regime bifurcates private recovery of wages from collection of certain statutory penalties, and it vests the Commissioner of Labor with exclusive authority to pursue—or collect—those penalties. At stake in this case was not whether employees could recover unpaid overtime (they can) but who may obtain the “average daily wage” penalties spelled out in Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a). By holding that those penalties are available only through the Commissioner’s demand-and-collection process, the court confirmed that Minnesota has chosen a hybrid enforcement model that preserves a central role for the labor agency in meting out a particular form of statutory deterrence. That posture has important doctrinal and policy consequences for private enforcement, deterrence incentives, and the allocation of litigation power between public agencies and private attorneys general.

Why this decision matters
The Cohen opinion matters for three related reasons. First, it clarifies the statutory text and structure of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA): employees can prosecute claims for unpaid wages in court, but the MPWA’s penalty scheme in § 181.101(a) is triggered by and implemented through the Commissioner’s demand for payment. Second, it demarcates the limits of private enforcement in Minnesota wage law, contrasting sharply with regimes that allow private plaintiffs to recover statutory penalties directly (for example, California’s Private Attorneys General Act (PAGA) and similar private-penalty statutes). Third, the ruling raises practical questions about how robust deterrence will be achieved where the state (not private plaintiffs) controls penalty collection—questions that implicate resource allocation, prosecutorial discretion, and litigation strategy.

The court’s reasoning in Cohen
The Eighth Circuit’s analysis is straightforward and textual. Cohen was an hourly licensed attorney document reviewer who challenged his employer’s elimination of overtime premium pay. Consilio paid the overtime and liquidated damages in full, and the parties stipulated that wages and liquidated damages were satisfied. The only contested relief was statutory penalties—most prominently the “average daily wage” penalty articulated in § 181.101(a), which the statute authorizes “the commissioner” to charge and collect if an employer fails to pay wages within ten days of a commissioner’s demand.

The court emphasized ordinary principles of statutory interpretation: when a statute’s language is plain and limits a remedy to an identified public official (here, the Commissioner), courts should give effect to that limitation. The text of § 181.101 repeatedly references the Commissioner as the actor who may serve a demand and then charge and collect wages and penalties; the court contrasted that text with other provisions in the wage statutes where the legislature expressly allows employees to collect penalties themselves. That juxtaposition—combined with Minnesota precedent and the Legislature’s demonstrated ability to delegate penalty authority to private plaintiffs in other sections—led the panel to conclude that the Legislature intended the § 181.101 penalty to be an agency-centered remedy. Accordingly, the district court did not err in denying Cohen the average daily wage penalties under the MPWA.

The court’s handling of the federal (MFLSA) statutory penalty claim was procedural and prudential. Because Consilio had paid Cohen the overtime and liquidated damages, and because it was ambiguous whether Consilio’s allegedly unlawful policy remained in place, the court could not be confident it had jurisdiction over a claim for federal statutory penalties that are payable to the government. The panel vacated the district court’s judgment on the MFLSA penalty issue and remanded so the district court could decide whether the claim was moot—or whether Cohen retained the requisite interest to press for penalties that would be paid to the Commissioner. The MWTA claim and Cohen’s request for an injunction were dismissed or deemed inadequately briefed.

Analytical perspective: statutory design and private enforcement
Cohen illuminates the normative and practical tension between two enforcement approaches: (1) agency-centered enforcement, where the public authority controls penalty assessment and collection, and (2) private attorney-general enforcement, where private plaintiffs can recover statutory penalties (sometimes for the benefit of the public fisc or the plaintiff, as in PAGA-style regimes).

Minnesota’s approach—at least as applied to § 181.101 penalties—leans toward the former. There are solid reasons legislatures might prefer this design. Channeling penalties through the Commissioner promotes uniformity in enforcement, allows the state to allocate scarce investigatory resources strategically, and can limit multiplicative or duplicative litigation and inconsistent outcomes that might follow if every individual plaintiff could seek identical penalties in multiple suits. Agency control may also better calibrate penalties to systemic violations and can enable coordinated remedies (injunctive relief, industry-wide notices, targeted compliance oversight) beyond individual monetary awards.

But agency-centric regimes carry tradeoffs. Agencies are resource-constrained and prioritize cases differently than private litigants. Where statutory penalties are a principal tool of deterrence, restricting private access can reduce overall deterrent pressure—especially if the Commissioner lacks the resources or political will to pursue every violation. Private attorneys general can supplement agency enforcement, filling gaps, increasing the volume of enforcement, and bringing novel claims or class-wide actions that agencies might not pursue. PAGA and similar statutes arose from the view that private plaintiffs are essential to deterring widespread, persistent labor violations when government enforcement alone is inadequate.

Cohen’s holding thus solidifies a policy choice: Minnesota, by statute, gives the Commissioner the specific power to collect § 181.101 penalties, and courts will enforce that textual allocation. The practical consequence is that plaintiffs who want the average-daily-wage penalty must either secure the Commissioner’s intervention or else pursue other remedies available directly to them (unpaid wages, liquidated damages under the federal statute, etc.). Plaintiffs’ counsel must therefore pivot their strategies—focusing on remedies that private suits can obtain immediately, or pressing administrative routes (petitioning the Commissioner) when penalty recovery is central to the case’s value.

Doctrinal implications and downstream effects
Several doctrinal and practical implications follow from Cohen:

1. Textualism and enforcement allocation. Cohen is a reminder that precise statutory drafting—who is the designated actor—matters. Courts will enforce explicit assignments of penalty authority to agencies, and litigants challenging those allocations will face an uphill battle unless the text or legislative history suggests otherwise.

2. Agency discretion and the mootness question. The Eighth Circuit’s remand on the MFLSA penalty issue highlights the intertwined questions of standing, mootness, and ongoing violations. Where defendants pay the primary monetary relief but the policy change remains disputed, courts must carefully assess whether a plaintiff still has a concrete interest in injunctive or penalty relief. That assessment becomes especially salient when penalties would be paid to the state rather than the plaintiff—do plaintiffs have Article III standing to pursue public-penalty claims? The concurrence’s skepticism, and the panel’s caution, signal courts will probe this terrain.

3. Litigation strategy and forum choice. Plaintiffs and defense counsel will adapt. Plaintiffs may intensify efforts to secure the Commissioner’s involvement (through complaints to the agency) or reframe cases to invoke remedies that are directly recoverable by private parties. Employers, conversely, may be more willing to make payments to neutralize private claims while arguing that statutory penalties are exclusively an agency prerogative—potentially insulating them from substantial private-liability risk.

4. The broader private-attorney-general landscape. Cohen provides a counterexample to the expansion of private-penalty regimes typified by PAGA. For states that wish to maintain centralized control over certain penalties, Cohen offers a doctrinal anchor: explicit statutory text will be enforced, and the presence of other provisions that confer private penalty powers can be read as evidence the legislature knows how to create private penalties when it wishes.

Policy tradeoffs: deterrence, resources, and fairness
Which model—agency-centered or private-penalty—better serves labor-law enforcement? There is no one-size-fits-all answer. Agency-centered enforcement can promote coherent, systemic responses and reduce the risk of opportunistic or duplicative suits. It also may better align public-policy priorities and enable comprehensive corrective action. But it hinges on the agency’s capacity and willingness to use its authority effectively. Where agencies are underfunded or overloaded, shifting enforcement power (or additional remedies) to private plaintiffs can produce stronger deterrence and better compensation for victims.

Cohen’s result leaves Minnesota with a mixed system: private suits remain the primary vehicle for recovering unpaid wages and some statutory damages, while certain penalties (the average-daily-wage penalty under § 181.101) remain within the Commissioner’s bailiwick. That division may produce incentives for employers to “pay to quiet” private suits while risking a separate agency enforcement action—if and when the Commissioner elects to pursue it. Whether that outcome promotes compliance depends heavily on the Commissioner’s enforcement focus.

Conclusion
Cohen v. Consilio reinforces that statutory allocations of enforcement power matter. The Eighth Circuit applied plain-text principles to hold that § 181.101 penalties are an agency-centered remedy, limiting the private recovery of those specific penalties. The decision underscores a broader policy choice in Minnesota’s wage-law architecture: private plaintiffs can vindicate unpaid wages through litigation, but the Commissioner retains the primary role for assessing and collecting certain statutory penalties. That framework may preserve administrative coherence but also risks under-deterrence where agency resources are insufficient—leaving lawyers and lawmakers to decide whether, and how, to rebalance private and public enforcement tools in service of robust labor-law compliance.



# Erie, Certification, and Reading State Wage Statutes in Federal Court

## Why this matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Cohen v. Consilio, LLC is a modest opinion on its face: the panel affirms the dismissal of most claims in an employee’s wage suit but vacates and remands on one issue—whether statutory penalties under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) remain justiciable in light of payments made by the employer. Less obviously, though, the panel’s treatment of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), especially Minn. Stat. § 181.101, raises an important set of interpretive and Erie-adjacent questions that recur whenever federal courts read state statutory schemes that split remedial authority between an administrative agency and private litigants.

At issue in the MPWA claim was whether the statute’s explicit penalty mechanism—under which the Commissioner of Labor and Industry may serve a demand and collect “a penalty in the amount of the employee’s average daily earnings” after a ten-day period—can be enforced directly by employees through federal suit under Minn. Stat. § 181.171. The Eighth Circuit concluded that the text, read alongside Minnesota precedent (principally Milner and Allan), plainly limits that penalty-collection power to the Commissioner. The result is tidy and text-driven, but it also prompts larger doctrinal questions: how much room should a federal court leave for state-law ambiguity before declaring a state remedial regime exclusive to an agency? When is certification to the state’s highest court the sensible option? And what are the practical consequences when a legislature channels penalties into the hands of an agency rather than private plaintiffs?

This post walks through the panel’s reasoning, assesses the room the opinion leaves for state-law ambiguity, examines the merits and prudence of certification, and sketches how federal courts should handle state statutory schemes that divide enforcement authority.

## What the panel did and why it relied on Milner and Allan

The Eighth Circuit’s analysis is straightforward. Section 181.171 authorizes civil suits for violations of enumerated MPWA provisions and makes an employer “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” But § 181.101—one of the provisions enforceable under § 181.171—contains its own, explicit penalty process: the Commissioner may serve a demand for wages, and if the employer does not pay within ten days the Commissioner “may charge and collect . . . a penalty in the amount of the employee’s average daily earnings” for each day after the ten-day period. The statute also directs that money collected by the Commissioner “must be paid to the employee concerned.” The Eighth Circuit emphasized that the statute “reserves” to the Commissioner the ability to seek the penalty, and that the legislature knew how to give private plaintiffs a similar remedy when it wanted to (e.g., §§ 181.13, 181.14).

The panel leaned on two Minnesota Supreme Court precedents. Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co. underscores the canon that plain, unambiguous statutory language should be given effect. Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exch., the court noted, clarified that while employees can enforce MPWA violations and may be entitled to the monetary recovery the Commissioner collects, the authority to bring the penalty action—at least in the form described in § 181.101(a)—is a power for the Commissioner to exercise. Taken together, the panel concluded that § 181.101’s penalty pathway is the Commissioner’s to use; private plaintiffs cannot commandeer that specific penalty process through § 181.171.

The Eighth Circuit’s reading is defensible on conventional textualist grounds: when a statute expressly prescribes an enforcement route that vests collection power in an agency, courts are rightly reluctant to read a separate grant of civil-action authority as implicitly duplicating that agency power. The opinion also sensibly points out that the legislature knows how to grant private penalties where it intends to do so.

## Is there any room for ambiguity — and if so, does that counsel certification?

Despite the panel’s clear reading, there are several plausible avenues for arguing that the issue is not entirely foreclosed by text and precedent, and that a certified question to the Minnesota Supreme Court could be appropriate.

First, § 181.171 authorizes a private civil action for “redress for violations” of specified sections, and it provides that an employer “is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.” A reasonable counter-reading is that, read in context, § 181.171 contemplates that when a particular section provides for civil penalties, a private plaintiff suing under § 181.171 should be able to recover the penalties that the violated section promises—regardless of whether the same section also gives the Commissioner a route to collect them. In other words, § 181.171’s cross-reference to “civil penalties . . . provided for in the section violated” could be read to create a statutorily cognizable private right to the same penalties that the Commissioner can also collect.

Second, the statutory architecture is not perfectly pellucid about exclusivity. Section 181.101 both directs the Commissioner’s enforcement authority and includes the proviso that “this section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages.” That proviso arguably preserves private enforcement of substantive wage claims even when the statute sets out agency-enforced penalties. Courts sometimes confront statutes that create parallel—but not necessarily exclusive—avenues of redress, and textual indicators of exclusivity matter. The Eighth Circuit found those indicators sufficient; a state supreme court might read them differently, particularly if it focuses on remedial purpose or on the one-way flow of funds to employees even when collected by the Commissioner.

Third, Minnesota precedent is not infinite. Milner said employees are entitled to be paid penalties “even when the Commissioner collects the money,” and it recognized the Commissioner’s role in collection. But Milner’s statements might admit multiple constructions about whether private suits can directly recover the statute’s specified penalty. If a robust argument can be made that the statutory scheme is ambiguous on the exclusivity question, Erie and comity principles make certification a live option.

So when should a federal court certify? Certification is especially apt when (1) the state-law question is outcome-determinative, (2) there is an unsettled or fairly debatable issue in state law, and (3) the state supreme court’s guidance would materially advance the case. Here, the penalty issue is dispositive for the MPWA claim; there is at least a non-frivolous argument that the statutory text and prior state decisions leave room for a different reading; and Minnesota’s interest in uniform interpretation of how its wage enforcement regime functions is substantial. A district court—or an appellate panel, in an interlocutory posture—should weigh these factors. The Eighth Circuit’s decision not to certify is not obviously wrong, but it is contestable on prudential grounds: certification could have spared federal courts from predicting how Minnesota’s highest court would reconcile the statutory text, the Commissioner’s role, and past decisions.

## Erie, federal courts, and state schemes that split enforcement

Cohen’s case sits at the intersection of two enduring federal questions. First is Erie’s mandate: federal courts adjudicating state-law claims must apply state law as authoritatively as possible. Second is the prudential comity norm that signals federal courts to avoid making novel, precedent-setting state-law interpretations when a state court is better positioned to do so.

When a state statute explicitly allocates some enforcement authority to an agency but also authorizes private suits for “redress,” federal courts should proceed cautiously. Textual clarity can and should resolve the question without certification. But when the text is fairly open and interpretation would require the court to divine, for the first time, whether the legislature intended exclusive agency enforcement, prudential comity and Erie’s goals push toward certification. Certification protects the federal-state balance: the state’s highest court is the official voice of state law and is likely to have a better sense of statutory purpose, administrative practice, and the practical consequences of construing enforcement regimes as exclusive or concurrent.

There is also a doctrinal nuance about statutory standing and remedies: Friends of the Earth suggests that civil penalties payable to the government can nonetheless confer Article III standing on private plaintiffs if the penalties serve a remedial, deterrent function tied to ongoing violations. But standing and the availability of a statutory remedy are different questions. Even if a private plaintiff has Article III standing to seek public penalties (because continued violations threaten the plaintiffs), a statutory text that channels the remedy to an agency may bar the private remedy. The Eighth Circuit recognized this separation by vacating the MFLSA penalty disposition for mootness/standing consideration while treating the MPWA’s statutory allocation as a textual limit on private recovery.

## Practical downstream effects

If employers can lawfully insulate themselves from private statutory penalties simply by making the statutory penalty available only through the Commissioner, the practical incentive landscape changes. Employers might embed enforcement-limiting language in policies and point to the statutory channeling to avoid private penalties. That is not necessarily bad policy—the legislature may have preferred uniform, centralized enforcement through the Commissioner to avoid multiplicity of suits or inconsistent penalties—but it does shift leverage away from private litigants who commonly drive wage enforcement.

For plaintiffs’ counsel, the decision highlights two tactical lessons. First, when pursuing state-law penalties in federal court, litigants should develop a thorough statutory and precedent-based argument showing why the state statute permits private recovery; if ambiguity exists, be prepared to urge certification. Second, where statutes have distinct administrative enforcement mechanics, plaintiffs should consider alternative remedies (e.g., damages, liquidated damages available under federal law, injunctive relief) and press early for discovery about whether the employer’s policy remains in force—to avoid mootness problems.

For agencies and state legislatures, the case is a reminder that express channeling of remedies matters. If a legislature wants private enforcement to complement agency action, it can and should say so plainly; if it wants centralized enforcement, it should be explicit about exclusivity and provide reasons.

## What federal courts should do going forward

Federal judges should keep a few practical principles in mind when confronting similar statutes:

1. Start with text and binding state precedent. If the statute unambiguously vests a remedy in the agency and other provisions show the legislature knew how to grant private penalties, a federal court may properly decline to create a private remedy.

2. If the text is ambiguous and a holding on the issue is dispositive, seriously consider certification. The criteria are familiar: outcome-determinative question, unsettled state law, and substantial state interest in authoritative interpretation.

3. Distinguish standing issues from statutory remedial availability. Even if a plaintiff can show ongoing injury that supports Article III standing to seek public penalties, the court must still ask whether state law permits a private plaintiff to obtain the remedy in that manner.

4. Be mindful of mootness and voluntary cessation. Payment of the underlying wages may moot some claims; whether the employer’s policy persists will often be dispositive for penalty and injunctive claims.

Cohen v. Consilio thus offers a compact lesson in statutory interpretation, Erie prudence, and the practical consequences of channeling enforcement authority. The Eighth Circuit’s text-focused approach is plausible and well-anchored in Minnesota precedent, but it leaves open an important question about whether, and when, federal courts should ask Minnesota’s courts to weigh in. Given the stakes for wage enforcement and the persistent role of private litigation as a complementary enforcement tool, certified questions remain an underused but often appropriate mechanism to ensure that federal courts defer to state law where doing so best serves Erie’s aims.
