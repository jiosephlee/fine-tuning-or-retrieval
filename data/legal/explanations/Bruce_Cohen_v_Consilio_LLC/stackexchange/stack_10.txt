### Could Cohen have obtained the injunctive relief he sought (rescission of the policy) without amending the complaint?
Question:
The panel says new claims must be raised by amended complaint, not opposition briefs. But is changing the form of requested injunctive relief (from one set of injunction terms to 'withdraw the 2019 policy') a new claim or just a different remedy? Are there circumstances under Rule 15(b) (issues tried by consent) or Rule 54(c) (grant relief to which a party is entitled) that would have allowed the district court to consider the revised injunction request at summary judgment?
Answer:
Short answer: Probably not. Under the posture and reasoning in the opinion, Cohen could not obtain the newly‑requested relief—“rescind the 2019 policy”—by springing it in his summary‑judgment opposition; he needed to put that claim/remedy into the complaint (or otherwise obtain the parties’ consent or a proper basis under the rules). The panel treated the revised injunction as a new claim/remedy that the district court was not asked to decide, and it relied on the settled principle that “new claims are properly raised through amended complaints, not opposition briefs.” Op. at Part II.D (citing InfoDeli, LLC, 136 F.4th at 801 n.6 and Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a)).

Why the change in requested injunction mattered
- The opinion explains that Cohen originally sought particular injunctive relief in the complaint but “later change[d] course and request[ed] different relief—withdrawing the 2019 policy—in his summary judgment briefing,” and that “the district court—understandably—did not consider it.” Op. Part II.D. That language treats the shift as more than stylistic: it was a discrete request the court had not been asked to adjudicate at summary judgment and therefore was not properly before the court on appeal.

Rule 15(b) (issues tried by consent) is not an effective route here
- Rule 15(b) permits a court to treat an issue as raised at trial if the opposing party has tried the issue by express or implied consent. It is a trial‑stage doctrine about amendment based on issues actually litigated with the parties’ notice and opportunity to address them. Nothing in the opinion suggests that Consilio consented to litigate an equitable rescission remedy at summary judgment; on the contrary, the panel emphasizes the absence of a properly pleaded request and the district court’s understandable refusal to decide a remedy not presented. Op. Part II.D. Because there was no trial on the rescission request and no indication of consent to try that issue, Rule 15(b) would not salvage Cohen’s belated remedy.

Rule 54(c) (relief to which a party is entitled) is likewise of limited help
- Rule 54(c) allows a final judgment to grant the relief to which the prevailing party is entitled even if not precisely demanded in the pleadings, but courts use it to avoid forfeiting relief proven at trial and to conform the judgment to the proofs. It does not permit a party to press a new claim or a fundamentally different form of equitable relief for the first time in an opposition brief without notice to the other side. The panel’s citation and reliance on the basic pleading rule (Rule 15(a) and InfoDeli) implies the district court needed proper pleading or at least notice and opportunity to respond before deciding a rescission remedy. Op. Part II.D.

Practical and jurisdictional constraints the opinion highlights
- The panel also flagged jurisdiction/standing and mootness issues as to penalties and prospective relief (MFLSA claims and injunctive relief depend on whether the policy remains in force). Op. Parts II.B–D. Even if Cohen had properly pleaded rescission, the court would have to resolve whether he had standing to seek an affirmative injunction (and whether the claim was moot if the policy were already withdrawn). The opinion vacated/remanded MFLSA issues for the district court to consider jurisdiction; it rejected attempting to press new injunctive relief in the summary‑judgment papers without amendment. Op. Parts II.B, II.D.

Bottom line
- Changing the requested injunction to “rescission” in an opposition brief amounted to a new request that the court could not (and did not) decide. Under the opinion’s reasoning, Cohen needed to amend his complaint (or obtain the defendant’s clear consent to litigate the rescission remedy) so the district court would have notice, the parties could develop the record and briefing on that remedy, and the court could address standing/mootness and equitable factors. The narrow doctrines you mention (Rule 15(b) and Rule 54(c)) would not reliably allow him to bypass amendment under the facts and timing shown in this opinion.
