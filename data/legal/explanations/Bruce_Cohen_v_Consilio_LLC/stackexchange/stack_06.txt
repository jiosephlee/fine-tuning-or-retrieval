Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC

### Should the district court have resolved Article III standing/mootness before granting summary judgment, and does the parties’ stipulation affect that sequencing?
Question:
The parties stipulated to a procedure where the court would decide summary judgment on the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA claims, acknowledging a full grant would end the case. The panel ultimately vacates the MFLSA ruling for a jurisdiction check. Under Steel Co.'s prohibition on hypothetical jurisdiction, should the district court have addressed standing/mootness on the MFLSA penalties issue before reaching the merits? Can a parties’ stipulation about an "expedient path" ever justify deciding the merits first, or must the court always confirm Article III jurisdiction sua sponte? If jurisdiction was uncertain only as to one claim, was it proper to reach the merits of the other claims while remanding the jurisdictional question for the MFLSA claim?
Answer:
Short answer
- Yes — where Article III jurisdiction (standing/mootness) is genuinely in doubt for a particular form of relief, the court should resolve that threshold issue before deciding the merits of that relief. The Eighth Circuit vacated the district court’s MFLSA penalty ruling for that reason.  
- A private stipulation about an “expedient” litigation path cannot manufacture Article III standing; it cannot substitute for the court’s obligation to be satisfied that a live case or controversy exists for each claim and each form of relief.  
- If jurisdictional doubt attaches only to one claim or one remedy, the court may properly decide the other claims whose jurisdiction is not in question and remand the doubtful claim for a jurisdictional determination — which is exactly what the panel did here.

Why (doctrine and how the opinion applies)
- Article III standing and mootness are threshold requirements that must exist for “each claim” and “for each form of relief,” and the “requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness).” The panel quotes Town of Chester and Sisney for this point and emphasizes the continuing nature of the personal interest requirement. (Cohen v. Consilio, slip op. § II.B.)
- The panel identified a concrete jurisdictional concern: Cohen stipulated that he had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages, and the parties agree that any statutory penalties available under the MFLSA would be payable to the Commissioner, not to Cohen. Because penalties payable to the government may or may not confer a continuing personal stake (they can sometimes provide redress by deterring future violations, but only if the wrongful conduct can reasonably be expected to recur), the court could not be sure the MFLSA penalty claim survived as a live controversy. The panel stated it “ha[d] a serious concern regarding our jurisdiction to decide this issue” and therefore vacated the MFLSA summary-judgment ruling and remanded for the district court to determine jurisdiction. (Id. § II.B.)
- The panel’s actions reflect the settled sequencing rule: you cannot reliably adjudicate a substantive claim when it is unclear whether Article III permits the court to do so. The parties’ joint request to take an “expedient and efficient path” and their stipulation that payment resolved wage/liquidated-damages claims did not resolve the distinct Article III question whether Cohen retained a personal stake in penalties payable to the Commissioner.

On the parties’ stipulation and the court’s duty
- Parties can agree to procedures and to which issues to prioritize, but they cannot by stipulation confer Article III jurisdiction where it does not exist. The opinion shows that the Eighth Circuit will not let a procedural stipulation substitute for the court’s independent obligation to satisfy itself that a live case or controversy remains for the relief sought. The district court may heed the parties’ agreed sequencing when jurisdictional facts are clear, but when jurisdiction is genuinely doubtful as to a claim or remedy, the court must address that doubt before the merits of that claim.

On deciding some claims but not others
- It was proper here for the court of appeals to affirm resolution of the MPWA and MWTA claims while vacating the MFLSA penalties ruling and remanding that single issue. The panel affirmed other rulings whose jurisdictional posture was not in doubt and remanded only the MFLSA claim because the jurisdictional question was unique to that claim and remedy. (See id. §§ II.A, II.C, III.)

Practical takeaway
- If a claim’s viability turns on facts bearing on standing or mootness (e.g., whether a challenged policy remains in effect; who would receive any penalty), the district court should — and the appellate courts expect it to — resolve jurisdictional questions for that claim before deciding its merits. A stipulation about case management or “efficiency” does not relieve the court of that Article III duty.
