A Casebook Chapter on: Bruce_Cohen_v_Consilio_LLC

# Orientation: Minnesota Wage-and-Hour Enforcement Meets Federal Justiciability

This chapter uses Cohen v. Consilio to teach two intertwined bodies of doctrine that a practicing employment litigator must master: the architecture of Minnesota’s wage-and-hour remedies (what the statutes provide, who can recover which items, and how the Department of Labor and Industry is involved) and the limits Article III places on private enforcement of those state-law remedial schemes in federal court. The interaction between remedial design (who receives penalties, how penalties are triggered) and federal justiciability (standing and mootness) is the central lesson: when a state statute routes statutory penalties or collection authority to a state official, that routing can alter whether a private plaintiff has a live, judicially cognizable interest in pursuing those penalties in federal court. Cohen illustrates the doctrinal tools courts use to answer those questions and the practical choices litigants must make to preserve federal jurisdiction.

## Purposes and scope of Minnesota’s wage statutes: timely payment, minimum wage and overtime, and wage theft prevention

Minnesota’s wage statutes operate together to achieve three broad statutory objectives: (1) to assure timely and accurate payment of earned wages and commissions; (2) to implement minimum-wage and overtime protections; and (3) to deter wage theft by imposing statutory penalties and administrative enforcement mechanisms. These statutes therefore provide a mix of compensatory relief (unpaid wages, overtime, sometimes liquidated damages), administrative enforcement powers (the Commissioner’s demand-and-collection authority), and statutory penalties intended to deter and punish employer misconduct.

The Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) centers on timely payment of employees’ earned wages and certain remedies for an employer’s failure to pay. One potent remedy in the MPWA is the “average daily wage” penalty found in section 181.101(a): when the Commissioner of Labor and Industry serves a demand for payment on an employer and the employer fails to pay within ten days, the statute authorizes the Commissioner to collect wages and a penalty “in the amount of the employee’s average daily earnings . . . for each day beyond the ten-day limit” until collection. That penalty is calculated to both compensate and create an economic incentive for timely compliance. Other MPWA provisions create remedies for discharged or resigned employees and include statutory-language authorizing penalties in those distinct contexts.

The Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) implements minimum-wage and overtime rules at the state level, mirroring federal FLSA principles but with its own remedial provisions. The MFLSA authorizes recovery of unpaid overtime wages and provides for liquidated damages (a form of compensatory doubling in many cases) and—separately—statutory civil penalties in some circumstances. The Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) supplements these protections with notice and recordkeeping requirements and carries its own penalties for failures to comply with statutory notice obligations.

Understanding these provisions requires careful parsing of the statutory text: not all statutes create the same portfolio of remedies, and where a statute assigns collection or penalty authority to the Commissioner, that assignment can have important consequences for private litigants. The statutes therefore must be read as a cluster: wages and liquidated damages are typically recoverable by an employee who has suffered an uncompensated injury, but statutory penalties may be limited by the statute’s own text or by whether the remedy is payable only to a public official.

## Public–private enforcement model: role of the Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry versus private litigants

Minnesota’s wage statutes embody a mixed public–private enforcement model. The state empowers the Commissioner of Labor and Industry to pursue, on behalf of employees, claims for unpaid wages and to collect specified penalties after serving a demand for payment. At the same time, the statutes typically preserve a private right of action: an employee may bring suit for unpaid wages, liquidated damages, and sometimes injunctive relief. The twinned design seeks both individualized redress (compensation for the worker) and public deterrence (penalties and administrative enforcement).

Two features of this model are important in practice. First, the statutes vary in whether the Commissioner’s exercise of authority is a prerequisite to penalties or in whether penalties may be pursued directly by an employee in court. Section 181.101 illustrates the “Commissioner-first” design: the Commissioner may serve a demand and, if payment does not follow, collect wages and a penalty. The statute explicitly contemplates the Commissioner as the actor who “may charge and collect” that penalty. By contrast, other provisions in Minnesota law explicitly authorize employees themselves to “charge and collect” a penalty upon making a demand or on termination—an important textual difference that the courts treat as meaningful when discern­ing legislative intent about the locus of penalty enforcement.

Second, even when the Commissioner collects money on behalf of an employee, the statutory text often directs how recovered funds are disbursed. Under §181.101, funds collected by the Commissioner “must be paid to the employee concerned.” That allocation means that although the Commissioner collects, the ultimate beneficiary can be the private employee. But the fact that collection routes through a public actor matters for who can initiate and who can pursue the pendent remedy—an issue that becomes critical when enforcement proceeds in federal court.

Courts ask how a particular statutory scheme allocates enforcement power: does the statute create an independent private right of action for the penalty, or is the Commissioner’s action a jurisdictional or procedural prerequisite? The Minnesota Supreme Court and other authorities have repeatedly applied standard canons of statutory interpretation to conclude that textual differences—such as whether a statute says “the commissioner may charge and collect” versus “an employee may charge and collect”—reflect deliberate legislative choices about who can pursue what relief. If the statute reserves penalty-collection authority to the Commissioner, a private plaintiff’s power to obtain that penalty directly in court is curtailed.

Cohen demonstrates how these textual allocations work in practice. Cohen sued under the MPWA and sought the average daily wage penalty under §181.101. The Eighth Circuit, following Minnesota precedent and ordinary interpretive principles, concluded that §181.101 assigns the power to seek those particular penalties to the Commissioner; the private suit provision (§181.171) grants a private right of action for many kinds of relief, but civil penalties are recoverable only “if they are provided for in the section” a plaintiff invokes. The court therefore held that the district court correctly concluded the private plaintiff could not obtain the average-daily-wage penalty directly because the statute makes the Commissioner the enforcement agent for that penalty.

## Article III overview: standing, redressability, and mootness constraints on state-law claims in federal court

Federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction; to sustain jurisdiction a plaintiff must satisfy Article III’s case-or-controversy requirements at the outset and must continue to have a live controversy throughout the suit. The three familiar elements of standing are (1) injury-in-fact, a concrete and particularized invasion of a legally protected interest that is actual or imminent; (2) causation, a traceable connection between the injury and the defendant’s conduct; and (3) redressability, a likelihood that a favorable judicial decision will remedy the injury. These elements must be pleaded with respect to each claim and each form of relief sought.

Redressability, the third element, is the doctrinal hinge most often implicated when statutory penalties are at issue. If the only relief a plaintiff seeks is a penalty that will be paid to the state (or to a state official), courts ask whether obtaining that penalty will redress the plaintiff’s personal injury. The Supreme Court’s decision in Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw supplies one important rule: a plaintiff injured by ongoing unlawful conduct might obtain redress from a civil penalty paid to the government because the civil penalty can produce deterrent effects that alleviate the plaintiff’s ongoing injury (for example, by compelling cessation of polluting conduct). Laidlaw therefore establishes that monetary relief that nominally benefits the public treasury can nonetheless produce a private plaintiff’s redress if it is likely to deter the defendant and thereby reduce or eliminate the plaintiff’s exposure to continuing harm.

But Laidlaw does not create an automatic right to pursue any penalty that will go to the government. The plaintiff must still show a concrete injury and must demonstrate that the requested relief is likely to redress that injury. Town of Chester v. Laroe Estates explains the related principle that standing must be demonstrated for every claim and for every form of relief sought: a plaintiff cannot bootstrap standing for relief that would not meaningfully alleviate their injury.

Mootness doctrine is the closely related temporal counterpart to standing. Even after a suit is commenced, it may become moot if events eliminate the parties’ legally cognizable interests and there is no effective relief the court can grant. The “voluntary cessation” rule states that a defendant’s mere cessation of the challenged conduct does not necessarily moot a case—defendants may resume the conduct at any time unless the record shows it is “absolutely clear” that the wrongful behavior will not recur. Courts also examine whether a challenged practice is “capable of repetition, yet evading review,” another exception to mootness that applies where the challenged conduct is by nature of limited duration but repeats and would otherwise avoid preclusion by time constraints.

The interplay between remedial design and these Article III constraints becomes evident when a private plaintiff secures payment of the wages she alleges were withheld and the only remaining relief is a statutory penalty payable to the state. Payment of wages may eliminate the plaintiff’s personal injury—if the only injury was the unpaid wages—so the claim for money paid to the plaintiff becomes moot. But if the plaintiff seeks a penalty payable to the state, the court must determine whether recovering that penalty would still redress the plaintiff’s interest (for example, by deterring the defendant from future unlawful practices) and whether that redress is sufficiently concrete and non-speculative to satisfy Article III. Cohen demonstrates precisely this situation: a private plaintiff, after receiving wages and liquidated damages, still seeks statutory penalties payable to the state; the court had to examine whether plaintiff still had a live interest to press in federal court.

Applying these principles requires granular fact-finding. A court asked whether Laidlaw-style deterrent redress suffices will look at (a) whether the challenged employer policy remains in place (continuing injury), (b) whether the penalty mechanism is capable of deterring the employer and preventing recurrence of the harm, and (c) whether the plaintiff’s interest—beyond past wages recovered—is concrete enough to show continuing injury that the penalty could redress. If the employer has rescinded the policy and there is no reasonable expectation of recurrence, the case may be moot even if the plaintiff requests penalties payable to the state.

## Using Cohen v. Consilio as a teaching vehicle for the intersection of remedial design and federal jurisdiction

Cohen v. Consilio provides a compact, real-world demonstration of how statutory remedial design and Article III constraints interact. The core facts are straightforward: Consilio changed its overtime pay policy for Minnesota hourly licensed-attorney document reviewers; Cohen sued under the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA on behalf of himself and a putative class. Consilio paid Cohen and his similarly situated coworkers a lump sum to cover overtime pay and liquidated damages under MFLSA, and the parties stipulated that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages owed under the MFLSA. The litigation’s live controversy therefore narrowed to whether statutory penalties were available to Cohen (and payable to whom), whether an injunction rescinding Consilio’s policy was appropriate, and whether the case remained justiciable in federal court.

First, the court’s analysis of the MPWA claim illustrates how statutory text and interpretive canons control who can seek a particular remedy. Cohen sought the “average daily wage” penalty under §181.101. The Eighth Circuit observed that §181.101 assigns the authority to serve a demand and to “charge and collect” that penalty to the Commissioner—the statute provides that the Commissioner “may charge and collect the wages . . . and a penalty” when an employer declines to pay after a demand. The court contrasted this text with other Minnesota statutory provisions that explicitly allow an employee to “charge and collect” a penalty upon demand or upon termination; that textual contrast suggested the legislature knew how to authorize a private collection right where it wanted to do so and did not do so in §181.101. On that basis the court affirmed summary judgment as to Cohen’s claim for average-daily-wage penalties under §181.101, reasoning that the private right of action in §181.171 does not confer a private right to obtain penalties that are reserved to the Commissioner elsewhere in the statutory scheme.

This holding demonstrates two doctrinal points. One is interpretive: small differences in statutory diction—who “may charge and collect” vs. who “may charge and collect” after the employee’s demand—often determine the locus of enforcement power. The other is jurisdictional in effect: if the statute gives the Commissioner exclusive authority to seek a particular penalty, a private plaintiff cannot pursue that penalty in a private suit because the statutory right of action does not include it.

Second, Cohen’s MFLSA claim shows how Article III’s redressability and mootness requirements operate when a plaintiff receives the very relief that gave rise to the suit. Because Consilio paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages Cohen claimed under the MFLSA, the district court granted summary judgment. On appeal, however, the Eighth Circuit vacated the grant of summary judgment as to MFLSA statutory penalties and remanded for the district court to determine whether it retained jurisdiction. The court recognized that even statutory penalties payable to a state official might afford redress to a private plaintiff by deterring future violations—a point the Supreme Court recognized in Laidlaw. But the Eighth Circuit also emphasized that the plaintiff must demonstrate a continuing interest: if Consilio’s unlawful policy remains in effect, statutory penalties might redress Cohen’s injury even if paid to the state because they could deter future violations; if the policy has been rescinded and there is no reasonable expectation of recurrence, the claim for penalties could be moot.

Judge Kobes’s concurrence highlights an unresolved question the court left for the district court on remand: whether a private plaintiff like Cohen has standing to seek statutory penalties that are payable to the Commissioner when the plaintiff has been otherwise made whole for past monetary harm. The concurrence expresses skepticism about automatic standing in this situation and points out that neither party had fully briefed the standing-to-seek-penalties question under Laidlaw. That skepticism is instructive: Laidlaw’s deterrence rationale does not uniformly apply to every statutory penalty; courts must assess whether the specific penalty at issue can provide non-speculative deterrence that would redress the plaintiff’s injury.

Third, Cohen’s MWTA and injunctive-relief dispositions teach additional procedural lessons. Cohen’s MWTA claim failed in part because he did not advance a developed argument that statutory penalties were available under the MWTA; the Eighth Circuit invoked settled waiver doctrine and affirmed. The injunctive-relief request similarly foundered because Cohen changed his requested relief late in the proceedings and the district court had not considered it. Together these rulings reinforce two practical rules: (1) litigants must preserve and adequately brief jurisdictional and remedy arguments at the district-court stage, and (2) new claims for relief should be pleaded in an amended complaint rather than sprung only in opposition briefs.

From Cohen we extract practical guidance for litigators confronting state wage claims in federal court:

- Preserve the live-controversy: avoid stipulating away the very relief that sustains jurisdiction. If the client demands money and the employer pays unconditionally, consider whether additional relief (injunctive relief, a declaratory judgment that a policy is unlawful, or inclusion of the Commissioner as a party) is needed to maintain a live case or an appealable controversy.

- Frame the requested relief with redressability in mind: if statutory penalties are payable to the state, be prepared to identify how obtaining those penalties would redress the plaintiff’s injury (for example, by deterring the defendant’s continuing policy). Gather evidence on whether the challenged policy remains in effect and whether recidivism is likely, because these facts are central to a Laidlaw-style redressability argument.

- Deploy administrative actors when beneficial: if the statute requires or favors Commissioner action to trigger penalties, plaintiffs might seek cooperation with the Commissioner or move to involve the Commissioner as a party, or supplement the case with parallel administrative complaints that preserve the ability to recover statutory penalties through the administrative process.

- Preserve injunctive and declaratory claims early: if the plaintiff seeks prospective relief (rescission of an unlawful policy, for example), plead and litigate those requests from the outset. Late changes in requested relief are unlikely to be considered and may be treated as waived.

Finally, Cohen demonstrates the procedural course a remand may follow. The Eighth Circuit remanded the MFLSA penalty question to the district court to allow factual findings on whether Consilio’s unlawful overtime policy remains in place and whether the penalties sought would plausibly deter future violations and thereby redress Cohen’s injury. The district court should therefore treat standing and mootness as threshold issues and resolve them before addressing the merits—precisely the approach the concurrence recommended.

Practical hypotheticals to test these lessons:

- Hypothetical A: An employee sues for unpaid wages under a state statute. The employer pays the unpaid wages but not the statutory penalty which, under the statute, the Commissioner is authorized to collect after serving a demand. The employee files a federal suit seeking the penalty in addition to an injunction. Under Cohen, the court will investigate whether the policy that caused the unpaid wages remains in place. If the policy continues, the penalty claim may give rise to redressability under Laidlaw; if it does not and there is no reasonable expectation of recurrence, the claim may be moot.

- Hypothetical B: An employee sues under a Minnesota wage statute that explicitly allows an employee to “charge and collect” the penalty after an employee demand. Even if the penalty would go to a public official in other statutes, here the textual grant to the employee strengthens the argument that the private plaintiff can seek that penalty directly in court; standing and redressability would then hinge less on Laidlaw-style deterrence and more on traditional money-damage principles.

- Hypothetical C: An employer admits liability and pays wages and liquidated damages but refuses to change an illegal pay policy. The employee seeks injunctive relief to prevent future harms. The district court should treat the request for injunctive relief as the primary remaining live controversy; a court that declines to decide injunctive relief risks dismissal for lack of a live case or controversy if the only remaining claims are for penalties payable to the state and the predicate policy has been rescinded.

Conclusion

Cohen v. Consilio shows that federal justiciability doctrines—standing, redressability, and mootness—operate as essential constraints on private enforcement of state wage laws in federal court. The statutory design of Minnesota’s wage remedies—who collects penalties, whether penalties go to employees or the state, and whether the Commissioner must act first—shapes the contours of those constraints. Effective litigation strategy requires understanding both statutory text and Article III doctrine: plaintiffs should craft claims and preserve fact records that demonstrate continuing injury or a realistic basis for Laidlaw-style deterrence if they aim to recover statutory penalties payable to the state, and they should avoid procedural missteps that foreclose injunctive relief or admission of material facts critical to standing.

# The Statutory Framework: MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA

This chapter maps the key Minnesota statutory provisions that regulate timely wage payment, overtime, written notice, and the available remedies when employers fail to comply. The Court of Appeals’ opinion in Cohen v. Consilio, LLC (No. 24‑2079) furnishes the contemporary practical context: it illustrates how those statutes allocate enforcement authority between the Commissioner of Labor and private plaintiffs, how different statutes award different kinds of penalties (and to whom those penalties are paid), and how those allocations affect standing, mootness, and litigation strategy. Read together, the statutes and the Cohen decision teach several recurring themes: statutory text controls; the legislature’s decision to grant or withhold a private statutory penalty matters; and the difference between money paid to an employee and money paid to the state has important consequences for adjudicatory jurisdiction.

## Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. § 181.101: timely payment, Commissioner’s demand authority, and employee wage claims

Section 181.101 of the MPWA addresses the Commissioner’s role in enforcing timely payment of wages and authorizes the Commissioner to serve a demand for unpaid wages and to assess an “average daily wage” penalty in certain circumstances. Read in isolation, § 181.101 does three things: (1) it creates a substantive right to be paid wages; (2) it authorizes the Commissioner of Labor and Industry (or her representative) to serve a demand for unpaid wages on behalf of an employee; and (3) if an employer fails to pay within ten days of that demand, the Commissioner “may charge and collect” the wages and a penalty “in the amount of the employee’s average daily earnings at the same rate or rates for each day beyond the ten‑day limit following the demand.” The statute further provides that money collected by the Commissioner “must be paid to the employee concerned,” and it preserves the employee’s ability to “prosecute a claim for wages” notwithstanding Commissioner action.

Cohen v. Consilio demonstrates the critical interpretive point that the statute ties the Commissioner’s demand authority to the availability of the average‑daily‑wage penalty. The Eighth Circuit relied on the plain language of § 181.101 and Minnesota interpretive canons to hold that only the Commissioner may obtain the average daily wage penalty under § 181.101. The court emphasized the statute’s repeated role for the Commissioner (the demand and the collection), the requirement that money collected be paid to the employee (indirectly confirming the Commissioner’s role as the collecting agent), and contrasted § 181.101 with other MPWA provisions where the legislature explicitly authorized employee penalties. Those textual features supported the conclusion that the penalty mechanism in § 181.101 is procedural and Commissioner‑driven rather than a standalone private right to obtain statutory penalties in district court.

From a doctrinal and practice standpoint, a student must learn to parse the statutory text for allocation of enforcement power. If the text conditions a penalty on Commissioner action, a private plaintiff who sues directly in district court under § 181.171 cannot—absent other statutory language—extract the § 181.101 average daily wage penalty as an element of relief. The practical consequence is that plaintiffs and their counsel confront a choice: either pursue their statutory wage claim in district court (to vindicate unpaid wages and seek compensatory relief and injunctive relief under § 181.171), or invoke the Commissioner’s office to exercise the statutory demand and claim the penalty that flows only from that administrative mechanism.

Two additional interpretive lessons appear in Cohen. First, when a statute grants penalties in one section but not another, courts will treat that omission as intentional: the legislature “knows how” to confer a private right to penalties (see Minn. Stat. §§ 181.13, 181.14 discussed below), and if it wanted to do so in § 181.101 it could have. Second, the statute’s provision that “this section does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages” clarifies that the Commissioner’s process is supplementary, but it does not convert the Commissioner’s penalty authority into a private cause of action for penalties.

Illustrative hypothetical: an hourly employee is not paid for 20 hours of overtime. If the Commissioner is invoked and serves a demand under § 181.101, and payment is not made within 10 days, the Commissioner may collect unpaid wages plus an average daily wage penalty for each day beyond that limit; those funds must be paid to the employee. If the same employee sues directly in district court under § 181.171, she may recover unpaid wages and any compensatory damages and seek injunctive relief, but she cannot claim the § 181.101 average daily wage penalty unless the Commissioner first exercises the statutory demand authority.

## Minn. Stat. §§ 181.13 and 181.14: discharge/quit wage provisions and employee‑accessible penalties

By contrast to § 181.101, other MPWA provisions expressly permit employees to obtain statutory penalties without conditioning such relief on Commissioner action. Sections 181.13 and 181.14 address the timing of final wage payments when an employee is discharged (181.13) or quits/resigns (181.14). Both statutes provide a private enforcement mechanism and include an explicit penalty provision: when an employer fails to timely pay unpaid wages after termination or resignation, the employee “may charge and collect a penalty” in an amount specified by statute. Those provisions therefore create a private right of action for penalties that flow directly to the employee in litigation.

Cohen illustrates the difference between such employee‑accessible statutory penalties and the Commissioner‑centered penalty in § 181.101. The court pointed to §§ 181.13 and 181.14 as examples of statutory text showing the legislature “knows how” to empower employees to recover penalties, and that the absence of similar language in § 181.101 signals a different enforcement design. This is a classic statutory‑interpretation exercise: an express grant of a remedy in one place and silence in another supports the inference that silence was intentional.

From a teaching perspective, students should be able to explain the strategic and remedial implications. If the statutory violation fits the contours of § 181.13 or § 181.14—i.e., the employer failed to timely pay at discharge or resignation—the employee can sue in district court and seek the statutory penalty in court. If the violation involves ordinary unpaid wages not tied to termination or resignation, and the relevant language is like § 181.101, the plaintiff must look to the Commissioner if she wants the specific penalty that statute creates.

Hypothetical application: an employee is fired on Friday and by law must be paid final wages on discharge. Employer delays payment for two weeks. Section 181.13’s express penalty provision empowers the employee to sue in district court and recover the statutory penalty payable to the employee. If, instead, the unpaid wages are for continued pay periods and the statute in question resembles § 181.101, the employee should either press the Commissioner to act or pursue compensatory relief under § 181.171 without expectation of recovering § 181.101’s average daily wage penalty.

## Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. §§ 177.25 and 177.27: overtime rights, liquidated damages, and agency civil penalties

The MFLSA codifies Minnesota’s overtime and minimum wage standards, reflecting many of the remedial structures of the federal FLSA, including private causes of action for unpaid wages and a statutory award of liquidated damages (often double damages) for wage violations. Section 177.25, for example, implements overtime requirements, while § 177.27 sets out remedies and enforcement mechanisms. Critical to litigation is subdivision 8 of § 177.27, which authorizes civil penalties and ties certain penalties to agency enforcement.

Cohen’s case shows how these MFLSA provisions work in the real world. In that case, Consilio paid Cohen the overtime wages and the liquidated damages he alleged were owed under the MFLSA. The parties stipulated that the monetary overtime and liquidated damage claims were satisfied. But Cohen nevertheless sought statutory civil penalties under the MFLSA. The appeals court identified two core problems that often recur in MFLSA litigation: (1) what is the source and recipient of statutory penalties; and (2) does payment of wages and liquidated damages render a penalties claim moot?

First, the court noted that statutory penalties under the MFLSA—as opposed to liquidated damages and unpaid wages—are payable to the Commissioner, not to the employee, at least in the scheme before the court. That allocation matters. Even if a private plaintiff’s wage loss is compensated, a penalty payable to the state may still serve a remedial purpose (deterrence and public enforcement) and thus may sometimes confer standing. The Supreme Court’s Friends of the Earth decision recognizes that a plaintiff can have standing to seek civil penalties payable to a government body when the penalties would deter future violations and thus redress the plaintiff’s interest in cessation of unlawful conduct.

Second, the Court of Appeals emphasized that nonprofit or public‑minded plaintiffs cannot automatically sue on an abstract public interest; the plaintiff must show a concrete, continuing interest. Cohen’s stipulation that he had been paid the substantive monetary relief raised a serious mootness question as to statutory penalties: if Consilio discontinued the violative policy and there is no reasonable expectation of recurrence, then any claim for penalties may be moot because the plaintiff lacks a continuing injury. The Eighth Circuit therefore vacated and remanded the MFLSA penalties ruling so the district court could assess jurisdiction and mootness before adjudicating penalties.

Students should take away the following rules for MFLSA litigation: unpaid wages and liquidated damages are classic private remedies; statutory penalties may belong to the state and are often enforced by the Commissioner or payable to the Commissioner. When plaintiffs pursue penalties payable to the state, courts scrutinize standing and mootness, particularly where employers have paid wages or changed policies after suit began. Plaintiffs must preserve the live controversy by demonstrating ongoing injury or a realistic prospect of recurrence; otherwise, the penalties claim will likely collapse as moot.

Practical litigation guidance: if counsel for a plaintiff seeks statutory penalties under the MFLSA (payable to the state), counsel should develop facts showing continuing violations or ongoing risk of recurrence (for example, a companywide policy that remains in effect, similar practices across offices, or evidence that an employer’s compliance was voluntary and easily reversible). Alternatively, plaintiffs should coordinate with the Commissioner’s office, if possible, to pursue penalties through administrative channels where statutory text requires it.

## Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. § 181.032: written notice and change‑notice requirements

The MWTA addresses employer obligations to provide written notice to employees at hiring and when certain terms of employment change, including pay rates and overtime policies. Section 181.032 requires written notice of an employee’s rate and the employer’s obligations, and contains specific provisions for employer notice when material terms change.

Cohen’s MWTA claim alleged that Consilio failed to provide statutorily required notice of the overtime policy change and that such failure warranted penalties. The Court of Appeals observed that Cohen “included little to no discussion” of statutory authority for penalties under that section and had stipulated that he had received all pay owed. The court therefore affirmed dismissal of the MWTA claim for lack of meaningful argument. But the statutory text itself matters: whether the MWTA authorizes penalties (and to whom those penalties are payable) is a textual question. Some MWTA provisions provide civil penalties, but their availability in private suits depends on the statutory language and the text’s allocation of remedies.

In practice, an MWTA claimant should do three things: (1) identify the exact subsection alleged to have been violated and whether that subsection includes a private penalty; (2) plead whether the penalty—if any—is payable to the employee or to the Commissioner; and (3) develop evidence that notice was not timely provided and that the statutory elements are met. If seeking injunctive relief (e.g., rescission of a policy or an order requiring revised notice), the plaintiff should also plead facts showing ongoing injury or threat of future injury to avoid mootness problems after monetary payments.

## Private right of action under Minn. Stat. § 181.171, subd. 1 and MFLSA § 177.27, subd. 8

Minn. Stat. § 181.171, subdivision 1, gives employees a private right of action for violations of certain enumerated MPWA provisions, allowing them to proceed directly to district court for “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief including but not limited to injunctive relief.” But § 181.171 also conditions recovery of civil penalties on whether the particular section violated “provides for” such penalties. Thus, the right to sue under § 181.171 is broad for wage claims, but specific statutory penalties are only recoverable when the violated section authorizes them.

Similarly, MFLSA § 177.27 creates remedies for violations of the state minimum‑wage and overtime law. Subdivision 8 addresses civil penalties and enforcement; courts and litigants must read the subdivision alongside § 177.25 and other statutory text to determine both the availability and intended recipient of penalties.

Cohen underscores the interplay between § 181.171’s broad private enforcement mechanism and the narrower penalty provisions dispersed elsewhere in statutory law. The Eighth Circuit emphasized the principle that § 181.171 permits employees to sue for MPWA violations in district court, but that does not automatically give employees access to every statutory penalty that appears elsewhere in the MPWA unless the statute expressly confers such a right. Consequently, careful statutory mapping is essential: identify the violated provision, determine whether it “provides for” a penalty, and then assess who is entitled to collect that penalty under the statutory scheme.

On the procedural plane, Cohen teaches important litigation lessons about standing and mootness. A private plaintiff seeking penalties payable to the state must still satisfy Article III standing and maintain a live controversy. Voluntary payment of wages and liquidated damages by a defendant can narrow the live issues and potentially render a penalties claim moot if the plaintiff cannot show a likelihood of recurrence. When penalties are payable to the employee, the payment of wages may foreclose only the monetary component but leave room for injunctive relief or for other statutory penalties.

## Cross‑cutting doctrinal points and litigation practice

Several cross‑cutting doctrines emerge from the statutes and from Cohen:

- Textualism and statutory drafting choices matter. Courts look to the plain language and where the legislature expressly provides for penalties. The presence of penalty language in one statutory provision and its absence in another is legally significant.

- The Commissioner’s role is not merely ceremonial. Where the statute conditions penalties on Commissioner demand and collection, plaintiffs must involve the Commissioner to trigger the specific statutory penalty. Conversely, where statutes expressly give employees access to a penalty, plaintiffs can seek those penalties directly.

- Recipient of the penalty affects standing and mootness. Penalties paid to the state rather than the employee can nonetheless redress an individual plaintiff’s interest if the plaintiff can show ongoing harm or a realistic threat of recurrence. Friends of the Earth supplies the governing standing principle, but plaintiffs must actually develop facts to meet it.

- Litigation strategy must address jurisdictional and mootness pitfalls. If a defendant pays wages and liquidated damages before trial, counsel should be ready to show continuing injury (e.g., policy remains in force) to preserve claims for statutory penalties or seek injunctive relief. Conversely, defendants may strategically change policies and make payments to limit litigation risk and induce mootness—but courts will scrutinize such voluntary cessation claims for whether recurrence is reasonably possible.

- Procedural posture matters when seeking injunctive relief. New claims should be pursued by amendment, not merely argued in opposition briefs, and requests for policy rescission should be pled expressly so district courts can adjudicate them.

Conclusion

Cohen v. Consilio crystallizes how Minnesota’s wage statutes allocate enforcement authority and remedies. The student who masters the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA must learn to read statutory text closely to determine who may enforce a right, what penalties exist, and to whom penalties are payable. Litigation counsel must couple statutory analysis with fact development aimed at avoiding Article III jurisdictional problems: showing ongoing injury or a reasonable prospect of recurrence where necessary, involving the Commissioner where the statute requires, and pleading injunctive claims properly when relief beyond monetary recovery is sought. These practical and doctrinal rules govern most modern wage‑and‑hour disputes in Minnesota and will guide both litigation planning and appellate review.

# Remedies and Enforcement Authority Under Minnesota Law

The interplay between uncompensated wages, statutory damages, and civil penalties is a frequent source of dispute in Minnesota employment litigation. The Eighth Circuit’s recent decision in Cohen v. Consilio, LLC crystallizes several important rules governing who may obtain which remedies and how those remedies are enforced. That opinion is an instructive vehicle for understanding (1) the doctrinal distinctions among back wages, liquidated damages, and civil penalties; (2) the limited circumstances in which a private employee may secure statutory penalties that the Minnesota Legislature has assigned to the Commissioner of Labor and Industry; (3) the scope of the “other appropriate relief” reserved to courts under Minn. Stat. § 181.171; and (4) how attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest fit into the remedial picture. This chapter draws from Cohen and related authority to explain those principles and to indicate how they are applied in practice.

The concrete facts in Cohen are straightforward and typical. Cohen, an hourly licensed-attorney document reviewer, alleged that his employer, Consilio, had adopted an overtime policy that eliminated overtime premium pay and required payment at base pay for all hours worked. Consilio paid Cohen overtime and liquidated damages demanded under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) but refused to concede liability for additional statutory penalties that Cohen sought under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the MFLSA, and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA). The district court granted summary judgment to Consilio; on appeal the Eighth Circuit affirmed in part, vacated and remanded in part, and explained several controlling legal principles about who may recover what remedies and by what process.

## Back wages versus statutory liquidated damages versus civil penalties

A litigation involving unpaid wages ordinarily implicates three distinct categories of monetary relief: (1) back wages (compensatory relief for unpaid earnings), (2) statutory liquidated damages (a form of statutory compensation, often designed to make the employee whole or to penalize knowing violations), and (3) civil penalties (designed primarily to punish or deter and, depending on the statute, to be collected by the state).

Back wages are remedial and compensatory: they represent the wages an employee actually earned but was not paid. In Cohen, Consilio compensated Cohen and other Minnesota employees for the overtime wages they alleged were due; the parties stipulated that those compensatory amounts (and corresponding MFLSA liquidated damages) had been paid. Once compensatory wages are paid and the employee does not contest that payment, the dispute commonly focuses on whether statutory penalties or other non‑compensatory relief remain available.

Liquidated damages occupy a middle position. Under many wage statutes, including the MFLSA (which mirrors the federal FLSA structure), an employer who violates the statute may be liable for liquidated damages in addition to back wages. Those damages commonly serve both to compensate the employee for delay or to reflect statutory condemnation of the violation; in practice they often amount to a multiple of unpaid wages (for instance, a second award equal to the unpaid wages). In Cohen the parties accepted that MFLSA liquidated damages had been paid; that concession narrowed the dispute to whether other statutory penalties could be recovered.

Civil penalties, by contrast, are often expressly characterized by statute as amounts collected by the state (or an administrative agency) for enforcement and deterrence rather than as funds payable to the individual employee. Such penalties are not compensatory in the ordinary sense. Because civil penalties may be allocated by statute either to employees, to the state, or to an enforcing agency, the statutory text — and the legislative design behind that text — determines who may seek them and how they are distributed. The Eighth Circuit in Cohen emphasized this statutory allocation principle: when the MPWA provision at issue (Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a)) expressly entrusts the commissioner with service of a demand and collection of penalties, courts should not read into the statute a private cause of action to collect those same penalties directly.

Why the distinction matters. The remedial character of each category affects (a) who has standing to sue for the relief, (b) whether the remedy survives payment of wages (i.e., whether a claim is mooted by payment), and (c) to whom any recovered sums are directed. Compensatory relief and liquidated damages secured by an aggrieved employee are distinguishable from penalties that the Legislature reserves for the Commissioner’s enforcement toolbox.

## Who can seek or collect civil penalties: Commissioner-only penalties under several provisions

The Cohen decision is primarily a statutory‑interpretation case about who may pursue “average daily wage” penalties under Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a). The statute establishes an administrative enforcement mechanism in which the Commissioner of Labor and Industry (or the Commissioner’s representative) may serve a demand for unpaid wages on an employer, and if wages are not paid within ten days, the Commissioner “may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty in the amount of the employee’s average daily earnings at the same rate or rates for each day beyond the ten‑day limit following the demand.” The statute further provides that “[m]oney collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” The question in Cohen was whether an aggrieved employee, suing under § 181.171 (which grants employees a private cause of action for violations of certain MPWA sections), could bypass the Commissioner and obtain the average daily wage penalty directly in district court.

The Eighth Circuit’s answer was no. Reading § 181.101(a)’s plain language, the court observed that the statute conditions the creation and collection of the penalty on an administrative demand served by the Commissioner. When a statute plainly gives a particular enforcement or collection power to an administrative officer, courts should not imply an alternative route for private litigants unless the statutory text permits it. The court relied on settled principles of statutory interpretation (e.g., “when the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect”) and on the Legislature’s demonstrated ability to grant private litigants penalty recovery in other contexts. Thus, because the statutory scheme expressly empowers the Commissioner to charge and collect the average‑daily‑wage penalty, the penalty is not available to a private plaintiff suing directly under § 181.171.

This allocation of enforcement authority has a few important consequences:

- Procedural starting point. An aggrieved worker seeking the average‑daily‑wage penalty under § 181.101(a) normally must involve the Commissioner’s office — the Commissioner must serve the demand and follow the administrative steps that the statute prescribes before collection of the penalty may occur.

- Receipt of collected funds. Although § 181.101(a) authorizes collection by the Commissioner, it also expressly requires that collected money be paid to the employee. That textual combination signals a dual role for the Commissioner: an administrative enforcer who collects penalties for the statutory purpose of deterrence, while ensuring that the penalty ultimately benefits the individual employee.

- Legislative drafting matters. The court emphasized that the Legislature knows how to entitle an employee to penalties directly when it wishes (for example, in other MPWA provisions concerning discharged employees or those who quit, where the statute expressly authorizes the employee to “charge and collect a penalty”). Courts will treat such drafting choices as informative of legislative intent.

These rules do not mean private plaintiffs have no path to monetary redress beyond wages and liquidated damages. Employees may still obtain compensatory relief and liquidated damages in private suits under § 181.171, and they may seek other forms of relief that § 181.171 contemplates. But where the statute carves enforcement authority to the Commissioner for a particular monetary sanction, private suits cannot be used to circumvent that mechanism.

## “Other appropriate relief” and injunctive relief under § 181.171

Minn. Stat. § 181.171 authorizes an aggrieved person to bring a civil action to obtain “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief” for violations of sections covered by the MPWA. That language is deliberately capacious and ordinarily encompasses equitable remedies, including injunctive relief, where appropriate. The Eighth Circuit in Cohen recognized the phrase and confirmed that injunctive relief is within the compass of § 181.171. But two important caveats govern the availability and procedural prosecution of such relief.

First, the judicial power to fashion equitable remedies like injunctions is exercised within the ordinary constraints of equitable jurisdiction. A plaintiff seeking a prospective injunction must satisfy the familiar elements — including irreparable harm absent relief, a likelihood of success on the merits (or serious questions and a balance of equities in some contexts), and that injunctive relief is necessary to prevent future violations. Where monetary compensation fully remedies past harm, equitable relief may be unnecessary. In wage cases, an injunction can have special utility: it can prohibit an employer from continuing a violative pay practice, prevent future recurrences, and thus serve the deterrent and remedial purposes that civil penalties and administrative enforcement also seek to accomplish.

Second, procedural posture matters. The Cohen opinion underscores that injunctive relief must be properly pleaded and prosecuted. The plaintiff in Cohen shifted his requested injunctive remedies mid‑litigation — his complaint sought certain forms of injunctive relief, but his summary‑judgment briefing requested different, affirmative relief (an order rescinding Consilio’s 2019 policy). The Eighth Circuit held the injunctive request was not properly before the court because a party cannot raise “new claims” for the first time in opposition briefs; such claims must be asserted via an amended complaint. The practical lesson is clear: if a plaintiff contemplates seeking prospective, structural relief (such as a judicial order rescinding a company policy), the complaint should plead equitable claims with specificity, and the plaintiff should preserve that relief through the litigation track.

Finally, § 181.171’s “other appropriate relief” language gives courts discretion to craft relief tailored to the statutory violation. That discretion is not unlimited: courts must respect statutory text, federal‑law limits (when federal law is implicated), and equitable doctrines such as laches and the limits on injunctive relief against private parties. But when properly pleaded and supported, injunctive relief under § 181.171 is a viable remedy to prevent continuing wage violations.

## Attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest under MPWA and MFLSA

Remedies in wage litigation often include not only the substantive relief described above but also awards for attorney’s fees, taxable costs, and prejudgment interest. The availability of these items depends on the statutory fee‑shifting text and on equitable doctrines.

Statutory fee shifting. Many statutes designed to protect employees include provisions awarding reasonable attorney’s fees and costs to the prevailing party. At the federal level, for example, the Fair Labor Standards Act permits prevailing plaintiffs to recover reasonable attorney’s fees (29 U.S.C. § 216(b)); many state counterparts contain parallel provisions. The MFLSA, as a state analogue, and the MPWA should be read carefully to determine whether the Legislature has authorized fee shifting. In Cohen the appellate record and the panel’s opinion did not resolve all fee‑related questions; one judge (Judge Kobes) noted that the district court’s rulings on attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest were vacated for further consideration on remand. That remark is a reminder that procedural and jurisdictional issues (for example, mootness of underlying claims) can affect ancillary fee awards.

Costs and taxable disbursements. Even where statutes do not provide for counsel fees, prevailing parties routinely recover taxable costs (filing fees, service costs, and certain other items) under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 54(d) or state analogues. Courts will examine the statutory scheme and prevailing‑party doctrine to allocate those costs.

Prejudgment interest. Prejudgment interest is an equitable mechanism to compensate a claimant for the time value of money lost by being deprived of the funds to which the claimant was entitled. Some statutes (or common law rules) authorize prejudgment interest; other times courts award it as part of equitable relief. Whether prejudgment interest is available in MPWA or MFLSA claims will turn on statutory text, controlling state law on interest, and whether the plaintiff is a prevailing party on claims giving rise to such relief. The Cohen court left these issues to the district court on remand where jurisdictional issues warranted further factfinding and legal analysis.

Practical considerations. Because fee awards and other ancillary remedies often flow from ultimate success on substantive claims, parties should preserve arguments about fees and costs early, plead the bases for equitable relief clearly, and track the procedural record to ensure any fee application is timely and supported by evidence of the work performed and the results obtained. If a defendant pays wages and some statutory damages during litigation, plaintiffs should also consider whether such payments moot certain fee claims or otherwise affect their entitlement to fees and costs — a question that may turn on who receives the payments and whether the plaintiff remains a “prevailing party.”

## Commissioner demand process and calculation of average daily wage penalties under § 181.101(a)

Section 181.101(a) creates a specific administrative avenue for enforcing payment of wages and for imposing an “average daily wage” penalty when wages are not paid promptly after a Commissioner’s demand. The process, as the statute frames it and as Cohen interprets it, involves these steps:

1. Commissioner demand. The Commissioner of Labor and Industry (or the Commissioner’s representative) may serve a demand upon the employer on behalf of an employee. The statute emphasizes that the Commissioner — not the private employee — is the actor who serves the demand in order to trigger the statutory penalty mechanism.

2. Ten‑day cure period. After service, the employer has ten days to pay the demanded wages. If the employer pays within that period, the collection process ends and no additional penalty under § 181.101(a) accrues under that demand.

3. Commissioner collection and penalty accrual. If the employer does not pay within ten days, the Commissioner may collect the wages and also impose a penalty equal to “the employee’s average daily earnings at the same rate or rates for each day beyond the ten‑day limit following the demand.” That language creates an accruing penalty measured by the employee’s average daily earnings.

4. Payment to the employee. Although the Commissioner collects the penalty, the statute requires that money collected by the Commissioner be paid to the employee. Thus, although the Commissioner is the collecting agent, the employee benefits from the recovery.

Because the statute does not fix a precise mathematical formula for “average daily earnings,” courts confronted with disputes over the calculation must interpret the phrase in light of its ordinary meaning and the statutory purpose. Common practical approaches include:

- Selecting an appropriate reference period (for example, a recent pay period, an average over several weeks or months, or a period tied to the contested unpaid wages) that fairly reflects the employee’s daily earnings; and

- Dividing the relevant total earnings by the number of days actually worked in the reference period (or by the number of calendar days, where context or statute supports that approach).

Which approach is appropriate will depend on the employment context (hourly vs. salaried, regular vs. intermittent work), the structure of the contested pay, and any legislative or regulatory guidance. Because the penalty accrues daily beyond the ten‑day cure period, accuracy in calculation materially affects the amount of the sanction.

The Cohen court’s primary holding — that only the Commissioner may initiate the demand-and-collection process leading to the average‑daily‑wage penalty — emphasizes that private plaintiffs cannot skip the administrative step and obtain that penalty directly in district court. That does not mean a private plaintiff cannot obtain other relief; it does mean that, where a statute delegates collection power to an administrative official, plaintiffs who desire the statute’s unique penalty must involve the Commissioner.

## Standing, mootness, and the continuing‑violation context: when penalties survive payment of wages

Cohen also illustrates the intersection of remedies law with the constitutional doctrines of standing and mootness. Two principles are particularly salient.

First, a plaintiff must have standing for each claim and for each form of relief sought; standing is assessed at the outset and must continue through the litigation. If the only interest that originally supported a plaintiff’s claim vanishes (for example, because the defendant pays all compensatory damages and no demonstrable prospect of future harm exists), the claim can become moot. The Supreme Court’s decision in Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw and later cases make clear that plaintiffs may still have standing to seek civil penalties payable to a government official if the challenged conduct is ongoing or is likely to recur — because penalties can redress a plaintiff’s interest in abating an ongoing violation and deterring future ones. But where the defendant’s conduct has ceased and the record makes it “absolutely clear” that the wrongful behavior will not recur, voluntary cessation can moot a claim.

Second, the identity of the remedial recipient matters. If penalties are payable solely to the government or the Commissioner, a private litigant’s interest in securing those penalties is attenuated, and courts will examine whether the plaintiff still has a personal stake in obtaining such relief (for example, through continued exposure to a violative policy or proof that the policy remains in effect). In Cohen, Consilio had paid the employee’s unpaid wages and MFLSA liquidated damages, but the company’s policy status remained disputed. Because the contested statutory penalties under the MFLSA were payable to the Commissioner (not the employee), the court concluded it could not confidently adjudicate the question of statutory penalties without first resolving whether jurisdiction remained — i.e., whether the dispute had become moot given subsequent developments. The Eighth Circuit therefore vacated summary judgment on the MFLSA penalty issue and remanded for the district court to consider its jurisdiction.

Practically, litigators should (a) preserve evidence of ongoing or recurring employer policies that could create future harm, (b) consider pursuing injunctive relief to prevent repetition of the violation (thereby aiding Article III standing), and (c) be mindful that a defendant’s interim payments may moot certain claims unless the plaintiff can demonstrate a continuing or threatened injury.

## Practical guidance for litigators and agencies

The lessons of Cohen are concrete and practical:

- Know the statute. Always read the statutory enforcement provisions carefully to determine whether the Legislature assigned collection or demand powers to the Commissioner or whether it authorized private enforcement of penalties. Plain statutory language often resolves whether a private litigant may directly recover a penalty.

- Use the Commissioner where required. If a statute — like § 181.101(a) — makes the Commissioner the necessary actor to trigger a penalty, coordinate with administrative agencies early. A private suit under § 181.171 for compensatory relief can proceed, but the MPWA’s administrative pathway must be followed to obtain the statutory average‑daily‑wage penalty.

- Preserve equitable relief claims. If injunctive or declaratory relief is sought, plead those remedies in the complaint and develop the evidentiary record showing the need for prospective relief. Do not attempt to convert a damages action into a broader equitable claim for the first time at summary judgment.

- Anticipate standing and mootness challenges. If a defendant pays wages in litigation, be prepared to show that a live controversy remains — for example, because an unlawful pay policy persists, because the employer continues to treat employees the same way, or because the plaintiff seeks prospective remedies that can prevent recurrence.

- Prepare for fee and interest disputes. Preserve fee and cost claims, document the time and results achieved, and know which statutory provisions can support an award of attorney’s fees and prejudgment interest. If the defendant pays during litigation, carefully assess whether you are a prevailing party for fee purposes under the applicable statute.

- Calculate penalties carefully. When a penalty is measured by “average daily earnings,” assemble payroll records and choose a reference period that fairly reflects the employee’s earnings. Be ready to brief the appropriate method for converting periodic pay into an average daily figure.

Conclusion

Cohen v. Consilio illuminates central features of Minnesota’s wage‑remedy architecture: compensatory wages and liquidated damages are recoverable by private action under § 181.171, but certain civil penalties — particularly the average‑daily‑wage penalty created by § 181.101(a) — are implemented through the Commissioner’s demand-and-collection role. The textual allocation of enforcement authority to an administrative official constrains private plaintiffs, channels some forms of relief through the state, and shapes the litigation strategies of employees and employers alike. Understanding these distinctions — and the procedural steps entailed — is indispensable for effective litigation and for ensuring compliance with Minnesota’s wage statutes.

# Case Study Setup: Cohen v. Consilio

This chapter sets out the facts and procedural posture that frame the legal disputes in Cohen v. Consilio. The opinion on appeal turns less on contested facts than on statutory text, the allocation of who may recover particular statutory penalties, and the procedural consequences of the parties’ stipulations and post‑complaint payments. Understanding these factual and strategic choices is essential for analyzing the legal issues that follow: (1) the scope of remedies available under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA); (2) how plaintiffs’ acceptance of payments affects jurisdiction and mootness; and (3) the standards that govern appellate review of a district court’s grant of summary judgment.

## Consilio’s 2019 change to eliminate overtime premium and pay base rate for all hours

The operative factual predicate of the case is simple and documentary. Cohen was employed by Consilio, a national legal-document-review company, as an hourly licensed-attorney document reviewer in Minnesota. In July 2019 Consilio sent an email to its Minnesota-based hourly licensed-attorney reviewers announcing a new premium-overtime policy to take effect in August 2019. The policy eliminated overtime premium pay and stated that affected employees would be paid “at [their] base pay rate” for all hours worked.

This change is important for two reasons. First, it establishes the conduct that Cohen alleged was unlawful: a uniform employment policy that purported to eliminate the overtime premium. Second, because the policy change was memorialized and disseminated by email, the case turns on statutory interpretation (what relief the statutes authorize when an employer adopts such a policy) rather than contested factual disputes about whether the conduct occurred. That contraction of factual dispute is why the parties and the courts proceeded to resolution on summary judgment.

When approaching cases of this genre—policy changes that set pay rules across a class of employees—students should note how the documentary nature of the employer’s notice often narrows issues to pure statutory questions: did the employer’s instituted pay practice comply with state and federal wage laws, and what remedies do those statutes allow when it did not? That is the dynamic here.

## Plaintiff’s claims under MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA; requests for wages, penalties, liquidated damages, and injunction

Cohen filed suit in August 2020 on behalf of himself and a putative class of similarly situated employees. He asserted three principal Minnesota statutory claims:

- violations of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), principally Minn. Stat. § 181.101 (and the remedies available under § 181.171);
- violations of the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. §§ 177.25 and 177.27 (which mirror the federal FLSA regime in many respects); and
- violations of the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. § 181.032 (alleging a failure to provide statutorily required notice of the change in overtime policy).

Cohen sought multiple forms of relief. At the core of his case were unpaid overtime wages. He also sought liquidated damages (a species of statutory double damages common to FLSA/MFLSA claims), statutory penalties, and prospective injunctive relief ordering Consilio to rescind the 2019 policy.

The complaint thus framed a typical mixed remedy approach: monetary recovery for past unpaid wages and associated statutory double‑damages, penalties that might be payable to either the employee or a public official depending on the statute, and injunctive relief to prevent recurrence. The interplay of those distinct remedies—who receives them and under what statutory pathway they are available—becomes the fulcrum of the appellate dispute.

## Employer’s payments of overtime and MFLSA liquidated damages; stipulation as to amounts owed

After filing suit, Consilio paid substantial sums to resolve the overtime wage and associated liquidated-damages aspects of the MFLSA claim. The company paid $256,010.01 to cover overtime wages and liquidated damages demanded in the MFLSA claim, of which Cohen personally received $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages. The parties stipulated that “Cohen has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that “Cohen does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].”

Those payments and the stipulation are strategically significant. When a plaintiff who sues for unpaid wages accepts payment of the wages and stipulates that the wage obligation has been satisfied, two consequences follow:

1. The core monetary claim for unpaid wages is no longer in dispute, which ordinarily undermines any Article III injury that the plaintiff could assert on that specific form of relief, and
2. The case will often narrow to collateral or prospective remedies (for example, statutory penalties payable to a government official, or injunctive relief aimed at preventing future violations).

In Cohen, the parties acknowledged these effects and agreed that the “most expedient and efficient path forward” was to resolve the statutory questions by summary judgment: if the court rejected Cohen’s claim for penalties and other statutory remedies, the case would be fully resolved.

This situation illustrates a common tactical sequence in wage-and-hour litigation: defendant pays wage claim amounts (sometimes calculated to address classwide exposure), the plaintiff stipulates wage satisfaction, and the litigation proceeds only on statutory penalties and injunctive relief. Such a sequence forces courts to confront jurisdictional doctrines—standing and mootness—and to scrutinize the statutory allocation of remedies.

## Issues preserved: statutory penalties (MPWA and MFLSA), MWTA notice remedies, and injunctive relief

After the payments, what remained in dispute were primarily statutory penalties and the availability of injunctive relief. Cohen sought three categories of penalties: average daily wage penalties under the MPWA (§ 181.101), penalties under the MFLSA, and penalties under the MWTA for failure to provide statutorily required notice. He also sought an injunction directing Consilio to rescind its 2019 policy.

But the character of those remedies differs dramatically under Minnesota law:

- Under the MPWA, the text of § 181.101 authorizes the Commissioner of Labor and Industry to serve a demand and, if wages remain unpaid ten days after demand, to “charge and collect . . . a penalty in the amount of the employee’s average daily earnings . . . for each day beyond the ten‑day limit.” The MPWA also contains a private‑right‑of‑action provision (§ 181.171) that allows employees to seek redress in district court for violations, but the statute’s text and Minnesota precedent suggest that the particular average‑daily‑wage penalty in § 181.101 is a remedy the Commissioner collects (and then pays to employees), not a penalty that an employee may independently seek in district court. The parties and the court invoked Minnesota cases that highlight that careful textual allocation.
- Under the MFLSA, the parties agreed that statutory penalties, if any, are payable to the Commissioner rather than to Cohen. That allocation placed the MFLSA penalties in a middle ground: monetary deterrence benefits the public fisc and is administered by a state official; nevertheless, as the Supreme Court recognized in Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw, civil penalties paid to the government can sometimes support a private plaintiff’s Article III standing when the plaintiff alleges continuing violations and seeks to deter future unlawful conduct.
- The MWTA claim was framed as a notice‑statute violation. Cohen alleged Consilio failed to provide the statutorily required notice when changing pay practices. But in the district court and on appeal Cohen identified no clear statutory provision authorizing a private plaintiff to collect penalties for that failure; he also stipulated he had received all pay he sought. Because he did not develop a statutory or doctrinal argument supporting continuing recovery on that front, the appellate court declined to disturb the district court’s dismissal.

Finally, Cohen’s requested injunctive relief (an affirmative order rescinding the 2019 policy) was a shifting target in the litigation. Cohen sought particular forms of injunctive relief in his complaint but later sought different relief in opposing summary judgment. The appellate court emphasized the correct procedural pathway: new or altered claims for relief must generally be presented in an amended pleading, not sprung on the court in opposition briefing. That failure to preserve the injunctive theory foreclosed appellate review.

## Appellate standard of review for summary judgment

The court’s disposition turned on summary judgment. The Eighth Circuit reiterated the familiar standards governing appellate review: summary judgment is reviewed de novo, viewing the evidence and all reasonable inferences in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party, and is appropriate if no genuine dispute of material fact exists and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

Those rules matter in two ways in this case. First, because the employer’s policy was memorialized in written communications and the parties stipulated about payments, the dispute was largely one of statutory interpretation and legal consequence—areas well suited to disposition on summary judgment. Second, even on de novo review the appellate court must treat as true, for purposes of the nonmoving plaintiff’s claims, any factual inferences that are reasonably supported by the record. That standard shaped the court’s treatment of whether a plaintiff still had a live controversy when (a) the wages and liquidated damages had been paid, and (b) the availability of statutory penalties depended on statutory text and on whether the challenged employer policy remained in effect.

The opinion cites precedent to illustrate these points: summary judgment standards drawn from Karlen and Placzek and the principle that “standing must continue throughout the litigation” (Sisney and Town of Chester). Those authorities underpin the court’s concern about jurisdiction: a plaintiff can lose Article III standing if post‑filing events make it “absolutely clear” that the wrongful conduct could not reasonably be expected to recur (Friends of the Earth), and the character of the remedy sought (private monetary award to the plaintiff versus penalties paid to a public official) can affect whether a case retains a live controversy.

Putting the standard into practice here, the Eighth Circuit divided the contested legal questions:

- MPWA: Because the statutory text of § 181.101 places the average‑daily‑wage penalty in the hands of the Commissioner (who must demand payment and collects penalties, which are then paid to the employee), the appellate court concluded the district court did not err in holding that Cohen could not independently obtain those MPWA “average daily wage” penalties in district court.
- MFLSA: Because the parties stipulated wages and liquidated damages had been paid but disagreed about whether Consilio still maintained the challenged policy (and because penalties under the MFLSA are payable to the Commissioner), the court concluded there are unresolved jurisdictional questions. Civil penalties paid to a government official can nonetheless support a private plaintiff’s standing if the plaintiff can show a prospective injury (deterrence, risk of recurrence) under Friends of the Earth. Because the court had insufficient record on whether the policy remained in effect—and because the parties had not briefed the standing/mootness contours adequately—the appellate court vacated the district court’s grant of summary judgment on the MFLSA claim and remanded for the district court to resolve its jurisdiction.
- MWTA and injunctive relief: On the MWTA claim, Cohen had not developed a statutory argument that would support private recovery of penalties, and he had stipulated wage recovery. The appellate court therefore declined to disturb dismissal of the MWTA claim. As to injunctive relief, the court refused to consider a newly framed request presented in opposition briefing rather than by amendment of the complaint.

Taken together, these rulings illustrate how appellate application of the summary judgment standard often interacts with statutory drafting choices and post‑filing plaintiff conduct (acceptance of payments and stipulations) to narrow or eliminate a plaintiff’s ability to pursue certain remedies. They also underscore that when a defendant pays the principal monetary claim, plaintiffs and counsel must think ahead to preserve prospective relief and to assemble a record that supports standing to pursue penalties or injunctive remedies.

Conclusion

Cohen v. Consilio provides a compact illustration of how (1) a documented change in an employer’s pay policy can convert a case into a statutory‑interpretation dispute; (2) post‑filing payments by a defendant and stipulations by a plaintiff can moot or narrow claims; and (3) statutory allocations of remedies (who may seek penalties and to whom penalties are paid) can be dispositive. For students, the case highlights the practical importance of (a) attentive pleadings that preserve the precise remedies sought, (b) understanding the statutory text that allocates relief between private plaintiffs and state officials, and (c) ensuring an adequate factual record to sustain standing for nonmonetary or government‑payable remedies when core wage claims are resolved before trial.

# Interpreting MPWA § 181.101: Commissioner-Only Average Daily Wage Penalties

This chapter examines the interpretive problem presented in Cohen v. Consilio, LLC: whether a private employee who sues under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) may recover the “average daily wage” penalties that § 181.101(a) authorizes when an employer fails to pay wages on demand, or whether that penalty mechanism is exclusively the Commissioner's to initiate and collect. Cohen teaches how textual structure, the MPWA’s private‑action provision, comparative statutory design, and controlling Minnesota precedents together point to a narrow, text‑based answer: employees may prosecute wage claims in court, but they may not privately obtain the specific average‑daily‑wage penalty created by § 181.101(a); that remedy belongs to the Commissioner.

## Textual structure of § 181.101(a): employee wage claim versus Commissioner’s penalty authority

The starting point for statutory interpretation is the text. Section 181.101(a) has a twofold structure. On the one hand, it recognizes and preserves an individual employee’s ability to “prosecute a claim for wages” in court; on the other hand, it vests a distinct enforcement and penalty power in the Commissioner of Labor and Industry (or the Commissioner’s representative). The text provides that the Commissioner “may serve a demand for payment on behalf of an employee,” and if the employer does not pay within ten days, “the commissioner may charge and collect the wages . . . and a penalty in the amount of the employee’s average daily earnings at the same rate or rates for each day beyond the ten‑day limit following the demand.” The statute also states that “money collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.”

Two separate functions are thus embedded in the provision: (1) a private right to pursue unpaid wages (the employee’s claim), and (2) an administrative remedy mechanism in which the Commissioner may demand wages and, upon noncompliance, collect a statutorily defined daily penalty and remit collected sums to the worker. Read in context, the text speaks in different voices—one that preserves the employee’s direct judicial remedy, and another that authorizes the Commissioner to act administratively to obtain both wages and a fixed penalty. That differentiation is the key to the Cohen court’s conclusion: § 181.101(a) plainly assigns the specific authority to assess and collect the average‑daily‑wage penalty to the Commissioner rather than to private litigants.

A careful student should note how the statute’s pronouns and verbs signal who acts. The statute repeatedly refers to the Commissioner as the actor who “may serve a demand,” “may charge and collect” wages and a penalty, and “must” pay collected money to the employee. In contrast, the employee’s role is described more modestly: the section “does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages.” The natural reading—also the one the Minnesota Supreme Court’s plain‑meaning approach would favor—is that the statute distinguishes between two remedies and two remedies‑makers: employees may sue for wages, but the § 181.101 penalty mechanism is exercised by the Commissioner.

## Private right of action under § 181.171, subd. 1 and the clause limiting recovery of civil penalties to those “provided for in the section violated”

Section 181.171, subdivision 1, creates and preserves a private civil action under specified MPWA provisions. It authorizes “a person” to bring a civil action “seeking redress for violations” of enumerated sections, and states that when an employer “is found to have violated the above sections” the employer “shall also be liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief including but not limited to injunctive relief.” Crucially, § 181.171 also provides that an employer “is liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated.”

That last clause is the bridge between a private plaintiff’s suit and the available statutory penalties. It confirms that a plaintiff suing under § 181.171 can recover those civil penalties that are “provided for in the section violated.” But what counts as being “provided for” within a section is a matter of text and structure. If, as in § 181.101, the section’s penalty language explicitly contemplates action by the Commissioner—and describes the penalty in the context of the Commissioner’s enforcement power—then it is a natural textual reading to conclude that the penalty is not a separately available private remedy. Section 181.171 does not automatically create new penalties; it only makes available the penalties that the violated section itself supplies. Thus, the private‑action provision does not override a textual allocation of enforcement authority within the violated provision.

Applied to Cohen, this means that his suit under § 181.171 could seek the remedies that § 181.101 itself “provides” in a form available to private litigants—principally, recovery of unpaid wages—while the distinct average daily wage penalty, phrased as something the Commissioner “may charge and collect,” is not “provided for” as a private civil penalty in the same way. The district court and the Eighth Circuit read § 181.171 in this statutoryly tethered way: private plaintiffs may vindicate the underlying wage right but cannot treat § 181.101(a)’s Commissioner‑administered penalty as a judicially collectible private statutory penalty.

## Comparative statutory design: §§ 181.13 and 181.14 expressly authorize employee penalties; negative inference canon

Interpretation of § 181.101 is aided by comparing it to neighboring provisions of the MPWA. Sections 181.13 (discharged employees) and 181.14 (employees who quit or resign) explicitly provide that an employee, upon making a demand, “may charge and collect a penalty” if wages are not paid timely. Those provisions grant express authority to employees themselves to charge and collect a penalty in particular circumstances. The contrast between those statutes and § 181.101 is telling. When the legislature has intended to empower an employee to collect a penalty, it has said so expressly; where the legislature wished to allocate collection authority to the Commissioner, as § 181.101 does, it wrote that allocation into the statute.

This pattern supports an inference: the legislature “knows how” to provide a private penalty remedy when it wants to. The negative‑inference canon (expressio unius est exclusio alterius) counsels that the omission of a similar private‑recovery clause from § 181.101 is meaningful. Courts use this canon to infer legislative intent from statutory contrasts: when one provision grants an express private remedy and another provision—covering related subject matter—grants a remedy to a public official without similarly empowering the private party, the absence of parallel language is persuasive evidence the legislature chose different enforcement schemes for different situations. The Eighth Circuit relied on exactly this comparative statutory design in Cohen: the Legislature’s explicit conferral of employee penalty power in other MPWA provisions undermines a claim that § 181.101 silently authorized private recovery of the average daily wage penalty.

But two caveats are important for students. First, expressio unius is not an inflexible rule; it is a tool of interpretation that gains weight when the statutory context and purposes align. Second, the comparative approach must be combined with plain‑meaning analysis and other textual indicators; it is supportive, not dispositive, evidence.

## Allan v. R.D. Offutt (plain meaning), Lagasse v. Horton (legislature “knows how” canon), and Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exchange (allocation of penalties)

Minnesota decisions illuminate the interpretive pathway that Cohen follows. In Allan v. R.D. Offutt Co., the Minnesota Supreme Court reiterated that when “the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, it is assumed to manifest legislative intent and must be given effect.” That bedrock rule undergirds the Eighth Circuit’s reliance on the explicit wording of § 181.101(a). Where the statute makes the Commissioner the actor authorized to impose the daily penalty, courts should refrain from rewriting the statute to create a private remedy that the Legislature did not plainly provide.

Lagasse v. Horton supplies the canonic observation that the Legislature “knows how” to accomplish a particular objective when it so chooses. The Lagasse court invoked the contrast between statutes to show that, if the Legislature wanted to confer a particular right or remedy in one place but not another, that asymmetry was presumed intentional. Cohen invokes precisely that reasoning: because §§ 181.13 and 181.14 allow an employee to charge and collect a penalty, the omission of such language in § 181.101 reinforces the conclusion that the average daily wage penalty there is intended to be administratively collected by the Commissioner, not litigated by private parties for statutory penalties.

Milner v. Farmers Insurance Exchange is especially germane because it addressed the relationship between Commissioner enforcement and employees’ entitlements under § 181.101. Milner holds that the penalties are collected by the Commissioner, and that any money collected must be paid to the employee. Importantly, Milner also underscores the allocation of collection authority to the Commissioner; it clarifies that the statutory mechanism is for the Commissioner to serve a demand and to collect stipulated penalties when employers do not comply. Milner therefore supports a reading that the statutory penalty is a public enforcement device that benefits the employee as the ultimate recipient of collected funds, rather than a private statutory entitlement that gives the employee a direct right to obtain that particular penalty through ordinary litigation.

Together, Allan, Lagasse, and Milner embody the interpretive pillars the Cohen court used: apply plain meaning first (Allan); acknowledge legislative drafting choices and infer meaning from contrasts (Lagasse); and respect the statutory allocation of enforcement and collection to the Commissioner (Milner).

## Application in Cohen: why employees may sue for wages but cannot privately obtain average daily wage penalties under § 181.101

Cohen puts the abstract interpretive rules into practice. The factual posture is straightforward: Consilio changed its overtime premium policy and paid the employee‑plaintiff and a class of Minnesota licensed attorney document reviewers at base pay for all hours, allegedly in violation of overtime requirements. After suit, Consilio paid Cohen the overtime wages and liquidated damages Cohen claimed under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), but the parties disputed whether Cohen could obtain additional average daily wage penalties under § 181.101(a).

The Eighth Circuit’s analysis begins with the statute’s plain language. Section 181.101(a) repeatedly assigns the demand, collection, and penalty authority to the Commissioner. The provision preserves an employee’s ability to “prosecute a claim for wages,” but it does not say that an employee may independently recover the average daily wage penalty. Reading the statutory text as a whole, the Eighth Circuit concluded that the Commissioner alone may pursue and collect the § 181.101 penalty after serving a demand for payment and waiting ten days.

The Cohen opinion then appeals to the comparative statutory design. Because the Legislature explicitly gave employees the power to “charge and collect a penalty” in other provisions— §§ 181.13 and 181.14—its omission of similar language in § 181.101 is meaningful. The court invoked Lagasse’s “knows how” reasoning: where the Legislature wanted to grant employees express penalty authority, it did so; where it instead authorized the Commissioner to act, it said that. That contrast supports the conclusion that the MPWA’s penalty scheme is heterogeneous and that § 181.101’s average daily wage penalty is not a private statutory penalty available in a private suit under § 181.171.

Finally, the Eighth Circuit relied on Milner’s allocation of collection authority to the Commissioner. Milner recognizes that, although procedures place the Commissioner in the enforcement driver's seat, any money the Commissioner collects is to be paid to the employee. That feature reconciles two concerns: it preserves an administrative enforcement channel (the Commissioner) while ensuring employees are not deprived of monetary benefit. But Milner does not blur the legal allocation of enforcement authority; it confirms that the collection mechanism is administrative rather than private litigation‑based.

Synthesizing these points, Cohen instructs that an employee who sues under § 181.171 may obtain judicially ordered wages and other remedies the statute makes available to private litigants (e.g., compensatory damages, injunctive relief where appropriate), but not the statutory average daily wage penalty that § 181.101 expressly authorizes the Commissioner to “charge and collect.” The civil‑penalty language in § 181.171—making an employer “liable to the aggrieved party for the civil penalties or damages provided for in the section violated”—does not convert the Commissioner’s enforcement power in § 181.101 into a private cause of action for the same penalty.

To illustrate the doctrinal boundary with a short hypothetical: if Employer X knowingly withholds final wages when an employee is discharged, § 181.13 gives the discharged employee an immediate, private penalty remedy—an employee may “charge and collect a penalty.” By contrast, if Employer Y fails to pay wages demanded under § 181.101(a), a private suit can recover the unpaid wages but cannot impose the statutory average daily wage penalty directly in court unless the Commissioner first exercises the administrative remedy in § 181.101(a).

## Practical implications and concluding rule

Cohen’s holding produces several practical consequences worth emphasizing for students and practitioners.

- For private plaintiffs: suits under § 181.171 remain a viable path to recover unpaid wages, compensatory relief, and potentially injunctive relief; however, where a sought statutory penalty is expressly structured as the Commissioner’s enforcement tool (as § 181.101 is), private plaintiffs cannot convert that particular penalty into a private statutory recovery. If the Commissioner does act and collects the penalty, the statute requires the Commissioner to remit collected funds to the employee, but that path depends on administrative action.

- For defendants: an employer’s payment of the underlying wages and liquidated damages may moot or curtail private plaintiffs’ claims, but the possibility of administrative demands or requests for penalties from the Commissioner remains; the Commissioner’s ability to act separately from private litigation is an independent enforcement backstop.

- For courts: when interpreting remedial statutes, courts should respect textual allocations of enforcement authority and use comparisons to related statutes to discern legislative design choices. Plain meaning is the lodestar; canons such as expressio unius provide persuasive context but are not stand‑alone trump cards.

The doctrinal rule distilled from Cohen and its precedential anchors is straightforward: under the MPWA, § 181.101 preserves employees’ private right to prosecute wage claims, but the statute’s text vests the average daily wage penalty mechanism in the hands of the Commissioner. A private plaintiff who sues under § 181.171 may recover only those civil penalties that the violated section itself plainly makes available as private remedies; where the section places penalty initiation and collection with the Commissioner, the private suit cannot independently obtain that statutory penalty. This interpretation follows the statute’s plain language, is reinforced by contrasting statutory provisions that explicitly authorize private penalties, and accords with Minnesota precedent allocating collection authority to the Commissioner.

For further study, consider how this allocation of enforcement authority interacts with administrative priorities and resource constraints: if the Commissioner declines to act, are employees practically denied a statutory penalty that legislative design intended as an enforcement incentive? Cohen points to the answer implicit in the MPWA’s structure: the Legislature chose different enforcement channels for different contexts, and courts will enforce those choices unless and until the Legislature revises the statutory scheme.

# MFLSA Civil Penalties and Article III: Redressability and Mootness

This chapter examines a recurrent doctrinal problem in private enforcement of wage-and-hour laws: when a private plaintiff seeks civil penalties that the statute directs to a government official (here, the Commissioner), can the plaintiff satisfy Article III’s redressability requirement? If the defendant voluntarily pays wages and liquidated damages, does any remaining claim for penalties become moot? We use Cohen v. Consilio, LLC (8th Cir. 2025) as a working example. The opinion illustrates both the statutory distinctions that create the problem and the Supreme Court and circuit precedents that shape its resolution: Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw, Town of Chester v. Laroe Estates, City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, and the Eighth Circuit’s own gloss in cases such as Sisney.

The doctrinal stakes are important. Wage statutes commonly give employees the private right to recover unpaid wages and certain statutory liquidated damages. Many statutes also provide for civil penalties that are collected by an enforcement agency and paid to the treasury or to an enforcement fund. That allocation of remedies—private recovery for wages, public allocation for penalties—creates a question whether a private litigant has standing to press for penalties and whether the claim survives steps by the employer (payments, policy changes) that might make the injury disappear.

## MFLSA remedies: private recovery of wages and liquidated damages; civil penalties available to and collected by the Commissioner (Milner)

A prerequisite to analyzing Article III redressability is a careful account of the statutory scheme and which remedies go where. In Cohen, the parties agreed that Consilio had paid the overtime wages and liquidated damages that Cohen claimed under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA). Those categories—compensatory unpaid wages and liquidated damages—are classic private remedies: when an employee proves unpaid overtime the employee is individually harmed (loss of wages) and can recover that loss in litigation.

By contrast, the parties also agreed (and the Eighth Circuit accepted) that any statutory civil penalties under the MFLSA are not payable to the employee but are collected by the Commissioner. The distinction matters: the employee’s private claim for wages seeks money that will flow directly to the plaintiff if she prevails. A claim for civil penalties, in which the statute assigns the collection and appropriation to a public official, may result in funds flowing to the state rather than to the individual plaintiff.

The Minnesota statutory framework illustrated another related nuance under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), considered in Cohen and earlier Minnesota cases such as Milner v. Farmers Ins. Exchange. Section 181.101 of the MPWA, for example, authorizes the Commissioner to levy an “average daily wage” penalty if the Commissioner serves a demand and the employer fails to pay within a statutory period; it does not expressly authorize individuals to recover that penalty in a private suit. Milner clarified that such penalties are collected by the Commissioner and paid to the aggrieved employee only to the extent the Commissioner collects money, but the enforcement power to seek the penalty rests with the Commissioner rather than with private actors. Cohen relied on these statutory distinctions to show that the allocation of remedy matters to the jurisdictional question.

Key lesson: when a wage statute assigns different remedial streams—wages and liquidated damages to the private plaintiff; civil penalties to a government agency—any Article III analysis separating injury and redressability must address where relief would actually flow and what relief would change the plaintiff’s legal position.

## Redressability when penalties go to the government: Friends of the Earth rationale

The Supreme Court’s decision in Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw provides the principal framework for understanding how a plaintiff can obtain redress through civil penalties that are paid to the government. Laidlaw held that environmental plaintiffs had standing to seek civil penalties even though the penalties would go to the Government rather than to the plaintiffs themselves. The Court reasoned that civil penalties can provide redress in two ways: (1) by encouraging defendants to discontinue their unlawful conduct and thereby abating the plaintiff’s injury, and (2) by deterring future violations that would otherwise harm the plaintiff. In short, a prospective or ongoing risk of injury can be redressed by an order imposing civil penalties because the order changes the defendant’s incentives and conduct.

Applied to wage cases like Cohen, Friends of the Earth supports the proposition that a plaintiff need not receive the monetary penalty directly to have standing. If a civil penalty will (or, if ordered, would likely) cause the employer to stop an ongoing unlawful pay practice—thereby removing the risk of future unpaid wages—then the penalty serves to redress the plaintiff’s injury. The Court’s focus is on the effect of the relief on the plaintiff’s concrete injury: does the requested relief make it more likely than not that the plaintiff’s injury will be prevented or remedied?

There are, however, two cautionary points that adjust Friends of the Earth to the wage-law context. First, Laidlaw dealt with an ongoing environmental discharge that continued to injure plaintiffs; its reasoning is strongest where the challenged conduct is ongoing or likely to recur. Where the challenged practice has ceased and the defendant disclaims the possibility of recurrence, redressability is less clear. Second, Laidlaw does not dispense with the requirement that the plaintiff demonstrate a concrete injury distinct from generalized grievance; the plaintiff must still show an injury in fact and that the requested relief will likely redress that injury.

Put simply: penalties payable to the government can still redress a private plaintiff’s concrete injury by changing the defendant’s behavior (abatement and deterrence), but whether they do so in a given case depends on the facts—especially whether the unlawful conduct is ongoing or likely to recur.

## Standing must persist through the case; Town of Chester and Sisney on claim-specific standing and mootness

Standing is not a one-time threshold that is satisfied at filing and thereafter ignored. The Supreme Court in Town of Chester v. Laroe Estates reiterated that a plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim and for each form of relief sought, and that standing must persist throughout the litigation. A related principle is that the facts supporting standing when suit is filed must continue to exist when judgment is entered; if those facts dissipate, the claim may become moot.

The Eighth Circuit has applied this continuous-standing principle in cases like Sisney v. Kaemingk, underscoring that the requisite personal interest must exist “at the commencement of the litigation” and must continue “throughout its existence.” Practically, this requires district courts to police their jurisdiction when intervening events—such as the defendant’s voluntary cessation of a wrongful practice or the plaintiff’s receipt of the full monetary relief he sought—could eliminate the plaintiff’s personal stake.

Cohen illustrates this point. The parties stipulated that Consilio had paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages the plaintiff alleged he was owed. That stipulation undercut any live controversy about recovery of wages and liquidated damages, leaving only the penalties claim. Because those penalties were payable to the Commissioner, the court had to ask whether Cohen retained a personal stake (an injury-in-fact capable of redress) sufficient to keep the penalties claim alive. If the allegedly violative overtime policy was still in place or likely to recur, Friends of the Earth’s deterrence rationale might support redressability and thus jurisdiction. If not, standing—and thus the claim—might have been extinguished.

The procedural consequence of this principle is practical: district courts should resolve jurisdictional questions early. If a defendant’s partial compliance or payment removes plaintiff’s individual monetary loss, the court should determine whether any remaining claims (especially ones seeking penalties that do not directly compensate the plaintiff) present a live controversy. The Eighth Circuit in Cohen vacated the district court’s summary judgment for the MFLSA penalties claim and remanded for the district court to determine whether it retained jurisdiction—effectively instructing the district court to take up the standing/mootness question before proceeding on the merits.

## Voluntary cessation doctrine and the “absolutely clear” burden; City of Mesquite

A common route by which a defendant seeks to defeat an otherwise live claim is voluntary cessation of the challenged conduct—payment of back wages, rescission of policies, or a representation that the practice will not recur. City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle established the controlling framework: voluntary cessation does not moot a case unless the defendant can show that “it is absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.” The Court’s concern is that defendants might strategically cease unlawful conduct when sued to avoid judicial review, then resume once the case is dismissed.

Mesquite’s “absolutely clear” standard places a heavy burden on the defendant who asserts mootness through voluntary cessation. The defendant must produce objective evidence that the conduct has been permanently terminated and that there is no realistic prospect of repetition. Examples of persuasive evidence include incontrovertible changes in corporate structure, binding changes in policy accompanied by systemic operational changes, or a statutory or regulatory change that makes recurrence impossible.

In Cohen, the Eighth Circuit flagged the voluntary-cessation problem. Consilio had paid the monetary components of Cohen’s claim, but whether its overtime policy remained in force was in dispute. Because the deterrence-based redressability analysis depends on the prospect of recurrence, the court concluded it could not safely resolve the penalties claim without a factual determination whether Consilio’s policy remained—and if it did not remain, whether it was “absolutely clear” it would not recur. The court’s remedy was to vacate the summary judgment on the MFLSA penalties claim and remand for the district court to determine jurisdiction.

A practical point for litigators: a defendant cannot rely on payment or policy statements alone to obtain dismissal; it should present affirmative, objective proof that the wrongdoing has ceased permanently. Plaintiffs, by contrast, can preserve their claims by putting in evidence suggesting the defendant’s statements are non-binding or plausibly reversible (for example, lack of written rescission, contradictory employee communications, or corporate incentives to resume the policy).

## Cohen’s remand: what the district court must decide and the implications for mootness

Cohen instructs what fact-finding and legal analysis remain appropriate on remand. The district court must determine—factually and with record support—whether Consilio’s allegedly unlawful overtime policy remains in force or whether there is a realistic prospect that the employer will resume the policy. That inquiry will likely require limited discovery and factual findings, focusing on both the employer’s contemporaneous actions and their systemic plausibility.

Key factual points the district court should develop include:

- Does Consilio currently maintain an overtime policy that eliminates premium pay for overtime hours and treats all hours as paid at base rate? Is that policy in writing, communicated to employees, and in current effect?
- If Consilio changed or rescinded the policy, did it do so in a manner that makes the cessation permanent—e.g., written rescission, new policy promulgated, system-level payroll changes, executive declarations, operational changes?
- Are there countervailing indicia of potential recurrence—e.g., prior history of changing policies back and forth, internal emails indicating intent to reinstate, financial incentives to re-adopt the challenged practice, or lack of corporate commitment (informal employee communications rather than binding directives)?
- Did Consilio’s payment of back wages or liquidated damages include any admission of liability or indicate a settlement limited to identified employees that leaves open other cases or future misconduct?
- What is the realistic incentive structure for Consilio to renew the challenged practice? (For example, would reinstatement produce significant cost savings that may tempt the employer to revert if no penalty attaches?)

On the legal side, the district court should apply the Mesquite standard: if Consilio seeks dismissal for voluntary cessation, it must show it is “absolutely clear” that the conduct will not recur. If the court finds the conduct remains or is likely to recur, Friends of the Earth supports jurisdiction because penalties could plausibly deter future violations and thus redress the plaintiff’s risk of injury. If, instead, the court finds the cessation is permanent and objectively reliable, the penalties claim may be moot.

The Eighth Circuit’s vacatur and remand recognize an important procedural priority: jurisdictional questions must be resolved before a court can enter dispositive relief on the merits. On remand, the district court should address mootness/standing early and, if it concludes the penalties claim survives, proceed to resolve it on the merits. If it concludes that the claim is moot, the court should dismiss for lack of jurisdiction.

A final implication concerns plaintiffs’ strategy. If monetary relief has been obtained by payment, plaintiffs should consider whether injunctive relief is viable and whether they have evidence showing the defendant’s policy remains. Conversely, if the defendant truly rescinds the policy and makes it impossible to resume (for instance, by changing corporate operations in irreversible ways), plaintiffs should expect an uphill battle to press penalties that go to the state.

Conclusion

Cohen v. Consilio underscores that statutory allocation of remedies matters for Article III jurisdiction. Although civil penalties collected by the government do not automatically strip private plaintiffs of standing, plaintiffs seeking such penalties must show the penalties would likely redress a concrete injury—normally by deterring or ending an ongoing or likely-to-recur violative practice. Defendants cannot avoid review merely by voluntarily ceasing conduct; they must demonstrate it is “absolutely clear” the wrongful behavior will not recur. District courts should decide these jurisdictional questions on a developed factual record before proceeding to the merits. The Cohen remand is a practical example of this approach: the court sent the case back to resolve whether Consilio’s policy remained or could reasonably be expected to recur, because that factual finding is decisive to whether an employee retains a live, redressable claim for MFLSA penalties that flow to the Commissioner.

# MWTA Notice Duties and Private Remedies

This chapter examines the notice duties imposed by the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) and what relief is available to an employee when an employer fails to comply. We use Cohen v. Consilio, LLC as a concrete illustration of the limits of private enforcement under the MWTA, the interaction between § 181.032 and the private‑enforcement provision § 181.171, the kinds of relief a plaintiff may realistically seek for notice violations, evidentiary and proof problems that frequently arise, and the appellate consequence of underdeveloped argument. The controlling appellate opinion in Cohen is useful because it bundles statutory interpretation, mootness/standing considerations, and procedural law about waiver into a single posture: the employee alleged failure to give required notice of a change to an overtime policy, but he (1) had been paid the wages he claimed, (2) pointed to no statutory provision in § 181.032 authorizing civil penalties payable to an employee, and (3) did not adequately develop the claim on appeal.

## MWTA § 181.032(d)(4) and (f): initial and change‑in‑terms notice requirements

The MWTA contains provisions that require employers to provide certain information to employees about the terms of their employment. The Cohen opinion focuses on two subsections of § 181.032—subdivisions (d)(4) and (f)—that govern notice obligations. Cohen’s claim alleged that Consilio failed to provide the statutorily required notice when it changed its overtime premium policy. The appellate court’s treatment of this claim is framed by two interpretive premises: (1) what the statute requires is a question of statutory text and purpose, and (2) a violation of a notice duty leads to civil enforcement only to the extent the statutory scheme supplies a private remedy.

Although the opinion itself does not quote the full statutory language, the relevant architectural features of § 181.032 are straightforward for present purposes. Subsection (d)(4) addresses the content of required notice (for example, what terms must be disclosed), while subsection (f) deals with notice obligations triggered by changes in terms—i.e., the employer must give employees notice when certain employment terms change. The policy rationale behind these provisions is equally plain: the statute is designed to ensure that employees have actual knowledge, in writing, of the pay terms and any material changes so they can vindicate wage rights and avoid surprise deductions or rate changes.

What Cohen illustrates practically is this: an employee who contends an employer changed the terms of pay or overtime rules without providing the statutorily required notice must press three things at minimum. First, identify exactly which statutory subsection the employer violated (delineating whether the failure was in the initial notice required by subsection (d) or in the notice-of-change rule in subsection (f)). Second, show the facts establishing both the change and the absence of adequate notice—dates, communications (or lack of them), written policies, and contemporaneous employer statements. Third, articulate the remedy the statute allows for that particular violation. The last element is crucial because the MWTA’s remedial framework does not automatically supply every kind of relief for every violation.

## Private enforcement via § 181.171: damages and “other appropriate relief,” but no civil penalties absent an express provision in § 181.032

Section 181.171 is the MWTA’s private‑enforcement provision. It authorizes an employee to “bring a civil action seeking redress” for violations of the specified sections of the chapter, and it provides that an employer “found to have violated the above sections shall also be liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief including but not limited to injunctive relief.” At first glance § 181.171 looks capacious: it permits lawsuits for violations of the MWTA and ordains compensatory damages and “other appropriate relief.” But Cohen demonstrates an important limiting principle embedded in the statutory text and confirmed by textualist canons of construction: civil penalties are available to an employee only where the specific section an employee invokes itself provides for such penalties.

In Cohen, the plaintiff sought monetary penalties for the alleged § 181.032 notice violation. The appellate court rejected that request because § 181.032 does not itself provide for civil penalties payable to an employee. The court contrasted § 181.032 with other MWTA provisions that do provide for penalties or that empower the Commissioner of Labor and Industry to assess and collect penalties (for example, § 181.101 in the opinion was examined as a provision where the Commissioner—not the private plaintiff—may demand and collect an “average daily wage” penalty after a ten‑day demand). The appellate court therefore read § 181.171 in context: it gives employees access to the courts and to equitable relief and compensatory damages, but it does not create a free‑floating statutory penalty that can be applied whenever any MWTA provision is violated.

This text‑sensitive approach flows from two related interpretive practices. First, remedial statutes are read against their text: when the legislature indicates on the face of a particular provision that a penalty is available, courts give effect to that grant; when a provision is silent, courts do not award penalties as a matter of judicial supplementation. Second, the presence of express penalty mechanisms in nearby statutory text signals that the legislature knows how to provide penalties when it wants to; absence of such language in § 181.032 suggests omission, not ambiguity.

For litigators and students: if your theory of relief depends on a statutory penalty under the MWTA, you must identify the subsection that authorizes the penalty and explain why that subsection applies. If the subsection is silent, § 181.171 will not magically create a statutory fine payable to the employee; rather, it will most reliably support claims for compensatory damages and injunctive relief.

## What damages might be recoverable for notice violations and proof challenges

When an employer violates a notice requirement under § 181.032, the universe of feasible remedies depends on (1) whether the MWTA substrate supplies a penalty, (2) whether the plaintiff suffered any compensable harm from the lack of notice, and (3) whether equitable relief (e.g., injunctive relief) is appropriate and available.

Compensatory damages. Section 181.171 makes compensatory damages available for violations of covered sections. In a notice‑violation case compensatory damages will typically be limited to actual, provable losses caused by the lack of notice. Examples may include: wages lost because the employee did not receive the overtime rate he or she would have obtained had the change been properly disclosed; lost business or placement opportunities caused by confusion about pay terms; or other out‑of‑pocket losses directly attributable to the employer’s failure to provide statutorily required information. Courts will expect concrete proof tying the lack of notice to these losses—time sheets, payroll records, depositions, contemporaneous communications, and other documentary evidence.

Consequential and non‑pecuniary harms. Plaintiffs sometimes seek compensation for consequential harms (e.g., emotional distress, reputational harm) allegedly flowing from a statutory violation. Whether such harms are recoverable under § 181.171 is an issue of statutory interpretation and proof. Because § 181.171 expressly provides for “compensatory damages,” plaintiffs should be prepared to show these damages fit within customary remedial categories and were proximately caused by the statutory violation. Absent a legislative indication that the statute contemplates non‑economic harm, courts may be reluctant to award such damages for a notice violation alone.

Injunctive and declaratory relief. Section 181.171 expressly authorizes “other appropriate relief including but not limited to injunctive relief.” Injunctive relief is particularly apt where the employer’s unlawful practice is ongoing (e.g., the employer continues to apply a changed overtime policy without giving notice). To obtain an injunction, an employee must show ongoing or imminent future injury and that legal remedies would be inadequate. This is why the continuing existence of the challenged policy is a recurring theme in the Cohen opinion: injunctive relief and many types of equitable redress turn on prospective risk, not past injury.

Attorney’s fees and costs. Whether attorney’s fees and costs are recoverable will depend on the statutory text and other Minnesota law. Section 181.171 does not by its terms specify an attorney‑fee award, but courts sometimes award fees under fee‑shifting provisions elsewhere or under common law equitable principles; in practice, plaintiffs should identify a statutory or contractual basis for fees if they seek them.

Practical and evidentiary challenges. Notice‑failure claims often founder on proof. Cohen illustrates two common proof problems:

- Mootness/paid wages. If the employer pays the wages the plaintiff sought before trial—or the parties stipulate that the plaintiff has been compensated—the plaintiff may lack a live injury for damages, and the claim may be dismissed as moot unless the plaintiff can show a reasonable expectation of recurrence or seek injunctive relief for prospective harms. The Cohen court explicitly observed that because Cohen had been paid all overtime wages due and liquidated damages under the MFLSA, his ability to seek penalties or other relief depended on whether the employer’s violative policy still existed and whether penalties were statutorily available. A litigant who has received payment must explain why supplemental relief remains necessary—e.g., the policy persists and other employees remain unremedied, or the statutory text provides for penalties irrespective of payment.

- Causation and quantification. Even when a plaintiff proves lack of notice, she must prove that the lack of notice caused a quantifiable loss. That proof requires attention to payroll records, communications announcing the change, the timing of payroll adjustments, and witness testimony. Courts will scrutinize corporate declarations about policy and compliance, and evidence that a notice was posted or sent will undermine a claim. Factual disputes about whether notice actually occurred frequently preclude summary judgment; a well‑developed evidentiary record—document requests targeted to internal communications, deposition testimony about form and distribution of notices, and contemporaneous employee complaints—helps the plaintiff survive summary judgment.

- Class certification considerations. Notice claims are often presented on a class basis. To succeed, plaintiffs must show commonality and predominance: that the notice practice was uniform and that damages or equitable relief can be determined class‑wide. If notice practices varied among offices or employee cohorts, class treatment may be denied.

- Timing and statutes of limitations. As with other wage‑and‑hour claims, plaintiffs must be mindful of limitations periods. The correct accrual date—when the alleged lack of notice yielded harm—can be contested.

In short, the recoverable damages for a § 181.032 notice violation are not limitless. They focus on making the employee whole for actual losses and on stopping ongoing noncompliance through injunctive relief when appropriate. Plaintiffs who cannot show ongoing violation or measurable loss face substantial hurdles.

## Cohen’s waiver on appeal for lack of developed argument and authority

Cohen’s MWTA claim was also affected by appellate‑procedure doctrine. The Eighth Circuit emphasized that Cohen “point[ed] to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute,” and that he failed to develop a meaningful argument in his briefs and district‑court filings for reversal on the MWTA claim. The court therefore declined to disturb the district court’s dismissal, citing precedent that a party waives an appellate argument by failing to develop it.

Several instructive principles emerge from the opinion about appellate advocacy and procedural preservation:

- Develop the statutory basis for relief in the district court. When your claim depends on an interpretation of statutory text—especially a claim that seeks a form of relief (such as civil penalties) that the statute does not obviously provide—present that textual argument with citations in the district court. Offer alternative readings, legislative history if relevant, and authorities interpreting adjacent or similar provisions.

- Preserve remedies and plead them clearly. If you later seek to expand the relief you request (for example, seeking rescission of a policy in addition to damages), do so through an amended complaint or by obtaining leave to amend. As the opinion notes, new claims raised only in briefing are not properly before the court.

- On appeal, brief the statutory and procedural rules fully. Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 28(a)(9) and the decisional law cited by the Eighth Circuit require that an appellant present argument and authorities supporting each claim of error. A bare assertion that the district court was wrong—with no statutory citation, no analytical chain, and no on‑point authority—is liable to be treated as waived.

- Use precedent to show a remedy’s provenance. The Cohen court contrasted § 181.032 with § 181.101 and other MWTA provisions that explicitly provide for penalties or that empower the Commissioner. Where the statute expressly authorizes relief, point to that text; where the statute is silent, be ready to explain why a court should nonetheless infer a private penalty remedy.

Two appellate decisions invoked in the opinion (Milligan and InfoDeli) reinforce the point: courts will not manufacture legal argument for litigants, and failure to adequately brief an issue is a forfeiture of that issue on appeal. The lesson for practitioners is blunt but indispensable: an argument not developed in the record and briefs is an argument lost.

## Practical takeaways

- Identify the precise subsection you claim the employer violated. Don’t rely on generalities. The structure of the MWTA matters because some subsections provide specific enforcement mechanisms while others do not.

- If seeking a penalty, show the statute actually authorizes that penalty to be awarded to a private plaintiff. § 181.171 opens the courthouse door, but it does not necessarily supply every form of relief—civil penalties are the exception, not the rule, absent textual authorization.

- Build a factual record that links the lack of notice to measurable harm, and preserve evidence of ongoing policy practices if you aim for injunctive relief. Stipulations that the plaintiff has been paid reduce leverage and create mootness and standing issues.

- Preserve claims and remedies in pleadings and district court briefing. If you intend to seek novel relief (e.g., rescission or a specific statutory penalty), plead it and argue it plainly and with authority. If you fail to develop the argument, appellate courts will likely treat it as waived.

- Consider administrative avenues as well as private suits. Some MWTA penalty schemes (or related wage statutes) vest enforcement power in the Commissioner of Labor and Industry; when statutory penalties are available only to the Commissioner, private plaintiffs may obtain relief only in the form of compensatory damages and equitable remedies unless the legislature provides otherwise.

Cohen v. Consilio, LLC is therefore a cautionary tale. The plaintiff alleged a failure to provide statutorily required notice of a change in an overtime policy, but he had been paid the wages he claimed, the subsection he relied on did not itself provide civil penalties payable to an employee, and he failed to develop an appellate argument grounded in statutory text and authority. The Eighth Circuit affirmed dismissal of the MWTA notice claim—underscoring that successful MWTA litigation requires attention to statutory detail, evidentiary discipline, and careful preservation of remedial arguments.

# Equitable Relief and Pleading Strategy

This chapter examines the availability and tactical necessities of seeking injunctive (prospective, equitable) relief in Minnesota wage-and-hour litigation, drawing directly on the lessons of Cohen v. Consilio, LLC. The opinion illustrates three recurring themes for practitioners and students: (1) statutory authorization and the scope of “other appropriate relief” (including injunctions); (2) the mechanics and limits of pleading and amendment under the Federal Rules, and the consequence of trying to introduce new claims or forms of relief in opposition briefs; and (3) how equitable relief intersects with Article III standing and mootness doctrine in wage-and-hour suits where an employer pays back wages before judgment. The following sections synthesize the doctrinal rules, explain the tests a court will apply, and translate those rules into concrete litigation practice.

## Availability of injunctive relief as “other appropriate relief” under § 181.171 and under the MFLSA

Statutory authorization is the first question. A court can only order equitable relief when a statute or the court’s equitable power permits it. Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) contains an express catchall for equitable remedies: § 181.171, subdivision 1, allows a “person” to bring a civil action for violations of certain wage statutes and makes an employer “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief including but not limited to injunctive relief.” That language is broad: it recognizes that, in addition to back pay and statutory penalties, a court may fashion injunctive relief to stop ongoing or threatened violations. In Cohen, the Eighth Circuit relied on that provision to explain that injunctive relief is within the statutory remedial palette for MPWA claims.

The MFLSA (Minnesota’s state analogue to the federal FLSA, cited in the Cohen opinion as sections of Chapter 177) is less explicit in the opinion about express injunctive language, but it is well settled that courts retain equitable powers to enjoin ongoing statutory violations where a statute provides a private right of action. For federal FLSA claims, courts routinely grant preliminary and permanent injunctions to prevent ongoing wage violations because the statute authorizes judicial enforcement of minimum-wage and overtime obligations and because equitable relief is necessary to prevent future harm. The same equitable principles apply to state wage statutes that create private causes of action for wage violations: if the statute permits judicial enforcement, a court may generally grant forward-looking relief where appropriate.

Even when a statute authorizes injunctive relief in principle, courts will not grant it automatically. Traditional equitable prerequisites apply: the plaintiff must show (1) an injury that is actual or imminent and likely to continue absent relief (which supports both Article III standing and the equitable requirement of irreparable harm), (2) that legal remedies (e.g., money damages) are inadequate to remedy the ongoing wrong, (3) a likelihood of success on the merits (for preliminary injunctions) or that the equities and public interest favor relief (for permanent injunctions), and (4) that the requested remedy is suitably tailored and not overbroad. The Eighth Circuit’s opinion in Cohen implicitly invokes these principles when it emphasizes the need for continuing injury to justify prospective relief and when it vacates judgment on the MFLSA penalties claim because the record did not make clear whether the allegedly unlawful policy remained in effect.

Practice point: When seeking injunctive relief under § 181.171 (or under the MFLSA), a plaintiff should plead both the statutory basis for equitable relief and facts showing that the employer has an ongoing policy, practice, or intent to continue noncompliance. Allegations that an employer applied a written policy eliminating overtime premiums, and that the policy remains in effect, are the core factual predicates courts look for to justify prospective relief.

## Pleading specific injunctive relief and amending claims; Rule 15(a) mechanics

Pleading specificity matters. Rule 8 requires a short and plain statement of the claim showing entitlement to relief, and Rule 54(c) limits a judgment to the relief demanded in the pleadings unless the opposing party has had fair notice and an opportunity to contest. If a plaintiff ultimately wants a court order that a defendant rescind or change an employment policy, that request should appear in the pleadings (and be described with adequate factual allegations).

Cohen’s case demonstrates the cost of failing to do so. The complaint sought certain forms of injunctive relief, but the plaintiff later attempted to “change course” in his summary judgment briefing by asking for a different form of injunctive relief—specifically, an order directing Consilio to rescind its 2019 overtime policy. The district court did not consider that new request, and the Eighth Circuit held that the issue was “not properly before” the court on appeal. That outcome flows from bedrock pleading rules: equitable remedies that were not pleaded and litigated below are generally forfeited.

If circumstances change mid-case—if, for example, a plaintiff learns new facts suggesting a different or more precise injunctive remedy is required—the correct procedural path is to move to amend the complaint under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a). Rule 15(a) instructs that leave to amend “shall be freely given when justice so requires,” and courts apply a liberal standard. But leave is not automatic: courts may deny amendment for reasons such as undue delay, bad faith, repeated failure to cure deficiencies, prejudice to the opposing party, or futility (i.e., amendment could not withstand a Rule 12(b)(6) motion).

Putting Rule 15(a) into practice requires tactical awareness. A motion to amend should be filed promptly after the need for amendment becomes apparent; it should include a copy of the proposed amended pleading and explain why amendment is timely and not prejudicial. When seeking to add an injunctive claim, a plaintiff should attach supporting affidavits or evidence showing the continuing nature of the violation and should explain why the remedy being added was not included earlier (for instance, by describing new facts learned through discovery). Motions to amend filed at the summary judgment stage after discovery is closed are more vulnerable to denial on grounds of delay or prejudice.

Practice point: Never rely on summary judgment briefing as a vehicle to add a new form of relief. If the relief was not pleaded, move to amend the complaint under Rule 15(a) as soon as the need is identified; ensure the amended complaint pleads the legal basis and the facts necessary to support injunctive relief (ongoing policy, threatened future violations, irreparable harm, etc.).

## Why new claims cannot be raised in opposition briefs (InfoDeli) and preservation on appeal

The Eighth Circuit’s opinion cites InfoDeli, LLC v. W. Robidoux, Inc. to reinforce a bright-line principle: new claims or new causes of action cannot be raised for the first time in an opposition brief. Appellate courts are especially reluctant to consider arguments or claims that were neither pleaded nor litigated in the district court. That doctrine protects both fairness and judicial efficiency: the defendant must have timely notice and an opportunity to answer, conduct discovery, and develop defenses to any new claim.

Preservation for appeal requires that the issue be presented to the district court and ruled upon. If a party attempts to introduce a new request for relief only in opposition to summary judgment, the district court can—and typically will—decline to consider it. The appellate court then generally declines to entertain the belatedly asserted claim. The Eighth Circuit in Cohen expressly applied this rule to decline reviewing the unpleaded request for an order rescinding Consilio’s policy.

To preserve a right to appellate review of an injunction claim, litigants should (1) plead the claim in the complaint, (2) present the claim in the pretrial and summary judgment phases, and (3) litigate it in the district court if the defendant contests it. If new facts or law justify changing course later in the litigation, a party should seek leave to amend under Rule 15(a) and, if necessary, move the district court for appropriate interim relief (e.g., a preliminary injunction) to crystalize the dispute. Only by preserving an issue in the district court record can a party ensure it will be reviewable on appeal.

Practice point: When drafting complaint and pretrial documents, include alternative forms of relief. If you might seek rescission of a policy, ask for it in the complaint (even if phrased as an alternative form of injunctive relief). That prevents “gotcha” forfeitures at summary judgment and preserves appellate rights.

## Interaction between injunctive relief and mootness/redressability in wage-and-hour cases

In suits for unpaid wages, mootness and redressability frequently determine whether a court can award prospective relief. Article III requires a live case or controversy at all stages of litigation: the party invoking federal jurisdiction must possess standing at the outset and throughout the case. The Eighth Circuit reemphasized this principle in Cohen: even where an employer has paid back wages and liquidated damages, a plaintiff’s claim for statutory penalties or injunctive relief may survive if the plaintiff can show a continuing risk of future harm—e.g., the employer’s policy remains in place and could cause future violations.

Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw sets the controlling standard: a plaintiff’s claim becomes moot only if “subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.” Voluntary cessation of the challenged practice by a defendant does not alone render a case moot; the plaintiff can continue to seek injunctive relief if the record supports a reasonable expectation of recurrence or if the defendant’s conduct is not completely and unequivocally disavowed. Conversely, when the only injury is historic and the defendant’s cessation of conduct (or complete correction) removes any reasonable expectation of recurrence, the claim may become moot.

In Cohen, Consilio paid the overtime and liquidated damages the plaintiff initially sought. That payment eliminated the plaintiff’s individual claim for past money damages. The Eighth Circuit nonetheless explained that the MFLSA penalties claim might survive if the employer’s unlawful policy remained in force, because penalties and injunctive relief can serve a prospective deterrent function and protect class members and future employees. Because the record left open whether the policy remained, the appellate court vacated and remanded the district court’s ruling on the MFLSA claim so that the district court could resolve mootness/jurisdiction.

Two lessons follow. First, plaintiffs seeking injunctive relief must plead and preserve facts showing ongoing or threatened conduct by the employer; otherwise, the claim risks mootness once the employer pays money or otherwise changes conduct. Second, plaintiffs should seek interim relief early—if the plaintiff waits until after a defendant has remedied the harm (or paid back wages), the court may find no live controversy remains.

Practice point: To protect a claim for injunctive relief after an employer pays back wages, preserve the claim by (1) seeking discovery into the employer’s current policies and practices, (2) obtaining affidavits or admissions regarding whether the policy remains in force, and (3) moving promptly for preliminary relief if necessary. If the employer does not retreat unequivocally from the policy, that evidence supports redressability and resists mootness arguments.

## Practical litigation roadmap and tactical checklist

1. Plead broadly but specifically. In the complaint, plead the statutory bases (MPWA § 181.171 and any MFLSA provisions), request “other appropriate relief including injunctive relief,” and include a specific, alternate request (e.g., an order rescinding a particular policy or requiring notice to employees). Describe the employer’s written policy or practice with dates, communications, and examples so the record will support a claim that the policy is ongoing.

2. Early discovery and evidence. Seek expedited discovery into whether the challenged policy remains in effect. Collect company emails, policy documents, and admissions. If the employer asserts it has ceased the practice, get contemporaneous evidence of the cessation and any efforts to communicate the change to employees.

3. Move to amend when necessary. If the need for a different or more specific injunction becomes apparent after filing (for example, after receiving documents in discovery), move to amend under Rule 15(a) promptly and attach the proposed amended complaint and factual support.

4. Avoid raising new claims in opposition briefs. If you have not pleaded an injunction or rescission claim, do not rely on summary judgment briefing to add it. Motions in limine, discovery motions, and amendments are the proper vehicles.

5. Preserve issues for appeal. Make timely, concrete objections to any district court rulings you intend to challenge. If the district court refuses to consider a late-blooming injunction request, make record of the refusal and the underlying reasons so appellate courts can determine preservation and harmlessness.

6. Be mindful of standing and mootness. Prepare evidence showing continuing injury and risk of recurrence. If the defendant pays back wages, be prepared to show why prospective relief still redresses a live injury (e.g., policy unchanged, company statements indicating intent to reapply policy, or systemic conduct affecting other employees).

7. Tailor remedies to statutory lines. Understand whether the statute you invoke provides penalties payable to the state (Commissioner) or to employees, and draft remedies accordingly. Injunctive relief is equitable and distinct from penalties; even when penalties are payable to the state, an injunction can still be appropriate to prevent future violations.

Conclusion

Cohen v. Consilio illustrates how statutory remedial language, pleading choices, and Article III limits interact in wage-and-hour litigation. Minnesota’s MPWA expressly contemplates “other appropriate relief including but not limited to injunctive relief,” but plaintiffs cannot obtain such relief unless they properly plead it, timely move to amend when needed, and maintain a live controversy by showing a continuing injury or a real threat of future violations. Practitioners should draft complaints with foresight, conduct prompt discovery on ongoing policies, move to amend under Rule 15(a) when the need arises, and avoid the temptation to present new claims only in opposition briefs. Doing so not only preserves equitable claims for decision on the merits but also respects the jurisdictional and procedural safeguards that protect defendants and the judicial process.

# Payments Mid-Litigation, Stipulations, and the ‘Pick-Off’ Problem

This chapter examines the legal landscape that arises when an employer, faced with a wage-and-hour suit, pays the plaintiff some or all of the money the plaintiff seeks before the case reaches a merits decision or class certification. Using Cohen v. Consilio, LLC (8th Cir. 2025) as the controlling example, we analyze how such mid‑litigation payments and stipulations interact with standing and mootness doctrine, the special rules that attend class and collective actions (and the so‑called “pick‑off” problem), and practical drafting and timing strategies for preserving unresolved issues (penalties, injunctive relief, and jurisdictional facts). The goal is to give a doctrinal map a practitioner or student can use to reason about new fact patterns and to draft litigation documents that protect client interests.

## The basic legal architecture: standing, mootness, and what payments do

Two constitutional principles drive most disputes when a defendant pays (or offers to pay) the plaintiff’s monetary demand mid‑suit: Article III standing and mootness. Standing requires an injury in fact that is traceable to the defendant and redressable by a favorable decision. Mootness requires that that injury persist for the life of the litigation; if subsequent events “make it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur,” the claim is moot. Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 189–92 (2000). Both doctrines are demandingly factual and depend on whether the plaintiff retains a sufficiently concrete and ongoing interest in the case after payments occur.

When a defendant pays the full amount of a plaintiff’s asserted monetary claim and that payment is accepted (for example, by stipulation or entry of judgment), the plaintiff’s injury tends to disappear and the Article III case‑or‑controversy generally ends as to those monetary claims. That is because redressability is exhausted: the court cannot give the plaintiff any additional monetary relief for that claim. The situation is different if other forms of relief remain (a live request for injunctive relief, statutory penalties payable to the government, or attorney’s fees that the plaintiff seeks to shift), or if collateral consequences persist that a court can remedy. The Supreme Court and the Eighth Circuit have recognized these nuanced distinctions; Cohen v. Consilio exemplifies them.

Cohen illustrates several permutations. Consilio paid back wages and liquidated damages to Cohen and other employees, and the parties stipulated that Cohen had been paid all overtime and liquidated damages he sought under the MFLSA. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the dismissal of Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) penalties in part because those penalties, under Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a), are recoverable by the Commissioner of Labor and Industry, not directly by the employee. The court vacated and remanded the district court’s summary judgment on MFLSA statutory penalties because (a) the stipulation meant the individual monetary claim (wages and liquidated damages) was satisfied, but (b) the continuing existence (or cessation) of Consilio’s overtime policy—and therefore the possibility of recurrence—was contested. Because the record did not make clear whether the offending policy remained in effect, the viability of a penalties claim under the MFLSA (and thus the court’s jurisdiction over that claim) required further factual development.

Two doctrinal points follow from Cohen and from principle:

- A defendant’s payment of the individual plaintiff’s monetary demand will often moot the plaintiff’s individual damage claims if the plaintiff accepts the payment or obtains a judgment disposing of that relief. That extinguishes the plaintiff’s injury insofar as it sought only those damages.

- But payment does not necessarily moot claims for other forms of relief that can still redress a continuing injury or have deterrent value—most notably injunctive relief or statutory penalties intended to deter future misconduct (and criminal or administrative sanctions). When those forms of relief could alter the defendant’s future conduct or the plaintiff’s future legal status, the case may remain live even after payment. Friends of the Earth holds that plaintiffs may have standing to seek such relief because civil penalties and other public remedies can be understood as “redress” insofar as they work to stop ongoing violations and deter future ones.

Cohen’s vacatur on the MFLSA penalty issue turned on that second point: payments resolved the private monetary claims, but if the employer’s challenged overtime policy remains in place, the plaintiff retains a concrete interest in securing penalties or injunctive relief to deter or stop that policy.

## Distinguishing Rule 68 and Campbell‑Ewald pick‑off issues from state wage statutes

Two distinct but related doctrinal threads arise in this area: (1) the fate of a case when the defendant makes an offer of judgment under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 68, or otherwise attempts to satisfy the plaintiff’s claim; and (2) the separate analysis required where a statute allocates penalty‑collection authority to a government official rather than to the private plaintiff.

Rule 68, offers of judgment, and Campbell‑Ewald. Rule 68 lets a defendant serve a formal offer of judgment for a fixed amount; if the plaintiff accepts, judgment follows and the case ends. Two Supreme Court cases shape modern analysis.

- Genesis Healthcare Group v. Symczyk, 569 U.S. 66 (2013), addressed whether an unaccepted settlement offer might moot an FLSA collective action. The Court suggested that an unaccepted offer that satisfies an individual claim could moot that claim but left open some complexities because the FLSA uses opt‑in collective actions rather than Rule 23 class procedures.

- Campbell‑Ewald Co. v. Gomez, 577 U.S. 153 (2016), clarified that an unaccepted settlement offer does not moot a plaintiff’s individual claim: a defendant’s offer that the plaintiff does not accept does not change the plaintiff’s legal status, so the plaintiff still has a live claim. But an accepted Rule 68 offer that becomes a judgment can end the litigation as to the individual plaintiff.

Put simply: an offer alone will usually not moot a claim; payment accepted by the plaintiff or entry of judgment under Rule 68 normally will.

Relating Campbell‑Ewald to pick‑off dynamics. The “pick‑off” is the practice in putative class or collective litigation where a defendant pays the named plaintiff(s) so as to defeat certification and end the case before classwide relief is considered. Courts have treated pick‑off as potentially abusive; how courts treat it turns on whether the plaintiff’s individual claims are truly resolved and whether class procedures are still viable. Campbell‑Ewald constrained defendants who think an unaccepted offer will moot the case, but it did not immunize aggressive pay‑and‑dismiss tactics when plaintiffs accept payment or stipulate to dismissal. Thus the pick‑off problem has an evolving doctrinal landscape: some circuits have recognized that strategic payments to named plaintiffs can be barred by tolling or equitable doctrines when the payments are designed solely to prevent class certification; others permit resolution of individual claims if the plaintiff genuinely accepts a full remedy.

State statutes and penalties payable to government officials. A separate wrinkle arises when state law assigns the authority to seek certain statutory penalties exclusively to an administrative agency or state official. In Cohen, Minnesota’s MPWA (Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a)) authorizes the Commissioner of Labor and Industry, after serving a demand for unpaid wages and waiting ten days, to “charge and collect” wages and “a penalty in the amount of the employee’s average daily earnings” for each day the employer fails to pay. The statute explicitly contemplates that “money collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned.” Crucially, the statutory mechanism conditions the commissioner’s ability to collect penalties upon the commissioner’s service of a demand. The Eighth Circuit concluded that under this scheme only the Commissioner may seek the average daily wage penalties set out in § 181.101(a); an individual employee does not have an independent statutory right to obtain those penalties directly through a private civil action in district court.

Practical significance: Where the statute vests the right to seek certain penalties in a public official (not the private litigant), a defendant’s payment to the private litigant may remove the private litigant’s personal stake while leaving the government’s independent power intact. That may mean the private litigant has no statutory claim for those particular penalties; their availability depends on the official’s actions. Conversely, if the statute permits private recovery of penalties, a defendant’s payment to the plaintiff may eliminate the plaintiff’s injury unless some other form of redress remains (injunctive relief, ongoing violation, attorney’s fees, etc.). Cohen demonstrates both. For its MPWA penalty claim the court relied on the statute’s terms, not on a broad federal mootness doctrine; because Minnesota law reserved the right to collect average‑daily‑wage penalties to the Commissioner, the plaintiff could not maintain a private suit for those penalties, and the district court’s disposition on that point was affirmed.

## Payments and the continuing controversy requirement: voluntary cessation and collateral consequences

A defendant’s voluntary cessation of the challenged conduct does not automatically moot a claim. The test is whether “subsequent events made it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.” Friends of the Earth, 528 U.S. at 189. This is a demanding standard: the defendant bears a heavy burden to show that cessation is permanent and unlikely to recur.

Where a defendant pays money to the plaintiff but the defendant’s underlying policy or practice remains in place, courts will often find a continuing controversy as to injunctive relief or penalties because the plaintiff can plausibly show a realistic risk of recurrence. That was central to the Eighth Circuit’s remand in Cohen: because the record did not clarify whether Consilio had withdrawn or changed its overtime policy, the court could not conclude that the MFLSA penalties claim was moot.

Collateral consequences: Even if a monetary claim is satisfied, the plaintiff may remain injured by collateral consequences linked to the litigation—such as having been subjected to an illegal policy, reputational harm, or being forced to expend time and money in litigation. Courts will sometimes treat such consequences as sufficient to preserve a live controversy, although plaintiffs must show a concrete and particularized injury to survive mootness. The Supreme Court has been attentive to the need for more than an abstract interest: courts will not retain jurisdiction to simply issue an advisory opinion about the legality of past conduct absent a live, ongoing injury.

In practice, an employer trying to moot a case by paying off an individual should recognize that lingering policies or practices, or credible threats of recurrence, preserve the plaintiff’s stake. A plaintiff seeking to preserve jurisdiction can and should gather evidence about whether the defendant’s policy remains, seek discovery targeted at the policy’s status, and press any prospective injunctive relief claims quickly.

## The pick‑off problem in class and collective actions: differences and doctrinal responses

The pick‑off problem is most acute in representative litigation—Rule 23 class actions and FLSA collective actions—because a defendant can defeat class or collective certification simply by resolving the named plaintiff’s claims before certification is granted. The doctrinal responses differ across procedural regimes and circuits.

Rule 23 class actions. For Rule 23 suits, the named plaintiff’s personal claim may be extinguished by acceptance of payment or entry of judgment, but the class can still be certified if other putative class members (who remain unnamed or unjoined) seek to pursue the same claims and the litigation posture permits it. A defendant’s payment to the named plaintiff does not automatically kill a putative Rule 23 class if other prospective members’ claims still present common questions and the requirements of Rule 23 can be satisfied. Courts are alert to gamesmanship: some have used equitable estoppel or tolling doctrines to bar defendants from policing their way out of class litigation by repeated pick‑offs, and some have concluded that defendants who engage repeatedly in this practice must show more to support mootness.

FLSA collective actions (opt‑in). FLSA actions are different because they are opt‑in rather than opt‑out: only those who affirmatively opt into the collective are bound. Genesis Healthcare v. Symczyk, 569 U.S. 66 (2013), held that a single plaintiff’s claim can become moot if an unaccepted settlement offer made the plaintiff whole and there are no further class members who have opted in or shown a desire to opt in; if the case is moot as to the lone plaintiff and no one else is a representative plaintiff, there is nothing left to litigate. Campbell‑Ewald, however, clarified that an unaccepted offer does not moot the claim. The net effect is that defendants cannot simply serve an offer of judgment and claim mootness until and unless the offer is accepted (or judgment is entered). But defendants can still moot a case if they pay and that payment is accepted or the plaintiff obtains a judgment. That allows pick‑off sometimes: payment accepted by the named plaintiff ends the individual’s claim; if no other plaintiff has opted in or the court declines to allow another named representative, the collective may be defeated.

Courts, aware of the pick‑off tactic’s potential for unfairness, have developed guardrails: early notification and opt‑in mechanisms, expedited conditional certification briefing under the FLSA, and skepticism toward defendants’ post hoc cancellations of policies that are not credible or permanent. Still, the doctrinal landscape is uneven among jurisdictions.

Practical lesson for plaintiffs. Given the risks that pay‑and‑dismiss tactics pose to collective or class litigation, plaintiffs’ counsel should be aggressive about early conditional certification or class certification motions, seek discovery about the policy’s scope and continued operation, and avoid voluntarily accepting full payments that might moot their representative role without preserving the ability to continue pursuing classwide or collective relief. Where possible, counsel should insist on settlements that preserve class or collective claims or obtain court approval that binds absent class members—an approach that constrains defendants from denying class relief by “picking off” named plaintiffs.

## Drafting stipulations: preserving unresolved issues and jurisdictional facts

Stipulations are a double‑edged sword. They save time and expense, but poorly drafted stipulations can inadvertently strip the court of jurisdiction or waive critical claims. Cohen v. Consilio shows how stipulations that admit payment of wages and liquidated damages combined with an insufficient record on whether the offending policy remains can create jurisdictional uncertainty. Here are drafting principles and specific drafting language to consider.

Principles

- Be precise about what the plaintiff is stipulating to: limit stipulations to narrowly defined facts (e.g., “Defendant has paid Plaintiff $X in back wages for the specific period and liquidated damages under 29 U.S.C. § 216(b) for the hours in dispute”) and avoid global concessions about “all claims” unless that is the clear intent.

- Preserve legal claims you intend to pursue. If the plaintiff intends to keep claims for statutory penalties, injunctive relief, or attorneys’ fees alive, explicitly reserve those claims in any stipulation or agreement of dismissal. If a statutory penalty is payable only to a government official, acknowledge that the plaintiff cannot recover it personally but reserve the right to seek a judicial declaration or injunctive relief, and to request the court to retain jurisdiction to adjudicate whether the defendant’s policy remains in effect.

- Tie payments to releases carefully. If the defendant wants a release, negotiate defined releases that specify the person and claims released, and carve out public‑law claims, claims for penalties recoverable by the state or federal government, pending motions, and any claims that require court approval to release.

- Address the policy’s status explicitly. A stipulation that plaintiff has been paid but that defendant’s policy “has been rescinded” (or remains in effect) should be backed by factual admissions and, if possible, documentary confirmation. Words alone can be disputed later unless the parties create evidentiary predicates.

- Decide how to treat residual remedies: who gets attorney’s fees, costs, prejudgment interest, and whether the court retains jurisdiction to enforce the stipulation. If the plaintiff seeks injunctive relief, leave that claim unadjudicated and confirm that the court may retain jurisdiction to decide it.

Suggested drafting clauses

- Narrow payment clause: “Consilio represents and stipulates that, as of [date], it has paid to Plaintiff Cohen $3,225.21 in back pay and $462.00 in liquidated damages, which payments Cohen accepts as satisfaction of his individual claims for unpaid wages and liquidated damages through [date]. This stipulation is not an admission of liability and does not resolve any other claims in this action, including claims for statutory penalties, injunctive relief, declaratory relief, or attorney’s fees and costs.”

- Reservation clause: “The parties expressly reserve all claims and defenses relating to (a) statutory penalties recoverable by the Commissioner of Labor and Industry; (b) injunctive or declaratory relief; (c) class or collective certification; and (d) attorney’s fees, costs, and prejudgment interest. Nothing in this stipulation shall be construed to affect the Commissioner’s independent authority to pursue penalties under Minn. Stat. § 181.101.”

- Policy‑status clause: “Consilio further represents as of [date] that its overtime policy communicated in August 2019 has [been rescinded / remains in effect]. The parties agree this fact may be proven at summary judgment or trial and is not conclusively established by this stipulation.”

- Retention of jurisdiction: “The parties agree that the Court shall retain jurisdiction to adjudicate all preserved claims and to enforce this stipulation.”

Warning: courts sometimes treat stipulations to payment as tantamount to satisfaction. When that happens, the court may conclude the plaintiff lacks standing for purely monetary claims. Explicit reservation language helps, but it must be precise and, preferably, incorporated into a court order or judgment that explicitly preserves the plaintiff’s right to pursue the non‑monetary claims.

## Timing considerations relative to class certification and adequacy of representation

Timing is crucial in representative litigation. Because “the requisite personal interest that must exist at the commencement of the litigation (standing) must continue throughout its existence (mootness),” plaintiffs whose representative role evaporates before class certification may find classwide relief foreclosed. Sisney v. Kaemingk, 15 F.4th 1181, 1194 (8th Cir. 2021).

Tactical implications for plaintiffs

- Move early for conditional certification (FLSA) or class certification (Rule 23). Early certification or conditional certification can lock in the representative’s role and make a pick‑off less successful.

- Seek expedited discovery on policy status and on whether other potential class members exist. Facts about the policy’s geographic reach, duration, supervisory control, and pay practices are often dispositive on commonality, typicality, and predominance—so early discovery serves dual purposes.

- Avoid accepting payment that resolves the entire case unless the settlement explicitly preserves class or collective claims or binds absent class members by court approval. If considering settlement, require express releases limited to the individual’s claims with a carve‑out for class‑ or policy‑based litigation.

- Preserve adequate representation. When a named plaintiff accepts payment but seeks to remain as class representative, some courts question whether the plaintiff remains an adequate and typical representative. Plaintiffs should be ready to show that despite accepting payment for personal damages, they still pursue classwide issues such as injunctive relief or penalties and that their interests remain aligned with class members.

Tactical implications for defendants

- If the goal is to avoid protracted class litigation, defendants should recognize that pick‑off can backfire: courts may view repeated payments to named plaintiffs as gamesmanship, and some circuits have developed doctrines to counteract that strategy. Payment accepted by a plaintiff ends that plaintiff’s individual claim, but it may spur additional named plaintiffs to step forward or prompt the court to consolidate repeated claims.

- When defendants offer to pay, consider structured settlements that require court approval to avoid later jurisdictional fights, or offers that preserve a binding right to seek dismissal only with court approval. If a defendant genuinely wants to end litigation, it may prefer a Rule 68 judgment (if accepted) or a fully executed settlement agreement accompanied by a motion to dismiss that leaves no doubt about the dismissal’s scope.

## Practical checklists and hypotheticals

Checklist for plaintiffs when a defendant pays mid‑litigation

- Confirm payments in writing and limit any acceptance to the narrow monetary category paid (wages, liquidated damages).

- Immediately file a notice with the court describing what was paid and stating what claims are preserved.

- If class or collective relief is at issue, promptly move for conditional or class certification and for expedited discovery on the defendant’s policy.

- Preserve claims for statutory penalties, injunctive relief, attorney’s fees, and costs—expressly if possible.

- If the defendant asserts mootness, be prepared to show a continuing controversy (policy remains in effect, realistic risk of recurrence, collateral consequences, or the continuing public interest in deterring future violations).

Checklist for defendants considering payment

- Decide whether the goal is genuine resolution of the dispute or a tactical pick‑off. The latter risks later litigation and courts’ disfavor.

- If attempting to resolve the dispute, seek a comprehensive settlement and release (with court approval when necessary for class actions) rather than a simple pay‑and‑dismiss.

- Ensure any stipulation is precise about what is and is not resolved; obtain court approval of dismissals or judgments to avoid future battles over mootness.

Hypotheticals

1) Employer pays named plaintiff full back pay and liquidated damages but insists its unlawful scheduling policy remains in place. Plaintiff accepts payment and stipulates only to the payment. Has the case been mooted? Likely not as to claims for injunctive relief or statutory penalties if the policy remains or there is a realistic chance it will be reinstated. The plaintiff must show continuing injury or risk of recurrence to preserve the claim.

2) Employer serves an unaccepted Rule 68 offer for the full amount of the named plaintiff’s damages. Does the plaintiff’s case become moot? Under Campbell‑Ewald, no. An unaccepted offer does not change the plaintiff’s legal status; only acceptance or entry of judgment (or other events that eliminate the plaintiff’s injury) can moot the case.

3) State statute vests collection of a daily‑wage penalty exclusively in the labor commissioner, and the employer pays the plaintiff wages but the commissioner has not issued a demand. Can the private plaintiff recover that penalty in court? Cohen teaches: when the statute gives the commissioner exclusive authority to collect that penalty, the private plaintiff generally cannot obtain that particular penalty directly; the remedy is left to the administrative mechanism the statute specifies.

## Conclusion

Cohen v. Consilio underscores that mid‑litigation payments frequently complicate — but do not automatically resolve — wage litigation. Payment accepted by a plaintiff will often eliminate the plaintiff’s individual claims for monetary relief, but claims for statutory penalties, injunctive relief, and class or collective relief can survive under the right circumstances. Two factors are particularly decisive: (1) who the statute empowers to collect penalties (private plaintiff or government official), and (2) whether the defendant’s underlying policy remains or there is a realistic prospect of recurrence. Parties and courts must therefore attend carefully to the precise statutory text, the nature of the relief sought, and the factual status of the employer’s policies when payments occur mid‑litigation. Drafting precise stipulations, pursuing early certification and discovery, and understanding the ripples created by Rule 68 and Campbell‑Ewald are essential tools for navigating the pick‑off problem.

# Building the Record and Summary Judgment Practice

This chapter uses Cohen v. Consilio (8th Cir. 2025) as a vehicle to teach how lawyers should build an evidentiary record and frame briefing to survive or win summary judgment in wage-and-hour litigation—especially where statutory penalties, notice violations, and equitable relief are at stake. The opinion illustrates three recurring themes in employment-labor litigation: (1) the centrality of precise statutory text to determine who may recover what remedies; (2) the way stipulations and partial payments can narrow the live controversy and implicate mootness; and (3) the practical consequences of underdeveloped argumentation at the district court and appellate level. This chapter draws those lessons into concrete practice advice for litigators and students.

## Evidence of ongoing or ceased policies relevant to voluntary cessation and mootness

A plaintiff seeking statutory penalties or equitable relief must show a live controversy. When a defendant voluntarily changes or disavows an allegedly unlawful policy during litigation, courts analyze whether that change moots the controversy. Cohen shows how the analysis works: the employer had changed its overtime-pay practice in response to litigation and made payments, and the parties agreed Cohen had been paid the overtime and liquidated damages he claimed. The district court nevertheless resolved the statutory claims on the merits; the Eighth Circuit vacated the MFLSA ruling and remanded so the district court could decide whether the claim was moot.

Legal framework. The voluntary-cessation and mootness inquiry starts with Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw: a defendant’s voluntary cessation does not automatically moot a case unless it is “absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.” Courts therefore focus on the reality of the change and the likelihood of recurrence. The plaintiff has the burden of establishing standing and the need for relief; the defendant bears a heavy burden to show that cessation is genuine and durable. Related doctrines—capable-of-repetition-yet-evading-review and equitable principles—may also apply in the right circumstances, but are fact-specific.

What evidence matters—and why. When the status of a policy is dispositive, assemble documentary and testimonial proof that speaks directly to the ongoing character of the practice:

- The controlling text of the employer’s policy(ies): the operative written policy, employee handbook provisions, payroll policies, and any written revision or rescission. A one-page HR memo that purports to rescind prior policy is probative; ambiguous internal drafts are less so.

- Communications showing implementation or nonimplementation: emails to affected employees announcing change (with timestamps), pay stubs demonstrating the rate used, timesheets, payroll calculations, and billing records. In Cohen, the August 2019 email describing a “new premium overtime policy” was central to the dispute; comparable documentation can prove whether a change was actually implemented.

- Declarations from HR or payroll personnel under penalty of perjury addressing what the policy actually required and how payroll was executed after the change; include attachments to those declarations (copies of preserved emails, payroll runs).

- Evidence of practice across locations and time: proof whether the policy applies nationwide or only to certain offices; whether changes were piloted or temporary; records showing future-dated policy terms.

- Post-litigation conduct: whether the employer has sought to reinstate the policy, issued clarifying emails, or taken steps inconsistent with the claimed cessation (e.g., terminating an employee for failure to comply with the rescinded policy). Courts treat subsequent repetition or retention of the policy as strong evidence that a change is not genuine.

- Public filings and regulatory interactions: filings with the Department of Labor or state agencies, or testimony that the company told regulators it had ceased the practice, can be highly persuasive.

Practical strategy for plaintiffs. When an employer voluntarily alters pay practices (or pays previously withheld wages), plaintiffs should immediately seek targeted discovery about the policy’s status and implementation across time and locations: interrogatories and document requests to produce the policy, communications, payroll runs, and the names of individuals with knowledge; depositions of HR/payroll and the manager who implemented the policy. If the defendant asserts cessation, seek evidence that the cessation is binding and irreversible (e.g., board minutes authorizing formal rescission, formal policy amendments, corporate resolutions). If the defendant refuses discovery, move quickly for discovery and resist summary disposition via a Rule 56 motion premised on an untested factual record.

Practical strategy for defendants. If you change a practice to moot a claim, understand courts scrutinize motives and the durability of the change. Consider memorializing rescission in a binding, company-wide policy amendment and producing that amendment in discovery. Produce payroll runs demonstrating uniform implementation. A prudential approach is to adopt written policies and process changes that make recurrence unlikely and to document those steps contemporaneously.

How courts should proceed. On remand in Cohen, the district court should examine the record, resolve factual disputes about the policy’s status, and determine whether the plaintiff faces an ongoing injury or a reasonable risk of recurrence. If the record is underdeveloped, the court should allow jurisdictional discovery focused on whether the challenged practice continues and whether the defendant’s representations are genuine.

## Proof of Commissioner demand and penalty eligibility under § 181.101

Statutory text controls both the availability of remedies and who may pursue them. Cohen’s MPWA claim turned on a textual allocation of enforcement authority: Minnesota’s § 181.101 permits the Commissioner of Labor and Industry to “serve a demand for payment on behalf of an employee” and—if wages are not paid within ten days—permits the Commissioner to “charge and collect . . . a penalty in the amount of the employee’s average daily earnings . . . for each day beyond the ten-day limit.” The Eighth Circuit interpreted this provision to mean that only the Commissioner may seek and collect the average-daily-wage penalty; an aggrieved employee suing directly in district court under § 181.171 cannot, by that statute, obtain those penalties. Cohen therefore lost on the MPWA claim to the extent he sought an average daily wage penalty under § 181.101(a).

Statutory-interpretation lesson. Two principles guided the result. First, courts begin with the statute’s plain language; if clear, legislative intent is effectuated as written. Second, the inclusion of explicit enforcement authority in adjacent statutes is informative: Minnesota’s legislature knew how to authorize employee-initiated penalties (for example in §§ 181.13 and 181.14, which expressly authorize a departing employee to charge and collect penalties), and its failure to similarly empower the employee in § 181.101 is evidence that only the Commissioner may invoke the § 181.101 penalty. Expressio unius est exclusio alterius (the expression of one thing implies the exclusion of another) is an interpretive aid, not an inflexible rule, but it is especially persuasive where statutory structure shows deliberate calibration of remedies.

Practical proof to assemble when § 181.101 is invoked. If you represent a plaintiff who believes a § 181.101 penalty is due, collect evidence of the Commissioner’s involvement:

- A copy of the Commissioner’s demand letter, including the date of service. The statute ties penalties to a demand by the Commissioner; absent that demand, the penalty will not be triggered.

- Evidence that ten days passed after service without payment: certified mail return receipts, email delivery confirmations, or a signed acknowledgment of receipt.

- Correspondence between the Commissioner and the employer showing the Commissioner’s exercise of the authority to collect wages and penalties.

- Records of money collected and its disposition: payment orders, checks issued by the Commissioner, deposit records showing amounts remitted to the employee, or a written determination by the Commissioner.

- If the Commissioner did not act, evidence of communications seeking the Commissioner’s involvement. Such evidence will not create statutory authority where the statute reserves the penalty to the Commissioner, but it may be relevant to equitable relief or to other statutory provisions.

Advocacy implication. If you are counsel for a plaintiff seeking MPWA penalties under § 181.101, you must either (a) obtain proof that the Commissioner has exercised the statutory enforcement mechanism or (b) proceed under statutes or theories that actually empower private plaintiffs to recover penalties. If the Commissioner has not acted and the statute reserves the penalty to that office, a court is likely to conclude private recovery is unavailable—unless other statutes or constitutional principles create a separate right to relief.

Defensive implications. If you represent a defendant, remember that the difference between an administrative enforcement pathway and a private civil penalty can be decisive. Production of the Commissioner’s correspondence showing either that the Commissioner never made a demand—or that the Commissioner collected and disbursed the penalties—will determine what remedies a plaintiff can pursue in court. Preserving administrative records and correspondence about demands should be a litigation priority.

## Documenting MWTA compliance or violations and resulting damages

Minnesota’s Wage Theft Act and related notice provisions (e.g., § 181.032) impose specific employer obligations to provide employees with certain notices, and failure to comply may trigger remedies. Cohen alleged the employer failed to provide statutorily required notice of an overtime-policy change. The Eighth Circuit treated that claim perfunctorily in Cohen because the plaintiff did not develop persuasive statutory authority or argument to support a contrary outcome—and because he had already been paid the wages he sought. The lesson: for notice-based statutory claims, develop the record and the statutory remedies comprehensively.

Understand the statutory requirement. Before asserting an MWTA claim based on notice, identify the exact statutory language that creates a private cause of action, who is authorized to recover penalties, and what elements must be proved. Some statutes impose immediate penalties or provide liquidated damages for failures; others empower regulators to seek penalties; still others provide no civil penalties but permit other forms of relief (injunctive relief, attorneys’ fees, compensatory damages). Read the full statutory scheme and related statutory cross-references.

Evidence to prove notice noncompliance. When prosecuting a failure-to-notify claim, gather proof that the employer did not provide the required notice and that the employee was harmed (to the extent required by the statute):

- A copy of the statutory notice form or the statutory text identifying required contents.

- The employer’s standard onboarding documents and contemporaneous handouts: produce the “notice” (or show its absence) as an exhibit.

- Testimony (affidavits or depositions) from the employee(s) confirming they were not given the specified notice and describing how they would have acted differently (where reliance is required for an element of damages).

- Records of distribution, e.g., a signed acknowledgment of receipt or an electronic delivery confirmation; absence of such records can be probative.

- Where the notice concerned a change in pay policy, payroll changes and paystubs that reflect the practical effect of the change.

- Communications from HR or management discussing the notice decision, including reasons for not providing notice; internal deliberations can be powerful evidence.

Quantifying damages. Many notice statutes either (a) authorize a fixed statutory penalty for each violation or (b) provide that violations give rise to actual damages or injunctive relief. Precisely plead and prove the damages authorized. If the statute is silent on monetary penalties but permits injunctive relief, focus on proving ongoing harm or a threat of future harm to support an injunction.

Practical drafting and litigation tips. If notice is required by statute, plead the specific statutory subsection, and track the statutory text in the complaint. Attach—or be ready to attach to summary judgment filings—the actual notice the employer claims to have provided. If the employer claims it provided notice (or provided notice in a different format), test the claim through document requests and deposition questioning: ask HR whether the notice was given to affected employees, whether the notice was individualized or general, and who bears responsibility for issuance and recordkeeping.

## Appellate preservation: avoiding waiver through developed, supported argument

Cohen demonstrates the costs of failing to develop issues adequately: the MWTA claim received short shrift because Cohen failed to flesh out the argument or point to statutory authority, and his request for a particular injunction was not properly presented. The Eighth Circuit repeatedly relied on waiver doctrine in declining to entertain inadequately developed claims.

Rules and consequences. Appellate courts require that issues be properly preserved and developed in the district court, and that appellate briefs meet procedural and substantive requirements (e.g., Fed. R. App. P. 28(a)(8)–(9)). Failure to brief an issue adequately before the district court typically waives it on appeal. Likewise, appellate courts deem an argument abandoned if the brief cites no authority or presents no analysis.

How to avoid waiver in practice:

- Preserve precise claims in the complaint. If you seek injunctive relief in a particular form, plead the specific forms of relief you want with enough detail to give the defendant notice and to frame discovery (Rule 8 and Rule 65 practice). If you plan to request additional or modified relief later, move to amend under Rule 15 rather than springing new demands in opposition briefs.

- Develop every claim in the district court. Present legal authority and factual support on each contested point in the district court. If the district court rules against you, the appellate court will not be a forum to add new theories.

- Include citations and analysis. Argument without citation is likely to be ignored. Include statutes, controlling precedent, and any persuasive authority. Where the law is undeveloped, explain the policy and doctrinal bases for your position.

- Use supporting evidence in opposition. For a summary judgment opposition, attach or cite specific admissible evidence: declarations, exhibits, deposition excerpts, contracts, payroll records. Pinpoint record citations for each material fact you assert.

- Avoid ambush. If you are changing positions (e.g., seeking different injunctive relief than in the complaint), formally move to amend the complaint. Courts and appellate tribunals dislike litigants who present new theories in opposition briefs without amendments.

Checklist for opposition briefs. In preparing a summary judgment opposition, include: (1) a concise statement of disputed material facts with pinpoint record citations, (2) admissible evidence (declarations with exhibits), (3) legal argument with supporting authority, (4) an explanation of the relief sought and its statutory or equitable basis, and (5) motions to supplement or amend the pleadings if necessary.

## Standards of review and burdens at summary judgment in wage-and-hour cases

Understanding the allocation of burdens on summary judgment and the standard of review is essential to effective advocacy. Cohen’s appeal was reviewed de novo; the Eighth Circuit reiterated the foundational principles courts apply when deciding Rule 56 motions.

Summary judgment basics. The moving party bears the initial burden to show there is no genuine dispute of material fact and that it is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. If the movant meets that burden, the nonmoving party must present specific facts showing there is a genuine issue for trial—conjecture and conclusory assertions will not suffice. The court views facts in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party and draws reasonable inferences in that party’s favor.

Applications to wage-and-hour litigation. Wage-and-hour cases often turn on mixed questions of law and fact: whether a given pay practice violates statutory requirements; whether hours were worked; or whether a purported policy actually governed employee conduct. These recurring evidentiary issues should guide practice:

- Pay records and timekeeping. Payroll data, time and attendance records, earnings statements, and spreadsheets showing how overtime was computed are central. A party cannot defeat summary judgment by asserting “we didn’t pay overtime” without producing records or admissible testimony supporting that assertion.

- Employee testimony and declarations. Declarations from affected employees can establish practices and damages. Ensure declarations comply with Rule 56(c)—made on personal knowledge, state facts that would be admissible, and show the declarant is competent to testify.

- Corporate testimony: HR and payroll. Declarations or deposition testimony from HR or payroll managers that explain payroll practices, the actual implementation of a policy, the calculation of pay, and the chain-of-command for policy adoption are critical. Courts treat such testimony as particularly persuasive when supported by contemporaneous documents.

- Documentary corroboration. Policies alone may be insufficient if payroll practice contradicts the policy; similarly, policy statements must be corroborated by payroll runs or emails showing implementation. Use both documentary and testimonial proof.

- Stipulations and their effect. Stipulations can resolve or narrow live issues. In Cohen, the parties stipulated that Cohen had been paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages; this concession narrowed the live controversy and precipitated the mootness inquiry about penalties and injunctive relief. Consider the tactical use and potential consequences of stipulating to damages or payments.

- Mixed claim adjudication. When the record is contested on a fact that is necessary to jurisdiction (e.g., cessation of a policy), the district court should resolve the factual dispute before disposing of the case on the merits. If the factual record is thin, allow jurisdictional discovery.

Standing and the forms of relief. A plaintiff must demonstrate standing for each claim and each form of relief. Monetary payments by a defendant can eliminate a particular injury, and thereby remove the plaintiff’s standing to obtain forward-looking remedies or statutory penalties unless other facts preserve a live controversy—e.g., the policy remains in force or there is a credible threat of recurrence. Plaintiffs should preserve their ability to obtain injunctive relief by seeking specific forms of equitable relief early and by developing evidence that the alleged harm is ongoing.

Standard of appellate review. Appellate courts review summary judgment de novo. Factual findings are reviewed for clear error only if they are the product of an evidentiary hearing or trial; when courts have resolved factual disputes at summary judgment (e.g., by crediting declarations and documentary evidence), appellate courts defer to admissible record support for those findings, but still review legal conclusions de novo.

## Practical steps and checklists for remand and further proceedings

When an appellate court vacates a summary judgment on the ground that jurisdiction or mootness was not adequately considered, the district court and litigants face a roadmap of practical steps. The Eighth Circuit vacated the MFLSA ruling and remanded Cohen for the district court to adjudicate its jurisdiction—this is the typical sequence.

What plaintiffs should do on remand:

- File a focused jurisdictional memo and targeted discovery plan. Identify precisely why jurisdiction exists (ongoing policy, injury-in-fact, capable of repetition), and propose discovery tailored to the status of the policy and any administrative actions (e.g., the Commissioner’s demand).

- Move for jurisdictional discovery if the record is inadequate on the issue of cessation or recurrence.

- Rearticulate injunctive relief claims and, if you seek a particular remedy that was not in the complaint, move to amend under Rule 15 to add the requested relief. Attach the proposed draft order or policy language you want the court to impose.

- If the Commissioner has been involved or can be persuaded to act, consider urging the Commissioner to issue a demand under § 181.101 (if applicable), because the availability of statutory penalties may turn on that office’s involvement.

What defendants should do on remand:

- Produce clear, unambiguous evidence of policy rescission and demonstrate the unlikelihood of recurrence (formal policy changes, payroll runs, HR attestations).

- If the defendant has changed practice only for a narrow cohort of employees or only in response to litigation, be prepared to show why the change is durable and company-wide (for example, board minutes or corporate policy revisions).

- Oppose jurisdictional discovery only when the requested discovery is truly irrelevant; otherwise produce targeted materials that demonstrate cessation and disprove the “reasonable expectation of recurrence” standard.

What the district court should do:

- Resolve jurisdictional questions first. If standing or mootness is contested, the district court should address those threshold issues before reaching the merits.

- Permit targeted discovery where necessary to decide jurisdictional facts. Blanket refusal to allow discovery on the status of a change in policy is a frequent error.

- Make specific factual findings about the status of the policy, the likelihood of recurrence, and any continuing injury. Such findings will be critical on appeal.

- If the court concludes the claim is not moot, proceed to adjudicate statutory remedies consistent with the statutory allocation of enforcement authority (for example, distinguishing between penalties that are payable to the Commissioner and damages payable directly to the employee).

Conclusion

Cohen v. Consilio illustrates how statutory text, a carefully developed factual record, and disciplined briefing interact at summary judgment. To preserve and vindicate statutory penalties, notice-based claims, and requests for injunctive relief, litigators must (1) understand the statutory enforcement framework and gather the precise administrative or corporate records that the statute contemplates; (2) build a record that proves whether a challenged policy continues to impose a real and imminent harm; and (3) preserve issues and remedies through proper pleading, discovery, and district-court briefing. Thoughtful, targeted discovery and contemporaneous documentary proof—policy documents, payroll runs, emails, declarations from key HR and payroll personnel, and any administrative communications—often determine whether a claim survives summary judgment or is resolved on jurisdictional grounds.

# Comparative and Policy Perspectives

This chapter situates the Cohen v. Consilio decision in a broader, comparative framework. Cohen exposes an important structural feature of Minnesota’s wage-enforcement scheme: some statutory penalties are allocated to the state’s labor commissioner to collect, while other remedies—most notably unpaid wages and liquidated damages under Minnesota’s FLSA analog—may be recovered directly by private employees. That allocation raises doctrinal questions (standing and mootness of private claims for penalties) and practical and normative questions about how best to combine public enforcement with private litigation. The discussion below uses Cohen as a focal point to examine (1) the parallel tracks of enforcement under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) and Minnesota law, (2) other states’ approaches to penalties and private enforcement, (3) the policy tradeoffs that follow from different allocations of enforcement authority and remedies, and (4) the practical mechanics and strategic choices that litigants and agencies face when private suits and agency processes run in parallel.

## FLSA remedies: private liquidated damages versus DOL civil money penalties; parallels to Minnesota’s allocation

The FLSA has long combined a robust private right of action with federal administrative enforcement. A private plaintiff who brings a successful FLSA suit may recover the unpaid wages owed, and in most cases an equal amount in liquidated damages—effectively doubling the employee’s recovery. That private liquidated-damages remedy exists to compensate for delay and to deter nonpayment by aligning the individual plaintiff’s incentive with enforcement goals: a single plaintiff can make herself whole and obtain a deterrent multiple of the wages withheld without relying on the Department of Labor (DOL) to act.

At the same time, the federal scheme also authorizes the government to impose civil-money penalties (CMPs) in particular circumstances—e.g., for child-labor violations and willful or repeat violations—through administrative or judicial processes. Those penalties are typically payable to the United States and serve a public-law deterrent function; they are not designed primarily to make the affected employees whole. The co-existence of private liquidated damages and public CMPs creates a two-track enforcement architecture: private suits secure compensation and private deterrence, while agency enforcement addresses systemic harms, repeat offenders, and the public interest.

Minnesota’s statutory architecture, as revealed in Cohen, mirrors this hybrid design but with key differences in allocation of authority and remedies. Minnesota’s MFLSA claim closely resembles the federal FLSA in allowing private suits that recover unpaid overtime and liquidated damages. Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), by contrast, contains a statutory mechanism that vests the Commissioner of Labor and Industry with authority to serve demands for unpaid wages and to “charge and collect” an “average daily earnings” penalty if wages are not paid within a defined time after demand. The critical interpretive point in Cohen was that although employees may sue civilly for violations under the MPWA, the MPWA’s specific penalty provision—by its text—treats only the Commissioner as authorized to collect the statutory penalty and to deliver the collected money to the employee. Because the statute ties the penalty’s enforcement and collection mechanism to the Commissioner, the court held that private plaintiffs cannot themselves obtain that particular penalty in their civil suits.

That allocation matters procedurally and substantively. Procedurally, it may limit the remedies that an individual litigant can secure through a private suit; substantively, it may reduce the private litigant’s incentive to pursue a case if the principal dollar deterrent resides with the state agency rather than the individual. The practical consequence shown in Cohen is: an employee can recover unpaid wages and liquidated damages directly but may have no statutory entitlement to the MPWA’s average-daily-wage penalty unless the Commissioner pursues it.

When comparing FLSA and Minnesota, two tendencies stand out. First, both systems mix private and public enforcement instruments; second, the allocation of who gets the penalty and who enforces it materially shapes incentives. Where the private remedy is comprehensive (wages + liquidated damages), private suits do much of the enforcement heavy lifting. Where statutory penalties are centralized with an agency, the agency’s capacity and willingness to act become decisive for deterrence.

## Other states’ penalty regimes and private standing to recover penalties

States differ dramatically in how they structure penalties and who can recover them. Two contrasting examples illustrate the range of choices and the practical consequences.

California—Waiting-time penalties and PAGA. California’s Labor Code provides a well-known example of a strong private remedy. Under the “waiting-time” penalty provision, an employer who fails to timely pay final wages on termination can be liable to the employee for daily penalties for each day the wages are late, up to a statutory maximum. Those penalties are payable to the employee, and the provision is enforceable in private suits. California also has the Private Attorneys General Act (PAGA), which authorizes employees to seek civil penalties on behalf of the state for Labor Code violations. PAGA claims are technically penalties payable to the state (with a statutory split directing a portion to the state and a portion to the private plaintiff), but PAGA vests private actors with the ability to enforce many Labor Code provisions that otherwise would be enforced by the Labor and Workforce Development Agency. PAGA litigation has thus become a mechanism for private enforcement of public penalties, subject to exhaustion/notice requirements and agency review procedures.

Massachusetts, New York, and other states—civil penalties in favor of employees. Some states, like New York and Massachusetts, provide statutory penalties that are recoverable by private plaintiffs and payable to them; others centralize penalties in state agencies. The particular design—who recovers penalties, whether penalties can be recovered in private suits, and whether a private plaintiff must first notify or obtain agency action—varies across jurisdictions. That variation has important consequences for litigation strategy, the volume of private enforcement, and the distribution of collected funds.

Comparative lessons. The landscape demonstrates three common regulatory choices: (1) make penalties private and payable to the employee; (2) make penalties public and payable to an agency; or (3) create a hybrid in which private plaintiffs may recover penalties on behalf of the state (with notice or exhaustion requirements), or where penalties are split between the state and private litigants. Each choice produces different incentives and enforcement patterns. Cohen’s interpretation of Minnesota law places the MPWA closer to model (2): certain penalties belong to the agency.

From a doctrinal perspective, those statutory allocations also affect standing and justiciability. If statutory penalties are payable only to the government, then a private plaintiff cannot claim an entitlement to those sums; standing to seek injunctive relief or prospective relief may remain, but claims for penalties could be moot or non-justiciable if the defendant ceases the challenged conduct and the agency does not pursue penalties. By contrast, where penalties are payable to the employee, the plaintiff has a concrete stake in monetary recovery, increasing the chance the claim is justiciable and not moot.

## Policy tradeoffs: deterrence, administrative coherence, and risks of over- or under-enforcement

No statutory design point is normatively free. The choice to centralize penalty enforcement in an agency, to authorize broad private penalties, or to create hybrid mechanisms is governed by conflicting policy goals. Four core tradeoffs animate the debate.

1. Deterrence and private incentives. Private recovery of significant penalties (or liquidated damages) creates strong incentives for employees or private counsel to bring claims: private plaintiffs internalize the benefits of enforcement and lower the government’s monitoring costs. This can produce extensive enforcement activity even where agency resources are limited. The downside is the danger of over-detention or opportunistic suits—claims motivated more by contingency-fee economics than by social harm—and the possibility of inconsistent outcomes across courts.

2. Administrative coherence and expertise. Centralized enforcement through an agency allows for coordinated responses to systemic violations, uniform policy interpretation, and the development of expertise. Agencies can pursue complex or repeat offenders, impose penalties that reflect broader policy aims, and seek remedies beyond individual recovery (e.g., industry-wide injunctions or regulatory changes). However, agencies are resource-constrained and politically accountable in ways that may impede aggressive enforcement. Where penalties are the agency’s principal tool, under-enforcement is a real risk if the agency lacks capacity or political will.

3. Predictability and uniformity versus local innovation. Private enforcement produces decentralized litigation and settlements that can reflect local norms or different judicial interpretations, creating a patchwork of enforcement. That patchwork can be a laboratory for innovative legal arguments and may allow courts to refine doctrine; but it also creates uncertainty for employers operating interstate and may shrink the deterrent effect of statutory schemes if enforcement is uneven.

4. Economic efficiency and distributional fairness. Remedies payable to employees (liquidated damages, waiting-time penalties) directly compensate victims and may better match harms, ensuring that those injured receive relief. Public penalties, by contrast, internalize social harms—compensation goes to the state or is used for public purposes—but may leave individuals undercompensated. The policy choice depends on whether the legislature seeks to prioritize victim compensation, public deterrence, or both.

Cohen brings these tradeoffs into relief. Minnesota’s structure—private recovery of liquidated damages under the MFLSA combined with agency control over certain MPWA penalties—reflects a hybrid policy choice. It allows private litigants to vindicate wage entitlements and to obtain liquidated damages, while preserving the state’s authority to impose an average‑daily‑wage penalty that may be better wielded as a public enforcement tool. The practical effect, however, is to shift the deterrence calculus onto the Commissioner: if the Commissioner does not pursue penalties, the statutory device intended to promote compliance can be dormant.

## Coordinating private suits with agency investigations and settlements

The reality of enforcement is that private suits and agency processes often run in parallel. The strategic and doctrinal interaction between the two tracks requires attention to standing, mootness, settlement bargaining, and notice or exhaustion requirements that some statutes impose.

Standing and mootness considerations. A leading doctrinal issue—illustrated by the district court and the Eighth Circuit in Cohen—is whether a plaintiff retains a live controversy over penalties when an employer has paid the wages demanded. The Supreme Court’s Friends of the Earth framework requires that plaintiffs maintain a personal stake in the litigation for the case to remain justiciable; a defendant’s voluntary cessation of unlawful conduct does not automatically doom the suit, but courts evaluate whether the defendant’s cessation is likely to recur. In Cohen the Court vacated the MFLSA ruling and remanded for the district court to consider whether the employer’s policy had been rescinded or whether the plaintiff retained a live controversy over statutory penalties payable to the government. Practitioners should take two lessons away: (1) when the sought relief is a penalty payable to an agency rather than the plaintiff, the private plaintiff’s stake is weaker, and courts scrutinize whether remaining claims (injunctive relief, prospective relief) suffice to preserve jurisdiction; and (2) a defendant’s payment of back wages and liquidated damages may moor the private plaintiff’s economic stake but may not address the public’s interest in penalties.

Preserving claims and coordinating with agencies. Plaintiffs and their counsel who wish to pursue monetary penalties that are payable to the state should consider notifying the appropriate agency or otherwise preserving the agency’s option to intervene. Some state statutes mandate notification or provide the agency with a period to act before a private plaintiff may pursue remedies (PAGA’s notice and agency review process is the canonical example). Even where statutes do not require it, early engagement with the agency can produce coordinated enforcement and avoid duplicative proceedings. From a defense perspective, employers often prefer to negotiate with the agency to resolve potential penalties in exchange for remediation or a consent order; settlements that include agency releases can extinguish future agency claims but cannot always bind private plaintiffs unless they sign releases.

Settlements, releases, and the role of the agency. Private settlements that compensate employees but do not involve the agency may leave public penalties uncollected unless the agency is involved or the employee had authority under the statute to recover penalties directly. Courts have been attentive to whether private settlements usurp public enforcement or improperly extinguish public claims. In some jurisdictions, agencies will not accept a private settlement that leaves statutory penalties unaddressed; in others, agencies welcome private settlements as de facto enforcement. Cohen demonstrates the practical consequences: Consilio paid wages and liquidated damages to resolve the MFLSA wage claims but did not pay MPWA average-daily-wage penalties because the statute assigns collection to the Commissioner. If the Commissioner does not act, the statutory penalty may never be imposed.

Practical litigation strategies. For plaintiffs: when statutory penalties are payable only to an agency, advise clients to (a) notify the agency early, (b) consider seeking injunctive relief or other prospective remedies that preserve a live controversy, (c) avoid stipulating away all elements of the controversy in ways that could moot outstanding claims, and (d) preserve the right to attorney’s fees and costs where awards are available. For defendants: consider engaging the agency promptly, correcting noncompliant policies (to reduce future risk), and obtaining clear releases where possible. Be careful: voluntary cessation may not moot a suit if recurrence is plausible, and courts scrutinize conduct aimed at manufacturing mootness.

Design choices to improve coordination. Legislatures can design mechanisms to harmonize private and public enforcement: mandatory notice-and-wait periods before private plaintiffs may sue (as in some PAGA-like frameworks); explicit allocation rules that clarify when private plaintiffs can recover penalties; statutory provisions allowing the agency to intervene in private suits; or requirement that private settlements be submitted to the agency for review where penalties are involved. Each design addresses different policy concerns—public oversight, avoiding duplicative litigation, and ensuring penalties reach their intended beneficiary.

## Concluding evaluation and doctrinal takeaways

Cohen v. Consilio highlights how statutory text and structural allocation of enforcement authority shape both results and litigation posture. Doctrinally, the case teaches three interrelated points:

1. Statutory text matters. If a statute explicitly confers penalty-collection authority on an administrative actor and conditions collection on agency procedures, courts will enforce that allocation and deny private plaintiffs a right to recover the penalty in a private action absent clear legislative language to the contrary.

2. Remedy allocation affects standing and mootness. When a plaintiff’s potential recovery is largely the collection of penalties payable to the government, courts must carefully consider whether the plaintiff has the requisite concrete stake to pursue penalty claims; voluntary remediation by the defendant may eliminate the private stake and raise mootness concerns, but it will not automatically protect against litigation if the defendant’s cessation is not credibly permanent.

3. Enforcement architecture is a policy choice with predictable consequences. Centralizing penalties with an agency prioritizes coordinated public enforcement and uniformity but risks under-enforcement if agency capacity is limited. Privatizing penalties (or allowing private plaintiffs to recover public penalties, as with PAGA) boosts private incentives and monitoring but risks over-enforcement, settlement-driven recoveries that do not reflect the public interest, and inconsistency.

For students and practitioners, the strategic lesson is to read statutory remedies closely and to plan litigation and settlement strategies in light of who the statute empowers to collect penalties. Where penalties are the state’s, coordinate with the agency; where private liquidated damages exist, plaintiffs can often proceed independently but should preserve non-monetary relief that sustains a live controversy. Legislatures that care about both victim compensation and public deterrence should consider hybrid designs: protect private compensation (so employees are made whole) and empower agencies to pursue broader penalties for systemic violations—while building procedural bridges that facilitate coordination rather than competition between private litigants and public enforcers.

# Significance of Cohen v. Consilio and Looking Ahead

Cohen v. Consilio is a compact but consequential decision for practitioners who litigate Minnesota wage claims and for federal courts wrestling with Article III jurisdictional doctrines in the context of statutory civil penalties. The Eighth Circuit’s opinion performs two principal functions. First, it construes the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) and concludes that one particular remedy—“average daily wage” penalties under Minn. Stat. § 181.101—is available only through the Commissioner of Labor and Industry, not by private suit. Second, it identifies and remands an unresolved jurisdictional question: whether a plaintiff who has been paid back wages and liquidated damages can still maintain a claim for civil penalties under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) when those penalties are payable to the state (through the Commissioner). The court’s treatment of these issues illuminates statutory interpretation principles, the role of administrative enforcement mechanisms, and standing/mootness doctrine where defendants change conduct mid‑litigation.

The remainder of this chapter isolates the doctrinal holdings in Cohen, explains their legal underpinnings, and draws practical lessons for lawyers and employers. Each section situates the Court’s reasoning in a broader doctrinal context so that you can apply these principles to new facts.

## Clarification that MPWA § 181.101 average daily wage penalties are Commissioner‑only

Cohen’s clearest holding concerns the MPWA’s allocation of enforcement authority. The Eighth Circuit read § 181.101 together with the general private‑action provision of the MPWA, § 181.171, and concluded that average‑daily‑wage penalties under § 181.101 are enforceable only by the Commissioner (via an administrative demand and collection), not by a private employee suing in district court.

The starting point for that conclusion is plain‑language statutory interpretation. Section 181.101(a) authorizes the Commissioner to “serve a demand for payment on behalf of an employee” and provides that “if payment of wages is not made within ten days of service of the demand, the commissioner may charge and collect . . . a penalty in the amount of the employee’s average daily earnings . . . for each day beyond the ten‑day limit.” Because the statute expressly describes the Commissioner’s power to assess and collect the penalty and because it says that money collected “must be paid to the employee concerned,” the Eighth Circuit concluded the legislature intended the Commissioner — rather than private litigants — to be the actor who imposes and collects that specific statutory penalty.

That conclusion is reinforced by what the statute does not say. The MPWA’s general private‑suit provision (§ 181.171) allows an employee to bring a civil action “for violations” of enumerated MPWA provisions and makes an employer liable for “compensatory damages and other appropriate relief including but not limited to injunctive relief.” But § 181.171 conditions the availability of “civil penalties or damages” on whether they are “provided for in the section” the plaintiff invokes. Because the particular penalty at issue—an average daily wage penalty—appears solely within § 181.101 and is expressly tied to the Commissioner’s demand and collection power, the Court read the statutory scheme to allocate that penalty to the Commissioner alone.

The Court also applied a classic canons‑of‑construction point: where the legislature knows how to create a private claim for penalties (and has done so elsewhere in the MPWA), its failure to do so in § 181.101 is meaningful. The opinion cites other MPWA provisions (for discharged or quit employees) that expressly allow a discharged employee to “charge and collect a penalty,” thereby supporting the inference that the legislature knew how to provide for private enforcement when it wished to do so.

For students and practitioners, this holding illustrates three lessons about statutory remedies. First, read the statutory text closely for who is authorized to act (commissioner, district court, private party) and for to whom the statute says the remedy is payable. Second, a general private‑suit grant does not automatically import all penalties found elsewhere in a statutory scheme—if a particular penalty is “provided for in the section” and that section ties enforcement to an administrative actor, the private action may not reach it. Third, legislative silence on private enforcement when comparable provisions include private enforcement can be a persuasive, though not dispositive, indicator of intent.

## Unresolved standing and mootness questions for MFLSA civil penalties payable to the state

The Eighth Circuit’s remand on the MFLSA claim exposes a less tidy area of federal law: whether a private plaintiff who recovers the wages and liquidated damages he sought nevertheless has Article III standing to pursue civil penalties that are payable to the government. Cohen stipulated that Consilio had paid all overtime wages and liquidated damages he was owed under the MFLSA. The parties further agreed those penalties, unlike wages and damages, would be payable to the Commissioner. The court recognized a real tension: if a plaintiff has been made whole with respect to the direct economic injury (wages and liquidated damages), does he retain a cognizable interest in obtaining penalties that the statute directs to the state? And if the defendant purports to have changed its policy, does the plaintiff retain a live controversy or has the claim become moot?

Two doctrinal strands govern. First, Article III requires that plaintiffs show, at the outset and throughout the litigation, injury in fact, traceability, and redressability. The redressability prong frequently proves decisive where plaintiffs seek non‑monetary or public‑interest remedies; the Supreme Court and the Eighth Circuit have also required that plaintiffs have a continuing personal stake when seeking prospective or deterrent relief. Second, mootness doctrine holds that a case becomes moot when events make it “absolutely clear” that the allegedly wrongful behavior cannot be reasonably expected to recur. Voluntary cessation by a defendant ordinarily does not render a case moot unless the defendant meets that high showing.

The Eighth Circuit recognized that civil penalties payable to the government can, in some circumstances, supply Article III redress because they can deter future violations and thus “afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct.” That principle comes from Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw, and it underpins many private enforcement suits aimed at stopping continuing violations. But the line between a cognizable deterrence interest and an attenuated grievance is fact‑sensitive. The Cohen court therefore declined to decide the standing question in the first instance and remanded to the district court to determine whether the MFLSA penalties sought by Cohen still present a live controversy given the parties’ stipulation about payment and the factual uncertainty about Consilio’s current pay policy.

Two further points sharpen the unresolved issue. First, the identity of the penalty’s recipient matters for redressability analysis. When statutory penalties are payable to the injured private party, redressability is usually straightforward—the plaintiff would receive money directly. When penalties flow to the government, plaintiffs must show that securing those penalties would nonetheless redress their injury, typically because (a) the sought penalties motivate the defendant to stop or refrain from future violations (a deterrent effect), (b) a declaratory or injunctive remedy paired with penalties would prevent recurrence, or (c) the plaintiff faces a credible threat of future injury that only the award of penalties will remedy. Second, the defendant’s conduct after litigation begins is crucial. If the defendant has not merely paid but also rescinded the challenged policy and can show the rescission is genuine and lasting, a court may conclude the plaintiff lacks a continuing stake. But if the defendant’s change in practice is ephemeral or ambiguous, the plaintiff can plausibly argue the suit remains necessary to secure future compliance.

The Eighth Circuit’s disposition—vacatur and remand—means district courts confronting similar contests must conduct a careful, record‑based inquiry into whether a plaintiff retains a concrete, redressable interest in penalties payable to the state. That inquiry will often require discovery targeted at whether the employer’s policy was actually changed and whether the employer is likely to revert to the prior, allegedly unlawful policy.

## Practical guidance: when to involve the Commissioner; preserving injunctive claims; evidentiary focus on ongoing policies

Cohen suggests several concrete tactical choices for both plaintiffs and defense counsel. First, because certain MPWA penalties are administratively controlled, counsel for employees who want penalties available should consider invoking the Commissioner’s administrative procedures early. Section 181.101 explicitly contemplates a Commissioner‑served demand for payment as the precursor to penalties; if a private plaintiff wants the average‑daily‑wage penalty available, the route remains administrative. That route has features lawyers should weigh: administrative collection can accelerate payment to employees, but it also means the Commissioner controls enforcement discretion and the timing of any litigation.

Second, plaintiffs who intend to pursue statutory penalties or injunctive relief should preserve those claims and plead them with care. Cohen’s change of tack on injunctive relief (seeking different relief in briefing than in the complaint) deprived the appellate court of a square record on whether injunctive relief should have been considered. Best practice is to plead alternative forms of relief (wages, liquidated damages, penalties, declaratory relief, and prospective injunctive relief) and, if one remedy is obtained before judgment (for example, by settlement of wages), to seek a declaratory judgment and continue to litigate prospective remedies. If a plaintiff accepts wages without expressly reserving claims for penalties or injunctive relief, a defendant can plausibly argue that the dispute is moot.

Third, where the issue turns on whether the defendant’s unlawful policy remains in place, the plaintiff must develop and preserve direct evidence that the practice continues or that there is a credible threat of recurrence. Useful discovery targets include contemporaneous emails and corporate communications announcing the pay policy; written policy documents; payroll rules as implemented in the payroll system; employee acknowledgments of changed pay rates; and testimony from human resources or operations managers about whether and why the company has retained the policy. Plaintiffs should also seek classwide or collective evidence where possible: company‑wide directives and files will be more persuasive than a single employee’s testimony.

Fourth, when statutory penalties are payable to the government, plaintiffs must be prepared to show — through documentary evidence, deposition testimony, or expert proof — that the sought penalties would have a deterrent or remedial effect sufficient to redress their continuing injury. If the employer claims to have discontinued the challenged practice, the plaintiff should use discovery to test the genuineness and durability of that claim (for example, by getting documents that show whether legacy payroll settings persist, whether managers were instructed informally to continue prior practices, or whether new hires are being paid under the old scheme).

Finally, litigants should raise jurisdictional issues early. Courts will not decide mootness or standing questions lightly, but they are obliged to determine Article III jurisdiction before reaching the merits. If the parties anticipate payment of wages or a change in policy during litigation, consider seeking interim injunctive relief or moving to preserve the record on whether the defendant’s practice has been altered.

## Employer compliance lessons: changing pay policies and providing timely, accurate notice

Cohen also contains lessons for employers about how to lawfully change pay practices and avoid litigation risk. Two discrete compliance points emerge from the statutory text and the opinion’s logic.

First, employers who alter overtime pay policies should do so transparently, with careful documentation and, where state law demands notice, with statutory compliance. Under Minnesota law, certain changes may trigger notice obligations. Even if no notice statute prescribes the exact sequelae of a change, employers should provide clear written notice to affected employees, maintain contemporaneous records showing when and how notice was given, and obtain employee acknowledgments where possible. The better the documentary trail showing a lawful, properly communicated change, the harder it will be for plaintiffs to show an unannounced or unlawful policy persisted.

Second, employers should remember the statutory mechanisms for penalty exposure. If a state statute ties penalties to administrative demand and collection (as § 181.101 does), an employer should anticipate that the Commissioner may be the vehicle through which penalties are first assessed. Employers should respond promptly to any demand from the Commissioner because the statute imposes a ten‑day window for payment; failure to meet that deadline invites administrative penalties. In addition, when changing pay structures for attorney document reviewers or similarly situated specialists, employers should confirm that the change complies with wage‑and‑hour law at both the federal and state levels (for instance, whether the employees are subject to certain exemptions, or whether state law provides a broader remedy than federal law).

Operationally, employers should (a) coordinate payroll systems to implement pay changes consistently and promptly; (b) preserve the business rationale and approvals that underlie a policy change; (c) train supervisors and payroll personnel so inconsistent on‑the‑ground practices do not create liability; and (d) consider consulting counsel before rolling out a change that reduces overtime premium pay. Documented, contemporaneous communications demonstrating lawful implementation will be the best evidence if enforcement action or litigation follows.

## Potential legislative responses to clarify private penalty rights or redressability concerns

Cohen’s decision highlights an area ripe for legislative clarification. The Minnesota legislature could respond in several ways depending on its policy preference about private enforcement and the role of the Commissioner.

If the legislature wants to empower private plaintiffs to seek the average daily wage penalty directly in court, it could amend § 181.101 and/or § 181.171 to state explicitly that employees may recover the average‑daily‑wage penalty in private suits under § 181.171. Such an amendment could specify whether the penalty, if awarded in a private suit, is payable to the employee (as a direct remedy), to the Commissioner (in line with the current administrative allocation), or split between the two. Clarifying payable recipient and enforcement forum would help avoid the interpretive conflict the Eighth Circuit resolved in Cohen.

Alternatively, the legislature could double down on the administrative enforcement model by clarifying that all penalties in § 181.101 must be assessed administratively and explicitly precluding private actions seeking those particular penalties. That approach would keep enforcement centralized with the Commissioner and preserve administrative discretion, but it would reduce private litigants’ leverage in civil litigation and could affect deterrence dynamics.

Legislative reform could also address the redressability problem affirmed by the Eighth Circuit’s remand: the legislature could create a private cause of action for penalties that are payable to the government but provide that a private plaintiff may retain standing to seek them if she pleads a credible threat of future injury or seeks injunctive relief alongside penalties. Alternatively, the legislature could provide that penalties assessed administratively remain payable to the employee (even if collected by the Commissioner), which would make redressability less problematic in private litigation.

Any legislative changes should be sensitive to the separation of powers and to the incentives created for both employers and enforcement authorities. Policymakers should weigh the administrative convenience and coherence of centralized enforcement against plaintiffs’ access to remedies and the overall deterrent effect of private litigation.

Conclusion

Cohen v. Consilio is not a sweeping rewrite of wage‑and‑hour law, but it is an instructive example of how text, structure, and remedial design interact in statutory interpretation and federal‑court jurisdiction. The decision clarifies that at least some MPWA penalties are administratively controlled by the Commissioner, it signals that plaintiffs and courts must take seriously Article III constraints when statutory penalties are payable to the state, and it underscores the practical need for careful pleading and discovery to preserve claims for penalties and injunctive relief. Practitioners on both sides of wage litigation should take note: counsel for employees should consider involving the Commissioner where the MPWA penalty is at stake and should preserve alternative remedies; employers should document and communicate pay‑policy changes carefully; and litigators should be prepared to litigate jurisdictional issues about whether an award of penalties payable to the government meaningfully redresses a plaintiff’s injury.

# Problems and Hypotheticals

This chapter applies the doctrinal lessons of Cohen v. Consilio to realistic fact patterns designed to test your grasp of (1) who may enforce Minnesota’s wage statutes and obtain the various forms of relief they authorize, (2) how federal standing and mootness doctrines operate where defendants change policies after suit is filed, and (3) the special problems that arise in putative class litigation when the named plaintiff is made whole. Each scenario below states facts, poses questions, and then develops a model analysis and practical litigation strategies for each side. The analysis ties doctrine to the controlling provisions of the Minnesota statutes at issue (principally Minn. Stat. §§ 181.101, 181.171, 181.032) and to the federal standing/mootness principles addressed in Cohen and the cases it relied on (Friends of the Earth/Laidlaw, Town of Chester, City of Mesquite, and related Eighth Circuit authority).

## Scenario 1 — Commissioner issues a § 181.101 demand; employer pays wages but not penalties—who can sue for what?

Facts. The Minnesota Commissioner of Labor serves a demand under Minn. Stat. § 181.101 on Acme Legal Review, demanding payment of unpaid overtime wages on behalf of a group of hourly attorney-reviewers. Acme promptly pays all unpaid wages to the affected employees but refuses to pay the “average daily wage” penalty provided for in § 181.101(a). Three employees who received the wage payments (including the named plaintiff, P) file a civil action under Minn. Stat. § 181.171 seeking (a) the average-daily-wage penalty under § 181.101; (b) compensatory damages and injunctive relief under § 181.171; and (c) any other statutory penalties they can identify.

Question. Which claims can private plaintiffs press in district court? Who may collect the § 181.101 “average daily wage” penalties? If the Commissioner has served a demand but declines to seek the penalty, can private plaintiffs obtain it?

Analysis. The Cohen opinion is our primary guide. Minn. Stat. § 181.101(a) authorizes the Commissioner to serve a demand for unpaid wages, and then—if the employer does not pay within ten days—the Commissioner “may charge and collect the wages earned . . . and a penalty in the amount of the employee’s average daily earnings . . . for each day beyond the ten-day limit.” Cohen interprets § 181.101 in light of § 181.171 and Minnesota precedent (Milner and Allan) and concludes that the statute reserves to the Commissioner the power to seek and collect the § 181.101 penalty. The statute expressly provides that “money collected by the commissioner must be paid to the employee concerned,” but the authority to charge and collect rests with the Commissioner, not with private plaintiffs. Accordingly, a private plaintiff suing under § 181.171 may bring a civil action to vindicate violations of the wage statutes and may recover compensatory relief and other “appropriate relief” when an employer is “found to have violated” the specified sections—but a private plaintiff cannot obtain the particular § 181.101 average-daily-wage penalty because that remedy is contingent on the Commissioner’s own statutory charge-and-collect power.

Put differently: private plaintiffs retain the ability to “prosecute a claim for wages” under § 181.101(a) and to enforce violations of the MPWA in district court under § 181.171, including recovery of unpaid wages and compensatory damages, injunctive relief, and other remedies provided in the statute they have pleaded. But the § 181.101 penalty mechanism is a distinct enforcement vehicle that requires a Commissioner demand and the Commissioner’s collection action. If the Commissioner never exercises that power (or declines to collect the penalty), private plaintiffs do not obtain the § 181.101 penalty in a private suit—there is no textual basis in § 181.101 or § 181.171 for a private right to the Commissioner’s § 181.101 penalty where the Commissioner has not invoked it.

Practical consequences and strategy.
- For plaintiffs: If the desired remedy is the § 181.101 penalty, involve the Commissioner early. Ask the Commissioner to serve a demand or to bring an enforcement action. Preserve alternative theories and remedies: plead § 181.171 claims for compensatory damages and injunctive relief, and plead any MFLSA claims that carry private remedies (see below). Document the Commissioner’s communications and any refusal to act; although you may not force the Commissioner to collect penalties, evidence of non-enforcement may be useful on the standing/mootness front (if the Company later reverts to the violative policy).
- For defendants: Payment of wages typically moots the plaintiff’s claim for unpaid wages, but it does not automatically dispose of statutory penalties reserved to the Commissioner. If you pay wages promptly after demand, keep contemporaneous evidence showing the payment was complete and company policy changes (if any). Demonstrate that penalty collection is exclusively the Commissioner’s function to argue against private recovery of § 181.101 penalties.
- For the Commissioner: If the Commissioner wishes the employee to have the benefit of the § 181.101 penalty, exercise the statutory charge-and-collect power; the statute states that collected money be paid to the employee.

Bottom line. Under Cohen, the § 181.101 average-daily-wage penalty is enforceable through the Commissioner’s statutory authority; a private plaintiff cannot obtain that statutory penalty in a private suit where the Commissioner has not collected it. Private plaintiffs may, however, pursue wages, compensatory relief, and statutory remedies that are expressly available to them under other provisions.

## Scenario 2 — Employer rescinds illegal overtime policy post-filing and adopts a compliant policy—mootness analysis under Laidlaw

Facts. After suit is filed, Acme changes its timekeeping and pay policy. Acme’s general counsel sends a firm-wide email rescinding the previous overtime policy that allegedly violated state law and substitutes a policy that expressly promises overtime premium pay for hours worked over 40. Acme produces an affidavit from the VP of HR asserting the change is permanent and a declaration from outside counsel confirming a company-wide compliance program. No directive indicates any intent to revert; Acme says the change was made for business reasons and to avoid litigation. P continues to seek statutory penalties payable to the state and seeks an injunction ordering Acme to rescind the offending policy as part of relief. The district court enters summary judgment for Acme, holding the case moot; plaintiff appeals.

Question. Does Acme’s voluntary rescission of the policy render P’s claims non-justiciable? What is the correct mootness test, and what evidence will courts consider?

Analysis. The Cohen opinion vacated summary judgment on the MFLSA statutory penalties claim and remanded for the district court to determine whether it had jurisdiction, expressly invoking Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw and the “voluntary cessation”/mootness framework. Laidlaw stands for the proposition that a defendant’s voluntary cessation of an allegedly unlawful practice does not automatically moot a case; the defendant bears a “formidable” burden of showing it is “absolutely clear the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.” Where deterrent remedies (penalties payable to the government) are at issue, the Supreme Court recognized that plaintiffs may have standing to seek those penalties because a judgment can redress an ongoing threat of repeated violations and can deter future wrongdoing.

The test therefore has these components:
1. Article III injury-in-fact at the outset: The plaintiff must demonstrate a concrete injury traceable to the defendant and likely to be redressed by the relief requested.
2. Continuing injury at the time of adjudication: The injury must persist throughout the case, or at least the court must find a concrete threat of repeated injury for forward-looking claims like injunctive relief or civil penalties intended to deter future violations.
3. Voluntary cessation analysis: If a defendant discontinues the challenged conduct, the defendant must show the cessation is genuine and that there is no reasonable expectation that the challenged conduct will recur. The defendant’s burden is “formidable”; courts will look beyond the defendant’s word to objective indicia.

Courts will consider:
- The formality and breadth of the policy change (was it merely a private, litigation-driven statement, or was it embodied in written, uniformly-applicable policies and rolled out across the company?).
- The company’s historical conduct and any prior cyclical reversions to illegal policies.
- Whether the policy change is limited to the litigating plaintiff or extends company-wide and across all jurisdictions.
- Operational evidence showing continued compliance (payroll records, audits, training) and whether remedial measures are accompanied by verifiable compliance mechanisms.
- The plausibility that the change was motivated by litigation risk rather than a binding corporate commitment (a change made solely to “pause” offending conduct until litigation ends is suspect).
- The availability of declaratory or injunctive relief to prevent recurrence even where monetary relief is satisfied.

Application to the facts. Acme has produced a rescission email, a declaration that the change is permanent, and evidence of a compliance program. That evidence tends in the defendant’s favor, but under Laidlaw it does not automatically establish lack of a real controversy. The district court must evaluate whether Acme’s actions make it “absolutely clear” the misconduct will not recur. If P can produce evidence that the change is narrowly tailored (e.g., applies only to Minnesota reviewers or only to employees represented by counsel), or that Acme has previously changed and later reverted policies, the court may find a reasonable expectation of recurrence and retain jurisdiction. Conversely, if Acme demonstrates full company-wide implementation, payroll records consistently showing overtime premiums have been paid after the change, and a compliance program with independent monitoring, a court may find the claims for forward-looking relief (injunction, statutory penalties to deter future violations) moot.

Practical consequences and strategy.
- For plaintiffs: Preserve evidence that the policy continues in some form (communications to subsets of employees, inconsistent pay practices), and seek discovery targeted at company-wide implementation and monitoring. Move quickly for class certification if class-wide injunctive relief is relevant—certification can change the mootness calculus. If the defendant pays the plaintiff’s past wages, preserve claims for penalties and seek declaratory judgments and records showing whether the policy remains in effect for other employees. Make a record that the defendant’s cessation coincided with litigation and that the defendant has motive to revert.
- For defendants: Create a clear, contemporaneous record of the policy change: written policies, employee acknowledgements, payroll evidence, training, audits, and a compliance program showing institutional commitment. If appropriate, offer a consent decree or a detailed injunctive-order proposal to eliminate the risk of reversion and give the court confidence that the conduct will not recur. The more formal and verifiable the change, the greater the chance the court will find no reasonable expectation of recurrence.

Bottom line. Voluntary policy change does not automatically moot statutory-penalty or injunctive claims. Under Laidlaw (as applied in Cohen), a defendant bears a heavy burden to show the cessation eliminates any reasonable expectation of repetition. The district court must make a fact-specific determination; appellate courts will remand where the record leaves the jurisdictional question open.

## Scenario 3 — MWTA notice omitted but no wage underpayment—what damages or relief can a private plaintiff seek?

Facts. Acme changes its overtime policy but fails to provide the statutorily required written notice to its Minnesota employees under Minn. Stat. § 181.032(f) and (d)(4). No wages are withheld or underpaid; every employee’s paychecks accurately reflect overtime premiums in accordance with the new policy. P sues under the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) alleging the failure to provide the statutorily required notice is itself a violation entitling P to penalties recoverable in private action.

Question. Can a private plaintiff obtain monetary penalties for the mere failure to give notice where no wage underpayment occurred? If not, what relief (if any) is available to a private plaintiff?

Analysis. The Cohen opinion rejected Cohen’s MWTA claim largely because he “point[ed] to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute.” That mirrors a basic interpretive rule used in Cohen regarding MPWA: where a statute provides a specific penalty mechanism (as § 181.101 does via the Commissioner), and where another provision lacks an express private penalty or civil-enforcement clause, a private plaintiff cannot simply read penalties into the statute. Section 181.171 allows a person to bring a civil action for violations of certain MPWA sections and provides that an employer found to have violated those sections is “liable for compensatory damages and other appropriate relief.” But the availability of specific monetary penalties depends on whether the violated section itself provides for such penalties or whether Minnesota law elsewhere authorizes a private collector to recover them.

Applying that framework here:
- If the MWTA or the specific provision requiring notice (here, § 181.032(f), (d)(4) in your hypothetical) expressly authorizes private statutory penalties or damages, a plaintiff may seek those remedies.
- If the statute is silent as to private monetary penalties, the plaintiff may still bring a § 181.171 action seeking compensatory damages and equitable relief (e.g., declaratory relief or an injunction requiring the employer to provide the notice). But where no wage loss has occurred, compensatory damages will typically be zero.
- The plaintiff could seek nominal damages, declaratory relief, attorney’s fees (if provided by statute or equitable authority), and injunctive relief to compel compliance or to prevent future misleading conduct. However, absent explicit statutory penalties, a court is unlikely to award a money penalty to a private plaintiff for a mere notice omission.

Practical consequences and strategy.
- For plaintiffs: If no wage underpayment has occurred, shift the litigation focus to injunctive and declaratory relief and to class action strategies where systemic notice failures injure many employees (even if each employee’s monetary loss is nil). Seek statutory attorney’s fees if the statute permits or, if available, an award of costs and fees under § 181.171 or other authority. Plead alternative theories that tie the deficient notice to other actionable conduct (e.g., misclassification resulting in unpaid overtime) so that the notice failure supports a broader claim that yields monetary relief.
- For defendants: Point out the absence of express statutory penalties and emphasize the lack of compensatory loss. Remediate the notice omission promptly and document remediation efforts (written notice, employee acknowledgments). Offer to provide notice and a declaration of compliance; this can undercut a plaintiff’s basis for injunctive relief and support a mootness argument.

Bottom line. A private plaintiff who suffers no wage loss from a mere notice omission has limited avenues for monetary recovery under Minnesota law unless the MWTA (or another statute) expressly supplies private statutory penalties. Equitable relief and declaratory judgments remain viable, and class litigation can amplify nominal injuries into system-wide remedies, but courts will not lightly invent private penalty remedies where the statute delegates or confines penalty collection elsewhere.

## Scenario 4 — Putative class case where only named plaintiff is made whole—effect on class claims and penalties

Facts. P files a putative class action alleging Acme maintained an unlawful overtime policy affecting 500 similarly situated employees. After suit is filed but before class certification, Acme pays P in full for all claimed unpaid wages and related liquidated damages, and P signs a release of his individual claims. No class has been certified and no class representative has been appointed. P continues to seek class-wide relief and statutory penalties payable to the state. Acme moves to dismiss for lack of Article III jurisdiction and mootness.

Question. Does the payment to the named plaintiff moot the putative class action? Can the named plaintiff continue to represent the class? What can plaintiffs do to keep class claims alive? How do penalties payable to the Commissioner affect the analysis?

Analysis. The interaction of Article III, Rule 23, and mootness doctrine creates distinct rules depending on timing and posture:

1. When the named plaintiff’s claim is satisfied before class certification. If the original named plaintiff’s individual claim becomes moot before the court certifies a class, there is generally no live Article III case or controversy because the plaintiff lacks a personal stake to seek relief on behalf of the putative class. Rule 23 requires an adequate representative with a live claim; without a live plaintiff, the suit cannot proceed as a class action. Courts have consistently held that a defendant’s voluntary payment that fully satisfies a plaintiff’s individual claim may moot the action when no class has been certified. Thus, where certification has not yet occurred, plaintiffs risk losing their case if the defendant pays the named plaintiff.

2. When the class has already been certified. If the court certifies a class before the defendant pays the named plaintiff in full, the case survives because the class as a whole retains live claims, and the class representative’s personal satisfaction does not extinguish the rights of absent class members. A certified class substitutes the interest of the class for the interest of the individual representative, and absent members’ claims continue. In that scenario, a defendant’s payment to the named plaintiff will not moot the case—though the defendant may seek decertification or settlement approaches.

3. Replacement of a named plaintiff. If the named plaintiff’s claim is mooted pre-certification, plaintiffs may attempt to replace the named representative with another class member whose claims are live. Courts sometimes permit substitution or amendment to name a new plaintiff, but doing so requires the court’s leave and the showing of timely action and adequate representation. Courts are wary of attempts to import a “new plaintiff” solely to avoid mootness; courts will scrutinize whether the substitution preserves Article III adverseness.

4. Penalties payable to the Commissioner. The Cohen opinion highlights an additional wrinkle: where the remedy sought is a statutory penalty payable to a public entity (the Commissioner), the named plaintiff’s being made whole may not necessarily deprive the plaintiff of standing to pursue forward-looking equitable relief or penalties if the plaintiff can show a continuing risk of injury and the penalties provide deterrence that indirectly redresses the plaintiff’s interest. Laidlaw recognizes that citizen-plaintiffs may have standing to seek civil penalties payable to the government if those penalties redress an ongoing or threatened harm by encouraging cessation and deterrence. But the same timing caveat applies: if the named plaintiff is fully satisfied and no class has been certified, courts remain likely to find no live controversy for the plaintiff personally—standing to seek penalties payable to the government is not automatic and requires a continuing injury or risk of future violations.

Applying these principles to the facts. P’s individual claim is fully satisfied; no class has been certified. Under established practice and the reasoning employed in Cohen, the court is likely to find that P lacks a continuing personal stake to maintain the action as a putative class action. The case may be dismissed for lack of jurisdiction unless plaintiffs quickly (a) move to certify the class before P’s claim is mooted, (b) substitute a live class representative, or (c) show that an exception applies (e.g., the defendant’s payment was part of a collusive scheme to moot the case, or the plaintiff retains a viable claim for injunctive relief or other relief not satisfied by payment).

What plaintiffs should do.
- Move for class certification early—ideally before defendants can satisfy the named plaintiff’s claim. Early certification protects the class cause of action from strategic mooting by the defendant.
- If the defendant pays the named plaintiff before certification, promptly move to substitute a new representative under Rule 23; explain why substitution is appropriate and show the new representative’s adequacy.
- Preserve non-monetary claims such as injunctions or declaratory relief that might not be mooted by payment of monetary relief, and marshal evidence that the defendant’s conduct continues to threaten class members (and therefore that an Article III controversy remains).
- If penalties payable to the Commissioner are sought, seek to develop a factual record showing reasonable likelihood of recurrence to support standing to seek penalties under Laidlaw.

What defendants should do.
- If a settlement payment to the named plaintiff is defensible, use it to argue there is no live controversy; but be careful—courts will scrutinize payments made solely to defeat class actions and may find them collusive.
- If you want finality, consider obtaining a global resolution (settlement with class release or seeking court approval of a settlement) rather than paying only the named plaintiff. If defendants attempt a “pocket payment” to the named plaintiff to moot the suit, courts can reject that tactic and remand or require more comprehensive relief.
- If penalties payable to the Commissioner are sought, emphasize that those penalties are different from individual remedies and that the plaintiff lacks a personal stake in a penalty payable to the government unless the plaintiff can show ongoing injury.

Bottom line. A putative class is vulnerable to mootness when the named plaintiff is made whole before certification. Plaintiffs should move early to certify or to substitute a new representative; defendants should avoid piecemeal payments to a single plaintiff if their goal is true finality because courts will be alert to attempts to manipulate the jurisdictional posture.

Conclusion and synthesis

The Cohen decision makes three durable points you should carry into litigation strategy and doctrinal analysis:

1. Statutory text controls who may obtain certain remedies. Minnesota’s MPWA divides enforcement functions: § 181.101 vests the Commissioner with a special enforcement tool (the average-daily-wage penalty), and § 181.171 provides a private civil enforcement vehicle for employees but does not necessarily duplicate the Commissioner's statutory penalties. Read statutory text carefully to identify which remedies are available to private plaintiffs and which are the Commissioner’s alone.

2. Voluntary cessation is an imperfect shield. A defendant’s policy change after litigation begins will not automatically bar a plaintiff’s challenge. Courts apply the Laidlaw standard, requiring careful fact-finding about the sincerity and permanence of any change. Plaintiffs should gather and emphasize evidence of recurrence, while defendants should produce objective proof of comprehensive compliance.

3. Class timing matters. Class actions change the standing and mootness calculus. Plaintiffs must be alert to the risk that defendants will try to moot individual claimants before certification; defendants should be cautious about paying only the named plaintiff if they seek finality. Where penalties are payable to the government rather than the individual, standing and redressability require a careful showing of ongoing harm or a credible threat of recurrence.

These hypotheticals illustrate how statutory interpretation, federal jurisdictional doctrine, and class-action timing intersect in wage-and-hour litigation. When you encounter a new fact pattern, ask first (a) which statute authorizes the relief and to whom it vests enforcement authority; (b) whether the plaintiff has a present, concrete injury and whether that injury persists; and (c) whether the class-action posture changes the analysis. Applying those three lenses will identify the material issues and the most productive litigation strategies.
