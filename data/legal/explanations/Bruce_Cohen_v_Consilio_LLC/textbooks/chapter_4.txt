Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC

Chapter 4: # MWTA Notice Claim and Injunctive Relief: Preservation and Waiver

This chapter explains the Eighth Circuit’s treatment of Bruce Cohen’s Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) notice claim and his request for injunctive relief in Cohen v. Consilio, LLC. The opinion’s disposition turns on two intertwined themes: (1) the statutory and remedial posture of the MWTA notice theory when the plaintiff has been paid what he sought, and (2) appellate preservation and waiver principles that preclude raising new theories or new forms of relief for the first time in briefing. Understanding these doctrines clarifies why the court affirmed dismissal of the MWTA claim and declined to grant the injunctive relief Cohen later requested.

## MWTA theory: alleged failure to provide required notice of pay policy change (§ 181.032(d)(4), (f))

Cohen alleged that Consilio violated Minnesota statutes requiring notice of a change in pay policy—specifically provisions cited by the court as § 181.032(d)(4) and (f). Read in context, Cohen’s MWTA claim rests on an assertion that Consilio failed to provide statutorily required notice when it changed its overtime-pay practice in 2019. That is a notice-and-procedure theory: the statutory command creates an obligation to inform employees of certain pay-rule changes, and a failure to give notice can be wrongful independent of whether the employee ultimately suffers uncompensated lost wages.

Two points matter for how the court treated the claim. First, Cohen had stipulated (for purposes of summary judgment) that Consilio had paid him all overtime and liquidated damages he claimed under the MFLSA. That concession narrows the practical relief available to him under any wage-theft theory: if all wages and MFLSA liquidated damages have been paid, the MWTA claim is not a straightforward vehicle for recovering additional wage awards tied to the same underlying unpaid overtime. Second, the opinion notes that Cohen did not identify—and the court did not find—any statutory authority within the MWTA provisions he invoked that authorizes civil penalties payable directly to an employee for the specific notice violation he asserted. Put simply, the court treated Cohen’s MWTA theory as limited by (a) his stipulation that no unpaid wages remain and (b) the absence of any cited provision creating employee-enforceable civil penalties for the particular MWTA notice violation alleged.

These two facts led the court to conclude there was no meaningful basis on appeal to disturb the district court’s disposition of the MWTA claim. The MWTA theory, as briefed and preserved, did not supply a live claim for additional money damages to Cohen or point to a statutory mechanism awarding the penalties he sought.

## Relief mismatch: stipulation of payment and absence of statutory basis for civil penalties

When evaluating statutory claims for penalties, courts look to whether the statute provides for the particular remedy a plaintiff seeks and to who is the proper payee for any penalties. The Cohen opinion emphasizes that an employee’s stipulation that he “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed” materially affects the remedial landscape. If no unpaid wages remain, a plaintiff must identify some separate statutory right to additional relief (for example, statutory penalties payable to the employee) to avoid mootness or dismissal. Here, Cohen failed to point the court to any MWTA provision that authorizes the civil penalties he requested payable to him on the facts as stipulated.

In short, Cohen’s stipulation eliminated any argument that unpaid wages remained as a basis for relief under the MWTA claim, and the opinion records that Cohen “points to no statutory authority allowing for civil penalties under this statute.” That failure of statutory hook is decisive: the appellate court will not reverse where the plaintiff both (i) has been made whole on wages and (ii) cannot point to a statutory basis for the additional penalties demanded.

## Appellate waiver: lack of developed argument or legal authority to reverse; Milligan waiver doctrine

A central procedural principle in the opinion is the waiver doctrine: issues not supported by developed argument or legal authority are forfeited on appeal. The court relied on Milligan v. City of Red Oak to state that where an appellant’s brief “does not support [an] assertion with any argument or legal authority, he has waived the issue,” and the appellate court will not address it. This doctrine enforces a basic appellate-administered rule: identify the specific error, explain why it is error, and back that explanation with legal authority and reasoned argument.

Applied to Cohen’s MWTA claim, the waiver principle operates two ways. First, Cohen’s stipulation and his failure to invoke a statutory source for employee penalties left him with little substantive argument to advance. Second, on appeal he did not meaningfully develop any alternative legal authority that could have supported reversal (for example, by pointing to a MWTA provision authorizing the penalties or explaining why the stipulated payment left a live controversy). The Eighth Circuit therefore treated the MWTA challenge as waived to the extent it was not meaningfully briefed and affirmed the district court on that claim.

For students: when reading opinions, watch carefully for language like “points to no statutory authority” and citations to waiver cases. Those cues often indicate that the court’s ruling rests as much on the appellant’s failures in advocacy and preservation as on the underlying statutory merits.

## Injunctive relief evolution: complaint vs. summary judgment briefing

Cohen’s requested injunctive relief underwent a substantive change during the litigation. The complaint sought certain particular forms of injunctive relief, but Cohen later abandoned those specific requests and, in his summary judgment briefing, sought a different form of injunctive relief—namely, an affirmative order requiring Consilio to rescind its 2019 policy. The court treated this as a change in the relief sought rather than merely a refinement of the request.

The district court did not consider this newly proposed remedy, and the Eighth Circuit held that it was not properly before the courts because Cohen introduced it only in briefing rather than by amending his complaint. The opinion cites InfoDeli, LLC to reiterate the settled rule: new claims or new forms of relief should be raised by amendment (via Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a)), not by asserting them for the first time in opposition papers. That rule serves important procedural and fairness objectives: it gives the defendant proper notice of the claim, allows the district court to frame the case, and ensures that discovery and adjudication proceed on a known record.

Put another way, a party cannot change the nature of the lawsuit in its brief and expect the court to treat the new request as if it were part of the complaint. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s refusal to consider Cohen’s late-shifted injunctive demand on that basis.

## Procedural bar: Rule 15(a) and why courts reject new theories raised only in briefing

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a) governs amendments to pleadings and provides the mechanism for adding new claims or altering the relief sought. The opinion cites Rule 15(a) to underline that amendment—where appropriate and timely—is the proper procedural path to present materially different claims or remedies to the court. The policy reasons for this rule are pragmatic and equitable: an amendment puts the opposing party on notice, permits targeted discovery and response, and allows the district court to manage the litigation with the full roster of claims and remedies identified.

InfoDeli, LLC (as cited by the Eighth Circuit) encapsulates the court’s refusal to entertain new claims shoehorned into briefing: courts expect adherence to pleading rules and will not reward litigants who bypass them by springing new theories in motion practice. The appellate court’s affirmance on the injunctive-relief point thus rests on familiar procedural grounds rather than a merits judgment about whether rescission of the 2019 policy would, in theory, be appropriate.

For students: when reading an opinion, identify whether the court’s refusal to consider a remedy is based on substantive law or on procedural posture. Here, the district court’s non-consideration—and the appellate affirmation—was procedural: the remedy was not pleaded and so was not properly before the court.

## Bottom line from the opinion

The Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s dismissal of Cohen’s MWTA notice claim and his request for injunctive relief for two related reasons grounded in the appellate record and basic procedural rules. First, Cohen had stipulated that he was paid the wages he sought and failed to identify statutory authority for additional civil penalties under the MWTA provisions he invoked; second, he attempted to obtain a materially different form of injunction (rescission of the 2019 policy) only in summary judgment briefing rather than by amending his complaint as Rule 15(a) requires. Because Cohen offered no developed argument or statutory hook to reverse the MWTA ruling and did not properly preserve the modified injunctive demand, the court treated those points as waived or unpreserved and affirmed.

When you read similar opinions, pay attention to three signals: stipulations that narrow the live controversy, whether the plaintiff identifies a statutory source for the relief sought, and whether the claim or remedy was pled (or properly amended) before being argued in briefing. Those procedural and remedial details frequently determine appellate outcomes as much as the underlying merits.
