Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC

Chapter 1: # Case Background, Facts, and Procedural Posture

## Parties and roles
This case arises from an employment dispute between Bruce Cohen, an hourly licensed-attorney document reviewer, and his employer, Consilio, LLC, a nationwide legal document review company with offices in Minnesota. Cohen sued on his own behalf and as a putative class representative for other Minnesota-based hourly licensed-attorney reviewers. The parties’ identities and roles are important because the asserted violations concern Minnesota wage statutes as applied to employees working for a company that operates nationwide but maintains Minnesota offices.

## 2019 policy change
In July 2019 Consilio sent an email to Minnesota-based hourly licensed-attorney reviewers announcing a new overtime policy to take effect in August 2019. The email eliminated the company’s overtime premium pay and stated that affected employees would be paid “at [their] base pay rate” for all hours worked. That corporate communication is the operative factual touchstone for Cohen’s claims: he alleges Consilio’s change deprived him and similarly situated employees of overtime pay to which they were entitled under Minnesota law.

## Claims filed in August 2020
Cohen filed suit in August 2020, asserting three state-law claims based on the 2019 policy change. His complaint invoked (1) the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), Minn. Stat. §§ 181.101 and 181.171; (2) the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), Minn. Stat. §§ 177.25 and 177.27; and (3) the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), Minn. Stat. § 181.032. His pleaded remedies sought unpaid overtime wages, MFLSA liquidated damages (a statutory double-recovery measure), statutory penalties under the various statutes, and injunctive relief to address the ongoing application of the policy. The complaint therefore sought both make-whole monetary relief for alleged underpayment and statutory penalties and injunctions intended to deter or prevent future violations.

## Consilio’s post-suit payments
After the suit was filed, Consilio paid $256,010.01 to the group of Minnesota licensed-attorney reviewers to cover the overtime wages and liquidated damages demanded under Cohen’s MFLSA claim. Cohen’s individual share of that payment was $3,225.21 in overtime and $462 in liquidated damages. Those payments materially narrowed the live controversy: they resolved the particular wage and liquidated-damages amounts Cohen alleged were owed under the MFLSA.

## Key stipulations and narrowed dispute
Consistent with the payments, the parties stipulated that Cohen “has been paid for all overtime wages he alleges he is owed in this lawsuit,” and that he “does not dispute that he has also been paid any liquidated damages to which he alleges he is entitled under the [MFLSA].” The parties could not agree about statutory penalties, however. Cohen sought substantial penalties under the MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA; Consilio argued the statutes do not authorize such penalties in these circumstances. Because the core wage and liquidated-damages claims were acknowledged as satisfied, the litigation was narrowed to the question whether Cohen may obtain statutory penalties and certain forms of injunctive relief.

## Summary judgment posture in the district court
The parties jointly proposed that the district court resolve Consilio’s summary judgment motion on the statutory claims (MPWA, MFLSA, and MWTA) before additional discovery or further litigation, stipulating that if the motion were granted in full the case would be over. The district court granted summary judgment to Consilio on all three statutory claims. Because the parties had elected to present the statutory-interpretation issues at summary judgment, the district court’s order disposed of the entire case in the district court, prompting Cohen’s appeal.

## Appellate standard of review
On appeal the court reviews the grant of summary judgment de novo. Consistent with the district-court standard explained in the opinion, the appellate court must “view[] the evidence and the inferences that may be reasonably drawn from the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party.” Summary judgment is appropriate only if there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Because Cohen is the nonmoving party on appeal, the court construes the record and permissible inferences favorably to him while assessing whether the statutory claims were correctly resolved as a matter of law.
