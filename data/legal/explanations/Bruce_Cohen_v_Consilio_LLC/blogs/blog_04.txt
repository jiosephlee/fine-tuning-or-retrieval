# Preservation Matters: The MWTA Claim and the Perils of Changing Remedies Midstream

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Cohen v. Consilio, LLC offers a compact but instructive lesson about two oft-overlooked features of modern litigation: the limits of appellate review when a party reframes its relief late in the case, and the practical obligation to align claims, remedies, and procedural posture before pressing an appeal. At first blush, this is an overtime-pay case; on closer inspection, it is a preservation case. The court affirmed dismissal of the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA) claim and declined to entertain a newly framed request for injunctive relief that Cohen raised in opposition to summary judgment. The opinion underscores (1) the risk of waiver and forfeiture when a party fails to develop arguments or to seek leave to amend the complaint, and (2) the distinct procedures—Rule 15 and appellate briefing rules—that govern introducing new theories of relief in district courts and on appeal.

Practitioners who litigate wage-and-hour claims (or any statutory claim for statutory and equitable relief) should take note: late changes in the remedy you seek, or a failure to tie a requested remedy to statutory authority, can be fatal to appellate review.

## What the court did

The Eighth Circuit affirmed summary judgment dismissal as to Cohen’s MWTA claim and declined to consider his request, made for the first time in opposition to summary judgment, that the court order Consilio to rescind its 2019 overtime policy. The panel relied on two principles.

First, the court applied ordinary forfeiture and waiver doctrine: Cohen had not developed a sustained, statutory- or precedent-based argument that the MWTA furnished a damages or penalty remedy, and he did not adequately present or preserve an argument in the district court or on appeal. The opinion relies on the familiar (and recently cited) line of Eighth Circuit decisions holding that failure to develop an argument in briefing amounts to abandonment and forfeiture — the opinion cites InfoDeli, LLC v. W. Robidoux, Inc., and earlier cases such as Milligan v. City of Red Oak. That jurisprudence places the burden on litigants to present legal authority and argumentation in district court and on appeal in order to preserve issues for review.

Second, the court emphasized pleading and amendment rules. Cohen sought different specific forms of injunctive relief in his complaint but then shifted course in his summary-judgment briefing to seek rescission of a policy he alleged was unlawful. The panel explained that a litigant who wishes to advance "new claims" or materially different remedies should do so through an amended complaint under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15, not by springing them in an opposition brief. Because Cohen did not seek leave to amend, and because the district court reasonably declined to consider remedies that were not properly pleaded, the appellate court treated the newly urged relief as not properly before it.

## Why the court’s reasoning makes sense — and its limits

The court’s reasoning is pragmatic and steeped in institutional comity. District courts adjudicate cases on the claims and remedies the parties present, and they depend on adversarial presentation to identify the legal contours of relief. Allowing litigants to transform their requested relief later in the case—without amendment and without giving the district court and the opposing party a chance to respond—would undermine notice and could unfairly sandbag defendants. The Eighth Circuit’s reliance on InfoDeli and Rule 28(a)(9)(A) (the rule requiring appellate briefs to include “appellant’s contentions and the reasons for them, with citations”) simply enforces these bedrock procedural norms.

There are nevertheless normative tensions. Modern summary-judgment practice often features fluid claims and iterative briefing; parties learn facts through discovery and may reasonably adjust the relief they seek. Courts must balance procedural regularity against the equitable imperative of resolving statutory rights. If a litigant frames a theoretically available but unpleaded equitable remedy late in the game, a strict bar on consideration can feel formalistic—particularly where the defendant’s policy is conceded to be unlawful (a circumstance the Eighth Circuit acknowledges elsewhere in the opinion). The court tried to cabin those concerns by noting that Cohen had previously sought other forms of injunctive relief, but that did not save his new, specific request from waiver.

Finally, the opinion correctly reminds practitioners that different remedies can implicate different pleading and standing issues; rescission or affirmative injunctive relief often requires distinct factual showings and may implicate mootness or redressability in ways that monetary claims do not. Thus, the procedural requirement to amend isn’t mere formality—it helps ensure courts and parties address whether equitable relief is appropriate given the available facts and statutory framework.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Several doctrinal takeaways flow from this disposition.

First, the decision reinforces the line between mere advocacy in briefs and formal pleading. Parties seeking new forms of relief should not expect appellate courts to entertain them if they were not pleaded and litigated below. That has particular salience in wage-and-hour litigation, where plaintiffs may seek a mix of statutory damages, penalties payable to state agencies, liquidated damages, and injunctive relief. Each remedy can raise distinct procedural and substantive hurdles; litigants must choose their path deliberately and, if they pivot, must use Rule 15 to do so.

Second, the opinion signals that appellate courts will not loosen forfeiture rules simply because statutory authorities are unsettled or because a defect in the district court’s analysis might otherwise exist. Cohen criticized the district court for providing “little to no discussion” of the MWTA claim, but the panel treated the omission as politically unremarkable given the failure to preserve the issue. That may discourage plaintiffs from “throwing in” alternative remedies in opposition briefing and expecting a later do-over on appeal.

Third, the decision may incentivize earlier engagement with statutory text and administrative procedures. The Eighth Circuit’s broader opinion (which, in other parts, discusses whether certain penalties are collectable only by the Commissioner) shows how statutory design can shape both remedies and process. Plaintiffs and their counsel must map statutory enforcement schemes before selecting remedies—penalties reserved for administrative collection cannot necessarily be commandeered as private causes of action without careful briefing and litigation strategy.

Fourth, lower courts will likely cite this decision when trimming untimely or unpleaded requests for equitable relief. The combined reliance on Rule 15 and appellate forfeiture doctrine gives trial judges solid footing to deny last-minute remedial pivots as lacking proper procedural foundation.

## Practical lessons for practitioners

The opinion offers concrete lessons about crafting litigation strategy and preserving issues for appeal.

First, plead with your remedies in mind. If you might ultimately seek rescission, an order requiring policy changes, or other equitable relief, put those requests in your complaint (or move to amend early). That gives you the best claim to appellate review if the district court declines to grant your requested relief.

Second, if you decide during the case that a different form of relief is necessary, move to amend under Rule 15 promptly. Rule 15 favors amendment, but courts are within their discretion to deny late amendments that prejudice the opposing party or disrupt the schedule. If you seek leave, explain the factual or legal development that justifies the change and be prepared to explain why the motion is timely.

Third, develop statutory arguments in the record. The Eighth Circuit’s reliance on waiver and forfeiture is rooted in the absence of developed argumentation regarding statutory authority for particular penalties or remedies. Don’t assume the panel will fill gaps for you; marshal statutory text, precedent, and administrative schemes in district court briefing.

Fourth, remember that appellate rules matter. Appellate counsel should ensure that briefs comply with Rule 28 and related local rules; failure to identify and brief an issue sufficiently can lead to waiver on appeal.

Finally, consider strategic sequencing. Where statutory penalties are payable to a state agency rather than to the employee, or where the employer has already paid monetary damages, consider whether injunctive relief is your only meaningful remedy — and if so, articulate that clearly from the outset so that the district court can evaluate standing and the availability of equitable relief.

## Conclusion

Cohen v. Consilio is not a sweeping pronouncement about the substantive scope of the MWTA or about when rescission is appropriate. Instead, it is a reminder of litigation fundamentals: you must present and preserve the remedies you seek, and you must tie those remedies to statutory authority and proper procedural practice. For litigators, the takeaway is straightforward: preserve early, brief clearly, and amend formally when your theory of relief changes. Courts will enforce those boundaries, and failure to do so risks losing review on appeal.
