# When Paying Up Isn’t the End: Voluntary Cessation, “Pick‑Offs,” and Injunctive Relief in Wage Cases

## Why this case matters

The Eighth Circuit’s decision in Cohen v. Consilio, LLC is a useful reminder that defendants who “pay up” after a suit is filed do not always succeed in foreclosing all relief. Employers can extinguish a plaintiff’s claims for past money damages by tendering payment, but the doctrine of voluntary cessation and Laidlaw’s standing principles mean plaintiffs may still have live claims for civil penalties payable to the state and for injunctive relief designed to stop ongoing or future violations. At the same time, Cohen underscores a cautionary lesson for plaintiffs: if you want injunctive relief or to preserve class/representative claims aimed at deterrence, you must plead them clearly and build the factual record early. And for defendants, the decision highlights both the utility and limits of tactical tenders — especially in the post‑Campbell‑Ewald era where “pick‑offs” can raise thorny mootness and jurisdictional questions.

The case arose after Consilio adopted a new overtime policy eliminating premium overtime pay for certain Minnesota‑based hourly licensed‑attorney document reviewers. Plaintiff Bruce Cohen sued under several Minnesota wage statutes, seeking overtime pay, liquidated damages, statutory penalties, and injunctive relief. Consilio paid the overtime and liquidated damages. The district court granted summary judgment for the employer; the Eighth Circuit affirmed in part, vacated in part, and remanded—most notably vacating the summary judgment dismissal of Cohen’s Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) penalties claim and sending that issue back for the district court to assess whether it retained jurisdiction.

The opinion is compact, but it ties together several recurring themes in modern wage‑and‑hour litigation: the difference between relief that benefits a private plaintiff directly (wages, liquidated damages) and relief that benefits the public (statutory penalties payable to an agency), the continuing vitality of Laidlaw’s voluntary‑cessation inquiry in assessing mootness, and the procedural importance of preserving injunction claims. Those themes are worth unpacking for practitioners and courts alike.

## How the court reasoned

The Eighth Circuit addressed three discrete buckets of relief: (1) penalties under Minnesota’s Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), (2) penalties under the MFLSA (state analogue), and (3) penalties under the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA), plus injunctive relief.

On the MPWA claim (Minn. Stat. § 181.101), the court applied ordinary statutory interpretation. While the MPWA allows employees to bring a civil action under § 181.171, the penalty machinery in § 181.101—an “average daily wage” penalty—can be triggered only after the Commissioner of Labor serves a demand and then collects penalties if the employer does not pay within ten days. The court concluded that the statute’s text ties the collection of that particular penalty to the Commissioner’s office, not to employees directly. The result: the district court did not err in denying Cohen an “average daily wage” award under § 181.101 because the statutory text reserves that remedy to the Commissioner.

By contrast, the MFLSA claim presented a different question. Cohen had been paid (and stipulated he had been paid) all overtime wages and liquidated damages, but he continued to press for statutory penalties. The Eighth Circuit recognized that civil penalties—even those payable to the government—can sometimes cure the plaintiff’s Article III injury by deterring ongoing or future unlawful conduct (citing Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw). But the court also emphasized that standing and mootness must be evaluated for each form of relief sought and throughout the case. Because the parties had not developed the factual record about whether Consilio’s unlawful overtime policy remained in place (and because that factual question is pivotal to whether the case is moot under Laidlaw/City of Mesquite principles), the court vacated the district court’s dismissal of the MFLSA penalty claim and remanded for the district court to determine jurisdiction—i.e., whether the voluntary cessation was permanent or whether the plaintiff retained a credible threat of future injury.

The MWTA claim, by contrast, was dismissed without extended discussion because Cohen failed to develop a statutory or factual argument showing a right to civil penalties under the MWTA, and he had stipulated that he received all compensation owed. Finally, Cohen’s request for an injunction to rescind the 2019 policy was not considered on appeal because he had altered his requested injunctive relief in briefing without amending the complaint or preserving the argument properly; the court treated that as forfeited.

Judge Kobes’ concurrence added a useful caveat: he was skeptical (but not definitive) about plaintiff standing to seek statutory penalties under Laidlaw, noting that the parties had not briefed that question, and he agreed remand to resolve mootness was the appropriate course.

## Putting the decision in context: Laidlaw, Campbell‑Ewald, and “pick‑offs”

Cohen sits at the intersection of two modern doctrinal currents. First is Laidlaw’s teaching about voluntary cessation and citizen‑plaintiff standing: even when a defendant stops an alleged wrongdoing, a plaintiff can retain standing to seek relief that has deterrent effect if the defendant’s cessation is not “absolutely clear” and if the challenged conduct could reasonably be expected to recur. That principle preserves courts’ ability to issue injunctions or awards serving public or prospective interests even after a monetary remedy for past harm has been offered.

Second is the more recent concern about defendants’ strategic tenders or “pick‑offs.” After the Supreme Court’s treatment of tenders in Campbell‑Ewald and subsequent cases, defendants sometimes try to extinguish representative or class claims by paying an individual plaintiff the full individual relief before class certification or before the plaintiff can expand relief to a class. Courts have split on whether such tenders moot the plaintiff’s claim and on how to treat the residual interests of the class or in deterrence. Cohen makes plain that tendering wages and liquidated damages will often not be the endgame: if statutory penalties are payable to the government or if injunctive relief is sought to stop an ongoing policy, defendants’ tender may not moot the whole case.

But Cohen also shows the limits of this protection for plaintiffs. If a plaintiff does not preserve an injunction claim in the complaint or fails to develop facts showing an ongoing policy (the “credible threat” needed under Laidlaw), a defendant’s tender may effectively neuter the case. That is precisely why the Eighth Circuit remanded the MFLSA penalty claim for a focused jurisdictional inquiry instead of accepting a premature summary‑judgment dismissal.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

For plaintiffs: Cohen underscores two important doctrinal points. One, differentiate the kinds of relief you seek and explain why each form of relief remains necessary even after monetary payments: if you seek statutory penalties payable to the state or injunctive relief, explain how those remedies will redress ongoing or future injuries and how enforcement would work. Two, plead injunctive relief clearly and early; don’t try to introduce a new form of equitable relief for the first time in opposition briefings or summary‑judgment papers. Failing to do so risks forfeiture.

For courts: the opinion is a reminder that mootness and standing questions require a fact‑specific inquiry. When defendants voluntarily cease conduct or tender individual relief, courts should not reflexively dismiss remaining claims without evaluating whether the cessation is permanent and whether the plaintiff has a continuing interest in deterrent remedies. This dovetails with Laidlaw’s caution against treating voluntary cessation as inherently rendering a case moot.

For defendants: the decision tempers the apparent allure of pick‑offs. Paying past wages and liquidated damages may resolve the individual plaintiff’s direct monetary claims, but it may not foreclose statutory penalties or injunctive remedies, particularly where those remedies serve the public interest or are payable to a state agency. Tactical tenders thus carry a risk: they buy a partial peace, but they may leave open the possibility of agency‑level penalties or a renewed plaintiff challenge if the policy persists.

For class litigation: Cohen is another reminder that representative litigation incentives (deterrence, systemic change) are often not fully satisfied by single‑plaintiff tenders. Plaintiffs seeking to preserve class or representative claims should seek class certification early where appropriate or ensure their complaints and discovery show ongoing, class‑wide practices that justify injunctive or statutory relief beyond individual make‑whole payments.

## Practical guidance for litigants and counsel

1. Plead and preserve injunctive relief from the outset. If your theory depends on stopping an ongoing policy (e.g., an illegal overtime policy), the complaint should say so and identify the relief sought: rescission of the policy, injunctive prohibition of its enforcement, and prospective monitoring or reporting if needed. Do not rely on introducing new equitable requests only at summary judgment or on appeal.

2. Build a factual record about current policies early. Discovery should target whether the defendant’s policy is still in force, who implements it, and whether pay practice changes were formal and durable. The stronger the record that a policy persists, the less likely a court will find a voluntary cessation to be “absolutely clear.”

3. Distinguish remedies in your pleadings and briefs. Explain why statutory penalties payable to a government agency can still provide non‑speculative deterrent benefits that remedy an Article III injury, and show how injunctive relief would redress a continuing or future injury.

4. Anticipate and litigate “pick‑off” strategies. If a defendant tenders individual relief, move quickly to preserve classwide or representative relief (seek class certification if fit), and file clear arguments on why the tender does not moot penalties or equitable claims. Be prepared to show the cessation is not permanent.

5. Consider coordinating with state agencies. When statutory penalties are administered by a state Commissioner or agency (as under Minnesota’s § 181.101), consider whether parallel administrative complaints or coordination with the agency are prudent. The statute’s allocation of collection authority to the Commissioner can materially affect remedies available in private litigation.

## Conclusion

Cohen v. Consilio is not a blockbuster ruling, but it is a practical and doctrinally instructive one. It reiterates that voluntary payment of past wages does not inevitably end litigation, especially when penalties payable to the public and injunctive relief remain in play. At the same time, it reminds plaintiffs that preserving those forms of relief takes care: plead them expressly, build the record about current practices, and resist the temptation to rely on last‑minute equitable pivots. For defendants, the decision tempers the promise of quick tolling through tactical tenders: paying the plaintiff may win one battle but leave other, institutional battles — and the courts’ jurisdictional scrutiny — very much intact.
