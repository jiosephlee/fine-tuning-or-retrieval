Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Under what specific circumstances does a 'failure to report' to the police become a fatal flaw in an asylum claim?
Question:
The court mentions that a failure to report to the government can 'undercut' a claim but is not necessarily 'fatal.' They also mention that an applicant can satisfy the requirement by showing that reporting would have been 'futile or dangerous.' What kind of evidence would the court require to prove 'futility'? In this case, since the officer told Jimenez that 'police officers were contributing to the problem,' does that count as sufficient evidence of a futile reporting environment?
Answer:
A failure to report past persecution to the government is not an automatic bar to asylum; however, it creates a significant evidentiary hurdle by "undercutting" an applicant's claim that the government is unable or unwilling to protect them. To overcome this, an applicant must demonstrate that reporting would have been "futile or dangerous."

**The Standard for Futility and Danger**
Under the doctrine established in *Jimenez*, the court evaluates the government's response by looking at whether the government stopped or attempted to stop the persecutors, or offered protection such as protective custody. Because the court cannot determine how the government would have reacted without notice, a lack of reporting generally makes it difficult for an applicant to prove the government's inaction.

To satisfy the "futility or danger" exception, the applicant must provide evidence that goes beyond a mere lack of reporting. The court looks for evidence that indicates the government is systemically unable or unwilling to provide protection.

**Evidence Required to Prove Futility**
The *Jimenez* court clarifies what does—and does not—constitute sufficient evidence of a futile reporting environment:

1.  **Systemic vs. Individual Failures:** Citing *Osorio-Morales*, the court notes that evidence of "systemic, rather than individual, failures" is often required. A "one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer" is insufficient to demonstrate a government-wide inability or unwillingness to protect a victim.
2.  **Evidence of Government Action:** The court also looks for contradictory evidence of government involvement. In *Jimenez*, the court rejected the petitioners' claim of futility because the record showed that the government was actually aware of the property in question and had attempted several "searches and seizures" to address criminal conduct. This evidence suggested the government was capable of (and was attempting) to act, thereby undermining the argument that the persecutors were so "closely aligned" with the government that reporting would be futile.
3.  **Access to Resources:** The court also considers whether the applicant had reasonable avenues for reporting. In *Jimenez*, the court highlighted that the petitioners had personal ties to the government (the father and Jimenez both worked for the government) yet failed to report wrongdoing or corruption through those professional channels.

**Application to the Officer's Statement**
Regarding your specific question, the officer’s statement that "police officers were contributing to the problem" does **not** count as sufficient evidence of a futile reporting environment in this case. 

The court explicitly characterizes that interaction as a "one-off conversation" that does not reflect the "Colombian government’s position." Because the court requires evidence of systemic failure, a single officer's comment—especially when contrasted with the fact that the government was actively attempting searches and seizures at the location—is insufficient to establish that the government as a whole was unable or unwilling to help.
