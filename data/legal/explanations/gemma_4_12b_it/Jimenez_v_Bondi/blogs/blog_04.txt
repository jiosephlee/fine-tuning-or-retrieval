Title: Blog about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

# Internal Relocation: Practical Geography vs. Systemic Infrastructure

In the final stages of an asylum claim, the "internal relocation" analysis often serves as a grueling geographic hurdle. Once an applicant establishes a well-founded fear of persecution, the government may still deny relief by demonstrating that the applicant could reasonably avoid that harm by moving to another part of their home country. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit confronted the central tension of this analysis: should a court weigh the "practical geography" of a specific threat against the "systemic infrastructure" of a failing state? By opting for a localized, practical approach, the court reinforced a high bar for applicants seeking to prove that a country is too dangerous to inhabit in any capacity.

The facts of *Jimenez* provide a stark backdrop for this inquiry. The petitioners, a family from Bogota, fled after being targeted by "Don Rafa," a narco-trafficking guerrilla leader. The threats were sparked by a property dispute in which the petitioner, a provisional lawyer, investigated a tenant who turned out to be a high-ranking criminal. While the petitioners argued that the threat was systemic—linked to a criminal leader with ties to the presidency—the Tenth Circuit focused on the specific scope of the violence. Because the threats were tied to a specific neighborhood and a specific property dispute, the court found that the family could have "mitigated any potential for future persecution" by relocating to the countryside, specifically to the home of the petitioner’s mother.

The court’s reasoning highlights a significant doctrinal preference for "geographic" safety over "institutional" safety. The petitioners attempted to argue that relocation was unworkable due to the country's crumbling judicial infrastructure and the pervasive nature of the persecutors' political ties. However, the court dismissed these arguments as "haphazard," choosing instead to focus on the fact that the persecutors told the family to "move out of the neighborhood" rather than to flee the country entirely. By doing so, the court signaled that if a threat can be geographically isolated, the broader decay of a nation's rule of law may not be sufficient to preclude asylum.

This distinction creates a difficult landscape for asylum seekers from countries characterized by "omnipresent" corruption or decentralized violence. If an applicant cannot prove that a persecutor has the reach or the intent to pursue them across provincial lines, the court is increasingly likely to find a "safe coordinate" within the country. In *Jimenez*, the court essentially prioritized the *availability* of a safe house over the *quality* of the national government. For the petitioners, the existence of a relative’s home in the countryside was enough to override their arguments regarding the nationalized risk posed by a narco-trafficking leader.

The downstream effect of this reasoning is a narrowing of the "systemic" argument in immigration law. To succeed in the future, applicants may need to do more than describe a broken judicial system or a corrupt executive; they must proactively demonstrate that their specific persecutors possess the logistical capacity or political mandate to track them nationwide. By emphasizing practical geography, the Tenth Circuit has reinforced a standard where asylum is not granted based on the general state of a country’s safety, but rather on the impossibility of finding a single, specific location where the shadow of the persecutor does not reach.
