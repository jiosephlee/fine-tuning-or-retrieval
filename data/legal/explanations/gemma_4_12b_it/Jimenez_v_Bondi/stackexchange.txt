Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### How does the 'administrative record' limitation affect the scope of the appellate court's review?
Question:
The court states that its review of the BIA’s decision is limited to 'the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.' As a student, I'm struggling to understand how this limits the court. If a petitioner submits a new, compelling piece of evidence during the appeal process that clearly proves they were persecuted, can the court ignore it because it wasn't in the original record provided to the IJ/BIA?
Answer:
The "administrative record" limitation means that the appellate court’s review is confined strictly to the evidence that was presented to, and considered by, the lower agency (in this case, the Board of Immigration Appeals). 

### The Doctrine of the Administrative Record
In administrative law and immigration appeals, the "administrative record" is the body of evidence—including testimonies, documents, and exhibits—that the petitioner submitted to the Immigration Judge (IJ) and the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA). When a court limits its review to this record, it is essentially saying that the appellate court is not conducting a *de novo* trial. Instead, it is reviewing the *legality* and *reasonableness* of the agency's decision based on the facts the agency was allowed to see.

The purpose of this limitation is to ensure due process. It prevents an appellate court from considering evidence that the lower agency never had the opportunity to evaluate. If a petitioner fails to include a piece of evidence in their initial application, they have deprived the agency of the chance to weigh that evidence. Consequently, the appellate court cannot "fix" that omission by considering the evidence for the first time during the appeal.

### Application to *Jimenez v. Bondi*
In *Jimenez*, the court explicitly cites this limitation under the Standard of Review:

> "Our review of the Board’s denial of asylum relief is limited to ‘the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.’" (quoting 8 U.S.C. § 1252(b)(4)(A)).

Because of this limitation, your hypothetical scenario—submitting a "new, compelling piece of evidence" during the appeal—would likely result in the court ignoring that evidence. If the evidence was not in the original record provided to the IJ/BIA, it is outside the scope of the court's jurisdiction to consider it. 

The court's role is to determine if the BIA’s decision was supported by "substantial evidence" within that specific record. In *Jimenez*, the court found that the record did not show that the Petitioners suffered persecution or that the government was unable to protect them. Even if the Petitioners were to present a "compelling" new document during the appeal, the court would likely maintain that it cannot use that document to overturn the BIA's decision because that document was not part of the "administrative record on which the order of removal [was] based."

**Summary for your studies:** If the evidence isn't in the record, it effectively doesn't exist for the purposes of the appellate review. To have a piece of evidence considered by the court, it must first be entered into the record at the initial agency level.

### What is the threshold for 'cumulative harm' when no physical violence has occurred?
Question:
The court acknowledges that they consider incidents collectively because 'cumulative effects' can constitute persecution. However, they also say 'threats alone are insufficient.' In this case, the petitioners faced multiple threats, a kidnapping/murder of a colleague, and a physical confrontation with their son. If there is no 'lasting physical harm,' what specific type of 'offensive' harm must be shown to cross the line from 'stressful' to 'persecution'?
Answer:
To determine the threshold for "cumulative harm" in the absence of physical violence, one must distinguish between experiences that are "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling" and those that rise to the level of "persecution."

Under the standard articulated in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, persecution is defined as the infliction of "suffering or harm" in a way that is "regarded as offensive." Crucially, the court specifies that such harm must entail "more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty." While the court recognizes that multiple incidents should be considered collectively because "cumulative effects" may constitute persecution, this doctrine does not lower the qualitative threshold of the harm itself.

The "line" between stressful events and persecution is defined by the following three limitations established in the opinion:

**1. Threats Alone are Insufficient**
The court explicitly states that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution." Even when multiple threats are received—such as the threatening phone calls and brochures received by the petitioners—they do not reach the threshold unless accompanied by actual "offensive" harm.

**2. Emotional and Psychological Harm Alone is Insufficient**
The petitioners in *Jimenez* argued that their "lasting emotional and psychological harm" (e.g., insomnia and panic attacks) should count toward the cumulative threshold. The court rejected this, holding that while these events may be "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling," they are insufficient to rise to the level of persecution on their own. The court notes that it has "rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone."

**3. The "Offensive" Requirement**
To cross the line, the harm must be "offensive" and "more than mere restrictions or sporadic threats." The court uses comparative case law to illustrate this threshold:
*   **Insufficient:** Four telephone threats and one vague in-person threat.
*   **Insufficient:** A 10-hour arrest and detention combined with threats (deemed "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling" but not persecution).
*   **Insufficient:** Threats with machetes and being robbed six times (deemed insufficient because the threats were "contingent on compliance with a demand").

**Summary for the Student**
In the context of *Jimenez*, the "cumulative harm" doctrine allows the court to aggregate various incidents to see if their total impact is "offensive." However, if the aggregate consists only of threats and the resulting psychological stress (no matter how severe), the harm remains in the category of "unfortunate circumstances" rather than "persecution." To meet the threshold without physical violence, a petitioner must show harm that is more severe than "stressful"—specifically, harm that is "offensive" and exceeds the level of "sporadic threats" or "restrictions."

### How does the court distinguish between a 'central' motive and a 'tangential' motive in mixed-motive cases?
Question:
I'm confused by the Nexus analysis. The petitioners were clearly involved in politics, but they were also involved in a property dispute. The court ruled the political opinion was 'tangential and subordinate' to the property dispute. What objective test does the court use to decide which motive is 'central'? If a persecutor is angry about both a person's political views and their professional actions, how can the court 'de-couple' those motives to determine the primary driver of the violence?
Answer:
To determine whether a motive is "central" or "tangential" in a mixed-motive case, the court applies a **nexus analysis** centered on whether a statutorily protected ground (such as political opinion) is a primary driver of the persecution.

### The Legal Standard: "At Least One Central Reason"
Under the governing standard in *Jimenez v. Bondi*, an applicant must demonstrate that their protected ground was "**at least one central reason**" for the persecution. The court clarifies that for a motive to be "central," it cannot be:
*   Incidental;
*   Tangential;
*   Superficial; or
*   Subordinate to another reason for harm.

In mixed-motive cases, the court acknowledges that a persecutor may have multiple motives. However, the burden remains on the petitioner to show that at least one of those motives is a "central" reason for the harm inflicted. If the harm is primarily driven by a non-protected ground—such as a personal grudge, a business dispute, or a criminal investigation—and the protected ground plays only a "minor role," the claim fails.

### How the Court "De-couples" Motives
While the opinion does not provide a mathematical formula, it demonstrates an analytical framework for distinguishing between central and tangential motives based on two primary factors: **temporal correlation** and **content of the threats**.

**1. Temporal Correlation (The Timeline)**
The court looks at when the persecution escalated relative to the petitioner's actions. In *Jimenez*, the court noted that the petitioners were initially "undeterred" by threats related to their political party membership and continued their campaigning. The "escalated threats" only began after Jimenez became involved in a specific property dispute and after the antagonist (Don Rafa) became aware of Jimenez's investigation into the property. This timeline suggests that the property dispute, rather than the political opinion, was the catalyst for the intensified violence.

**2. Content of the Threats**
The court analyzes the specific language used by the persecutors to identify the "predominant motive." In this case, the men who threatened the petitioners explicitly accused Jimenez of being a "**snitch**" for the prosecutor’s office and held him responsible for "searches and seizures at the property." 

By focusing on these specific accusations, the court concluded that the primary driver of the violence was Jimenez’s **professional and personal involvement** with Don Rafa (the property dispute). Because the threats were specifically tied to his actions as a provisional lawyer investigating a specific location, the court found that his political opinion was "merely tangential and subordinate" to the fact that he threatened the antagonist's revenue and security.

### Summary
The court distinguishes these motives by identifying the **predominant driver** of the harm. If the evidence shows that the persecution was triggered by a non-protected activity (like a property dispute) and that the protected ground (political opinion) was not a primary motivation for the specific acts of violence, the court will characterize the protected ground as "subordinate" and deny the claim.

### Under what specific circumstances does a 'failure to report' to the police become a fatal flaw in an asylum claim?
Question:
The court mentions that a failure to report to the government can 'undercut' a claim but is not necessarily 'fatal.' They also mention that an applicant can satisfy the requirement by showing that reporting would have been 'futile or dangerous.' What kind of evidence would the court require to prove 'futility'? In this case, since the officer told Jimenez that 'police officers were contributing to the problem,' does that count as sufficient evidence of a futile reporting environment?
Answer:
A failure to report past persecution to the government is not an automatic bar to asylum; however, it creates a significant evidentiary hurdle by "undercutting" an applicant's claim that the government is unable or unwilling to protect them. To overcome this, an applicant must demonstrate that reporting would have been "futile or dangerous."

**The Standard for Futility and Danger**
Under the doctrine established in *Jimenez*, the court evaluates the government's response by looking at whether the government stopped or attempted to stop the persecutors, or offered protection such as protective custody. Because the court cannot determine how the government would have reacted without notice, a lack of reporting generally makes it difficult for an applicant to prove the government's inaction.

To satisfy the "futility or danger" exception, the applicant must provide evidence that goes beyond a mere lack of reporting. The court looks for evidence that indicates the government is systemically unable or unwilling to provide protection.

**Evidence Required to Prove Futility**
The *Jimenez* court clarifies what does—and does not—constitute sufficient evidence of a futile reporting environment:

1.  **Systemic vs. Individual Failures:** Citing *Osorio-Morales*, the court notes that evidence of "systemic, rather than individual, failures" is often required. A "one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer" is insufficient to demonstrate a government-wide inability or unwillingness to protect a victim.
2.  **Evidence of Government Action:** The court also looks for contradictory evidence of government involvement. In *Jimenez*, the court rejected the petitioners' claim of futility because the record showed that the government was actually aware of the property in question and had attempted several "searches and seizures" to address criminal conduct. This evidence suggested the government was capable of (and was attempting) to act, thereby undermining the argument that the persecutors were so "closely aligned" with the government that reporting would be futile.
3.  **Access to Resources:** The court also considers whether the applicant had reasonable avenues for reporting. In *Jimenez*, the court highlighted that the petitioners had personal ties to the government (the father and Jimenez both worked for the government) yet failed to report wrongdoing or corruption through those professional channels.

**Application to the Officer's Statement**
Regarding your specific question, the officer’s statement that "police officers were contributing to the problem" does **not** count as sufficient evidence of a futile reporting environment in this case. 

The court explicitly characterizes that interaction as a "one-off conversation" that does not reflect the "Colombian government’s position." Because the court requires evidence of systemic failure, a single officer's comment—especially when contrasted with the fact that the government was actively attempting searches and seizures at the location—is insufficient to establish that the government as a whole was unable or unwilling to help.

### How does the court weigh 'national' reach of a persecutor against 'localized' threats for the safe relocation analysis?
Question:
The petitioners argued they couldn't relocate because the persecutors were associated with the Colombian government (implying a national threat). However, the court found they could relocate because the threats were to 'move out of the neighborhood.' How does the court distinguish between a persecutor whose reach is limited to a specific city/neighborhood versus one whose reach is national? If the persecutors have ties to the President, isn't the threat inherently national regardless of where the specific incidents occurred?
Answer:
In the context of asylum law, the "safe relocation" analysis focuses on whether an applicant can avoid the specific harm they face by moving to a different part of their home country. In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit distinguishes between the **political stature** of a persecutor and the **geographic scope** of the threat expressed by that persecutor.

### The Legal Standard for Safe Relocation
Under 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i), the government may rebut an applicant’s well-founded fear of future persecution by demonstrating that the applicant "could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the applicant’s country." The court’s task is to determine if such relocation is "reasonably available."

### Geographic Scope vs. Persecutor Identity
The court weighs "national" vs. "localized" reach by looking at the specific instructions or demands issued by the persecutors. The petitioners argued that because their persecutor (Don Rafa) was "closely aligned and associated with the Colombian government," the threat was inherently national and relocation would be impossible.

However, the court rejected this by focusing on the **content of the threats**. The court noted:
> "The men who threatened Petitioners told them to move out of the neighborhood; they did not threaten the family to move out of the country."

By identifying the threat as neighborhood-specific rather than country-wide, the court concluded that moving to a different jurisdiction within Colombia would mitigate the risk. Even if a persecutor has high-level political ties (such as to the President), those ties do not automatically grant the persecutor a "blanket" license to pursue the applicant across the entire nation unless the evidence supports a country-wide reach.

### Evidence of Feasibility
To support the finding that relocation is reasonable, the court also looked for "substantial evidence" of the applicant's ability to live elsewhere safely. In *Jimenez*, the court highlighted two key facts:
1. **Prior Successful Relocation:** The petitioners had already lived in the Bogota countryside for three weeks unharmed before fleeing to the United States.
2. **Rejection of Hypotheticals:** The court explicitly refused to grant asylum based on "hypothetical hardships." While the petitioners argued that national ties made relocation unworkable, the court found these arguments were "haphazardly" briefed and outweighed by the fact that the specific threats were localized to the neighborhood.

### Summary for the Student
The court distinguishes between the **identity/power** of the persecutor and the **geography** of the threat. If a persecutor is a national figure but their specific threat is to "leave this neighborhood," the legal standard for safe relocation is satisfied by relocating to a different neighborhood or city. The court will not treat a localized threat as a national one simply because the persecutor has high-level political connections, provided the record does not show a country-wide pursuit.

### Why does failing an asylum claim automatically result in a failure of a withholding of removal claim in this context?
Question:
The conclusion states that because the petitioners failed to meet asylum standards, they 'necessarily fail' to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal. I thought withholding of removal was a non-discretionary relief with a different legal standard. If the facts of the case are identical, why does the outcome have to be the same? Does this mean that in practice, if you can't prove the 'persecution' element for asylum, it's impossible to win withholding?
Answer:
In the context of *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the reason failing an asylum claim results in an automatic failure of a withholding of removal (WOR) claim is due to the **hierarchical relationship between the two legal standards.**

### The Threshold Hierarchy
While asylum and withholding of removal are different forms of relief with different consequences (asylum is discretionary, while withholding is non-discretionary), they share a common foundational requirement: a well-founded fear of persecution. However, the legal "bar" for proving that fear differs significantly between the two:

1.  **Asylum:** Requires a "well-founded fear" of persecution. This is generally considered the lower of the two thresholds.
2.  **Withholding of Removal:** Requires a "higher standard" of well-founded fear (often interpreted as a "clear probability" that persecution will occur).

Because the withholding of removal standard is a higher bar than the asylum standard, it is logically impossible to satisfy the higher requirement if the lower one has not been met. As the court notes in the conclusion, because the petitioners failed to demonstrate the fear of persecution required for asylum, they "necessarily fail" to meet the more stringent requirements for withholding.

### The Role of the "Persecution" Element
To your specific question regarding the "persecution" element: Yes, in practice, if an applicant cannot prove that the harm they suffered (or fear) rises to the legal definition of "persecution," they generally cannot win withholding of removal.

In *Jimenez*, the court looked at the collective harm suffered by the petitioners—which included threatening phone calls, a brochure with a death threat, and instances of emotional and psychological distress. The court held that while these events were "physically stressful and psychologically unsettling," they were insufficient to rise to the level of "persecution" as defined by the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). 

Since "persecution" is a prerequisite for both asylum and withholding of removal, failing to establish that the harm reaches that threshold is a "fatal" error for both claims. Even though the standards for the *probability* of that persecution differ, they both rely on the underlying fact that the harm must actually constitute "persecution." If the facts do not support a finding of persecution, the claim fails at the first hurdle, regardless of which form of relief is being sought.
