Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Factual Background: Political activity and the Liberal Party

The petitioners, Wilson Leonardo Canon Jimenez and his wife, Angelica Parada Duarte, are natives of Bogota, Colombia. They moved to the United States in December 2022 after a series of escalating events in their home country. The family were established business owners who operated a furniture factory in Bogota. Their involvement in Colombian politics dates back to 2012, during which time they became members of the Colombian Liberal Party. 

In 2022, the family actively campaigned for Federico Gutierrez, a candidate in the Colombian presidential election. Their activities included door-to-door campaigning in local neighborhoods. It was during this period of political engagement that the petitioners began to face adversity. Starting in May 2022, Jimenez received threatening phone calls regarding his Liberal Party involvement. The climate of danger was underscored by the fact that a colleague of Jimenez, who had also received threats, was kidnapped and murdered in early June 2022. Despite these events, the petitioners continued their political activities.

## The Conflict: Jimenez’s professional involvement with 'Don Rafa'

The trajectory of the petitioners' situation shifted significantly due to Jimenez’s professional work as a provisional lawyer and law student. In June 2022, Jimenez took on a case for a client regarding a property dispute. While investigating the matter, Jimenez discovered that the tenant occupying the property was Rafael Augusto Montanez Lancheros, widely known as "Don Rafa." 

The court's record establishes that Don Rafa was not merely a private citizen but a leader of a narco-trafficking guerrilla group aligned with the current Colombian president. Jimenez’s investigation revealed that the property was being used as a base for narco-trafficking, prostitution, and as a day-rental for migrants. Furthermore, the premises were linked to reports of kidnapping and murder. When Jimenez attempted to navigate the situation, a local police officer explicitly warned him to stay away from the property and to avoid seeking further "problems."

## Escalation of Threats: From phone calls to physical harassment and witness intimidation

Following his investigation into Don Rafa's property, the threats against the petitioners intensified and became more specific. Jimenez received a brochure at his factory threatening "death to snitches from Liberal Party." Shortly thereafter, while working at the factory at night, Jimenez was held at gunpoint by two men. These men accused him of being a "snitch" for the National Prosecutor’s office and held him responsible for the law enforcement searches and seizures at the property in question.

The harassment continued as the two men returned to the factory while the petitioners were out of town. On this occasion, the men assaulted a factory employee and demanded to know the location of the petitioners, again labeling them "snitches" and stating that the order to target them came directly from Don Rafa. The violence moved closer to the family's children when Jimenez witnessed a man approaching his son at school, covering the boy's mouth and ordering him to "run to your dad." These incidents prompted the family to move to the countryside for three weeks before they decided to flee the country.

## The Flight to the United States

Before leaving Colombia, Jimenez sought assistance from the authorities. He reported the incident involving his son to a police patrol officer. However, the officer informed Jimenez that nothing could be done and suggested that police officers were contributing to the underlying problems. The officer advised the family to leave the country, which is what the petitioners ultimately did. They entered the United States without being admitted or paroled by an immigration officer.

## Procedural History: From the Notice to Appear to the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA)

The legal process began three months after the petitioners' arrival when the Department of Homeland Security served them with individual Notices to Appear (NTA). These notices charged the petitioners as removable from the United States. In August 2023, the petitioners admitted the allegations in the DHS notices, and the Immigration Judge (IJ) charged them as inadmissible, directing their removal to Colombia.

During these proceedings, the petitioners applied for asylum and withholding of removal. The IJ denied these applications, finding that the petitioners failed to prove they suffered past persecution, that their harm had a nexus to a statutorily protected ground, that internal relocation was unavailable, and that the Colombian government was unable or unwilling to protect them. The BIA subsequently affirmed the IJ's decision and rejected the petitioners' due process arguments regarding their right to counsel.

## Standard of Review: Substantial evidence and the limitations of the administrative record

The Tenth Circuit reviews these cases under specific jurisdictional and evidentiary standards. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, the court has the authority to review final orders of removal, including consolidated reviews of asylum claims. However, this review is strictly limited to the "administrative record"—the body of evidence and documents upon which the BIA based its order.

The court applies a "substantial evidence" standard to the BIA’s findings of fact. Under this standard, the BIA’s findings are considered conclusive unless the administrative record contains enough evidence to compel a reasonable adjudicator to reach a different conclusion. While the court's primary focus is the BIA's decision, it is permitted to consult the IJ’s more complete explanation of the grounds for the initial denial to clarify the record.
