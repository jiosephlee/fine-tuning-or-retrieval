Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# The Perils of a 'Pivoting' Expert: Technical Testimony and the Substantial Evidence Standard

In the high-stakes arena of patent litigation, the distance between a $166 million jury verdict and a total reversal often comes down to the clarity of a single expert’s testimony. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit sent a pointed message to patent holders: while technical complexity is expected, "pivoting" testimony—where an expert witness shifts their technical mapping mid-trial to avoid a logical trap—may fail to meet the "substantial evidence" standard required to sustain a jury's finding of infringement.

The case involved Finesse’s patents regarding the mitigation of passive intermodulation products (PIM) in radios. After a jury found that AT&T and Nokia infringed these patents, the defendants moved for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL). The Federal Circuit agreed with the defendants, reversing the verdict on both asserted patents. The common thread in both reversals was not a disagreement over the underlying physics of radio waves, but rather a failure by Finesse’s expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, to provide a consistent, coherent map between the accused technology and the patent claims.

## The Logic of the "Pivot"

The court’s analysis of the ’134 patent highlights the danger of an expert who is forced to change their story under cross-examination. Finesse’s claim required the accused radio to sample a passband of received signals that included "interference generating signals." During trial, Dr. Wells testified that the radio infringed by sampling a specific "modeled PIM path." However, the defendants pointed out a fundamental physical impossibility: the modeled PIM path was generated *after* the sampling had already occurred. In short, the radio could not sample a signal that did not yet exist.

When confronted with this logical dead end, Dr. Wells attempted to "pivot." He tried to argue that the "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals" were actually two different inputs (x1 and x2) on a specific path. Yet, the court found his testimony "confusing and unclear." He never explicitly stated that x1 and x2 mapped onto the specific claim limitations, and his testimony remained muddled even after he was alerted to his apparent misimpression. By trying to move the goalposts in response to a devastating cross-examination, Dr. Wells stripped the jury’s verdict of its evidentiary foundation.

The court applied a standard familiar to patent law: a jury’s finding of infringement must be supported by "substantial evidence." While this is a relatively deferential standard—requiring only that a reasonable jury *could* have reached the conclusion—the Federal Circuit clarified that such evidence cannot be built on the back of self-contradictory expert testimony. When an expert’s mapping is so incoherent that the court cannot discern which technical component corresponds to which claim limitation, the "substantial evidence" evaporates.

## Mapping and the Burden of Clarity

The court’s treatment of the ’775 patent further underscored the importance of precise "mapping." This patent required the generation of signals through seven specific multiplications. Dr. Wells pointed to a Nokia document showing three distinct multiplications, and Finesse argued that these three accounted for the seven. However, the court noted that the document actually showed complex conjugate and modulus operations, and Dr. Wells failed to provide a clear, step-by-step mapping of how the three multiplications in the document satisfied the seven requirements of the claim.

This highlights a critical doctrinal reality in high-tech litigation: the burden of proof lies heavily on the plaintiff to bridge the gap between "the way the machine works" and "the way the claim is written." In cases involving sophisticated FPGA code or digital signal processing, it is easy for an expert to point at a complex diagram and declare "infringement." But as this case demonstrates, if that expert cannot withstand a "how" and "where" analysis—specifically, if they cannot point to the exact moment a claim limitation is met—the verdict is vulnerable.

## Downstream Implications

The *Finesse* decision serves as a cautionary tale for patent practitioners regarding expert "hardening." It suggests that the Federal Circuit is increasingly less tolerant of experts who rely on "inference" to bridge gaps in testimony. If an expert’s testimony requires the jury to piece together disparate fragments of testimony over ninety pages of record to reach a conclusion, the court may view that as a failure of the plaintiff's burden.

For plaintiffs, the takeaway is clear: the "mapping" must be robust enough to survive a technical autopsy. An expert who can be forced into a "pivot" by a skilled opposing counsel has already lost the battle. In the eyes of the Federal Circuit, a confusing map is no map at all, and a jury verdict built on a mirage of technical consistency will not survive appellate review.
