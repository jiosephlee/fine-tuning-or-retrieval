Title: Blog about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# The Perils of a 'Pivoting' Expert: Technical Testimony and the Substantial Evidence Standard

In the high-stakes arena of patent litigation, the distance between a $166 million jury verdict and a total reversal often comes down to the clarity of a single expert’s testimony. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit sent a pointed message to patent holders: while technical complexity is expected, "pivoting" testimony—where an expert witness shifts their technical mapping mid-trial to avoid a logical trap—may fail to meet the "substantial evidence" standard required to sustain a jury's finding of infringement.

The case involved Finesse’s patents regarding the mitigation of passive intermodulation products (PIM) in radios. After a jury found that AT&T and Nokia infringed these patents, the defendants moved for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL). The Federal Circuit agreed with the defendants, reversing the verdict on both asserted patents. The common thread in both reversals was not a disagreement over the underlying physics of radio waves, but rather a failure by Finesse’s expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, to provide a consistent, coherent map between the accused technology and the patent claims.

## The Logic of the "Pivot"

The court’s analysis of the ’134 patent highlights the danger of an expert who is forced to change their story under cross-examination. Finesse’s claim required the accused radio to sample a passband of received signals that included "interference generating signals." During trial, Dr. Wells testified that the radio infringed by sampling a specific "modeled PIM path." However, the defendants pointed out a fundamental physical impossibility: the modeled PIM path was generated *after* the sampling had already occurred. In short, the radio could not sample a signal that did not yet exist.

When confronted with this logical dead end, Dr. Wells attempted to "pivot." He tried to argue that the "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals" were actually two different inputs (x1 and x2) on a specific path. Yet, the court found his testimony "confusing and unclear." He never explicitly stated that x1 and x2 mapped onto the specific claim limitations, and his testimony remained muddled even after he was alerted to his apparent misimpression. By trying to move the goalposts in response to a devastating cross-examination, Dr. Wells stripped the jury’s verdict of its evidentiary foundation.

The court applied a standard familiar to patent law: a jury’s finding of infringement must be supported by "substantial evidence." While this is a relatively deferential standard—requiring only that a reasonable jury *could* have reached the conclusion—the Federal Circuit clarified that such evidence cannot be built on the back of self-contradictory expert testimony. When an expert’s mapping is so incoherent that the court cannot discern which technical component corresponds to which claim limitation, the "substantial evidence" evaporates.

## Mapping and the Burden of Clarity

The court’s treatment of the ’775 patent further underscored the importance of precise "mapping." This patent required the generation of signals through seven specific multiplications. Dr. Wells pointed to a Nokia document showing three distinct multiplications, and Finesse argued that these three accounted for the seven. However, the court noted that the document actually showed complex conjugate and modulus operations, and Dr. Wells failed to provide a clear, step-by-step mapping of how the three multiplications in the document satisfied the seven requirements of the claim.

This highlights a critical doctrinal reality in high-tech litigation: the burden of proof lies heavily on the plaintiff to bridge the gap between "the way the machine works" and "the way the claim is written." In cases involving sophisticated FPGA code or digital signal processing, it is easy for an expert to point at a complex diagram and declare "infringement." But as this case demonstrates, if that expert cannot withstand a "how" and "where" analysis—specifically, if they cannot point to the exact moment a claim limitation is met—the verdict is vulnerable.

## Downstream Implications

The *Finesse* decision serves as a cautionary tale for patent practitioners regarding expert "hardening." It suggests that the Federal Circuit is increasingly less tolerant of experts who rely on "inference" to bridge gaps in testimony. If an expert’s testimony requires the jury to piece together disparate fragments of testimony over ninety pages of record to reach a conclusion, the court may view that as a failure of the plaintiff's burden.

For plaintiffs, the takeaway is clear: the "mapping" must be robust enough to survive a technical autopsy. An expert who can be forced into a "pivot" by a skilled opposing counsel has already lost the battle. In the eyes of the Federal Circuit, a confusing map is no map at all, and a jury verdict built on a mirage of technical consistency will not survive appellate review.



# Prosecution History and the 'Unique Signal' Trap: Navigating Claim Scope in Signal Processing

In the high-stakes arena of telecommunications patent litigation, the boundary between a broad, enforceable claim and a narrow, unenforceable one often hinges on a single word of technical phrasing. The Federal Circuit’s recent decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* highlights this precarious balance, particularly regarding how prosecution history affects the scope of mathematical and signal-processing limitations. By examining the '775 patent, the court offered a nuanced look at the "separately identifiable" versus "unique" distinction, providing a roadmap for how practitioners must navigate the tension between maintaining claim breadth and avoiding prosecution history estoppel.

The dispute centered on a method for reducing intermodulation product (IMP) interference in radios. Specifically, the '775 patent claimed a method of generating interference cancellation signals by "digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2, and S3" in seven specific multiplications. At the heart of the infringement analysis was a fundamental question of quantity: Did the patentee’s requirement for "three signals" exclude a device that utilizes only two unique input signals?

The district court had construed "three signals" to mean "separately identifiable" but not necessarily "unique." This construction allowed for a scenario where a device might use two unique signals plus a copy of one of those signals to satisfy the "three" requirement. The court’s analysis of the prosecution history was pivotal here. Finesse had originally sought to claim "two or three signals" but was forced by the examiner to narrow that language to "three signals" to overcome an obviousness rejection based on the *Filipovic* reference. 

The Federal Circuit’s refusal to view this amendment as a disavowal of "two signals" is a significant observation for patent practitioners. The court reasoned that because the *Filipovic* rejection was based on the *type* of intermodulation (active vs. passive) rather than the *number* of signals, the amendment to "three" did not constitute a "clear and unambiguous" abandonment of products using only two unique signals. Essentially, the court found that a patentee can sometimes narrow a claim to overcome a specific prior art hurdle without completely surrendering the underlying technology if the reason for the narrowing is distinct from the technical difference between the prior art and the claimed invention.

However, the court’s ultimate reversal of the infringement finding underscores a sobering reality for technical patents: construction is only half the battle. Even though the court accepted that "three" did not require "three unique" signals, the patentee still failed to prove that the accused radio actually performed the seven specific multiplications required by the claim. The expert testimony failed to bridge the gap between the three distinct multiplications found in the Nokia technical documents and the seven multiplications listed in the patent. This highlights a "technical literalism" that often defeats broad constructions; if the underlying mathematical operations of the accused device do not map precisely onto the claim limitations, the claim’s breadth becomes irrelevant.

The downstream implications for hardware and signal-processing patents are clear. Practitioners must be wary of the "unique signal" trap. While the *Finesse* court provided some breathing room by allowing "separately identifiable" signals (like a signal and its copy) to count toward a numerical limit, the decision reinforces that the specific *process*—the "how"—is just as important as the "how many." For litigators, the case serves as a reminder that while prosecution history might not always be a death knell for claim scope, a lack of precise evidentiary mapping during trial can be equally fatal. To succeed in these spaces, a patent must be drafted with enough specificity to be defensible against prior art, yet with enough functional flexibility to survive the rigorous technical scrutiny of a jury.



# When Mathematics Fails the Mapping Test: The Stricture of Specific Claim Limitations

In the high-stakes arena of telecommunications patent litigation, the distance between "functionally similar" and "literally infringing" can be measured in mere variables. The Federal Circuit’s recent decision in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC* serves as a poignant reminder of this reality. By reversing a district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) on noninfringement, the court underscored a fundamental principle of patent law: when a claim specifies a precise mathematical or numerical limitation, the plaintiff must provide a clear, explicit bridge—a "mapping"—between the accused product’s operations and those exact requirements.

The case centered on two patents held by Finesse Wireless regarding the mitigation of passive intermodulation products (PIM) in radios. The jury initially found that AT&T’s Nokia radios infringed both patents, awarding Finesse over $166 million. However, the Federal Circuit dismantled that verdict by focusing on the inherent logic of the claims and the insufficiency of the plaintiff’s expert testimony to bridge the gap between the two.

The first patent, the ’134, required the accused radio to sample a passband of received signals that include both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." The problem, as the court identified, was one of temporal and causal logic. Finesse’s expert testified that the "interference generating signals" were the "modeled PIM path." However, the court noted that the modeled PIM path is generated *after* the sampling occurs. Because a receiver cannot sample a signal before it has been generated, the expert’s mapping was logically impossible. The court’s refusal to allow the jury to "fill in the blanks" of the expert's self-contradictory testimony highlights the judiciary's role in ensuring that infringement is grounded in the physical and logical reality of the claim language.

The second patent, the ’775, presented an even more granular mathematical hurdle. The claim required a method for performing interference cancellation by "digitally multiplying and filtering" three signals (S1, S2, and S3) in seven specific third-order multiplications. While the Nokia radios certainly performed interference cancellation, the court found that the evidence failed to show they performed the *seven* specific multiplications required by the patent. 

The expert testimony here was particularly revealing. Finesse attempted to map three distinct multiplications found in Nokia's technical documents onto the seven required by the patent. The court rejected this, noting that the mapping was never clearly presented to the jury and relied on a flawed interpretation of the technical documents, which involved complex conjugate and modulus operations not explicitly accounted for in the claim’s literal requirements. Even though Finesse argued that the radios performed *more* than seven multiplications (ten, in fact), the plaintiff failed to specify which subset of those ten corresponded to the seven claimed. In the eyes of the Federal Circuit, a "close enough" mathematical approximation is not a substitute for a literal fulfillment of the claim's limitations.

This decision carries significant doctrinal implications for how complex, algorithm-based hardware is litigated. It reinforces a "bright-line" approach to literal infringement: if a patentee chooses to define their invention through specific numerical steps or mathematical formulas, they are bound by those specifications. The court is signaling that it will not permit a "functional equivalence" argument to override a specific claim limitation unless the plaintiff can provide a clean, 1:1 mapping of the accused device’s architecture to the patent’s prose.

For plaintiffs, the takeaway is a mandate for evidentiary precision. In cases involving sophisticated signal processing, "vague" expert testimony that relies on the jury to infer a mapping between similar-looking operations will likely fail at the JMOL stage. For defendants, the ruling provides a potent shield; if a competitor’s device achieves a similar result but uses a different mathematical shortcut or a different number of iterations, the defendant can successfully argue noninfringement by highlighting the literal gap between the two. Ultimately, *Finesse Wireless* confirms that in the world of patent law, the map is not just the territory—the map *is* the territory.
