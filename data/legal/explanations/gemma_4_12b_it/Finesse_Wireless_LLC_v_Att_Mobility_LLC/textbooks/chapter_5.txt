Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 5: # Disposition and Conclusion

## Reversal of the district court's denial of JMOL

The court’s primary holding in this appeal is the reversal of the district court’s denial of Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) of noninfringement. To understand this disposition, a student must recognize the standard of review the Federal Circuit applied here. Because the district court denied a motion for JMOL, the appellate court reviewed that denial under the law of the regional circuit (the Fifth Circuit). Under this standard, the appellate court would only reverse the district court if the evidence pointed so "strongly and overwhelmingly" in favor of one party that a reasonable jury could not have reached a contrary conclusion. 

However, the court also noted that a jury’s finding of infringement is reviewed for "substantial evidence." Evidence is substantial if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party based on the evidence presented at trial. In this case, the court determined that Finesse failed to meet this burden. By reversing the district court’s denial of JMOL, the Federal Circuit is effectively ruling that the jury’s verdict of infringement was not supported by substantial evidence. Therefore, the court concluded that the district court erred in allowing the infringement verdict to stand.

## The logic for vacating the $166 million damages award

A critical component of the court's disposition is the vacatur of the $166,303,391 damages award. In patent litigation, a damages award is predicated on the finding of infringement. If a court determines that a product does not infringe a patent, there is no legal basis for a damages award arising from that infringement. 

Because the Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL, it essentially nullified the jury’s finding that AT&T and Nokia infringed the ’134 and ’775 patents. Once the court established that the evidence did not support a finding of infringement, the massive lump-sum damages award became legally unsustainable. Consequently, the court vacated the award, meaning the award is wiped from the record as if it had never been granted.

## Summary of the court's findings on substantial evidence

The court’s decision to reverse the JMOL denial rested on specific evidentiary failures regarding both asserted patents. For the ’134 patent, the court found that the plaintiff’s expert, Dr. Wells, provided "self-contradictory" testimony. He initially mapped the "interference generating signals" to a modeled path that was generated *after* sampling, which made it impossible for the radio to sample that signal as claimed. While he attempted to "pivot" his testimony during cross-examination, the court found his explanation for the change was unclear and insufficient to support the jury's verdict.

For the ’775 patent, the court found a failure in the mapping of the technical operations. The patent required seven specific third-order multiplications. However, the evidence—specifically the Nokia technical documents—only showed three distinct multiplications. The court noted that Finesse provided no clear evidence during the trial showing how these three multiplications corresponded to the seven required by the claim. Because the expert could not demonstrate that the accused radios performed all seven multiplications, the court held that no reasonable jury could have found infringement.

## Final order: Reversed and Vacated

The final disposition of the case is "Reversed and Vacated." This means that the Federal Circuit has overturned (reversed) the district court’s judgment regarding the infringement of the patents and has cancelled (vacated) the court's order regarding the damages. 

By reversing the denial of JMOL, the court has signaled that there was no substantial evidence of infringement. By vacating the damages, the court has removed the financial penalty from the judgment. Ultimately, the court's ruling strips the plaintiff of the jury's victory, concluding that the technical evidence presented at trial was insufficient to prove that the accused radios met the specific limitations of the ’134 and ’775 patents.
