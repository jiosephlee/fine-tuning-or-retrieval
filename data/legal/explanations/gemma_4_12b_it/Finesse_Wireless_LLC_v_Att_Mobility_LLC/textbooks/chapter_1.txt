Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

Chapter 1: # Introduction and Technical Background

To understand the legal issues in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, one must first grasp the specific technical problem the patents aim to solve. In telecommunications, radios must operate on precise frequencies to avoid interfering with one another. However, when transmit signals encounter physical obstacles—such as metal fences, loose cable connections, or rusted connectors—those signals can combine to form new, unwanted frequencies known as "intermodulation products" (IMPs). When these IMPs are caused by passive obstacles rather than active electronic components, they are specifically referred to as "passive intermodulation products" (PIM).

The technology at the heart of this litigation involves "PIM cancellation" (PIM-C). Because PIM can interfere with the clarity and reliability of radio signals, the patents in suit describe methods to identify and neutralize these products. Finesse Wireless LLC owns two primary patents addressing this issue: the ’134 patent and the ’775 patent. The ’134 patent focuses on a method for reducing IMP interference by isolating "signals of interest" from "interference generating signals," computing an estimate of the resulting IMPs, and then canceling those products out. The ’775 patent describes a specific digital process for reducing IMP interference in a receiver by multiplying three signals (S1, S2, and S3) in seven specific multiplications to generate cancellation signals.

The litigation involves three primary parties. Finesse Wireless LLC is the plaintiff and patent owner. AT&T Mobility LLC is the defendant, accused of infringing the patents by using the PIM-C feature in radios provided by Nokia of America Corporation. Nokia intervened in the lawsuit, as it is the manufacturer of the hardware used by AT&T. The core of the dispute rests on whether the specific technical operations performed by Nokia’s radios actually meet the limitations described in Finesse's patent claims.

The procedural posture of this case is a critical component of the Federal Circuit’s review. The litigation began in the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas, where a jury heard evidence regarding the validity and infringement of the ’134 and ’775 patents. The jury ultimately found that all asserted claims were valid and infringed by the accused radios, awarding Finesse approximately $166.3 million in lump-sum damages. 

Following the verdict, AT&T and Nokia moved for Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) of noninfringement, as well as JMOL and a new trial regarding the damages award. The district court denied these motions, essentially upholding the jury’s verdict. The defendants then appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The court's task on appeal is to determine whether the district court erred in denying JMOL—specifically, whether the evidence of infringement was so weak that no reasonable jury could have reached a finding of infringement.
