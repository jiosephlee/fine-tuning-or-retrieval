Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### What is the distinction between 'separately identifiable' and 'unique' signals in the context of patent claim construction?
Question:
For the ’775 patent, the court construed 'three signals' to mean 'separately identifiable' but not necessarily 'unique.' I'm struggling to understand the practical difference in a technical patent claim. If a signal is 'separately identifiable' but not 'unique,' could that mean the same signal is being counted multiple times to meet the 'three signals' requirement? How would that affect the infringement analysis?
Answer:
The distinction between "separately identifiable" and "unique" lies in whether the signals must originate from distinct sources or merely be distinguishable as individual inputs within the technical process.

### The Legal Distinction
In patent construction, "unique" would typically require that $S_1$, $S_2$, and $S_3$ be three different, non-overlapping signals. If a claim required "unique" signals, a device using only two distinct signals would fail to infringe because it would lack a third unique input.

By constructing the term as "separately identifiable" rather than "unique," the court allows for a broader scope of infringement. This construction means that as long as the system can distinguish three signals during the operation, those signals do not need to be independent of one another.

### Practical Application in the '775 Patent
Your intuition regarding the "counting" of signals is correct. Because the court held that the signals need only be "separately identifiable" and not "unique," the same signal (or a copy of it) can be used to satisfy the numerical requirement of the claim.

The court explicitly confirmed this in its analysis of the district court's construction:
> "The court also found using two signals, plus a copy of one of those signals, was sufficient to meet its construction of three separately identifiable signals."

In this context, if a radio uses Signal A and Signal B, it can satisfy the "three signals" limitation by treating the inputs as:
1. Signal A ($S_1$)
2. Signal B ($S_2$)
3. A copy of Signal A ($S_3$)

Because these three inputs are "separately identifiable" in the mathematical process of multiplication, they meet the claim limitation even though there are only two "unique" source signals.

### Impact on Infringement Analysis
This distinction significantly impacts the scope of the patent's protection:

1.  **Broadened Scope:** It prevents a defendant from escaping infringement simply by using a smaller number of physical signal sources. If the court had required "unique" signals, the accused radios (which only used two signals, $x_1$ and $x_2$) would have been a clear case of noninfringement.
2.  **Focus on Process vs. Source:** The construction shifts the focus from the *source* of the signals to the *application* of the signals. The patent owner is protected as long as the accused device performs the claimed multiplications using three distinguishable inputs, regardless of whether those inputs are derived from the same original source.

However, as noted in the opinion, meeting the "three signals" requirement is only one hurdle. Even though the court allowed for the "two signals plus a copy" interpretation, it still found noninfringement because the accused device failed to perform the specific **seven multiplications** required by the claim.
