Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

# Introduction and Technical Background

To understand the legal issues in *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, one must first grasp the specific technical problem the patents aim to solve. In telecommunications, radios must operate on precise frequencies to avoid interfering with one another. However, when transmit signals encounter physical obstacles—such as metal fences, loose cable connections, or rusted connectors—those signals can combine to form new, unwanted frequencies known as "intermodulation products" (IMPs). When these IMPs are caused by passive obstacles rather than active electronic components, they are specifically referred to as "passive intermodulation products" (PIM).

The technology at the heart of this litigation involves "PIM cancellation" (PIM-C). Because PIM can interfere with the clarity and reliability of radio signals, the patents in suit describe methods to identify and neutralize these products. Finesse Wireless LLC owns two primary patents addressing this issue: the ’134 patent and the ’775 patent. The ’134 patent focuses on a method for reducing IMP interference by isolating "signals of interest" from "interference generating signals," computing an estimate of the resulting IMPs, and then canceling those products out. The ’775 patent describes a specific digital process for reducing IMP interference in a receiver by multiplying three signals (S1, S2, and S3) in seven specific multiplications to generate cancellation signals.

The litigation involves three primary parties. Finesse Wireless LLC is the plaintiff and patent owner. AT&T Mobility LLC is the defendant, accused of infringing the patents by using the PIM-C feature in radios provided by Nokia of America Corporation. Nokia intervened in the lawsuit, as it is the manufacturer of the hardware used by AT&T. The core of the dispute rests on whether the specific technical operations performed by Nokia’s radios actually meet the limitations described in Finesse's patent claims.

The procedural posture of this case is a critical component of the Federal Circuit’s review. The litigation began in the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas, where a jury heard evidence regarding the validity and infringement of the ’134 and ’775 patents. The jury ultimately found that all asserted claims were valid and infringed by the accused radios, awarding Finesse approximately $166.3 million in lump-sum damages. 

Following the verdict, AT&T and Nokia moved for Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) of noninfringement, as well as JMOL and a new trial regarding the damages award. The district court denied these motions, essentially upholding the jury’s verdict. The defendants then appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The court's task on appeal is to determine whether the district court erred in denying JMOL—specifically, whether the evidence of infringement was so weak that no reasonable jury could have reached a finding of infringement.

# Standards of Appellate Review

To understand how an appellate court reaches its conclusion, a student must first identify the "standard of review." This is the set of rules that dictates how much deference the appellate court must give to the lower court’s decisions. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the Federal Circuit applies different standards depending on whether it is reviewing a judge’s legal ruling, a jury’s factual finding, or a district court’s discretionary decision.

## The Distinction Between De Novo Review and Substantial Evidence Review

The court distinguishes between two primary modes of review in this opinion: *de novo* and "substantial evidence." 

*De novo* review means the appellate court looks at the issue "anew," giving no deference to the lower court’s legal conclusions. In this case, the Federal Circuit applies *de novo* review to the district court’s claim constructions and its denial of Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL). Because these are legal determinations, the Federal Circuit is free to disagree with the district court’s interpretation of the law or the patents.

In contrast, a "substantial evidence" standard is more deferential. This standard is used when reviewing a jury’s factual findings, such as whether a specific device actually infringes on a patent. Under this standard, the appellate court does not ask if it *agrees* with the jury; it only asks if a "reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial."

## Reviewing the Denial of Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) for Noninfringement

A motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) occurs when a party argues that, even when viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the opposing party, no reasonable jury could possibly find in favor of that opponent. 

The Federal Circuit reviews the district court’s denial of such a motion under the law of the regional circuit (in this case, the Fifth Circuit). The standard is demanding: the appellate court will only reverse the district court's denial if the evidence "points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the court believes that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion." 

In *Finesse*, this standard was central to the analysis of the ’134 and ’775 patents. While the district court felt there was enough evidence for a jury to find infringement, the Federal Circuit ultimately found that the evidence—specifically the contradictory testimony of the plaintiff’s expert—was not "overwhelming" enough to support the jury's verdict.

## The 'Substantial Evidence' Standard and the 'Reasonable Juror' Test

While the JMOL standard looks for "overwhelming" evidence, the "substantial evidence" standard used to review the jury’s infringement finding is slightly different. A jury’s finding of infringement is supported by substantial evidence if a "reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party."

This creates a nuanced hierarchy of evidence:
1. If the evidence is so weak that *no* reasonable jury could have found infringement, the court should have granted JMOL.
2. If the evidence is sufficient that a *reasonable* jury could have found infringement (even if the appellate court thinks the evidence is thin), the jury’s verdict stands.

In this opinion, the court used these tests to evaluate the plaintiff’s expert, Dr. Wells. Because Dr. Wells provided contradictory testimony regarding what signals were being sampled and multiplied, the court concluded that the evidence did not meet the threshold required to sustain the jury's verdict. Because the evidence was not sufficient to support a finding of infringement, the court reversed the denial of JMOL.

## Reviewing the Denial of a New Trial on Damages (Abuse of Discretion)

Finally, the court addresses the district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial on damages. The standard of review here is "abuse of discretion." 

An "abuse of discretion" standard is the most deferential level of review. It means the appellate court will only overturn the district court’s decision if it was arbitrary, capricious, or clearly erroneous. However, there is a procedural shortcut in this specific case: because the Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s JMOL ruling (meaning they found no infringement occurred), the damages award was automatically vacated. Since there was no infringement, there were no damages to be awarded, making the standard of review for the new trial motion secondary to the finding of noninfringement.

# Infringement Analysis of the ’134 Patent

## Claim 1 Limitation: Sampling a Passband of Received Signals

To establish infringement of the ’134 patent, the plaintiff, Finesse Wireless, bore the burden of proving that the accused Nokia radios perform every limitation of the patent claims. A critical limitation in Claim 1 involves the initial step of the method: the radio must "over-sample, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream." Crucially, the claim specifies that these "received signals" must include both "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." For a jury to find infringement, the evidence must show that the hardware—specifically the radio frequency analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC)—actually receives and samples both types of signals simultaneously to create the bit stream.

## The 'Modeled PIM Path' vs. 'Interference Generating Signals' Distinction

At trial, Finesse relied on the testimony of its infringement expert, Dr. Jonathan Wells, to bridge the gap between the technical specifications of the Nokia radios and the language of the patent. Dr. Wells testified that the accused radios infringed the ’134 patent by identifying specific components in a Nokia technical document. He mapped the "signals of interest" to a "downlink transmit (DL(TX)) reference" and identified the "interference generating signals" as the "modeled PIM path." By making this identification, Dr. Wells intended to show the jury that the RF ADC was sampling the inputs necessary to satisfy the patent's requirements.

## The Logical Impossibility of Sampling a Signal Before it is Generated

The technical validity of Dr. Wells’ testimony was challenged by the defense during cross-examination. The defense pointed out a fundamental logical and chronological flaw in Dr. Wells’ mapping: the "modeled PIM path" is not an input to the RF ADC; rather, it is generated *downstream* of the RF ADC. In other words, the radio samples the signals first, and only after that sampling occurs does the system generate the modeled PIM path. Because a signal cannot be sampled before it exists, the "modeled PIM path" cannot logically be one of the "received signals" that the RF ADC samples to create the bit stream. This discrepancy directly undermines the claim that the accused device performs the first limitation of Claim 1.

## Analysis of Dr. Wells' Contradictory Testimony regarding Signal Mapping

In response to the defense’s critique, Dr. Wells attempted to "pivot" his testimony. He suggested that there were two signals, identified as "x1" and "x2," on the red path in the Nokia document, and implied that these were the actual signals of interest and interference-generating signals. However, the court found this testimony to be confusing and insufficient. Dr. Wells failed to clearly or consistently map "x1" and "x2" to the specific terms used in the patent claims. Instead, he continued to rely on the "modeled PIM path" terminology. The court noted that Finesse's argument relied on "piecing together parts of Dr. Wells’ testimony over ninety pages apart" to infer a mapping that Dr. Wells never explicitly or clearly stated.

## The Impact of Self-Contradictory Expert Testimony on the 'Substantial Evidence' Standard

The Federal Circuit's review of a jury's infringement finding is governed by the "substantial evidence" standard. A finding is supported by substantial evidence if a reasonable jury could have reached that conclusion based on the evidence presented. However, when a plaintiff's case rests almost entirely on the testimony of an expert who provides self-contradictory or unclear testimony, the evidence may fail to meet this standard. Because Dr. Wells failed to provide a clear mapping of the radio's functions to the patent's limitations—and because his primary identification was logically impossible—the court concluded that the jury's verdict was not supported by substantial evidence. Consequently, the court reversed the district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) of noninfringement.

# Infringement Analysis of the ’775 Patent

To determine whether a product infringes a patent, a court must compare the specific limitations of the patent claims against the actual operation of the accused device. In the case of the ’775 patent, the analysis centers on a complex mathematical method for generating intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals. The Federal Circuit’s analysis of this patent involves three distinct layers of inquiry: how the claim language is constructed, whether the patent owner surrendered rights during the application process, and whether the technical evidence provided at trial actually supports the claim limitations.

## Claim Construction: 'Three Signals' as Separately Identifiable vs. Unique

A foundational step in infringement analysis is claim construction, where the court defines the scope and meaning of the words used in the patent. For the ’775 patent, the primary point of contention was the phrase “three signals $S1, S2$ and $S3$.” The defendants argued that for the claim to be met, the patent owner must use three *unique* input signals. If the accused radio only used two unique signals, they argued, it could not infringe a claim requiring three.

However, the district court construed these signals as “separately identifiable” rather than “unique.” This distinction is critical. Under this construction, a device could satisfy the claim by using two unique signals plus a copy of one of those signals (e.g., $S1$, $S2$, and a copy of $S1$). Because the court held that the signals did not need to be unique, the fact that the accused radios utilized only two unique input signals was not, by itself, a bar to infringement.

## Prosecution History: Whether Finesse Surrendered Scope

The defendants further argued that even if the signals didn't need to be unique, Finesse had "surrendered" the right to claim products using only two unique signals during the patent prosecution process. This involves looking at the "prosecution history"—the record of communications between the patent applicant and the Patent Office. 

Finesse had amended their claims during prosecution to specifically require "three signals" to overcome a rejection based on prior art (the *Filipovic* reference). The defendants argued this amendment constituted a disavowal of any products using only two signals. The Federal Circuit rejected this argument, clarifying the standard for such a surrender. To be binding, an amendment must constitute a "clear and unambiguous disavowal" of claim scope. The court found that Finesse’s amendment merely required three "separately identifiable" signals and did not clearly disavow products that use two unique signals plus a copy of one. Therefore, the prosecution history did not preclude a finding of infringement.

## The Failure to Map Three Distinct Multiplications to the Seven Claimed

While the court found that the "three signals" requirement could be met by the accused radios, the infringement analysis ultimately failed on the specific mathematical operations required by the claim. Claim 1 of the ’775 patent requires the generation of an $n$-th order cancellation signal by performing seven specific third-order multiplications of the three signals (e.g., $S1 \times S1 \times S2$, $S1 \times S2 \times S3$, etc.).

At trial, Finesse’s expert witness, Dr. Wells, attempted to show that the accused radios performed these seven multiplications by pointing to a Nokia technical document. However, the court observed a significant evidentiary gap. The Nokia document only listed three distinct multiplications: $x1 \cdot |x1| \cdot |x1|$, $x1 \cdot x1 \cdot x2'$, and $x1 \cdot |x2| \cdot |x2|$. 

The court held that no reasonable jury could find infringement because the evidence only showed three distinct multiplications, whereas the patent claim requires seven. Finesse attempted to argue that the document actually showed ten multiplications, but they failed to provide a clear "mapping" to show which subset of those ten corresponded to the seven specific multiplications required by the patent. Without an explicit connection between the accused device's operations and the claim's specific mathematical requirements, the jury’s verdict of infringement lacked substantial evidence.

## Insufficiency of Complex Conjugate and Modulus Operations

Finally, the court highlighted a technical mismatch between the claim language and the accused device's actual engineering. The patent claim requires the digital multiplication of the signals ($S1, S2, S3$) themselves. However, the Nokia document showed that the accused radios performed operations involving the "complex conjugate" ($x2'$) and the "modulus" ($|x2|$) of the signals.

Finesse attempted to argue that these operations could be mapped onto the claim (for example, by mapping $S3$ onto the complex conjugate of $x2$). The court found this mapping flawed. Because the claim specifies the multiplication of the signals themselves, and the accused device performed operations on the *derivatives* of those signals (the modulus and conjugate), the evidence failed to show that the device performed the literal steps of the claim. This discrepancy confirmed that the accused radios did not perform the seven multiplications as defined by the ’775 patent.

# Disposition and Conclusion

## Reversal of the district court's denial of JMOL

The court’s primary holding in this appeal is the reversal of the district court’s denial of Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL) of noninfringement. To understand this disposition, a student must recognize the standard of review the Federal Circuit applied here. Because the district court denied a motion for JMOL, the appellate court reviewed that denial under the law of the regional circuit (the Fifth Circuit). Under this standard, the appellate court would only reverse the district court if the evidence pointed so "strongly and overwhelmingly" in favor of one party that a reasonable jury could not have reached a contrary conclusion. 

However, the court also noted that a jury’s finding of infringement is reviewed for "substantial evidence." Evidence is substantial if a reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party based on the evidence presented at trial. In this case, the court determined that Finesse failed to meet this burden. By reversing the district court’s denial of JMOL, the Federal Circuit is effectively ruling that the jury’s verdict of infringement was not supported by substantial evidence. Therefore, the court concluded that the district court erred in allowing the infringement verdict to stand.

## The logic for vacating the $166 million damages award

A critical component of the court's disposition is the vacatur of the $166,303,391 damages award. In patent litigation, a damages award is predicated on the finding of infringement. If a court determines that a product does not infringe a patent, there is no legal basis for a damages award arising from that infringement. 

Because the Federal Circuit reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL, it essentially nullified the jury’s finding that AT&T and Nokia infringed the ’134 and ’775 patents. Once the court established that the evidence did not support a finding of infringement, the massive lump-sum damages award became legally unsustainable. Consequently, the court vacated the award, meaning the award is wiped from the record as if it had never been granted.

## Summary of the court's findings on substantial evidence

The court’s decision to reverse the JMOL denial rested on specific evidentiary failures regarding both asserted patents. For the ’134 patent, the court found that the plaintiff’s expert, Dr. Wells, provided "self-contradictory" testimony. He initially mapped the "interference generating signals" to a modeled path that was generated *after* sampling, which made it impossible for the radio to sample that signal as claimed. While he attempted to "pivot" his testimony during cross-examination, the court found his explanation for the change was unclear and insufficient to support the jury's verdict.

For the ’775 patent, the court found a failure in the mapping of the technical operations. The patent required seven specific third-order multiplications. However, the evidence—specifically the Nokia technical documents—only showed three distinct multiplications. The court noted that Finesse provided no clear evidence during the trial showing how these three multiplications corresponded to the seven required by the claim. Because the expert could not demonstrate that the accused radios performed all seven multiplications, the court held that no reasonable jury could have found infringement.

## Final order: Reversed and Vacated

The final disposition of the case is "Reversed and Vacated." This means that the Federal Circuit has overturned (reversed) the district court’s judgment regarding the infringement of the patents and has cancelled (vacated) the court's order regarding the damages. 

By reversing the denial of JMOL, the court has signaled that there was no substantial evidence of infringement. By vacating the damages, the court has removed the financial penalty from the judgment. Ultimately, the court's ruling strips the plaintiff of the jury's victory, concluding that the technical evidence presented at trial was insufficient to prove that the accused radios met the specific limitations of the ’134 and ’775 patents.
