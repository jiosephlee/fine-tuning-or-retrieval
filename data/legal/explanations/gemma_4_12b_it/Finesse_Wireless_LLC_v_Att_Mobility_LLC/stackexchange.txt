Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC"

### What is the difference between the 'substantial evidence' standard and the 'overwhelming' evidence standard in this opinion?
Question:
I'm confused about the standard of review. The court says it reviews the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement under a standard where it reverses only if the evidence points 'so strongly and overwhelmingly' to one side that a reasonable jury could not arrive at any contrary conclusion. However, it says it reviews the jury’s finding of infringement for 'substantial evidence.' Why are there two different standards being applied to the same underlying issue of infringement?
Answer:
The distinction between the two standards arises from the different **procedural postures** the court is reviewing. Although both standards involve the same underlying facts (whether the defendants' radios infringed the patents), the court is evaluating two different legal questions: one regarding the validity of the jury's verdict and the other regarding the trial court's decision to let that verdict stand.

**1. The "Substantial Evidence" Standard (Reviewing the Jury's Verdict)**
The court uses the "substantial evidence" standard to review the jury’s actual finding of infringement. Under this standard, the appellate court defers to the jury's role as the finder of fact. The court will only reverse the finding if there was not enough evidence for a reasonable jury to have reached that conclusion. Essentially, this standard asks: *"Was the jury's conclusion reasonable based on the evidence presented?"*

**2. The "Overwhelming" Standard (Reviewing the Denial of JMOL)**
The court uses the "overwhelming" standard to review the district court’s **denial of a Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL)**. A JMOL motion is a request for the judge to rule in favor of one party immediately because the opposing party's evidence is so weak that no reasonable jury could possibly find in their favor. 

Because the district court *denied* the defendants' motion to have the case decided by the judge, the appellate court must decide if the district court made a mistake. To reverse that denial, the appellate court requires a much higher burden: the evidence must be so one-sided that "reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion." Essentially, this standard asks: *"Was the evidence so lopsided that the judge should have stopped the trial and ruled for the defendants immediately?"*

**Summary of the Difference**
The confusion stems from the fact that these are two different "hurdles":

*   **Substantial Evidence** is the hurdle the plaintiff's evidence had to clear to get a jury verdict. If the evidence was "substantial," the verdict stands.
*   **Overwhelming Evidence** is the hurdle the defendants' arguments had to clear to get the judge to overrule that jury verdict. Because the defendants failed to show that the evidence was "overwhelmingly" in their favor, the district court was correct to let the jury's "substantial" evidence-based verdict stand. 

In this specific opinion, the court ultimately found that the evidence was **not** substantial enough to support the infringement verdict (specifically regarding the '134 and '775 patents), leading it to reverse the denial of JMOL.

### How does 'self-contradictory' expert testimony specifically disqualify a jury's verdict under the substantial evidence standard?
Question:
In the analysis of the ’134 patent, the court reversed the jury's verdict because Dr. Wells' testimony was 'confusing and unclear' and 'self-contradictory.' If a jury is allowed to believe a witness (as per the substantial evidence standard), why does the court's finding that the witness was contradictory lead to a reversal? Does the court believe that a jury *could not* have reasonably believed such a witness, or is it a different legal principle?
Answer:
In patent litigation, the "substantial evidence" standard requires a court to determine whether a "reasonable jury could have found in favor of the prevailing party in light of the evidence presented at trial." *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, 2024 WL [Docket No. 2024-1039]. While this standard provides a degree of deference to the jury's role as the finder of fact, it does not grant a jury license to reach a verdict based on evidence that is internally inconsistent or fails to establish the necessary factual nexus.

The court’s finding that Dr. Wells’ testimony was "self-contradictory" disqualified the verdict because the contradiction undermined the very foundation of the infringement claim: the mapping of the accused technology to the patent's claim limitations. In the '134 patent analysis, Finesse bore the burden of proving that the accused radios sampled "signals of interest" and "interference generating signals." Dr. Wells initially mapped these terms to a specific "modeled PIM path." However, when cross-examined, it was revealed that the radio could not physically sample that path because the path was generated *after* sampling occurred.

The court explains that Dr. Wells' subsequent "pivot"—where he attempted to claim the signals were "x1 and x2" without clearly mapping those specific signals to the claim terms—resulted in testimony that was "confusing and unclear." The court’s logic follows a two-step progression:

1.  **Failure of Mapping:** Because Dr. Wells failed to provide a clear, consistent explanation of which signals corresponded to which claim limitations, the testimony failed to provide the necessary evidence to support a finding of infringement.
2.  **The "Reasonable Jury" Test:** The court did not necessarily conclude that a jury *could not* be confused by a messy witness. Rather, it concluded that a **reasonable** jury, presented with an expert who provided contradictory mappings and failed to clarify those contradictions, would not have been able to find that the accused device met the claim limitations.

The court reinforces this by citing *Johns Hopkins Univ. v. Datascope Corp.*, noting that when a party rests its case on an expert's self-contradictory testimony, the evidence may be insufficient to satisfy the substantial evidence standard. In short, the "self-contradictory" nature of the testimony is the mechanism by which the evidence fails to be "substantial"; if the evidence is so internally inconsistent that it fails to establish the required facts, then no reasonable jury could have reached the verdict based on that evidence.

### In patent law, when does a claim amendment during prosecution constitute a 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal of prior claim scope?
Question:
Regarding the ’775 patent, the Appellants argued that Finesse surrendered the right to claim products using only 'two signals' when they amended the claim to say 'three signals.' The court disagreed, saying it wasn't a 'clear and unambiguous' disavowal. Can someone explain the test the court uses to determine when an amendment effectively 'gives up' a certain scope of the invention?
Answer:
### Clear and Unambiguous Disavowal in Patent Prosecution

To determine whether an amendment during prosecution constitutes a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal of prior claim scope, the court applies a standard that requires more than a mere narrowing of the claim language. The patentee must have surrendered the specific scope of the previous claim in a way that is unmistakable.

**The Standard for Disavowal**
The court looks for evidence that the patentee explicitly or implicitly gave up the right to claim a certain embodiment. In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the court emphasized that simply amending a claim to a more specific set of limitations does not automatically result in a disavowal of the broader scope previously claimed. For a disavowal to be "clear and unambiguous," the amendment must be interpreted in a way that effectively excludes the previous scope.

**Application to the ’775 Patent**
In the case of the ’775 patent, the Appellants argued that by changing the claim language from "two or three signals" to "three signals," Finesse had surrendered the right to claim products that use only "two signals." The court rejected this argument based on the following reasoning:

1.  **Interpretation of the New Limitation:** The court found that the amended limitation—reciting "three signals S1, S2 and S3"—did not necessarily require the signals to be unique. Instead, the court held that the amendment required the signals to be "separately identifiable."
2.  **Compatibility with Prior Scope:** Because the court determined that "three signals" could be satisfied by using two unique signals plus a copy of one of those signals, the amendment did not actually preclude the accused radios (which used only two unique signals). 
3.  **Insufficient Disavowal:** The court concluded that the transition from "two or three" to "three" did not rise to the level of a "clear and unambiguous" disavowal because the new claim language could still encompass the same underlying technology (two unique inputs) that the previous claim covered.

**Conclusion**
The test, therefore, is whether the amended claim language *precludes* the prior scope. If the amended limitation can be interpreted to include the same configurations as the original claim—even if the phrasing is different—the patentee has not "given up" that scope. In *Finesse*, the court held that Finesse maintained its scope over products with two unique signals because the requirement of "three signals" was not strictly interpreted as a requirement for "three unique signals."

### What is the distinction between 'separately identifiable' and 'unique' signals in the context of patent claim construction?
Question:
For the ’775 patent, the court construed 'three signals' to mean 'separately identifiable' but not necessarily 'unique.' I'm struggling to understand the practical difference in a technical patent claim. If a signal is 'separately identifiable' but not 'unique,' could that mean the same signal is being counted multiple times to meet the 'three signals' requirement? How would that affect the infringement analysis?
Answer:
The distinction between "separately identifiable" and "unique" lies in whether the signals must originate from distinct sources or merely be distinguishable as individual inputs within the technical process.

### The Legal Distinction
In patent construction, "unique" would typically require that $S_1$, $S_2$, and $S_3$ be three different, non-overlapping signals. If a claim required "unique" signals, a device using only two distinct signals would fail to infringe because it would lack a third unique input.

By constructing the term as "separately identifiable" rather than "unique," the court allows for a broader scope of infringement. This construction means that as long as the system can distinguish three signals during the operation, those signals do not need to be independent of one another.

### Practical Application in the '775 Patent
Your intuition regarding the "counting" of signals is correct. Because the court held that the signals need only be "separately identifiable" and not "unique," the same signal (or a copy of it) can be used to satisfy the numerical requirement of the claim.

The court explicitly confirmed this in its analysis of the district court's construction:
> "The court also found using two signals, plus a copy of one of those signals, was sufficient to meet its construction of three separately identifiable signals."

In this context, if a radio uses Signal A and Signal B, it can satisfy the "three signals" limitation by treating the inputs as:
1. Signal A ($S_1$)
2. Signal B ($S_2$)
3. A copy of Signal A ($S_3$)

Because these three inputs are "separately identifiable" in the mathematical process of multiplication, they meet the claim limitation even though there are only two "unique" source signals.

### Impact on Infringement Analysis
This distinction significantly impacts the scope of the patent's protection:

1.  **Broadened Scope:** It prevents a defendant from escaping infringement simply by using a smaller number of physical signal sources. If the court had required "unique" signals, the accused radios (which only used two signals, $x_1$ and $x_2$) would have been a clear case of noninfringement.
2.  **Focus on Process vs. Source:** The construction shifts the focus from the *source* of the signals to the *application* of the signals. The patent owner is protected as long as the accused device performs the claimed multiplications using three distinguishable inputs, regardless of whether those inputs are derived from the same original source.

However, as noted in the opinion, meeting the "three signals" requirement is only one hurdle. Even though the court allowed for the "two signals plus a copy" interpretation, it still found noninfringement because the accused device failed to perform the specific **seven multiplications** required by the claim.

### Why did the court vacate the damages award instead of merely remanding it for a new trial or a different calculation?
Question:
The court reversed the JMOL of noninfringement and then vacated the damages award. Usually, if a court finds there was no infringement, the damages are naturally void. But I'm confused about the procedural step: since the court also reviewed the denial of a 'new trial on damages,' does vacating the award imply that the original calculation was flawed, or is it simply a procedural consequence of the noninfringement finding?
Answer:
The court vacated the damages award because the legal basis for those damages—a finding of infringement—was overturned. In patent litigation, damages are a secondary remedy that can only flow from a primary finding of liability (infringement).

### The Dependency of Damages on Infringement
To understand why the court vacated the award rather than remanding it for a new calculation, it is helpful to view the judgment as a two-step logical progression:
1.  **Liability:** Did the defendant infringe the patent?
2.  **Remedy:** If so, how much should the defendant pay?

If an appellate court determines that the evidence was insufficient to support a finding of infringement (which is what happens when a court reverses a denial of JMOL of noninfringement), the "Liability" step fails. Because the "Remedy" step is entirely dependent on the "Liability" step, the damages award becomes legally unsupported. There is no need to remand for a "different calculation" because there is no longer a legal basis to perform any calculation at all.

### Application to the Opinion
In *Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC*, the court conducted a de novo review of the JMOL of noninfringement. It concluded that:
*   For the **’134 patent**, the infringement verdict was not supported by substantial evidence because the plaintiff’s expert provided "self-contradictory testimony."
*   For the **’775 patent**, no reasonable jury could have found infringement because the evidence failed to show the "seven claimed multiplications" required by the patent.

By reversing the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement, the Federal Circuit effectively ruled that the jury's finding of infringement was incorrect as a matter of law. The court's statement—*"Because we reverse the denial of JMOL of noninfringement... we vacate the damages award"*—indicates that the vacatur is a direct procedural consequence of the reversal of the infringement finding.

### Why Not a New Trial on Damages?
You noted that the court also reviewed the denial of a "new trial on damages." Generally, a motion for a new trial on damages is granted when the court agrees that **infringement did occur**, but the jury’s **calculation** of the money owed was flawed (e.g., the lump sum was disproportionate or the calculation method was erroneous). 

In this case, the court did not need to address whether the $166 million figure was "correct" because it determined that the defendant did not infringe in the first place. If the court had found that infringement *did* occur but the calculation was flawed, it would have granted the new trial. However, since the court found that infringement *did not* occur, the calculation became moot, necessitating a vacatur of the award rather than a remand for a recalculation.
