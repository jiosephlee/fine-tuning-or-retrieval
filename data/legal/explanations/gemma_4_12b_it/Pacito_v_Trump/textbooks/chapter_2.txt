Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 2: # Presidential Authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

## Statutory Basis: 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and the 'Detrimental Interests' finding

To understand the court’s analysis of Executive Order (EO) 14163, students must first identify the specific statutory source of the President's power. The court focuses on 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), a provision of the Immigration and Nationality Act that grants the President the authority to regulate the entry of aliens into the United States. Specifically, this statute allows the President to suspend the entry of any aliens, or any class of aliens, for a period he deems necessary if he "find[s] that the entry of the covered aliens... would be detrimental to the interest of the United States."

The court emphasizes that the legal threshold for exercising this power is relatively low. The statute does not require the President to provide a detailed roadmap or an exhaustive evidentiary record to the judiciary. Instead, the "sole prerequisite" is the President’s finding that the entry of the aliens would be detrimental to the national interest. In the context of *Pacito*, the court noted that by issuing EO 14163, the President had made this finding and fulfilled the statutory requirement.

## Judicial Deference: The 'exudes deference' standard from Trump v. Hawaii

A critical component of the court's reasoning is the degree of judicial deference owed to the Executive Branch in matters of immigration. The court relies heavily on the Supreme Court's decision in *Trump v. Hawaii* to establish the standard of review. According to the court, the authority granted by 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President. 

For a law student, "exudes deference" signifies that the court will not act as a secondary fact-finder or a substitute for the President's judgment. The court explicitly stated that it cannot engage in a "searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications." In other words, the court's role is not to decide whether the President’s reasons for suspending the refugee program are "correct" or "reasonable" in a political or social sense; rather, its role is to ensure the President has exercised the power granted by Congress. Because the President made the finding of "detriment," the court finds the action to be within the scope of his authority.

## The President's Prerogative: Discretion to suspend 'any class of aliens'

The court further highlights the breadth of the President's discretion regarding *who* can be restricted. The language of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) allows the President to suspend the entry of "any class of aliens." The court interprets this to mean that the President has the power to target specific categories of immigrants, including refugees.

By characterizing "refugees" as a "class of aliens," the court affirms that the President’s authority is not limited to broad, country-wide bans, but extends to specific programs or categories of entry. This "broad discretion" reinforces the idea that the Executive Branch has significant latitude in determining which groups may be excluded from the United States based on the President’s determination of national interest.

## Analysis of EO 14163: Whether the order exceeded textual limits of the statute

The plaintiffs challenged EO 14163 on the grounds that it violated the Refugee Act of 1980. However, in evaluating the validity of the suspension of refugee admissions, the court performed a textual analysis of the Executive Order in relation to 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). The court found that the Executive Order did not exceed any "textual limit" placed on the President by the statute.

Because the statute allows for the suspension of "any class of aliens" and the President has already declared the entry of refugees to be "detrimental," the court concluded that the order was a valid exercise of statutory power. The court did not find that the Refugee Act of 1980 (which is codified in the Immigration and Nationality Act) created a conflict that would override the specific, high-level authority granted to the President under § 1182(f) to impose entry restrictions.

## The Court's Conclusion on Executive Authority

The court’s analysis of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) leads to a specific conclusion regarding the government's likelihood of success on the merits of the challenge to the refugee admission suspension. Applying the first factor of the stay standard—whether the applicant is likely to succeed on the merits—the court determined that the government is "likely to prevail" on the plaintiffs' challenge to the validity of EO 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions.

This conclusion is based entirely on the "broad discretion" conferred by the statute and the high level of deference the court must afford the President. While the court eventually finds a different issue regarding the government's mandatory obligations under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) and 8 U.S.C. § 1522 (concerning the provision of services), its ruling on the *suspension of entry* itself is a clear affirmation of the President's expansive authority under § 1182(f).
