Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 4: # Standards for Judicial Stays and the Court's Order

## The Nken v. Holder Four-Factor Test for Stays

To understand the Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Pacito v. Trump*, a student must first understand the procedural mechanism of a "stay." A stay is a court order that temporarily halts the enforcement of a lower court’s judgment or injunction while an appeal is pending. In this case, the government sought to stop the district court’s preliminary injunctions, which prohibited the administration from suspending the refugee program and defunding resettlement services.

The court applies the four-factor test established in *Nken v. Holder* to determine whether such a stay should be granted. These factors include: (1) whether the applicant has shown a strong likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will suffer irreparable injury absent the stay; (3) whether the stay will substantially injure other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. The court emphasizes that the first two factors—success on the merits and irreparable injury—are the most critical in this balancing test.

## Likelihood of Success on the Merits: Balancing the EO vs. the APA claims

The court’s analysis of the "likelihood of success on the merits" is bifurcated because the plaintiffs’ challenge relies on two different legal theories: the validity of the Executive Order (EO) and the government’s compliance with the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) regarding funding.

On the issue of the Executive Order (EO 14163), the court finds the government is likely to prevail. The court relies on the principle of "exuded deference" regarding the President’s authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). Because this statute allows the President to suspend the entry of any class of aliens that he "finds" would be detrimental to U.S. interests, the court concludes the President has fulfilled the statutory prerequisite. The court notes that it is not the court's role to engage in a "searching inquiry" into the President’s specific justifications, as the language of the statute provides "ample power" to impose such restrictions.

Conversely, the court finds the government is unlikely to prevail on the plaintiffs’ challenge regarding the defunding of resettlement services under the APA. The court identifies a specific statutory mandate in 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to provide "reception and placement" services—such as employment training, English language training, and cash assistance—to refugees who have been admitted into the United States. Because these services are mandated by statute (to the extent of available appropriations), the court views the government's termination of the cooperative agreements as a violation of that legal duty.

## Irreparable Injury and Public Interest considerations

Beyond the merits of the legal arguments, the court considers the practical impacts of the stay. Regarding irreparable injury and the public interest, the court finds that these factors generally favor the United States government. The court points to the "broad discretion" conferred on the President in matters of immigration and foreign policy. Because the stay would allow the government to continue its policy decisions—which the court views as generally aligned with the President's broad authority—the court finds that the public interest and the potential for injury to the government's policy objectives weigh in favor of the government.

## The Court's Stay Order: Vacating prior stays and the limited reinstatement of cooperative agreements

The court’s disposition involves a complex "vacatur" and "stay" of several prior orders. To clarify the current status of the litigation, the court vacates (cancels) its previous administrative stay and the stays issued in March, April, and May. In its place, it issues a new stay pending the final resolution of the appeal.

Crucially, this new stay applies to the district court’s preliminary injunctions "in their entirety, except as specified." This means that while the government is generally permitted to proceed with the policies challenged by the plaintiffs (the suspension of refugee admissions under the EO), it is *not* permitted to continue the suspension of the cooperative agreements used to provide mandatory resettlement services. The court effectively carves out the mandatory statutory obligations from the broader executive policy.

## The Court's Directive to the Government to fulfill statutory obligations

The final portion of the order serves as a specific directive to the government. The court notes that the government admitted it was "uncertain" whether it had made progress in securing new agencies to provide the mandated resettlement services after terminating the previous cooperative agreements. 

Because 8 U.S.C. § 1522 creates a legal duty to provide these services to refugees already admitted to the country, the court directs the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide those services. The court specifies that these must include the "usual and customary services" provided under the previous agreements. By issuing this directive, the court ensures that while the President may limit *future* admissions (a policy matter), the government must still fulfill its existing legal obligations to those refugees already within the U.S. (a statutory mandate).
