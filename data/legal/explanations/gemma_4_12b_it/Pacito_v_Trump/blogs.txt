Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# The 'Hawaii' Doctrine and the Limits of Judicial Review in Refugee Policy

The Ninth Circuit’s recent order in *Pacito v. Trump* offers a striking illustration of the modern "deference" regime surrounding presidential immigration authority. By grounding its stay of the district court’s preliminary injunction in the principles of *Trump v. Hawaii*, the court has signaled a profound retreat from judicial oversight of the President’s power to suspend the United States Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP). While the court maintains a tether to the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) regarding the funding of existing services, its treatment of entry restrictions creates a nearly unreviewable zone of executive discretion.

At the heart of the court’s reasoning is the interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which allows the President to suspend the entry of any class of aliens if he "finds" that such entry would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States." In *Pacito*, the court leaned heavily on the "exudes deference" standard established in *Trump v. Hawaii*, asserting that the President has "ample power" to impose entry restrictions. Crucially, the court held that it cannot engage in a "searching inquiry" into the actual persuasiveness of the President’s justifications. Because the President has technically made a "finding" of detriment, the court viewed the executive order as satisfying the "sole prerequisite" of the statute.

This approach effectively transforms a finding of "detriment" into a self-executing legal shield. If the judiciary is barred from probing the factual basis or the proportionality of the President's determination, the court’s role is reduced to a mere check on whether the President *made* a finding at all, rather than whether that finding is reasonable or consistent with the underlying goals of the Refugee Act of 1980. By choosing not to look behind the curtain of the executive's "finding," the Ninth Circuit has reinforced a high wall of non-justiciability around the USRAP.

However, the court did not grant the executive a blank check. A fascinating doctrinal distinction emerged between the President's power to *exclude* and the government's duty to *provide*. While the court deferred to the President on the suspension of new admissions, it found that the government was unlikely to prevail on the plaintiffs' challenge to the defunding of resettlement services. The court interpreted 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as a statutory mandate requiring the government to provide "reception and placement" services to refugees already admitted to the country. This suggests a bifurcated view of the Refugee Act: the "entry" provisions are subject to nearly absolute executive discretion, while the "domestic resettlement" provisions are treated as hard statutory obligations that the President cannot unilaterally dismantle via executive order.

The downstream effects of this reasoning are significant. By narrowing the scope of judicial review to the existence of a "finding" rather than the quality of that finding, the court has created a framework where the USRAP can be suspended with minimal risk of successful litigation. This creates a "deference trap" where the executive can bypass the spirit of the Refugee Act—which is intended to be a "general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees"—by invoking the broad powers of § 1182(f). While the court’s insistence on funding for those already admitted preserves a floor of protections, the "Hawaii" doctrine ensures that the door to the country remains subject to the whims of the executive, shielded from the traditional hallmarks of administrative law review.



# Authorization vs. Mandate: A Textualist Tussle Over 8 U.S.C. § 1522

The Ninth Circuit’s recent decision in *Pacito v. Trump* presents a fascinating study in the limits of executive discretion. While the court reaffirmed the President’s sweeping authority to gatekeep the nation’s borders, it simultaneously signaled a judicial willingness to intervene when the executive branch attempts to dismantle domestic obligations to those already admitted. At the heart of this split is a classic constructionist debate: does a statute titled "Authorization" contain a mandatory command, or does it merely provide the permission for the government to act as it sees fit?

The case centers on two distinct components of the Trump administration’s overhaul of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP). First, the President used the broad authority of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend the entry of refugees, a move the Ninth Circuit stayed, citing the "exuding deference" typically afforded to the President in immigration matters under *Trump v. Hawaii*. However, the court took a different path regarding the second component: the government’s termination of "reception and placement" (R&P) services—the logistical and financial infrastructure that supports refugees once they arrive on U.S. soil.

The majority’s reasoning rests on a functionalist reading of 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The court observed that the statute requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to provide specific services—such as employment training, English language acquisition, and cash assistance—"to the extent of available appropriations." Because the government terminated its cooperative agreements with nonprofit agencies and admitted it had no immediate replacement ready, the court concluded that the government was in breach of its statutory duty. By ordering the government to reinstate those agreements, the court effectively held that the "shall" in § 1522(a) creates a mandatory floor of service that the executive cannot simply "switch off" by severing contracts.

Judge Lee’s partial dissent, however, offers a purer textualist counter-narrative that highlights a growing tension in administrative law. Lee argues that the court overstepped by transforming a permissive framework into a mandatory mandate. His argument is anchored in the section's title: "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." In statutory interpretation, a title often serves as a primary indicator of a statute’s purpose. Lee contends that if Congress intended to create a mandatory requirement, it would not have framed the entire section as an "authorization."

Lee’s analysis of the "shall" in § 1522(a) is particularly sharp. He argues that the mandatory language is a "condition of performance"—a set of rules the government must follow *if* it chooses to exercise its discretion to fund a program. In Lee’s view, the repeated use of the word "authorized" in the subsequent subsections (b) through (e) reinforces the idea that the executive branch holds the ultimate "on/off" switch. If the government chooses not to fund the programs, the conditions for those programs are never triggered, and no violation occurs.

The doctrinal implications of this split are significant for the future of the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) and executive power. If the majority’s view prevails, the judiciary maintains a robust check on the executive's ability to "sunset" programs by simply withdrawing funding or terminating contracts, provided the underlying statute uses mandatory language. This prevents the executive from using budgetary maneuvers to circumvent what Congress intended as a persistent obligation.

Conversely, if Lee’s textualist interpretation gains traction, it would grant the executive a much broader "pocket veto" over domestic policy. It would suggest that as long as a statute is framed as an "authorization," the President can effectively repeal the program’s effects by withholding resources, regardless of how many "shalls" appear in the accompanying regulations. *Pacito* leaves us with a striking paradox: the President may have the power to decide who enters the country, but the court is signaling that once they are inside, the "Authorization" of Congress may carry more weight than the Executive’s discretion to provide for them.



# Administrative Law and the Boundaries of Executive De-funding

In the shifting landscape of executive power, few areas offer as much latitude as immigration. The Ninth Circuit’s recent order in *Pacito v. Trump* highlights a sophisticated tension within that latitude: the distinction between a President’s authority to determine who *enters* the country and the government’s statutory obligations to those already *inside* it. While the court reaffirmed the broad deference traditionally afforded to the Executive Branch under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), it simultaneously signaled that such deference does not grant a "blank check" to bypass specific, mandatory funding and service requirements established by Congress.

The core of the dispute involves two distinct executive actions. First, the administration issued Executive Order 14163 to suspend the refugee admissions program. Second, it issued Executive Order 14169 to defund the refugee services necessary for those already admitted. The Ninth Circuit’s handling of these two actions reveals a bifurcated judicial philosophy. On the former, the court leaned heavily into the precedent of *Trump v. Hawaii*, noting that the "sole prerequisite" for a suspension under § 1182(f) is a Presidential finding of "detriment." By refusing to engage in a "searching inquiry" into the President’s justifications, the court upheld the hallmark of modern immigration law: the President’s near-plenary power to gatekeep the border.

However, the court took a markedly different path regarding the defunding of "reception and placement" services. Here, the court found that the government was unlikely to prevail on the plaintiffs' challenge under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). The reasoning hinges on 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which contains various "shall" provisions requiring the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to provide employment training, English language training, and cash assistance "to the extent of available appropriations." Unlike the admissions power, which is a discretionary tool of national security, these services are framed as a statutory obligation for a specific class of individuals—those already admitted to the United States.

The court's analytical pivot is significant. It suggests that while the President may have the authority to stop the flow of new arrivals, that authority does not automatically dissolve the government's duty to fulfill existing statutory commitments to those currently in the country. Because the government admitted it had terminated all cooperative agreements without a viable plan to replace them—despite the existence of billions in appropriated funds—the court characterized the abrupt termination as a violation of the mandatory requirements of § 1522. By ordering the reinstatement of these contracts, the court asserted that "available appropriations" create a functional mandate that the Executive cannot summarily ignore via the "detriment" finding used for admissions.

The concurring dissent by Judge Lee captures the precise doctrinal fault line at issue: is a statute that "authorizes" a program also a "mandate" to perform it? Lee argues that the title of § 1522 and the language of its subsections denote a permissive grant of power to the Executive, not a requirement to act. If Lee’s interpretation prevails on appeal or in the final opinion, the government’s ability to "zero out" programs becomes much broader. However, the majority's current stance suggests a more constrained view of executive power, seeking to protect the "substantive" rights of individuals who have already entered the legal fold.

Ultimately, *Pacito* serves as a cautionary note for the "Unitary Executive" theory as applied to immigration. It suggests that the "detriment" clause of § 1182(f) is not a jurisdictional trump card that allows the President to override every other provision of the Immigration and Nationality Act. Instead, the court is carving out a "statutory floor." The takeaway for future litigation is clear: the Executive may have the power to close the door, but it may not be permitted to abandon those who have already walked through it if Congress has mandated a specific standard of care.
