Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Case Background and Procedural History

## Executive Order 14163: Suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)

The litigation stems from a series of executive actions taken by the President in early 2025. On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order (EO) 14163, titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” Under the authority of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the President determined that allowing refugees to enter the United States under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States." Consequently, the Order directed a suspension of refugee entry pending further findings. 

## Executive Order 14169: Suspension of USRAP funding

Following the suspension of entry, the government took further action regarding the logistics of the program. On January 24, 2025, the Department of State suspended all funding for the USRAP. This action was purportedly taken under Executive Order 14169, "Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid." This funding suspension was significant because it included the money used for domestic resettlement services—the logistical support required to help refugees integrate into American communities after they arrive in the country.

## The Plaintiffs: Refugees and non-profit resettlement organizations

The plaintiffs in this case represent two distinct groups affected by these executive orders. The first group consists of individual refugees who had either already been admitted to the United States or had been approved for resettlement but were still outside the country awaiting arrival. The second group consists of three non-profit organizations. These organizations held cooperative agreements with the State Department to provide overseas processing for refugee applications and to provide resettlement services for those already admitted to the U.S. Because the executive orders halted funding and suspended the program, both the individuals and the organizations faced significant disruptions to their status and operations.

## Legal Claims: Violations of the Refugee Act of 1980 and the Administrative Procedure Act (APA)

The plaintiffs filed suit in the Western District of Washington in February 2025, seeking to block the executive orders. Their legal challenge rests on two primary pillars. First, they allege that the suspension of the refugee program under EO 14163 violates the Refugee Act of 1980, which serves as the general rule for the treatment of refugees and displaced persons. Second, they argue that the defunding of the USRAP under EO 14169 violates the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), which governs how federal agencies may issue regulations and allocate resources.

## District Court Preliminary Injunctions: The initial prohibition of the Executive Orders

The district court acted quickly, issuing a series of preliminary injunctions to halt the government's actions. On February 25, 2025, the court issued an oral preliminary injunction prohibiting the enforcement of the primary sections of EO 14163. In response, the government moved to terminate every cooperative agreement for refugee reception and placement services in the United States. 

The district court followed this with a written order on February 28, 2025, which expanded the scope of the injunction to specifically prohibit the suspension of USRAP funding. On March 24, 2025, the court issued a second preliminary injunction ordering the State Department to reinstate all the cooperative agreements it had terminated following the initial February ruling.

## The Appeals Process: Government motions for stays and the Ninth Circuit's initial stay orders

The government appealed these injunctions, leading to a complex series of "stays"—temporary freezes on the lower court's orders—within the Ninth Circuit. On March 25, 2025, a motions panel of the Ninth Circuit granted a partial stay of the February 28 order. The court noted that the President's authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) "exudes deference," yet the court refused to stay the injunction for individuals who had been conditionally approved for refugee status before January 20, 2025. This created a "limited carveout" where some refugees were still protected by the injunction while others were not.

The procedural history grew more complex in July 2025. After the district court attempted to establish a framework to manage this carveout, the government filed an emergency motion. On July 18, 2025, the Ninth Circuit issued an administrative stay of that framework. The court then scheduled expedited oral argument for September 3, 2025, resulting in the current order, which vacates the previous stays and issues a new stay pending a final decision on the merits.

# Presidential Authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)

## Statutory Basis: 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and the 'Detrimental Interests' finding

To understand the court’s analysis of Executive Order (EO) 14163, students must first identify the specific statutory source of the President's power. The court focuses on 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), a provision of the Immigration and Nationality Act that grants the President the authority to regulate the entry of aliens into the United States. Specifically, this statute allows the President to suspend the entry of any aliens, or any class of aliens, for a period he deems necessary if he "find[s] that the entry of the covered aliens... would be detrimental to the interest of the United States."

The court emphasizes that the legal threshold for exercising this power is relatively low. The statute does not require the President to provide a detailed roadmap or an exhaustive evidentiary record to the judiciary. Instead, the "sole prerequisite" is the President’s finding that the entry of the aliens would be detrimental to the national interest. In the context of *Pacito*, the court noted that by issuing EO 14163, the President had made this finding and fulfilled the statutory requirement.

## Judicial Deference: The 'exudes deference' standard from Trump v. Hawaii

A critical component of the court's reasoning is the degree of judicial deference owed to the Executive Branch in matters of immigration. The court relies heavily on the Supreme Court's decision in *Trump v. Hawaii* to establish the standard of review. According to the court, the authority granted by 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President. 

For a law student, "exudes deference" signifies that the court will not act as a secondary fact-finder or a substitute for the President's judgment. The court explicitly stated that it cannot engage in a "searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications." In other words, the court's role is not to decide whether the President’s reasons for suspending the refugee program are "correct" or "reasonable" in a political or social sense; rather, its role is to ensure the President has exercised the power granted by Congress. Because the President made the finding of "detriment," the court finds the action to be within the scope of his authority.

## The President's Prerogative: Discretion to suspend 'any class of aliens'

The court further highlights the breadth of the President's discretion regarding *who* can be restricted. The language of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) allows the President to suspend the entry of "any class of aliens." The court interprets this to mean that the President has the power to target specific categories of immigrants, including refugees.

By characterizing "refugees" as a "class of aliens," the court affirms that the President’s authority is not limited to broad, country-wide bans, but extends to specific programs or categories of entry. This "broad discretion" reinforces the idea that the Executive Branch has significant latitude in determining which groups may be excluded from the United States based on the President’s determination of national interest.

## Analysis of EO 14163: Whether the order exceeded textual limits of the statute

The plaintiffs challenged EO 14163 on the grounds that it violated the Refugee Act of 1980. However, in evaluating the validity of the suspension of refugee admissions, the court performed a textual analysis of the Executive Order in relation to 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). The court found that the Executive Order did not exceed any "textual limit" placed on the President by the statute.

Because the statute allows for the suspension of "any class of aliens" and the President has already declared the entry of refugees to be "detrimental," the court concluded that the order was a valid exercise of statutory power. The court did not find that the Refugee Act of 1980 (which is codified in the Immigration and Nationality Act) created a conflict that would override the specific, high-level authority granted to the President under § 1182(f) to impose entry restrictions.

## The Court's Conclusion on Executive Authority

The court’s analysis of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) leads to a specific conclusion regarding the government's likelihood of success on the merits of the challenge to the refugee admission suspension. Applying the first factor of the stay standard—whether the applicant is likely to succeed on the merits—the court determined that the government is "likely to prevail" on the plaintiffs' challenge to the validity of EO 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions.

This conclusion is based entirely on the "broad discretion" conferred by the statute and the high level of deference the court must afford the President. While the court eventually finds a different issue regarding the government's mandatory obligations under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) and 8 U.S.C. § 1522 (concerning the provision of services), its ruling on the *suspension of entry* itself is a clear affirmation of the President's expansive authority under § 1182(f).

# Statutory Mandates for Refugee Services: 8 U.S.C. § 1522

## Overview of 8 U.S.C. § 1522: Reception and Placement Services

To understand the Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Pacito v. Trump*, a student must distinguish between the President's broad authority to regulate immigration and the specific statutory mandates the government faces once a refugee has been admitted to the United States. While the President possesses significant discretion to determine who may enter the country, 8 U.S.C. § 1522 governs the "reception and placement" services that the government is required to provide to refugees once they are within U.S. borders. These services are designed to facilitate the transition of refugees into American society, ensuring they have the support necessary to become self-sufficient.

The court’s analysis centers on whether the executive branch can unilaterally cease these services. The court distinguishes the President's power under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)—which "exudes deference" and allows the President to suspend refugee admissions if they are "detrimental to the interests of the United States"—from the requirements of § 1522. While the President may have the authority to stop new refugees from arriving, the court suggests that the government has a continuing legal obligation to provide for those already admitted.

## Specific Mandatory Obligations: Employment Training, English Language Training, and Cash Assistance

The core of the government's legal obligation lies in the specific language of 8 U.S.C. § 1522(a)(1)(A). The court identifies several "mandatory" conditions that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement must fulfill, provided there are appropriations available. These include the requirement to make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement, provide opportunities for English language training, and ensure that cash assistance is available in a manner that does not discourage economic self-sufficiency. Additionally, the statute requires that women be given the same opportunities as men to participate in these programs.

For the law student, the key linguistic trigger here is the word "shall." By using "shall," Congress signaled a mandatory duty rather than a discretionary option. The court notes that while these obligations are limited "to the extent of available appropriations," the government cannot simply ignore the requirements of the statute. The fact that Congress had appropriated billions of dollars for migration and refugee assistance for FY 2025, despite some rescissions, reinforced the court’s view that the government had the resources to fulfill these mandates.

## Unaccompanied Minor Refugees: Legal and Financial Responsibility under § 1522(d)(2)(B)

The statute provides additional, specific protections for a vulnerable class of refugees: unaccompanied minors. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522(d)(2)(B), the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement is required to assume legal and financial responsibility for such children "if necessary" during the period they are in the United States or in transit before being placed. Furthermore, the Director is authorized to make "necessary decisions" to provide for the child's immediate care.

While these provisions include qualifying phrases like "if necessary," the court interprets them as part of a broader framework of government responsibility. These sections ensure that the government maintains a safety net for minors who lack parental supervision, creating a non-discretionary duty to act when the circumstances of the minor's arrival necessitate it.

## Funding Mechanisms: The Role of Cooperative Agreements with Private and Public Non-profits

A critical procedural element of § 1522 is how the government delivers these services. The statute allows for these services to be provided through "contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with 'public or private nonprofit agencies.'" This is significant because it explains the standing of the organizational plaintiffs in *Pacito*. These organizations were the primary vehicles through which the State Department historically delivered the mandated "reception and placement" services.

The court highlights that the government’s decision to terminate these cooperative agreements—without a clear plan to replace them—was a primary driver of the litigation. Because the statute authorizes the use of these private and public partnerships to fulfill its mandates, the government’s unilateral termination of those agreements effectively stalled the delivery of services required by law.

## The Government's Failure to Provide Services: The Court's Finding of a Non-Discretionary Duty

The Ninth Circuit’s ultimate order reflects a finding that the government has a non-discretionary duty to provide for refugees already admitted to the U.S. While the court acknowledged the President’s broad power to suspend the entry of new refugees under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), it found that the government was unlikely to prevail on the plaintiffs' challenge to the defunding of services under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA).

The court’s reasoning was grounded in the government's own admissions. During oral argument, the government admitted it was "unaware whether any progress had been made" toward securing new agencies to provide the services mandated by § 1522. Because the government had essentially halted the existing service infrastructure and failed to establish a replacement, the court determined that the government was in violation of its statutory duties. Consequently, the court issued a specific directive: the government must reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide the "usual and customary services" to refugees already admitted to the United States.

This distinction is further highlighted by Judge Lee’s dissent, which offers a counter-argument for students to consider: the "Authorization" vs. "Mandate" debate. Judge Lee argues that because the title of § 1522 is "Authorization for programs," the word "shall" in the subsequent subsections merely imposes conditions on the government *if* it chooses to fund the programs, rather than requiring it to do so. This highlights a fundamental tension in statutory interpretation—whether "shall" creates a mandatory duty or a mandatory condition on a discretionary act.

# Standards for Judicial Stays and the Court's Order

## The Nken v. Holder Four-Factor Test for Stays

To understand the Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Pacito v. Trump*, a student must first understand the procedural mechanism of a "stay." A stay is a court order that temporarily halts the enforcement of a lower court’s judgment or injunction while an appeal is pending. In this case, the government sought to stop the district court’s preliminary injunctions, which prohibited the administration from suspending the refugee program and defunding resettlement services.

The court applies the four-factor test established in *Nken v. Holder* to determine whether such a stay should be granted. These factors include: (1) whether the applicant has shown a strong likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will suffer irreparable injury absent the stay; (3) whether the stay will substantially injure other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. The court emphasizes that the first two factors—success on the merits and irreparable injury—are the most critical in this balancing test.

## Likelihood of Success on the Merits: Balancing the EO vs. the APA claims

The court’s analysis of the "likelihood of success on the merits" is bifurcated because the plaintiffs’ challenge relies on two different legal theories: the validity of the Executive Order (EO) and the government’s compliance with the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) regarding funding.

On the issue of the Executive Order (EO 14163), the court finds the government is likely to prevail. The court relies on the principle of "exuded deference" regarding the President’s authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). Because this statute allows the President to suspend the entry of any class of aliens that he "finds" would be detrimental to U.S. interests, the court concludes the President has fulfilled the statutory prerequisite. The court notes that it is not the court's role to engage in a "searching inquiry" into the President’s specific justifications, as the language of the statute provides "ample power" to impose such restrictions.

Conversely, the court finds the government is unlikely to prevail on the plaintiffs’ challenge regarding the defunding of resettlement services under the APA. The court identifies a specific statutory mandate in 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to provide "reception and placement" services—such as employment training, English language training, and cash assistance—to refugees who have been admitted into the United States. Because these services are mandated by statute (to the extent of available appropriations), the court views the government's termination of the cooperative agreements as a violation of that legal duty.

## Irreparable Injury and Public Interest considerations

Beyond the merits of the legal arguments, the court considers the practical impacts of the stay. Regarding irreparable injury and the public interest, the court finds that these factors generally favor the United States government. The court points to the "broad discretion" conferred on the President in matters of immigration and foreign policy. Because the stay would allow the government to continue its policy decisions—which the court views as generally aligned with the President's broad authority—the court finds that the public interest and the potential for injury to the government's policy objectives weigh in favor of the government.

## The Court's Stay Order: Vacating prior stays and the limited reinstatement of cooperative agreements

The court’s disposition involves a complex "vacatur" and "stay" of several prior orders. To clarify the current status of the litigation, the court vacates (cancels) its previous administrative stay and the stays issued in March, April, and May. In its place, it issues a new stay pending the final resolution of the appeal.

Crucially, this new stay applies to the district court’s preliminary injunctions "in their entirety, except as specified." This means that while the government is generally permitted to proceed with the policies challenged by the plaintiffs (the suspension of refugee admissions under the EO), it is *not* permitted to continue the suspension of the cooperative agreements used to provide mandatory resettlement services. The court effectively carves out the mandatory statutory obligations from the broader executive policy.

## The Court's Directive to the Government to fulfill statutory obligations

The final portion of the order serves as a specific directive to the government. The court notes that the government admitted it was "uncertain" whether it had made progress in securing new agencies to provide the mandated resettlement services after terminating the previous cooperative agreements. 

Because 8 U.S.C. § 1522 creates a legal duty to provide these services to refugees already admitted to the country, the court directs the government to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary to provide those services. The court specifies that these must include the "usual and customary services" provided under the previous agreements. By issuing this directive, the court ensures that while the President may limit *future* admissions (a policy matter), the government must still fulfill its existing legal obligations to those refugees already within the U.S. (a statutory mandate).

# Statutory Interpretation: Authorization vs. Mandation

In many cases involving the executive branch and the use of federal funds, a central legal question arises: Does a statute *require* the government to perform an action, or does it simply grant the government *permission* to do so? This distinction between "mandates" and "authorizations" is a cornerstone of statutory interpretation. In *Pacito v. Trump*, Circuit Judge Lee’s dissenting opinion provides a masterclass in how judicial interpretation shifts depending on whether a judge views a statute as a command or a menu of options.

## The Dissenting View: Section 1522 as 'Authorization' (Permissive) rather than 'Mandate' (Required)

To understand Judge Lee’s dissent, one must first understand the difference between permissive and mandatory language. A "mandate" creates a legal obligation for the government to act; if the government fails to act, it is in violation of the law. An "authorization," conversely, provides the legal authority for the government to act but does not compel it to do so. Judge Lee argues that the primary purpose of 8 U.S.C. § 1522 is to provide the executive branch with the power to run refugee programs, not to force it to do so.

Lee grounds this interpretation in the title of the statute: “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” In statutory construction, the title of a section often serves as a primary guide to its legislative intent. Lee argues that the word "Authorization" is inherently permissive. By framing the section as an authorization, Congress was granting the executive branch "permission to do something," rather than creating a "general rule" that the government must spend money on these specific services.

## Textual Analysis: Interpreting 'shall' in the context of 'to the extent of available appropriations'

The majority in *Pacito* relied heavily on the word "shall" in 8 U.S.C. § 1522(a) to conclude that the government has a mandatory duty to provide services. In many legal contexts, "shall" is interpreted as a command. However, Judge Lee offers a more nuanced textual analysis by looking at the surrounding qualifiers. Specifically, Lee points out that the "shall" provision is tied to the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations."

Lee argues that the word "shall" in § 1522(a) does not create a mandate to spend; rather, it creates a "condition" or a "consideration" for *if* the government chooses to fund the programs. Under Lee’s interpretation, the statute says: "If you choose to provide these services, you *shall* ensure they meet these specific standards (e.g., providing English training and employment placement)." In this view, the "shall" describes the *manner* in which a discretionary program must be run, not a requirement that the program be run in the first place.

## Executive Discretion: The meaning of 'as needed' and 'if necessary' in the statute

A key component of statutory interpretation is identifying "qualifiers"—words that limit the scope of a command. Judge Lee highlights several such qualifiers within Section 1522 that suggest the executive branch retains broad discretion. For example, the statute mentions that the Director shall take responsibility for unaccompanied children "if necessary" and is authorized to make decisions "to provide for the child’s immediate care." 

Lee argues that the inclusion of "if necessary" and "as needed" (regarding medical and cash support) acts as a signal of executive discretion. If Congress intended to create a mandatory, uniform requirement for every refugee, it would likely use "bright-line" language that applies regardless of circumstances. By using language that leaves the decision-making to the executive's judgment on what is "necessary" or "needed," the statute reinforces the idea that the government has the power to decide whether and when to allocate these resources.

## The Conflict: Mandatory spending vs. discretionary program implementation

The core conflict in *Pacito* rests on whether 8 U.S.C. § 1522 creates a "mandatory spending" obligation. Mandatory spending occurs when Congress has already decided that a program will be funded and established the rules for that spending. Discretionary spending occurs when Congress provides the authority to spend money but leaves it to the executive branch to decide if, when, and how to do so.

Judge Lee contends that the structure of Section 1522—specifically subsections (b) through (e)—consistently uses the phrase "is authorized." He argues that these provisions provide the "metes and bounds" of the programs, allowing the executive to craft programs at its discretion as long as appropriations are available. Because the statute provides the authority to craft these programs but does not explicitly command the government to fund them, Lee concludes that the government is not legally required to maintain the "reception and placement" services even if they have historically done so.

## Implications for Judicial Review of Executive Branch funding decisions

Judge Lee’s dissent carries significant implications for the scope of judicial review over the executive branch. If a statute is interpreted as an "authorization," the court’s role is limited: the court can only intervene if the government acts in a way that exceeds the permission granted by Congress. If the government chooses not to act at all (i.e., chooses not to spend the money), the court generally has no power to compel action because there is no underlying mandate.

By contrast, if a statute is interpreted as a "mandate," the court has a much broader role. In that scenario, the court can review the executive’s funding decisions and order the government to spend money or provide services if it fails to do so. Lee’s dissent serves as a reminder to students that the same set of facts and the same set of words can lead to vastly different outcomes based on whether a judge views the law as a "must" or a "may."
