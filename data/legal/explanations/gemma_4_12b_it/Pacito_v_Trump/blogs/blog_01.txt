Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# The 'Hawaii' Doctrine and the Limits of Judicial Review in Refugee Policy

The Ninth Circuit’s recent order in *Pacito v. Trump* offers a striking illustration of the modern "deference" regime surrounding presidential immigration authority. By grounding its stay of the district court’s preliminary injunction in the principles of *Trump v. Hawaii*, the court has signaled a profound retreat from judicial oversight of the President’s power to suspend the United States Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP). While the court maintains a tether to the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) regarding the funding of existing services, its treatment of entry restrictions creates a nearly unreviewable zone of executive discretion.

At the heart of the court’s reasoning is the interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which allows the President to suspend the entry of any class of aliens if he "finds" that such entry would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States." In *Pacito*, the court leaned heavily on the "exudes deference" standard established in *Trump v. Hawaii*, asserting that the President has "ample power" to impose entry restrictions. Crucially, the court held that it cannot engage in a "searching inquiry" into the actual persuasiveness of the President’s justifications. Because the President has technically made a "finding" of detriment, the court viewed the executive order as satisfying the "sole prerequisite" of the statute.

This approach effectively transforms a finding of "detriment" into a self-executing legal shield. If the judiciary is barred from probing the factual basis or the proportionality of the President's determination, the court’s role is reduced to a mere check on whether the President *made* a finding at all, rather than whether that finding is reasonable or consistent with the underlying goals of the Refugee Act of 1980. By choosing not to look behind the curtain of the executive's "finding," the Ninth Circuit has reinforced a high wall of non-justiciability around the USRAP.

However, the court did not grant the executive a blank check. A fascinating doctrinal distinction emerged between the President's power to *exclude* and the government's duty to *provide*. While the court deferred to the President on the suspension of new admissions, it found that the government was unlikely to prevail on the plaintiffs' challenge to the defunding of resettlement services. The court interpreted 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as a statutory mandate requiring the government to provide "reception and placement" services to refugees already admitted to the country. This suggests a bifurcated view of the Refugee Act: the "entry" provisions are subject to nearly absolute executive discretion, while the "domestic resettlement" provisions are treated as hard statutory obligations that the President cannot unilaterally dismantle via executive order.

The downstream effects of this reasoning are significant. By narrowing the scope of judicial review to the existence of a "finding" rather than the quality of that finding, the court has created a framework where the USRAP can be suspended with minimal risk of successful litigation. This creates a "deference trap" where the executive can bypass the spirit of the Refugee Act—which is intended to be a "general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees"—by invoking the broad powers of § 1182(f). While the court’s insistence on funding for those already admitted preserves a floor of protections, the "Hawaii" doctrine ensures that the door to the country remains subject to the whims of the executive, shielded from the traditional hallmarks of administrative law review.
