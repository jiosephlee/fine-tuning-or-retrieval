Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Authorization vs. Mandate: A Textualist Tussle Over 8 U.S.C. § 1522

The Ninth Circuit’s recent decision in *Pacito v. Trump* presents a fascinating study in the limits of executive discretion. While the court reaffirmed the President’s sweeping authority to gatekeep the nation’s borders, it simultaneously signaled a judicial willingness to intervene when the executive branch attempts to dismantle domestic obligations to those already admitted. At the heart of this split is a classic constructionist debate: does a statute titled "Authorization" contain a mandatory command, or does it merely provide the permission for the government to act as it sees fit?

The case centers on two distinct components of the Trump administration’s overhaul of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP). First, the President used the broad authority of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend the entry of refugees, a move the Ninth Circuit stayed, citing the "exuding deference" typically afforded to the President in immigration matters under *Trump v. Hawaii*. However, the court took a different path regarding the second component: the government’s termination of "reception and placement" (R&P) services—the logistical and financial infrastructure that supports refugees once they arrive on U.S. soil.

The majority’s reasoning rests on a functionalist reading of 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The court observed that the statute requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to provide specific services—such as employment training, English language acquisition, and cash assistance—"to the extent of available appropriations." Because the government terminated its cooperative agreements with nonprofit agencies and admitted it had no immediate replacement ready, the court concluded that the government was in breach of its statutory duty. By ordering the government to reinstate those agreements, the court effectively held that the "shall" in § 1522(a) creates a mandatory floor of service that the executive cannot simply "switch off" by severing contracts.

Judge Lee’s partial dissent, however, offers a purer textualist counter-narrative that highlights a growing tension in administrative law. Lee argues that the court overstepped by transforming a permissive framework into a mandatory mandate. His argument is anchored in the section's title: "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." In statutory interpretation, a title often serves as a primary indicator of a statute’s purpose. Lee contends that if Congress intended to create a mandatory requirement, it would not have framed the entire section as an "authorization."

Lee’s analysis of the "shall" in § 1522(a) is particularly sharp. He argues that the mandatory language is a "condition of performance"—a set of rules the government must follow *if* it chooses to exercise its discretion to fund a program. In Lee’s view, the repeated use of the word "authorized" in the subsequent subsections (b) through (e) reinforces the idea that the executive branch holds the ultimate "on/off" switch. If the government chooses not to fund the programs, the conditions for those programs are never triggered, and no violation occurs.

The doctrinal implications of this split are significant for the future of the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) and executive power. If the majority’s view prevails, the judiciary maintains a robust check on the executive's ability to "sunset" programs by simply withdrawing funding or terminating contracts, provided the underlying statute uses mandatory language. This prevents the executive from using budgetary maneuvers to circumvent what Congress intended as a persistent obligation.

Conversely, if Lee’s textualist interpretation gains traction, it would grant the executive a much broader "pocket veto" over domestic policy. It would suggest that as long as a statute is framed as an "authorization," the President can effectively repeal the program’s effects by withholding resources, regardless of how many "shalls" appear in the accompanying regulations. *Pacito* leaves us with a striking paradox: the President may have the power to decide who enters the country, but the court is signaling that once they are inside, the "Authorization" of Congress may carry more weight than the Executive’s discretion to provide for them.
