Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

Chapter 2: # Legal Framework: The Doctrine of Consent

## Consent as an exception to the Fourth Amendment warrant requirement

The Fourth Amendment generally protects individuals against unreasonable searches and seizures, typically requiring the government to obtain a warrant supported by probable cause before entering a private residence. However, consent serves as a well-established exception to this warrant requirement. If an individual with the authority to do so voluntarily allows the police to enter a premises or search an area, the officers do not need a warrant to proceed. In *United States v. Coleman*, the government's primary argument rests on this doctrine: the officers were permitted to enter the home because the defendant’s wife, Lisa Coleman, provided consent.

## Third-party consent and the expectations of privacy

A critical component of the consent doctrine is determining who has the authority to grant it. The court clarifies that consent does not necessarily have to come from the defendant. A third party may provide consent to search a location in which both that third party and the defendant have a "legitimate expectation of privacy." In this case, because Lisa lived in the home and had a legitimate expectation of privacy there, her permission was sufficient to allow the officers entry, even though Jaison Coleman was also present in the home and his privacy was also at stake.

## The 'freely and voluntarily' standard for valid consent

For consent to be constitutionally valid, it must be given "freely and voluntarily." This means the police cannot use physical force, threats, or other forms of coercion to compel an individual to allow a search. To determine whether consent was truly voluntary, courts apply a "totality of the circumstances" test. The court identifies several factors to consider in this analysis: the person’s age, intelligence, and education; whether they were advised of their rights; the length of time they were detained before consenting; whether the consent was immediate or prompted by repeated requests; whether physical coercion was used; and whether the individual was in police custody at the time.

In *Coleman*, the court looked at the body camera footage to evaluate these factors. It noted that Lisa was not in custody and that while Officer Kizer made two requests for entry, the interaction was brief. Although Lisa initially balked, the court found that her eventual agreement was not the result of coercion, but rather a response to the officer’s expressed concern for her children's safety.

## The 'objective reasonableness' standard for the scope of consent

Even when consent is found to be voluntary, the government's search is still limited by the "scope of consent." The police are not permitted to conduct a search that exceeds what the consenting party would reasonably expect. To determine this scope, the court applies an "objective reasonableness" standard: what would a "typical reasonable person" have understood by the specific exchange between the officer and the individual giving consent?

In the *Coleman* case, the court had to decide if the officers overstepped by scanning the home and identifying everyone inside. The court concluded they did not. Because the officers' stated purpose was to check the welfare of the children and ensure Coleman posed no immediate danger, a reasonable person would understand that the officers needed to scan the home and ask basic questions to fulfill that purpose. The fact that this initial "sweep" led to the discovery of firearms did not mean the officers exceeded the scope of Lisa’s consent to enter for the welfare of the children.

## Standard of review for appellate courts (Clear Error)

Because the determination of whether a person gave consent involves weighing facts and interpreting specific interactions, the district court is the primary finder of fact. Consequently, when the Seventh Circuit reviews the district court's decision, it does not perform a de novo review (starting from scratch). Instead, the appellate court reviews the lower court’s finding for "clear error." This means the appellate court will uphold the lower court's ruling unless there is a glaring mistake in how the facts were interpreted or how the law was applied to those facts. In *Coleman*, the court found no such error in the district court's conclusion that Lisa's consent was both voluntary and sufficient to cover the officers' initial actions.
