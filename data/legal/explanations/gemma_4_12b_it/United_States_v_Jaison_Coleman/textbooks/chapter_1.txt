Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Procedural History

## The 911 call and initial police response
The facts of this case began in the early morning hours of April 20, 2023, when Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department responded to a 911 call involving a domestic violence incident. The caller reported that Jaison Coleman had threatened to kill his wife, Lisa Coleman, and was still inside the residence. Upon arriving at the scene shortly after 4:00 a.m., Officer Kizer knocked on the door and engaged in a conversation with Lisa Coleman. During this initial interaction, Lisa appeared hesitant and vague when asked if her children were safe, stating only that they were located in her bedroom.

## The interaction between Officer Kizer and Lisa Coleman
Officer Kizer attempted to gauge the situation by asking for the location of Lisa’s husband. When she responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer requested permission to enter the home. At this point, Lisa explicitly declined, stating that she "preferred" the officers not enter. The conversation paused as Lisa went inside to retrieve her shoes. After a period of time passed without Lisa returning to the porch, Officer Kizer cracked the door and called out to her to ensure she was okay. Lisa eventually returned to the doorway, leading to a second, more specific inquiry from Officer Kizer regarding the status of the children and the location of her husband.

## The critical exchange regarding entry and the 'need' to check on children
A pivotal exchange occurred during this second interaction. When Lisa stated that the children were "okay right this second," Officer Kizer responded by saying, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa responded with "Okay." Following this, the two discussed the presence of dogs in the home; Lisa assured the officers that the dogs were friendly and would not bite. She then informed the officers that the children were in her bedroom and that she believed her husband was in the back bedroom.

## Entry into the home and the discovery of threats and firearms
Following this exchange, Lisa opened the door and facilitated the officers' entry by brushing her dogs away. She led the officers to the children's bedroom. While the officers did not immediately see Coleman, a quick canvas of the house led them to find him in a separate bedroom. Coleman subsequently permitted the officers to conduct a protective pat-down. During the interaction with the children, the officers learned that Coleman had been aggressive and had pointed a gun at Lisa, threatening to shoot her and set the house on fire. The children confirmed they had seen the firearm both that night and on previous occasions.

## The search warrant and subsequent federal indictment
Based on the information gathered during the initial entry—specifically the children's reports of a firearm and the threats made by Coleman—the officers sought and obtained a search warrant for the residence. The subsequent execution of the warrant revealed several firearms. These findings led to a federal indictment against Coleman for unlawful possession of a firearm as a convicted felon, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1).

## The motion to suppress and the district court's ruling
Coleman moved to suppress the evidence of the firearms, arguing that Lisa Coleman never gave valid consent for the officers to enter the home in the first instance. The case first went before a magistrate judge, who issued a report finding that Lisa had indeed consented to the entry and recommended that the motion to suppress be denied. The district court adopted the magistrate judge’s findings, ruling that the officers' entry was based on valid consent and that their actions inside the home did not exceed the scope of that consent.

## The defendant's guilty plea and preservation of the suppression issue
Following the district court’s denial of his motion to suppress, Coleman chose to plead guilty to the underlying charge. However, he took the specific procedural step of "reserving his right" to challenge the suppression ruling on appeal. This preservation allows the Seventh Circuit to review the constitutionality of the initial entry and the validity of the consent even though the underlying criminal case has been resolved by a plea agreement. The matter is now before the Seventh Circuit for review.
