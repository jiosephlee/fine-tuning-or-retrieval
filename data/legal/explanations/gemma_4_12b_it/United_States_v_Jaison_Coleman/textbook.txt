Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Jaison Coleman"

# Case Background and Procedural History

## The 911 call and initial police response
The facts of this case began in the early morning hours of April 20, 2023, when Officer Jamie Kizer of the Marshfield, Wisconsin police department responded to a 911 call involving a domestic violence incident. The caller reported that Jaison Coleman had threatened to kill his wife, Lisa Coleman, and was still inside the residence. Upon arriving at the scene shortly after 4:00 a.m., Officer Kizer knocked on the door and engaged in a conversation with Lisa Coleman. During this initial interaction, Lisa appeared hesitant and vague when asked if her children were safe, stating only that they were located in her bedroom.

## The interaction between Officer Kizer and Lisa Coleman
Officer Kizer attempted to gauge the situation by asking for the location of Lisa’s husband. When she responded that she did not know, Officer Kizer requested permission to enter the home. At this point, Lisa explicitly declined, stating that she "preferred" the officers not enter. The conversation paused as Lisa went inside to retrieve her shoes. After a period of time passed without Lisa returning to the porch, Officer Kizer cracked the door and called out to her to ensure she was okay. Lisa eventually returned to the doorway, leading to a second, more specific inquiry from Officer Kizer regarding the status of the children and the location of her husband.

## The critical exchange regarding entry and the 'need' to check on children
A pivotal exchange occurred during this second interaction. When Lisa stated that the children were "okay right this second," Officer Kizer responded by saying, “I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that.” Lisa responded with "Okay." Following this, the two discussed the presence of dogs in the home; Lisa assured the officers that the dogs were friendly and would not bite. She then informed the officers that the children were in her bedroom and that she believed her husband was in the back bedroom.

## Entry into the home and the discovery of threats and firearms
Following this exchange, Lisa opened the door and facilitated the officers' entry by brushing her dogs away. She led the officers to the children's bedroom. While the officers did not immediately see Coleman, a quick canvas of the house led them to find him in a separate bedroom. Coleman subsequently permitted the officers to conduct a protective pat-down. During the interaction with the children, the officers learned that Coleman had been aggressive and had pointed a gun at Lisa, threatening to shoot her and set the house on fire. The children confirmed they had seen the firearm both that night and on previous occasions.

## The search warrant and subsequent federal indictment
Based on the information gathered during the initial entry—specifically the children's reports of a firearm and the threats made by Coleman—the officers sought and obtained a search warrant for the residence. The subsequent execution of the warrant revealed several firearms. These findings led to a federal indictment against Coleman for unlawful possession of a firearm as a convicted felon, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1).

## The motion to suppress and the district court's ruling
Coleman moved to suppress the evidence of the firearms, arguing that Lisa Coleman never gave valid consent for the officers to enter the home in the first instance. The case first went before a magistrate judge, who issued a report finding that Lisa had indeed consented to the entry and recommended that the motion to suppress be denied. The district court adopted the magistrate judge’s findings, ruling that the officers' entry was based on valid consent and that their actions inside the home did not exceed the scope of that consent.

## The defendant's guilty plea and preservation of the suppression issue
Following the district court’s denial of his motion to suppress, Coleman chose to plead guilty to the underlying charge. However, he took the specific procedural step of "reserving his right" to challenge the suppression ruling on appeal. This preservation allows the Seventh Circuit to review the constitutionality of the initial entry and the validity of the consent even though the underlying criminal case has been resolved by a plea agreement. The matter is now before the Seventh Circuit for review.

# Legal Framework: The Doctrine of Consent

## Consent as an exception to the Fourth Amendment warrant requirement

The Fourth Amendment generally protects individuals against unreasonable searches and seizures, typically requiring the government to obtain a warrant supported by probable cause before entering a private residence. However, consent serves as a well-established exception to this warrant requirement. If an individual with the authority to do so voluntarily allows the police to enter a premises or search an area, the officers do not need a warrant to proceed. In *United States v. Coleman*, the government's primary argument rests on this doctrine: the officers were permitted to enter the home because the defendant’s wife, Lisa Coleman, provided consent.

## Third-party consent and the expectations of privacy

A critical component of the consent doctrine is determining who has the authority to grant it. The court clarifies that consent does not necessarily have to come from the defendant. A third party may provide consent to search a location in which both that third party and the defendant have a "legitimate expectation of privacy." In this case, because Lisa lived in the home and had a legitimate expectation of privacy there, her permission was sufficient to allow the officers entry, even though Jaison Coleman was also present in the home and his privacy was also at stake.

## The 'freely and voluntarily' standard for valid consent

For consent to be constitutionally valid, it must be given "freely and voluntarily." This means the police cannot use physical force, threats, or other forms of coercion to compel an individual to allow a search. To determine whether consent was truly voluntary, courts apply a "totality of the circumstances" test. The court identifies several factors to consider in this analysis: the person’s age, intelligence, and education; whether they were advised of their rights; the length of time they were detained before consenting; whether the consent was immediate or prompted by repeated requests; whether physical coercion was used; and whether the individual was in police custody at the time.

In *Coleman*, the court looked at the body camera footage to evaluate these factors. It noted that Lisa was not in custody and that while Officer Kizer made two requests for entry, the interaction was brief. Although Lisa initially balked, the court found that her eventual agreement was not the result of coercion, but rather a response to the officer’s expressed concern for her children's safety.

## The 'objective reasonableness' standard for the scope of consent

Even when consent is found to be voluntary, the government's search is still limited by the "scope of consent." The police are not permitted to conduct a search that exceeds what the consenting party would reasonably expect. To determine this scope, the court applies an "objective reasonableness" standard: what would a "typical reasonable person" have understood by the specific exchange between the officer and the individual giving consent?

In the *Coleman* case, the court had to decide if the officers overstepped by scanning the home and identifying everyone inside. The court concluded they did not. Because the officers' stated purpose was to check the welfare of the children and ensure Coleman posed no immediate danger, a reasonable person would understand that the officers needed to scan the home and ask basic questions to fulfill that purpose. The fact that this initial "sweep" led to the discovery of firearms did not mean the officers exceeded the scope of Lisa’s consent to enter for the welfare of the children.

## Standard of review for appellate courts (Clear Error)

Because the determination of whether a person gave consent involves weighing facts and interpreting specific interactions, the district court is the primary finder of fact. Consequently, when the Seventh Circuit reviews the district court's decision, it does not perform a de novo review (starting from scratch). Instead, the appellate court reviews the lower court’s finding for "clear error." This means the appellate court will uphold the lower court's ruling unless there is a glaring mistake in how the facts were interpreted or how the law was applied to those facts. In *Coleman*, the court found no such error in the district court's conclusion that Lisa's consent was both voluntary and sufficient to cover the officers' initial actions.

# Analysis of Voluntariness in Domestic Violence Calls

To determine whether a search of a home is lawful under the Fourth Amendment, courts must first establish whether the permission to enter was given voluntarily. In *United States v. Jaison Coleman*, the Seventh Circuit applied the "totality of the circumstances" test to evaluate whether the consent provided by Lisa Coleman was "freely and voluntarily" given. This analysis is particularly nuanced in domestic violence cases, where the urgency of police action can sometimes blur the line between a persuasive request and unlawful coercion.

## Officer Kizer's Demeanor: Concern vs. Coercion

A primary factor in determining voluntariness is the demeanor of the officer. The court looked closely at Officer Kizer’s conduct, noting that his tone was one of concern rather than coercion. In the context of a domestic violence call, police officers are often tasked with making split-second decisions to ensure the safety of residents. The court reasoned that while Officer Kizer expressed "urgency and intensity" regarding the safety of the children, this did not equate to the use of physical force or psychological pressure intended to overbear the resident’s will. Because Kizer spoke calmly and respectfully, his demeanor supported the finding that the request for entry was a legitimate police inquiry rather than a coercive demand.

## The Significance of the Initial Refusal of Entry

One of the most compelling pieces of evidence regarding voluntariness in this case was Lisa Coleman’s initial refusal to let the officers inside. When first asked, she explicitly stated, “um … I’d prefer you not.” The court viewed this as a critical indicator of her autonomy. By initially saying "no," Lisa demonstrated that she understood she had the right to deny entry and that she was not being subjected to a situation where her choice was already made for her. The fact that she chose to change her mind later, after further dialogue, suggests a conscious decision-making process rather than a submission to immediate police pressure.

## The Impact of Repeated Requests and the Word 'Need'

The defense argued that Officer Kizer’s use of the word “need”—specifically, "I’m sensing that we almost need to come inside to make sure of that"—was coercive. However, the court rejected this narrow focus. Under the "totality of the circumstances" standard, the court looks at whether the request was "prompted by repeated requests" over a long period of detention. Here, the entire exchange took place over only a few minutes, and Lisa was not in custody. The court held that expressing a "need" to ensure the safety of children in a domestic violence situation is a reasonable justification for urgency. The court concluded that while the officers were persistent, their persistence was tied to the immediate safety of the children, which the district court found did not cross the line into coercion.

## The 'Totality of the Circumstances' in the Context of Child Safety

The court’s analysis was heavily influenced by the specific context of the 911 call: a report of a man threatening to kill the mother and remaining inside the home. In such high-stakes environments, the "pressure" a resident feels to cooperate with police is often a result of the dangerous situation itself, not the actions of the officers. The court balanced the police's need to act quickly against the resident's right to privacy. Because the officers were responding to a potential life-safety emergency, their request to enter to check on the children was viewed as a balanced and sound decision-making process. The court found that the tension between police urgency and resident choice did not tip the scale toward coercion.

## Lisa’s Subsequent Actions as Evidence of Voluntary Participation

Finally, the court looked at Lisa’s behavior immediately following her "okay" as a confirmation of her voluntary intent. Rather than appearing hesitant or forced, she actively facilitated the officers' entry. She opened the door, brushed her dogs away to ensure the officers wouldn't be bitten, and led them to the specific bedroom where her children were located. These proactive steps served as "clear" evidence that she was a willing participant in the police action. Her actions demonstrated that she was not merely complying with a command, but was actively cooperating with the officers to achieve the stated goal of ensuring her children's safety.

# Scope of Consent and Judicial Conclusion

## Determining the Objective Scope of Lisa's Consent

When a third party provides consent for a police search, that consent is not a "blank check" allowing the government to conduct an exhaustive search of the premises. Instead, the Fourth Amendment requires that the police stay within the "scope of consent." To determine this scope, the court applies an objective reasonableness standard. As established in *Florida v. Jimeno*, the inquiry is not what the specific person giving consent intended, but rather "what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer and the person giving consent." In *United States v. Coleman*, the court looked at the dialogue between Officer Kizer and Lisa to determine how far the officers were permitted to go once they crossed the threshold.

## Justifying the Initial Sweep and Welfare Check

The court determined that the scope of Lisa’s consent was defined by the stated purpose of the officers' entry: ensuring the safety and welfare of her children. Because the primary concern was whether the children were safe and whether Coleman posed a continuing threat, the court held that the officers were entitled to perform a "quick canvas" of the home. This included scanning the rooms to locate everyone, identifying the children's location, and asking basic questions to assess the situation. The court reasoned that these actions were a necessary component of a welfare check and fell within what a reasonable person would expect the police to do given the domestic violence context of the call.

## The Transition from Consent-Based Entry to a Warrant-Based Search

A critical procedural distinction in this case is the transition from a consent-based entry to a warrant-based search. The officers did not rely on Lisa’s consent to conduct a full-scale search of the residence for contraband. Instead, they used the information gathered during the initial, consent-based welfare check—specifically the children's accounts of Coleman’s aggressive behavior and the presence of a firearm—to secure a formal search warrant. By obtaining a warrant before discovering the firearms, the officers ensured that the ultimate recovery of the weapons was supported by a neutral magistrate’s authorization rather than just the initial, limited consent provided by Lisa.

## The Court's Affirmation of the District Court's Findings

As an appellate court, the Seventh Circuit reviewed the district court’s findings for "clear error." This is a deferential standard, meaning the appellate court will not overturn the lower court's decision unless it is obviously wrong. The court found no such error in the district court’s conclusion that Lisa’s consent was voluntary and that the officers' actions remained within that scope. The court specifically rejected Coleman's narrow argument that Officer Kizer’s use of the word "need" was coercive. Instead, the court looked at the "totality of the circumstances," noting that Lisa had the opportunity to refuse, was not in custody, and actively facilitated the officers' entry by leading them to the children and clearing the dogs.

## The Court's Commentary on Balanced Police Decision-Making

In its concluding remarks, the court offered an overarching observation regarding the officers' conduct. While the legal analysis focused on the Fourth Amendment's requirements, the court highlighted that Officer Kizer and his colleagues exhibited "sound and balanced decision making in delicate and difficult circumstances." This commentary serves to contextualize the legal ruling: the court viewed the officers' actions not as a grab for evidence, but as a measured response to a high-stakes domestic violence situation. By affirming the conviction, the court signaled that police are permitted to use common sense and urgency to protect lives, provided they do not abandon the procedural boundaries of consent and the warrant requirement.
