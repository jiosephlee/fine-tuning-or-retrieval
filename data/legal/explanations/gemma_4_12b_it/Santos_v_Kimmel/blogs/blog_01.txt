Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# The 'Reasonable Observer' Standard: Navigating Transformative Use in the Wake of *Warhol*

In the evolving landscape of digital content and late-night satire, the line between protected speech and copyright infringement often blurs. The Second Circuit’s recent decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* provides a clear roadmap for how courts will navigate this boundary, particularly when public figures attempt to use copyright law to shield their content from mockery. By affirming the dismissal of George Santos’s claims against Jimmy Kimmel, the court reinforced a crucial principle: the "transformative" nature of a work is determined by the perspective of a reasonable observer, not the subjective intentions of the creator.

The facts of the case are tailor-made for a modern era of "personal" content. Santos, a former Congressman, fulfilled several requests for personalized videos on the Cameo platform—videos he characterized as intended to convey "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." Kimmel then curated these videos into a mocking series on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* titled "Will Santos Say It?" Santos sued for copyright infringement, arguing that Kimmel’s use of his videos was not transformative because it misappropriated the "purpose" of his original creations.

The Second Circuit’s analysis centers on the first factor of the fair use doctrine: the purpose and character of the use. Following the Supreme Court’s guidance in *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*, the court looked for whether the secondary use "adds something new, with a further purpose or different character." Santos’s argument rested on a distinction of intent. He contended that because his intent was positivity and Kimmel’s intent was mockery, the use was not transformative. The court, however, rejected this "subjective intent" argument entirely.

The court’s reasoning highlights a vital check on copyright ownership. It held that whether a secondary use is transformative depends on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the internal motives of the parties involved. To a reasonable observer, Kimmel’s segment was clearly a commentary on Santos’s willingness to say "absurd things for money." By prioritizing the audience's perception over the creator's stated purpose, the court ensures that copyright cannot be used as a "gag order" to prevent public figures from being satirized. If a creator could successfully argue that their work was "intended" for a different purpose than the one used in a parody, the fair use doctrine would be hollowed out by the subjective whims of the copyright holder.

Furthermore, the court’s treatment of the fourth fair use factor—the effect on the potential market—underscores the protective shell the law provides to satire. The court noted that a parody or critical review does not "usurp" the market for the original work because creators typically do not license the right to be mocked. This reinforces the idea that there is no "licensing market" for criticism, a standard that provides significant breathing room for late-night television and political commentary alike.

The doctrinal implications of *Santos v. Kimmel* are particularly relevant for public figures. For politicians and influencers, the ruling confirms that once content is released into the public sphere, it is effectively fair game for transformative commentary. Because the "reasonable observer" standard is objective, public figures cannot shield themselves from criticism by claiming that the "true meaning" of their content is being misrepresented. Instead, they must accept that the transformative use of their likeness and words is a byproduct of a free and open marketplace of ideas—one where the court prefers to protect the satirist’s right to comment over the creator’s right to control the narrative.
