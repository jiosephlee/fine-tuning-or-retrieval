Title: Blog about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Market Substitution vs. Satirical Commentary: Defining the Boundaries of Fair Use

In the high-stakes arena of late-night television and political commentary, the line between "content" and "criticism" is often where the most consequential legal battles are fought. The Second Circuit’s recent decision in *Santos v. Kimmel* serves as a vital waypoint in this evolution, clarifying the boundaries of the fair use doctrine when public figures attempt to exert copyright control over the mockery of their own personas. By dismissing former Congressman George Santos’s copyright claims against Jimmy Kimmel, the court reinforced a foundational principle of American media: that the law protects the right to critique, but does not grant creators a monopoly over how their works are used as fodder for public discourse.

The heart of the dispute lies in the Fourth Factor of the fair use analysis: the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. Santos argued that Kimmel’s use of his personalized "Cameo" videos—produced under false pretenses—infringed upon his copyright. However, the Second Circuit adopted a nuanced distinction that is central to the preservation of satire. The court clarified that for a use to be infringing under this factor, it must "usurp" the market for the original work by offering a "competing substitute." In other words, the question is not whether the new work makes the original work less appealing or damages the creator's reputation; it is whether the new work functions as a replacement for the original in the marketplace.

The court’s reasoning relies heavily on the distinction between "devaluation" and "usurpation." When Kimmel aired the "Will Santos Say It?" segments, he was not attempting to sell a service that competed with Santos’s Cameo videos. Rather, he was using those videos as the raw material for a comedic critique. As the court noted, citing *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.*, the fact that a creator might not want to license a lampoon of their own work does not mean a licensing market exists for such a use. By framing the use as a "parody or criticism," the court insulated late-night media from the threat of "reputation-based" copyright litigation, where a plaintiff might seek to use copyright law as a shield against unfavorable publicity.

From an analytical perspective, the court’s focus on the "reasonable observer" test for transformativeness—rather than the subjective intent of the parties—is a crucial procedural safeguard. Santos argued that his original purpose for the videos was to provide "positivity," while Kimmel’s purpose was to mock his willingness to say "absurd things for money." The court correctly identified that the law does not care about the internal motivations of the creator; it cares about how the public perceives the new work. Because a reasonable viewer would see the *JKL* segments as a commentary on Santos's character, the use was transformative. This prevents copyright holders from "vetoing" the meaning of their work by claiming that a critic's interpretation is a violation of their creative intent.

The doctrinal implications of *Santos v. Kimmel* are significant for the future of personality-driven content. It sends a clear signal that public figures cannot use copyright law to litigate the "vibe" of a satire. If a creator produces content that is then repurposed by a third party to mock that creator, the creator's primary recourse is likely in the realm of defamation or "right of publicity" (where applicable), not copyright. By narrowing the scope of "market harm" to exclude the devaluation of a person’s reputation through critique, the Second Circuit has fortified a safe harbor for political satire and late-night comedy, ensuring that the "marketplace of ideas" remains robust against the chilling effects of copyright-based censorship.
