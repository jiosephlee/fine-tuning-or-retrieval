Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Santos v. Kimmel"

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## Parties and Factual Context: The interaction between George Santos and Jimmy Kimmel

The litigation in *Santos v. Kimmel* involves a dispute between former Congressman George Santos and late-night television host Jimmy Kimmel, along with the entities responsible for Kimmel’s show: American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”) and its parent company, The Walt Disney Company. The conflict arose from the intersection of social media commerce and traditional broadcast entertainment. Specifically, the dispute centers on the use of personalized video content created by Santos on the Cameo platform, which was subsequently broadcast by Kimmel on his show, *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* (*JKL*).

## The Cameo Transactions: Fictitious names and personalized videos

The foundational facts of the case involve a series of transactions on Cameo, a platform where public figures are paid to create personalized video messages for fans. In this instance, Kimmel utilized the platform to request videos from Santos. However, rather than identifying himself, Kimmel used "various fictitious names" to place these requests. Santos, unaware of the true identity of the requester, fulfilled each of these requests by producing the personalized videos. These videos became the core property at the center of the subsequent copyright and state law litigation.

## The 'Will Santos Say It?' Segments: Mockery and criticism as a defense

The dispute escalated when Kimmel aired these personalized videos on *JKL*. The videos were featured as part of a specific series of segments titled “Will Santos Say It?” The court notes that the purpose of these segments was to mock Santos and comment on his willingness to "say absurd things for money." This context is vital to the litigation, as it establishes the nature of the defendants' use of the videos. While Santos viewed the videos as his own creative work, the defendants framed their use of that work as a form of sarcastic criticism and commentary—elements that are central to the fair use analysis that follows in the opinion.

## Procedural History: From the District Court to the Second Circuit

Santos originally filed his lawsuit in the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York, seeking damages for copyright infringement and various state law claims, including breach of contract and fraudulent inducement. The District Court dismissed the complaint under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. In its dismissal, the District Court concluded that Santos’s copyright claims were barred by the fair use doctrine and that his state law claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act or failed to state a valid legal claim. Santos appealed this final judgment to the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit.

## The Rule 12(b)(6) Standard: De novo review and the scope of 'facts alleged'

In reviewing the District Court’s dismissal, the Second Circuit applies a *de novo* standard of review. This means the appellate court does not defer to the lower court’s legal conclusions but instead examines the issues independently. To do this, the court limits its scope to the "facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference in the complaint." Additionally, the court may take judicial notice of copyright registrations from the Copyright Office’s registry. At this pleading stage, the court is not determining the ultimate truth of the facts, but rather whether the allegations presented in the complaint are sufficient to state a legally viable claim for relief.

# The Fair Use Doctrine and Transformative Use

## Purpose of Fair Use: Balancing Copyright Protection and Creative Freedom

The fair use doctrine serves as a critical "safety valve" in copyright law. While the Copyright Act is designed to protect the exclusive rights of creators, the court notes that a rigid application of these rights can stifle the very creativity the law seeks to promote. Fair use "excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior," allowing for the use of copyrighted material in ways that benefit the public interest or foster new artistic expression. In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the court reaffirms that fair use is a flexible concept, meaning its application must be determined by the specific context of each case rather than a one-size-fits-all rule.

## The Four-Factor Test (17 U.S.C. § 107)

When a court evaluates whether a use is "fair," it analyzes four non-exclusive factors established by 17 U.S.C. § 107. These factors are: (1) the purpose and character of the use; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. 

In the present case, the court focuses heavily on the first and fourth factors. To determine the "purpose and character" of the use, the court looks for whether the secondary use is "transformative." To determine the "effect on the market," the court looks for whether the new work acts as a "competing substitute" for the original.

## Defining 'Transformative Use': Adding New Purpose or Character

The modern standard for determining the "purpose and character" factor centers on whether a use is transformative. A use is transformative if it "adds something new, with a further purpose or different character," rather than merely "superseding" the objects of the original creation. 

In *Santos*, the court finds Kimmel’s use transformative because his purpose was "to comment on the willingness of Santos... to say absurd things for money." This constitutes criticism and commentary—classic examples of transformative use. Even though Kimmel "instigated" the creation of the original videos, his use of them was for a different purpose than the purpose for which they were originally made.

## The Role of Subjective Intent vs. Objective Observation

A critical nuance in the transformative use analysis is the distinction between what the creator *intended* to do and what a "reasonable observer" perceives the work to be doing. The court clarifies that fair use is not determined by the subjective intent of the copyright holder or the secondary user. 

Santos argued that his original purpose for the videos was to convey "hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity," and therefore Kimmel's mocking use was not a transformation of that purpose. However, the court rejected this, noting that a reasonable observer would view the videos as a commentary on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money. Because the court looks to the objective perception of the audience rather than Santos’s internal subjective intent, the use was found to be transformative.

## Fair Use at the Pleading Stage: Standards for Summary Dismissal

This case also illustrates the procedural standard for fair use at the pleading stage. Under Rule 12(b)(6), a court can dismiss a complaint if the plaintiff fails to state a claim upon which relief can be granted. In copyright cases, if the facts alleged in the complaint (and accompanying exhibits) show that the use is "so clearly established" as fair use that no triable issue of fact remains, the court may dismiss the claim immediately.

The court reached this conclusion by examining the "original version" of the videos against the *JKL* segments. It determined that because the use was transformative criticism and did not offer a "competing substitute" that would usurp the market for the original videos (as creators typically do not license the right to lampoon them), the fair use defense was sufficiently clear to warrant dismissal at the earliest stage of litigation.

# Application of Fair Use to the Santos Litigation

In copyright litigation, the fair use doctrine serves as an affirmative defense that allows for the use of copyrighted material without permission under specific circumstances. The court views fair use as a "flexible concept" designed to prevent the Copyright Act from becoming so rigid that it stifles creativity. To determine whether a use is "fair," courts evaluate four non-exclusive factors: (1) the purpose and character of the use; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the Second Circuit focuses primarily on the first and fourth factors to determine if Kimmel’s use of Santos's Cameo videos was permissible.

## Factor 1: Purpose and Character of the Use

The primary inquiry under the first factor is whether the new use is "transformative." A use is transformative if it does not merely supersede the objects of the original creation but instead adds something new, with a further purpose or a different character. In the context of *Santos*, the court must decide if Kimmel’s "Will Santos Say It?" segments were transformative uses of the original videos Santos created for the Cameo platform.

## Reasonable Observer Test: Assessing the 'mockery' vs. 'positivity' of the videos

To determine transformativeness, the court clarifies that the analysis does not depend on the "subjective intent" of the copyright holder (Santos) or the secondary user (Kimmel). Instead, the court applies an objective standard: what would a "reasonable observer" think? Santos argued that his original purpose for the videos was to provide "feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity." Conversely, the court found that a reasonable observer would recognize that Kimmel’s use had a different purpose: to comment on and mock Santos’s "willingness to say absurd things for money." Because Kimmel’s use added a layer of sarcastic commentary and commentary on Santos's behavior, rather than simply redistributing the "positive" content for its original purpose, the court found the first factor strongly supported a finding of fair use.

## The Defense against 'Bad Faith' Claims: The evolving stance on *Harper & Row*

Santos attempted to undermine the fair use defense by arguing that Kimmel’s use of fictitious names to solicit the videos demonstrated "bad faith." While the Supreme Court previously noted in *Harper & Row* that fair use "presupposes good faith and fair dealing," the court notes that the Supreme Court has since distanced itself from that specific limitation. More importantly, the court finds that even if Kimmel used false identities, the complaint itself portrays a defendant motivated by "sarcastic criticism and commentary" rather than an attempt to "supplant" Santos’s commercially valuable rights. Because the use was aimed at criticism, it remained within the bounds of fair use.

## Factor 4: Effect on the Potential Market

The fourth factor examines whether the secondary use usurps the market for the original work by offering a "competing substitute." The court emphasizes a critical distinction here: the inquiry is not whether the new work devalues the original through parody or criticism, but whether it replaces the original in the marketplace. 

## Competing Substitutes vs. Critical Reviews: Why criticism is not market harm

In applying this to the *Santos* litigation, the court notes that the videos were published on the same day they were created, and Santos provided no evidence of actual market harm. The court relies on the principle that the "unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons" means that such uses do not constitute a "competing substitute." Because Kimmel’s segments were a mockery of Santos rather than a replacement for the original videos, they do not harm the potential market for Santos’s work. Consequently, the court affirms the dismissal of the copyright claims, finding that the fair use doctrine bars the suit even at the pleading stage.

# State Law Claims: Contract and Fraud

## Copyright Preemption: A brief overview of the interplay between state and federal law

In many cases involving intellectual property, a central question is whether a state law claim is "preempted" by the federal Copyright Act. Preemption occurs when federal law occupies a specific field so completely that state law claims are barred from proceeding. In *Santos v. Kimmel*, the District Court originally dismissed Santos’s breach of contract claims on the grounds of preemption, suggesting that these claims were essentially federal copyright issues in disguise. 

However, the Second Circuit chose not to resolve the preemption issue in this instance. The court adopted a standard procedural shortcut: if a state law claim is fundamentally deficient under the requirements of state law itself, the court does not need to reach the question of whether federal law preempts it. Because the court found that Santos’s claims failed to meet basic state law pleading requirements, the question of whether they were preempted became moot.

## Direct Breach of Contract: The presumption against third-party beneficiaries

Santos sought to hold Kimmel liable for breaching the Terms of Service (ToS) of the Cameo platform. This presented a significant hurdle regarding "privity of contract"—the relationship between the parties who actually signed the agreement. To win a breach of contract claim, a plaintiff generally must be a party to the contract. 

The court analyzed this through two different lenses of law. First, the Cameo Terms of Service are governed by Illinois law. Under Illinois law, there is a "strong presumption" against conferring benefits on third parties who are not signatories to a contract. For a third party like Santos to be able to sue for a breach of a contract between Kimmel and Cameo, the agreement would need to contain a statement so clear that it is "practically an express declaration" of his status as a beneficiary. The court found that the Cameo ToS lacked such a declaration, especially since other parts of the ToS explicitly included third-party beneficiary language where it was intended.

Second, the court looked at New York law, which governs the claim itself. To establish an "implied-in-fact" contract, a plaintiff must show the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances. Santos failed to meet this burden because his complaint did not plead the "essential terms" of the contract in nonconclusory language. Because Santos was not a party to the agreement and the complaint failed to show a specific agreement to bind him to the ToS, the direct breach of contract claim could not stand.

## Breach of Implied Contract: Pleading 'essential terms' and 'meeting of minds'

Beyond a direct breach of a written contract, Santos attempted to argue for a breach of an implied contract. An implied-in-fact contract is one where the agreement is inferred from the conduct of the parties rather than a signed document. However, the law requires a high standard of pleading for these claims to survive a motion to dismiss.

To survive, a plaintiff must allege the "essential terms" of the agreement—the specific provisions upon which liability is predicated—and provide an "indication of a meeting of minds." A "meeting of minds" refers to the mutual understanding that the parties intended to enter into a binding legal obligation. The Second Circuit found that Santos's complaint failed on both counts. He did not sufficiently describe the specific terms of the putative implied contract, nor did he provide evidence that Kimmel and the defendants ever reached a mutual understanding that his videos would be governed by specific contractual constraints.

## Fraudulent Inducement: The requirement of actual out-of-pocket loss

Finally, Santos asserted a claim for fraudulent inducement. This claim typically arises when one party is tricked into entering into a contract or agreement through dishonest means. However, New York law imposes a specific requirement for such claims: the plaintiff must allege an actual "out-of-pocket loss."

The court noted that while Santos may have alleged that Kimmel used fictitious names to induce him into creating the videos, he failed to allege any tangible financial loss resulting from that fraud. In the context of New York law, simply being misled is insufficient to sustain a claim for fraudulent inducement; the plaintiff must demonstrate that the fraud resulted in a specific, quantifiable loss of money or property. Because Santos’s complaint was silent on any actual out-of-pocket damages, the court dismissed the claim.

## Conclusion: Final disposition of the appeal

The Second Circuit’s analysis of the state law claims demonstrates a rigorous application of pleading standards. By finding that Santos failed to establish himself as a contract beneficiary, failed to plead the essential terms of an implied contract, and failed to allege any out-of-pocket losses for fraud, the court determined that the District Court’s dismissal was correct. The court affirmed the judgment, concluding that the state law claims were legally insufficient to proceed.
