Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

### Why was the Board's limitation of the 'relevant public' to only credit card consumers considered a legal error under the sixth DuPont factor?
Question:
This is the most confusing part of the ruling. The Board had already decided in the second factor that the services were 'highly similar' (even 'legally identical'). Why did the court rule that the Board was then required to use that same broad scope when looking at third-party marks in the sixth factor? Why can't the Board use a narrower scope for 'similar marks' than it did for 'similarity of services'?
Answer:
The court’s ruling centers on the principle of **internal consistency** in the application of the *DuPont* factors. Specifically, the court held that the Board cannot "cherry-pick" a narrow definition of similarity for one factor while employing a broad definition for another.

### The Doctrine of Consistent Scope
The sixth *DuPont* factor requires an analysis of "the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." The core of the legal error identified by the court is that the Board failed to maintain a consistent scope of "similarity" across the different factors of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis.

In Section III.A, the Board conducted its analysis of the second *DuPont* factor (similarity of services) and concluded that CC Serve’s "credit card services" and Apex’s "banking and financing services" were "legally identical, in part." By making this finding, the Board established a legal premise: that the two types of services are sufficiently related to potentially cause consumer confusion.

### Why the Board Could Not Narrow the Scope
The student’s confusion—why the Board can't use a narrower scope for "similar marks" than for "similarity of services"—is addressed directly by the court's reasoning in Section III.B. The court identified three specific reasons why the Board's narrow limitation was a legal error:

1.  **The "Similar vs. Identical" Standard:** The court clarified that the sixth *DuPont* factor does not require the Board to look for *identical* goods or services; it only requires the Board to consider *similar* ones. Because the Board had already admitted the services were highly similar in Factor 2, it was legally required to include marks used for those similar services (i.e., the broader banking and financing marks) in its Factor 6 analysis.
2.  **The Crowdedness Theory:** The purpose of the sixth factor is to determine if a field is "crowded" with similar marks, which would indicate that consumers are adept at distinguishing between marks and that the mark in question is "weak." By excluding the 42 other "Aspire" marks used in the banking and financing industry, the Board artificially "thinned out" the field. This prevented the court from seeing the true level of competition and crowding in the marketplace.
3.  **Prohibition of Varying Legal Standards:** The court explicitly stated: *"We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor."* If the Board determines that a consumer might confuse a credit card service with a banking service (Factor 2), it is logically and legally inconsistent to then claim that a mark used for a banking service is "essentially irrelevant" to a consumer of credit card services (Factor 6).

### Summary
The Board’s error was a failure of logic: it could not find the services "highly similar" to establish a likelihood of confusion (Factor 2) while simultaneously claiming the marks for those same services were "irrelevant" to the crowdedness of the marketplace (Factor 6). Once the services were deemed similar, the universe of "similar marks" for the sixth factor was automatically expanded to include all marks used in that broader category.
