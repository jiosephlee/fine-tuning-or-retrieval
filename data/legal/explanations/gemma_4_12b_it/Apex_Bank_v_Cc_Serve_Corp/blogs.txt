Title: Blog about the opinion: "Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp."

# Consistency is Key: The Federal Circuit’s Warning on 'Schizophrenic' Likelihood of Confusion Analyses

In trademark law, the "likelihood of confusion" analysis is rarely a matter of simple arithmetic. Instead, it is a holistic inquiry where various factors—the *DuPont* factors—interact to create a portrait of how a consumer perceives a brand. However, a recent decision by the Federal Circuit in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* serves as a sharp reminder that these factors cannot be treated as isolated silos. The court sent a clear message to administrative bodies: you cannot move the goalposts between questions. If you define the scope of a market broadly to establish similarity, you are bound to that same broad universe when evaluating the strength of a mark.

The dispute centered on whether Apex Bank could register the marks "ASPIRE BANK" for banking and financing services. CC Serve, which held a prior registration for "ASPIRE" in connection with credit card services, opposed the application. The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) ultimately sided with CC Serve, finding a high likelihood of confusion. However, the Federal Circuit took issue with the Board's internal logic, specifically how it pivoted its factual "universe" depending on which part of the *DuPont* test it was answering.

The Board’s analysis was, in some respects, remarkably expansive. In evaluating the second *DuPont* factor—the similarity of goods and services—the Board looked at the dictionary definitions of "banking," "bank," and "finance." It concluded that because these terms encompass extending credit and providing funds, Apex’s banking services were "legally identical" to CC Serve’s credit card services. By taking this broad view, the Board successfully established that the two companies were operating in the same sphere.

Yet, when the Board moved to the sixth *DuPont* factor—the number and nature of similar marks in use—it suddenly retreated into a much narrower silo. Instead of looking at the entire universe of "banking and financing" marks (which would have shown a crowded field of "Aspire" marks), the Board restricted its search to only those marks used specifically for "credit card services." Because there were fewer marks in this smaller niche, the Board concluded that CC Serve’s mark was entitled to a normal scope of protection rather than being weakened by a crowded marketplace.

The Federal Circuit rightly identified this as a logical fallacy. The court's reasoning is grounded in the principle of analytical consistency. If the Board has already determined that the services are "legally identical" for the purpose of similarity (Factor 2), it cannot then claim those services are distinct enough to ignore the broader market of banking marks for the purpose of commercial strength (Factor 6). To do so creates a "schizophrenic" analysis where the legal reality of the parties' relationship shifts depending on which question is being asked.

This ruling reinforces a "holistic" approach to trademark analysis. It prevents administrative bodies from engaging in a form of analytical arbitrage—cherry-picking the most favorable factual boundaries to suit the specific requirements of a single factor. From a doctrinal perspective, this creates a higher burden of consistency for the TTAB. Once a body makes a finding of fact regarding the nature and scope of a service, that finding must "lock in" the parameters for all subsequent factors in the likelihood-of-confusion test.

The downstream effects of this decision are significant for trademark practitioners. It means that when arguing for similarity, the "universe" of services defined by an advocate will also define the "universe" of third-party marks available to the opposing side. If you want to show that a competitor's services are similar to yours, you must be prepared for the consequence that a broader market of competing marks may be used to argue that your own mark is weak. The Federal Circuit has ensured that while the *DuPont* factors are distinct, the facts underlying them must remain unified.



# Expanding the Field: How Factor 6 Impacts the Commercial Strength of Financial Trademarks

In the high-stakes world of trademark litigation, the "likelihood of confusion" analysis often hinges on a delicate balancing act between two competing interests: a brand's desire to protect its identity and the public’s need to navigate a crowded marketplace without being misled. The Federal Circuit’s recent decision in *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.* clarifies a critical component of this balance. By requiring the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) to consider a broader universe of third-party marks when determining a mark’s commercial strength, the court has signaled that trademark protection cannot be insulated from the reality of a saturated industry.

The dispute centered on the word mark "ASPIRE." CC Serve held a long-standing registration for "ASPIRE" in connection with credit card services. Apex Bank sought to register "ASPIRE BANK" for general banking and financing services. The TTAB initially refused Apex’s registration, finding that the services were highly similar and that the marks were likely to cause consumer confusion. While Apex appealed, the core of the legal friction lay in how the Board calculated the "crowdedness" of the marketplace—the sixth *DuPont* factor.

The TTAB had attempted to narrow the scope of the sixth *DuPont* factor by looking only at other marks used specifically for "credit card services." It essentially argued that while Apex’s banking services might be similar to CC Serve’s credit card services, the "crowded field" of marks only applied to the credit card niche. This allowed CC Serve to argue that its mark was relatively "strong" because, in the specific silo of credit cards, there weren't enough other "Aspire" marks to dilute its protection.

The Federal Circuit rejected this compartmentalized approach. The court’s reasoning is rooted in a principle of internal consistency: if the Board finds that two sets of services are "legally identical" or "highly similar" under the second *DuPont* factor, it cannot then arbitrarily shrink the scope of the marketplace when evaluating the sixth factor. The court held that because banking and financing services encompass the issuance of credit cards, the "relevant public" for both companies is essentially the same. Consequently, the crowded field must include every "Aspire" mark used in *any* related banking or financing capacity, not just credit cards.

This decision has significant doctrinal implications for how "commercial strength" is measured in saturated industries. By forcing the inclusion of a broader universe of marks, the court makes it substantially harder for plaintiffs to claim that their mark enjoys a "wide" scope of protection. In a crowded field, the legal theory is that consumers become more adept at distinguishing between similar marks; thus, a mark's exclusive territory naturally shrinks. By expanding the "field" to include all banking and financing marks, the court ensures that the "crowdedness" of the entire sector—rather than just a specific sub-niche—determines the limits of a trademark’s reach.

For trademark practitioners, this suggests a shift toward a more holistic marketplace analysis. A brand can no longer easily wall off a specific service line from the surrounding industry's noise if those services are functionally related. If you operate in a saturated sector like finance, tech, or retail, your trademark's "strength" is now tied to the presence of every competitor using similar terminology in any adjacent service. This ruling serves as a check on "trademark bullying," preventing dominant players from using a narrow definition of a market to block out competitors in overlapping fields. Moving forward, the "scope of protection" for an inherently distinctive mark will likely be more modest, reflecting the reality of a marketplace where many players are vying for the same conceptual space.



# Defining the Financial Frontier: Is 'Credit Card Service' Just Banking by Another Name?

In the rapidly evolving landscape of fintech, the lines between traditional banking, lending, and credit card processing have become increasingly porous. However, in the eyes of the trademark office, those lines may be about to become much blurrier. In *Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit addressed a fundamental question of trademark law: when two companies offer different financial products, are they sufficiently "related" to cause consumer confusion? By affirming a finding that "credit card services" and "banking and financing services" are "legally identical, in part," the court has signaled a broad, dictionary-driven approach to service similarity that could significantly complicate trademark clearance for new entrants in the financial sector.

The dispute began when Apex Bank, a retail bank, sought to register the mark "ASPIRE BANK" for "banking and financing services." The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) refused the registration, citing a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve’s mark, "ASPIRE," which is used for credit card services. CC Serve argued that it does not provide banking services—it merely partners with banks to manage credit card programs—and that its business model is distinct from a retail bank like Apex. The Board, however, took a different view, leading to the current litigation.

The Federal Circuit’s treatment of the second DuPont factor—which assesses the similarity of the goods or services—is the crux of the opinion’s significance. The Board determined that because the dictionary definitions for "banking," "bank," and "finance" encompass the issuance of credit cards to provide funds or extend credit, the services offered by Apex and CC Serve were "legally identical, in part." The court affirmed this reasoning, effectively deciding that the operational reality of a business—how it actually functions or its specific corporate structure—is less important than the conceptual category it falls into. If a service provides a financial "end result" that can be described by a broad dictionary term, it may be swept into the same trademark category as traditional banking.

This "conceptual" approach to similarity creates a significant hurdle for fintech entities. By merging credit card services into the broader umbrella of banking and financing, the court has expanded the "danger zone" for trademark infringement. A startup offering a niche lending platform or a specialized payment processor may find its marks blocked by established banks, even if the two entities do not compete for the same customers or operate in the same manner. The court’s willingness to rely on dictionary definitions suggests that "relatedness" will be interpreted expansively, potentially granting established players a "monopoly" over broad financial concepts.

Interestingly, the court did offer a corrective to the Board’s logic regarding the sixth DuPont factor, which examines the number of similar marks in use. The Board had attempted to limit the "crowded field" analysis to only those marks used for credit card services, while excluding marks used for general banking. The Federal Circuit vacated this, holding that the Board cannot have it both ways: if the services are "highly similar" (or legally identical) under the second factor, then the scope of third-party marks to be considered under the sixth factor must be equally broad. The court essentially ruled that if the services are in the same bucket, the marks must be judged against the entire contents of that bucket, not just a subset.

The doctrinal implication here is a move toward a more homogenized trademark landscape for financial services. By emphasizing "legal identity" over "commercial competition," the court makes it harder for innovators to carve out small, distinct niches within the broader financial industry. For trademark practitioners, this means that a "clearance" search can no longer rely on distinguishing a client's specific business model from a competitor's. If the services can be described by the same broad financial terms, the risk of a likelihood-of-confusion rejection increases exponentially. This case serves as a warning that in the world of trademarks, the dictionary may carry more weight than the actual business plan.
