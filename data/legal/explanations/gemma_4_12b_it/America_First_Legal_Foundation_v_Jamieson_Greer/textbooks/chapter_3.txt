Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 3: # Analysis of the FOIA Records Standing Theory

## The Plaintiff's Claim of Informational Injury

To establish Article III standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate a "concrete and particularized" injury that is "actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical." In this case, the America First Legal Foundation (AFL) sought to establish standing by linking the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) refusal to investigate the Department of Justice’s (DOJ) FOIA practices to a specific informational injury: the fact that AFL had not yet received the records it requested. 

AFL’s theory of standing rests on the idea that the OSC’s failure to perform its duty under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 created a procedural vacuum. They argued that if the OSC had conducted the mandated investigation, the DOJ would have been compelled to rectify its FOIA policy, which in turn would have resulted in the production of the requested emails. Under this framework, AFL claimed that the lack of records constituted a concrete injury caused by the OSC’s inaction.

## The Problem of Attenuated Causation

The court rejected this theory primarily because it failed the "causation" requirement of standing. For a plaintiff to have standing, the injury must be traceable to the conduct of the defendant. Here, the court noted that any informational injury resulting from the DOJ’s refusal to disclose records is "plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC." 

The court characterized the link between the OSC's failure to investigate and the DOJ's failure to produce records as "too speculative and attenuated." In legal terms, an attenuated causal chain is one where the connection between the defendant's action (or inaction) and the plaintiff's harm is so indirect or weakened by intervening factors that it cannot support a claim for standing. Because the primary obstacle to AFL receiving its records was the DOJ’s policy, the OSC’s failure to investigate that policy did not constitute the proximate cause of the injury.

## Speculation Regarding Independent Actors (MSPB and DOJ)

In analyzing the causal chain, the court highlighted a series of "if-then" scenarios that moved further away from the parties involved in the lawsuit. AFL’s argument required the court to assume a multi-step sequence: first, that the OSC would investigate; second, that the OSC would decide to pursue corrective action; third, that the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) would decide to sanction the employees; and finally, that the DOJ would then decide to produce the records.

The court emphasized a fundamental principle of standing: causation "generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." Because the MSPB and the DOJ were not parties to this specific lawsuit, the court could not grant AFL standing based on what those agencies *might* choose to do in the future. The court cannot compel a judicial remedy based on the hypothetical behavior of third-party agencies that are not bound by the court's immediate order.

## The Limits of Redressability and Judicial Binding Power

Finally, the court addressed the "redressability" requirement of standing, which asks whether a favorable decision by the court would "very likely" correct the injury. Even if the court were to find a causal link, it determined that a judgment against the OSC would not provide a sufficient remedy for AFL's lack of records.

The court clarified a key distinction in judicial power: redressability generally turns on how a judgment *binds* the parties involved in the suit, not whether the judgment's rationale "might persuade" outside actors to change their behavior. A court order compelling the OSC to conduct an investigation does not bind the DOJ; it only dictates the actions of the OSC. Therefore, a victory for AFL in this lawsuit would not "very likely spur" the DOJ into producing the records, making the claim for standing redressable. Because the judgment would not force the DOJ to change its policy, the court concluded that AFL could not satisfy the requirements for Article III standing.
