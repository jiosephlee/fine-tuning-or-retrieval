Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### What does 'procedural right in vacuo' mean in the context of Article III standing?
Question:
I'm reading the section where the court discusses AFL's claim that the OSC violated its procedural right to an investigation under section 1216. The court says that a 'deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected... is insufficient' for standing. Can someone explain what a 'procedural right in vacuo' is? If the statute specifically says the OSC 'shall' conduct an investigation, isn't that a right? Why does the court require a 'concrete interest' on top of that right?
Answer:
In the context of Article III standing, a **"procedural right in vacuo"** refers to a statutory procedure that, while technically mandated by law, does not protect a "concrete" or "particularized" interest of the plaintiff. To have standing, a plaintiff must show an "injury in fact"—an invasion of a legally protected interest that is "concrete and particularized" and "actual or imminent" (*Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife*).

The court in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* explains that simply proving a government agency failed to follow a mandatory procedure (e.g., a statute saying an agency "shall" conduct an investigation) is not enough to establish standing if that procedure does not result in a tangible harm to the plaintiff. Because the "right" exists in a vacuum—detached from any real-world impact on the plaintiff's specific interests—it is insufficient to create a "Case or Controversy" under Article III.

To understand why the court requires a "concrete interest" on top of the procedural right, it is helpful to look at how the court analyzed AFL’s two theories:

**1. The Distinction Between Process and Outcome**
AFL argued that because section 1216 mandates an investigation, they had a right to that investigation. However, the court clarified that for a procedural right to confer standing, the violation of that right must "result in injury" to a "concrete, particularized interest" (*Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ.*). 

**2. The Failure of Causation and Redressability**
Even when courts apply a "relaxed" standard for procedural injuries (where they are less strict about whether a win in court will actually fix the problem), they still require the plaintiff to prove **injury in fact** and **causation**. The court rejected AFL's claim because:
*   **Causation:** AFL could not prove that a mandatory investigation by the OSC would actually cause the Department of Justice to produce the FOIA records. The chain of events was too speculative, involving the independent choices of multiple agencies (OSC, MSPB, and DOJ).
*   **Lack of Entitlement:** AFL's second theory—that they were entitled to the "fruits" of the investigation (like notices and information)—failed because section 1216 does not actually grant a right to receive that information. The court noted that the OSC's decision to provide such information was discretionary, not a mandated entitlement.

**Summary**
A "procedural right in vacuo" is a legal "hollow" right. While the statute says the government *must* do something, if the plaintiff cannot show that the government's failure to do that thing caused them a specific, concrete harm (like losing a specific piece of information they were entitled to receive), the court cannot hear the case. The court requires a concrete interest to ensure that litigation is reserved for actual injuries rather than mere grievances over government non-compliance with administrative procedures.

### How does the court define 'speculative' causation when multiple independent agencies are involved?
Question:
The court rejects AFL's first standing theory because the causal chain is 'too speculative and attenuated.' The chain involves OSC investigating, the MSPB potentially sanctioning employees, and then the DOJ potentially producing records. The court says we can't rely on the 'unfettered choices' of the MSPB and DOJ. Does this mean that if a causal chain involves more than one independent government agency, it is automatically too speculative to satisfy the 'redressability' requirement of standing?
Answer:
No, the court does not hold that a causal chain involving multiple independent government agencies is *automatically* too speculative to satisfy the redressability requirement of Article III standing. Instead, the court’s analysis focuses on whether the causal connection relies on the "unfettered choices" of "independent actors" who are not parties to the litigation.

Under the standing doctrine established in *Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife*, a plaintiff must demonstrate that their injury was caused by the defendant’s conduct and is "likely to be redressed" by a favorable judicial decision. In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the court rejected the plaintiff’s theory because the proposed causal chain was "too speculative and attenuated" due to the following factors:

**1. Independence and Discretion of Non-Parties**
The court emphasized that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." In this case, the causal chain required the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) and the Department of Justice (DOJ) to make specific decisions (sanctioning employees and producing records, respectively). Because these agencies are independent and their actions are not mandated by the court's judgment against the Office of Special Counsel (OSC), the court cannot assume they will act in a way that redresses the plaintiff's injury.

**2. Binding vs. Persuasive Effect**
The court clarified that redressability turns on how a judgment *binds* the parties currently before the court, rather than whether the rationale of the judgment "might persuade actors who are not before the court." Because the MSPB and DOJ were not parties to the lawsuit, a favorable judgment against the OSC does not legally compel them to act. Therefore, the plaintiff failed to show that a victory would "very likely spur the next steps" required to remedy the injury.

**3. Enforcement Discretion**
The court also noted that even regarding the defendant itself (the OSC), a court generally may not compel the exercise of "enforcement discretion." This reinforces the idea that standing is undermined when the path to redress depends on discretionary choices—whether by a third-party agency or the defendant itself—rather than a direct legal consequence of the court's order.

**Summary for the Student**
To satisfy standing in a multi-agency context, a plaintiff must show a direct causal link where the defendant's actions (or inactions) are the proximate cause of the injury. If the redress of that injury depends on a "daisy chain" of discretionary decisions by outside agencies, the court will likely find the theory too speculative. The test is not the *number* of agencies involved, but the *independence* of those agencies and the *discretionary* nature of their actions relative to the parties in the case.

### How does the court distinguish between the 'mandatory' duty of Section 1216 and the 'discretionary' procedure of Section 1214?
Question:
I'm confused by the interaction between these two statutes. The district court suggested that Section 1216 mandates an investigation, but the appellate court notes that the procedure for that investigation (the notices/information from Section 1214) is discretionary. How can a statute create a mandatory requirement for an action (investigation) while leaving the manner in which that action is performed (notice requirements) up to the agency's discretion? How does this affect a plaintiff's ability to seek a judicial order?
Answer:
The court distinguishes between the **substantive duty** to act and the **procedural manner** in which that act is performed. Under the interplay of Sections 1214 and 1216, the law creates a mandatory obligation for the agency to perform a specific function (the investigation), but provides the agency with "enforcement discretion" regarding the specific procedural mechanics (the notices and information) used during that function.

### The Mandatory Duty (The "What")
Section 1216(a)(3) uses the word "**shall**" to command the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to conduct an investigation into arbitrary or capricious withholdings of FOIA information. This creates a clear, non-discretionary obligation: when such an allegation is made, the OSC is legally required to initiate an investigation. The district court recognized this, noting that the OSC’s refusal to investigate at all was a legal error.

### The Discretionary Procedure (The "How")
The court distinguishes this from Section 1216(c), which uses the word "**may**." This section allows the OSC to use the procedures of Section 1214 (which include specific notice requirements) as if the matter were a "prohibited personnel practice." 

Because the statute uses "may" here, the OSC is not required to follow the Section 1214 roadmap. The OSC has the authority to choose whether or not to engraft those specific procedural requirements onto its Section 1216 investigation. As the court notes by citing *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*, the decision to follow Section 1214’s procedural requirements in a Section 1216 investigation is "discretionary."

### Impact on Judicial Orders and Standing
This distinction significantly limits a plaintiff’s ability to seek a judicial order for specific procedural outcomes. The court’s reasoning regarding Article III standing highlights two key points:

1.  **No Procedural Right to Information:** To seek a judicial order based on a procedural violation, a plaintiff must show they have a "concrete interest" that is affected by that procedure. Because the OSC is not *required* to follow Section 1214's notice requirements, a plaintiff does not have a "procedural right" to receive those notices. Therefore, a plaintiff cannot claim a "procedural right in vacuo."
2.  **Limits on Compelled Action:** Because the "manner" of the investigation is discretionary, a court cannot compel the OSC to provide specific notices or information from Section 1214. A judicial order could theoretically compel the OSC to perform the mandatory investigation (the "shall" of 1216), but it cannot force the OSC to use a specific set of procedures (the "may" of 1216(c)) because the agency is not legally bound to use them.

In short, the statute mandates the **existence** of an investigation but grants the agency **latitude** in its execution. A plaintiff can only seek a judicial order to compel the agency to do what it is *required* to do, not to do it in a specific *manner* the agency has the discretion to avoid.

### Why does an interest in the 'fruits' of a government investigation fail to constitute a 'concrete' informational injury?
Question:
AFL argued that they have an informational interest in the notices and information they would receive if the OSC conducted the investigation. The court held that because Section 1216 doesn't create an 'entitlement' to receive that information, it isn't a 'concrete' injury. Is the court saying that 'informational standing' is only granted if the statute explicitly guarantees the plaintiff will receive specific documents, rather than just granting the agency the power to provide them?
Answer:
Your intuition is correct. The court's reasoning hinges on the distinction between a statutory **power** (the agency's authority to act) and a statutory **entitlement** (the plaintiff's right to a specific outcome).

To establish Article III standing for an informational injury, a plaintiff must demonstrate a "concrete" injury. In this context, "concrete" means the plaintiff must be denied information that a "statute entitled him to receive." *Am. Soc’y for Prevention of Cruelty to Animals v. Feld Ent., Inc.*, 659 F.3d 13, 23 (D.C. Cir. 2011).

The court's analysis breaks down into three key components:

### 1. The Distinction Between Investigation and Entitlement
The court notes that while Section 1216(a)(3) may mandate that the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) "conduct an investigation," it does not create a corresponding legal entitlement for the complainant to receive specific documents or notices as a result of that investigation. Because the statute does not explicitly guarantee the delivery of information to the plaintiff, the plaintiff cannot claim a "legally protected interest" in that information has been violated.

### 2. Discretionary vs. Mandatory Procedures
The core of the "fruits" argument is that the notices and information AFL seeks are governed by Section 1214. However, the court highlights a critical statutory limitation: the OSC's decision to follow the procedural requirements of Section 1214 (which includes the notices AFL wants) during a Section 1216 investigation is **discretionary**. 

Because the law allows the OSC to choose whether or not to "engraft" those notice requirements onto a Section 1216 investigation, the plaintiff has no legal right to demand them. If the agency has the power to provide the information but is not required to do so, the plaintiff's interest in receiving it is not a "concrete" injury; it is merely a hope for a favorable discretionary outcome.

### 3. The "Procedural Right in Vacuo" Problem
The court reinforces this by stating that a "deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest that is affected... is insufficient" to confer standing. *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009). 

By failing to show that the law *requires* the OSC to provide those specific notices, AFL is left with a "procedural right in vacuo." They are attempting to sue over a process (the investigation) that they believe should lead to a specific result (the notices), but because the law doesn't tie that process to a mandatory result, the "injury" remains conjectural rather than concrete.

### Summary
The court is not saying that *all* informational standing requires an explicit guarantee of documents. Rather, it is saying that **informational standing cannot be predicated on the discretionary "fruits" of an agency's power.** If the statute gives the agency the power to provide information but does not mandate that it must do so, a plaintiff cannot claim a concrete injury for the agency's failure to exercise that power.

### Why did the court 'vacate and remand' for lack of jurisdiction instead of simply dismissing the appeal?
Question:
The court concludes that AFL lacks Article III standing, which is a matter of subject-matter jurisdiction. Since the court found that the district court never had the power to hear the case in the first place, why is the proper move to 'vacate and remand with instructions to dismiss' rather than just issuing a final judgment dismissing the appeal? I'm trying to understand the procedural distinction between a merits-based dismissal and a jurisdictional vacatur.
Answer:
The distinction between a merits-based dismissal and a jurisdictional vacatur lies in whether the court had the legal power to hear the case in the first place.

### The Doctrine of Subject-Matter Jurisdiction
As the court notes, Article III standing is a prerequisite for subject-matter jurisdiction. Subject-matter jurisdiction is the court’s constitutional and statutory authority to hear a specific type of case. If a plaintiff lacks standing, the court lacks the "judicial Power" to resolve the "Case" or "Controversy."

When a court lacks subject-matter jurisdiction, any judgment it issues is technically void *ab initio* (from the beginning). Because the district court never had the power to hear the case, its judgment—no matter how well-reasoned or correct it might have appeared on the face of the merits—was legally unauthorized.

### Why Vacate instead of Dismiss?
When an appellate court finds that a lower court lacked jurisdiction, it cannot simply "affirm" the lower court's judgment. To affirm would be to validate a judgment that the appellate court has just determined was issued by a court without the power to issue it.

Instead, the appellate court must perform two distinct procedural steps:

1.  **Vacate:** The appellate court "vacates" the district court’s judgment to nullify it. This effectively "erases" the lower court's order from the record because that order was a legal nullity.
2.  **Remand with Instructions:** The appellate court then "remands" the case back to the lower court (or the relevant record) with specific instructions to dismiss the case for the correct reason: lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

### The Difference in Outcome
In a **merits-based dismissal**, the court says: *"We have the power to hear this case, but we have decided that the plaintiff's legal arguments are insufficient."* In this scenario, the district court's judgment is valid and remains on the books.

In a **jurisdictional vacatur**, the court says: *"The district court never had the power to hear this case at all. Therefore, its judgment is invalid. We are throwing that judgment out (vacating) and telling the record to reflect that the case must be dismissed because the court lacked jurisdiction."*

By vacating and remanding, the court ensures that the official record reflects the procedural reality: the case did not end because AFL lost on the facts; it ended because the court was never permitted to hear the case in the first place.
