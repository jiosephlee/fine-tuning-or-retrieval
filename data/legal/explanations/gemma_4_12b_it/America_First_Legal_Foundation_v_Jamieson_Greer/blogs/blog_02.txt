Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# The Speculative Chain: Causation and the Limits of 'Persuasive' Redressability

Article III standing serves as the ultimate gatekeeper of the federal judiciary, ensuring that courts only adjudicate "Cases" and "Controversies" rather than abstract grievances or hypothetical harms. In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the D.C. Circuit provided a rigorous exercise in this gatekeeping function. By dismissing a challenge to the Office of Special Counsel’s (OSC) enforcement discretion, the court clarified a critical boundary in standing doctrine: a plaintiff cannot establish a justiciable injury by constructing a "speculative chain" of events involving the discretionary choices of multiple independent government actors.

The dispute centered on a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request made by America First Legal Foundation (AFL) to the Department of Justice (DOJ). When the DOJ refused to search employee emails without consent, AFL sought an investigation by the OSC under 5 U.S.C. § 1216. The OSC declined, arguing it lacked the mandate to investigate unless specific FOIA predicates were met. AFL then sued to compel the investigation, arguing that the OSC’s inaction prevented them from receiving the records they sought.

The court’s analysis of AFL’s first standing theory provides a masterclass in the limitations of "speculative" causation. AFL attempted to link the OSC’s refusal to investigate directly to the DOJ’s failure to produce records. However, the court rightly identified that this link was not a direct causal line, but a series of "what ifs." For AFL to succeed, the court would have had to assume that: (1) the OSC would have conducted the investigation; (2) the OSC would have moved for corrective action; (3) the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) would have sanctioned the employees; and (4) the DOJ would have subsequently complied with the FOIA request.

The court’s rejection of this theory underscores a fundamental principle of standing: causation cannot rely on the "unfettered choices" of independent actors who are not parties to the litigation. Because the OSC, the MSPB, and the DOJ are distinct entities with their own independent authorities and discretionary powers, a judgment against the OSC has no inherent power to force the DOJ's hand. The court signaled that the judiciary is not a tool for "persuading" third-party agencies to change their behavior; rather, it is a venue for adjudicating the legal obligations of the specific defendants before it.

This distinction leads to the second pillar of the court’s holding: the difference between "binding" and "persuasive" redressability. Under *Lujan*, a plaintiff must show that a favorable decision will "very likely spur" the steps necessary to redress the injury. The D.C. Circuit emphasized that redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties in the case, not whether the judgment’s rationale "might persuade" outside actors. If the only way to solve the plaintiff's problem is to hope that a judicial opinion inspires another agency to voluntarily comply, the case fails the redressability test.

The doctrinal implications of *America First Legal Foundation* are significant for litigants seeking to challenge multi-agency government policies. It suggests that plaintiffs cannot use the "domino effect" of administrative procedures to manufacture standing. If a plaintiff’s injury is caused by Agency A, they cannot seek relief by suing Agency B on the theory that Agency B’s failure to act is the "root cause" of Agency A’s behavior—unless they can demonstrate a direct, non-speculative legal nexus.

Ultimately, the court’s reasoning protects the judiciary from being transformed into a forum for speculative policy-making. By requiring a concrete, direct causal link and a high probability of redressability, the court ensures that Article III power is reserved for instances where the court can actually provide a remedy, rather than merely offering a judicial "suggestion" that might, in a best-case scenario, move a distant bureaucrat to act.
