Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Mandatory Duties vs. Discretionary Procedures: Navigating OSC’s Enforcement Maze

When a federal agency fails to comply with the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) serves as a critical watchdog, tasked with investigating "arbitrary or capricious" withholdings. However, a recent decision by the D.C. Circuit in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* highlights a significant hurdle for litigants attempting to use the judiciary to compel agency oversight: the distinction between a mandate to *act* and a right to a specific *result*.

The dispute began when the Department of Justice (DOJ) refused to search employee emails for FOIA records without the employees' consent. America First Legal Foundation (AFL) sought to compel the OSC to investigate this policy under 5 U.S.C. § 1216, which states that the OSC "shall" conduct an investigation into such withholdings. The OSC declined, arguing that the specific statutory triggers for a mandatory investigation (found in FOIA’s own enforcement provisions) were not met. While the district court agreed that the OSC had a mandatory duty to initiate an investigation, it also noted that the specific procedural steps—such as notifying the complainant—remained within the agency's discretion. When the OSC again refused to act, AFL sued to compel the investigation, but the D.C. Circuit ultimately dismissed the case for lack of Article III standing.

The court’s analysis provides a masterclass in the limitations of "procedural standing." AFL argued that it had a procedural right to an OSC investigation and that the denial of this right caused a concrete informational injury. The D.C. Circuit, however, rejected the notion that a "procedural right in vacuo" is sufficient to bring a case into federal court. To have standing, a plaintiff must show a concrete injury caused by the defendant that is likely to be redressed by a favorable court judgment. The court found that AFL’s claims failed on both counts, primarily because the causal chain between an OSC investigation and the desired outcome—the production of DOJ records—was too speculative to satisfy the requirements of redressability.

The court’s reasoning identifies a "causal chain" of uncertainty that serves as a warning to public interest litigants. For AFL to be made whole, the court would have to assume that: (1) the OSC would initiate an investigation; (2) the OSC would pursue corrective action; (3) the Merit Systems Protection Board would sanction the employees; and (4) the DOJ would, in response to those sanctions, finally produce the records. The D.C. Circuit noted that because this chain relies on the "unfettered choices" of multiple independent actors (the OSC, the MSPB, and the DOJ) who are not parties to the lawsuit, the court cannot find that a judgment against the OSC would "very likely spur" the production of the records.

Furthermore, the court clarified the limits of "informational standing." AFL argued that it was entitled to the "fruits" of the OSC investigation—specifically, the notices and information required during a "prohibited personnel practice" inquiry. The court countered that while § 1216 mandates an investigation, it does not create a statutory entitlement to the specific procedural outputs of that investigation. Because the OSC has the discretion to decide whether to follow those specific procedural notices, AFL cannot claim a concrete injury based on the denial of those notices.

This opinion reinforces a significant doctrinal boundary: the judiciary is not a tool for micromanaging agency enforcement processes. By distinguishing between the mandatory duty to *begin* an inquiry and the discretionary power to *manage* that inquiry, the court protects the agency’s operational autonomy. For litigants, the takeaway is clear: to succeed in compelling agency action, a plaintiff must show a direct, non-speculative path from a court order to the redress of a concrete injury. In the "enforcement maze" of the OSC, the court will not bridge the gap between an agency's duty to look into a matter and the plaintiff's desire for a specific, multi-agency outcome.
