Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Procedural Rights in Vacuo: The High Bar for Compelling Agency Action

In the complex machinery of federal oversight, statutes often command that agencies "shall" perform specific functions—investigate misconduct, conduct audits, or initiate disciplinary proceedings. For public interest groups and advocacy organizations, these mandatory duties provide a powerful lever: the ability to sue to compel an agency to do its job. However, the District of Columbia Circuit recently signaled that these statutory mandates do not automatically grant a private plaintiff a seat at the judicial table. In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the court reinforced a rigorous interpretation of Article III standing, holding that a procedural right to an investigation, divorced from a concrete personal interest, is insufficient to sustain a lawsuit.

The case arose from a dispute over the Department of Justice’s (DOJ) handling of Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) requests. America First Legal Foundation (AFL) sought records regarding judicial recusals but was rebuffed by the DOJ on the grounds that the agency does not search employee emails without consent. AFL then turned to the Office of Special Counsel (OSC), the agency tasked with investigating "prohibited personnel practices" and arbitrary FOIA withholdings. When the OSC declined to investigate the DOJ’s email policy, AFL sued to compel the investigation, arguing that the OSC was violating a mandatory duty under 5 U.S.C. § 1216.

The D.C. Circuit’s analysis centers on the "case or controversy" requirement of Article III, which demands that a plaintiff suffer a "concrete and particularized" injury that is "actual or imminent," not "conjectural or hypothetical." The court identified two distinct theories of standing offered by AFL, both of which failed to clear this hurdle. First, AFL argued that the OSC's refusal to investigate prevented them from obtaining the records they sought from the DOJ. The court rejected this as a "speculative" causal chain. To win on this point, AFL would have had to prove that a court order would *very likely* compel the OSC to investigate, which would then *very likely* compel the Merit Systems Protection Board to sanction employees, which would then *very likely* cause the DOJ to produce the records. Because this chain relies on the discretionary choices of multiple independent actors—none of whom were before the court—it failed the redressability test.

The more doctrinally significant portion of the opinion, however, concerns AFL’s second theory: that they had a procedural right to an investigation and the information resulting from it. Here, the court deployed the "procedural right in vacuo" doctrine. While the court acknowledged that plaintiffs alleging violations of procedural rights can sometimes enjoy "relaxed" requirements regarding imminence and redressability, those relaxations do not excuse a lack of injury in fact. The court reasoned that while § 1216 might mandate that the OSC *conduct* an investigation, it does not grant a third party a statutory *entitlement* to receive the fruits of that investigation. Without a specific right to receive notices or records from the OSC, AFL suffered no concrete injury from the agency's inaction.

This decision reflects a tightening of the gatekeeping function that standing serves in federal litigation. By distinguishing between an agency’s internal obligation to perform an oversight function and a private citizen’s right to demand that function be performed, the court is insulating the executive branch from "oversight-only" litigation. For public interest groups, this creates a significant hurdle: it suggests that simply pointing to a statute that says an agency "shall" do something is not enough to bring a suit. A plaintiff must instead demonstrate a tangible, personal harm—such as the actual withholding of a record or a specific financial loss—that the court can directly remedy.

The downstream effects of this ruling are noteworthy for administrative law and FOIA advocacy. It effectively narrows the "private attorney general" model of litigation, where NGOs use the courts to force systemic administrative reforms. If a plaintiff cannot sue to compel an investigation because the investigation itself is a discretionary administrative process, then the only remaining path for redress is to sue the primary actor (the DOJ in this case) for the underlying harm (the withholding of records). While this maintains a clear boundary for judicial power, it may also make it more difficult for outside groups to use the judiciary to ensure that internal government watchdogs are fulfilling their statutory mandates. By requiring a "concrete" interest to anchor a procedural right, the D.C. Circuit has signaled that the judiciary is less interested in auditing the internal processes of the executive branch and more focused on redressing specific, individual grievances.
