Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

Chapter 2: # Statutory Interpretation of the MPWA

## The Plain Language Rule: Applying Legislative Intent to Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171

In interpreting statutes, courts often begin with the "plain language rule." This principle dictates that when the language of a statute is plain and unambiguous, the court must assume that the text manifests the legislative intent and must give effect to that text as written. In *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, the Eighth Circuit applied this rule to determine whether an employee could seek "average daily wage penalties" under the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA).

The court examined Minn. Stat. Ann. § 181.171, which allows a person to bring a civil action for violations of certain provisions, including § 181.101. While the statute states that an employer found in violation is liable for "compensatory damages and other appropriate relief," it further clarifies that an employee may recover civil penalties or damages only "so long as they are provided for in the section" of the MPWA that the employer allegedly violated. Because the underlying section—§ 181.101—does not explicitly provide for employee-initiated penalties, the plain language of the statute suggests such a claim is barred.

## Agency Authority vs. Individual Rights: Distinguishing the Commissioner's Power to Collect Penalties from the Employee's Right to 'Prosecute a Claim for Wages'

To understand the limitation on Cohen’s claim, the court distinguished between the administrative authority of the state and the private rights of an individual employee. Under § 181.101(a), the authority to charge and collect a penalty is reserved exclusively for the Commissioner. The process is specific: the Commissioner serves a demand for payment; if the employer fails to pay within ten days, the Commissioner may then charge and collect the wages earned plus a penalty.

In contrast, the statute describes the employee’s role differently. It explicitly states that the section "does not prevent an employee from prosecuting a claim for wages" and provides employees with a "substantive right" to the payment of wages and commissions. By separating the Commissioner's power to "collect a penalty" from the employee's right to "prosecute a claim for wages," the court concluded that the legislative framework allows the state to penalize employers while limiting the employee's personal recovery to the wages themselves.

## Legislative Intent and 'How the Legislature Knew': Using Other Statutes (e.g., § 181.13 and § 181.14) as Evidence of What the Legislature Could Have Authorized

A secondary tool in statutory construction is examining what the legislature *could* have done but chose not to do. This is often expressed as the principle that if a legislature knows how to accomplish a particular objective in one area of the law, its failure to do so in another area indicates a deliberate choice. The court used this logic to reinforce its conclusion regarding § 181.101.

The court pointed to other sections of the MPWA, such as § 181.13 (concerning discharged employees) and § 181.14 (concerning employees who quit or resign). Both of those sections explicitly grant the employee the right to "charge and collect a penalty." The court reasoned that because the legislature knew how to authorize penalties for employees in those specific contexts, its omission of such language in § 181.101 was intentional. This evidence of "knowing" how to grant a right, but choosing not to do so in this specific instance, confirmed that Cohen was not entitled to penalties under the section he cited.

## Conclusion on MPWA Penalties: Why the District Court's Summary Judgment Was Correct

Because the court determined that the plain language of § 181.101 provides for penalties only through the Commissioner's actions, Cohen had no statutory basis to seek average daily wage penalties as an individual plaintiff. The statute’s structure clearly separates the state’s enforcement mechanism from the employee’s private cause of action. Consequently, the district court’s grant of summary judgment regarding the MPWA claim was correct, as there was no genuine issue of material fact regarding the availability of those specific penalties.
