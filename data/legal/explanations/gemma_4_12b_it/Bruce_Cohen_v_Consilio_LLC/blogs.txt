Title: Blog about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

# The Persistence of Interest: Standing and Mootness in Government Penalty Claims

In the architecture of Article III standing, the "personal interest" requirement serves as a gatekeeper, ensuring that federal courts remain forums for private grievances rather than vehicles for generalized public enforcement. However, the boundary between a private grievance and a public interest becomes blurred in cases involving statutory penalties—remedies designed not to make a plaintiff whole, but to punish a defendant and deter future misconduct. The Eighth Circuit’s recent decision in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* highlights the jurisdictional tightrope courts must walk when a plaintiff has been made "whole" in every personal sense, yet continues to seek a penalty that flows exclusively to the government.

The facts of *Cohen* are straightforward: a licensed attorney sued his employer, Consilio, for failing to pay overtime wages under the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA) and related statutes. In a move common in high-stakes wage litigation, the parties reached a settlement of sorts during the litigation. Consilio paid Cohen all the overtime wages and liquidated damages he was owed. At that point, Cohen’s personal ledger was balanced. However, Cohen remained interested in seeking statutory penalties—funds that, by statute, would be paid to the Commissioner, not to him.

The Eighth Circuit’s treatment of these competing interests provides a sophisticated roadmap for handling the "private attorney general" problem. In resolving the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA) claim, the court took a traditional, text-based approach. It found that because the statute specifically reserved the power to collect daily wage penalties for the Commissioner, Cohen lacked the statutory authority to seek them personally. This is a standard "no-standing" result based on the limitations of the underlying law.

The case becomes significantly more complex, however, regarding the MFLSA claim. Here, the court faced a classic Article III dilemma: Does a plaintiff retain a "personal interest" in a government penalty once their own wages have been paid? The court turned to the landmark Supreme Court precedent *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, which held that civil penalties can provide standing because they "encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones." 

The analytical pivot in *Cohen* is the requirement that this personal interest must persist throughout the life of the litigation. The Eighth Circuit correctly identified a "jurisdictional gap": if Consilio has actually abandoned its illegal overtime policy, Cohen’s claim for a deterrent penalty may be moot, as there is no longer an ongoing wrong to deter. Conversely, if the policy remains in place, Cohen may still have standing under *Laidlaw* because the continued violation creates a continuing injury to the public interest in which he has a stake. By vacating the summary judgment and remanding for a determination of jurisdiction, the court is essentially demanding a factual inquiry into the defendant’s current operations.

From an analytical perspective, the court’s reasoning suggests that "making the plaintiff whole" is not a per se trigger for mootness in penalty-based litigation. Instead, the court is treating the penalty as a "quasi-public" remedy. The court is asking a fundamental question about the nature of the plaintiff's role: Is Cohen seeking a personal check, or is he seeking a public reckoning? If the latter, his standing depends entirely on the "persistence" of the defendant's conduct.

The doctrinal implications of this approach are significant for a wide range of statutory schemes, including environmental law, consumer protection, and labor regulations. It reinforces a fact-intensive approach to mootness that prevents defendants from "buying" their way out of a lawsuit by settling the private damages while maintaining the underlying illegal practice. Downstream, this means that in cases where the primary remedy is a government fine, the "disappearance" of the private harm (the unpaid wages) does not automatically extinguish the court's jurisdiction. The court will instead look to the "life" of the violation itself to determine if the litigation remains a live issue.



# The ‘Legislature Knows How’ Test: Limits of Private Enforcement in State Wage Acts

In the landscape of labor and employment law, a fundamental question often arises: Who has the power to "punish" a company for violating the rules? Is it the state, acting through its regulatory agencies, or is it the injured worker, seeking redress through the private judicial system? The United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit recently addressed this tension in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC*, offering a sharp reminder of the limitations of private rights of action when a statute grants enforcement powers exclusively to a state official.

The case involved Cohen, a licensed attorney who sued his employer, Consilio, for failing to pay overtime wages under three different Minnesota statutes: the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA), the Minnesota Fair Labor Standards Act (MFLSA), and the Minnesota Wage Theft Act (MWTA). While Cohen successfully secured his unpaid wages and liquidated damages—effectively "making him whole"—he sought additional statutory penalties. The court’s treatment of these penalty claims, particularly under the MPWA, highlights a significant doctrine in statutory construction: the "legislature knows how" test.

## The Scope of Statutory Authority

The core of the dispute regarding the MPWA centered on whether Cohen could recover "average daily wage penalties." The text of the MPWA is somewhat ambiguous; it states that a person may bring a civil action for redress, and that an employer found to have violated the act is liable for "compensatory damages and other appropriate relief." Cohen argued that "average daily wage penalties" fell under that broad umbrella of "appropriate relief."

The Eighth Circuit, however, took a strictly textualist approach. It observed that the statute specifically authorizes the Minnesota Commissioner to "charge and collect" wages and penalties after a demand for payment is ignored. Crucially, the court noted that while the statute allows an employee to "prosecute a claim for wages," it remains silent on the employee’s right to collect penalties.

To resolve the ambiguity, the court employed the "legislature knows how" doctrine. This principle suggests that if the legislature intended to grant a specific power to a private citizen—such as the power to levy a penalty—it would have included that power in the text, especially when it has done so in other, similar statutes. The court pointed to other sections of Minnesota law where employees *can* clearly collect penalties upon resignation or discharge. By contrasting the MPWA with these other statutes, the court concluded that the legislature's silence in the MPWA was a deliberate choice to reserve penalty-collecting power for the Commissioner alone.

## Analytical Perspective: The Shield of Statutory Text

The court’s reasoning reflects a conservative, almost formalist approach to statutory interpretation. By relying on the "legislature knows how" doctrine, the court effectively creates a high bar for plaintiffs seeking to expand their rights of action. It suggests that a private party cannot "read into" a statute a right to a remedy that the legislature has clearly granted to a state agency elsewhere.

From a policy perspective, this distinction is vital. There is a significant difference between compensatory damages (which restore the victim to their original position) and statutory penalties (which serve a regulatory and deterrent function). The court’s holding suggests that the judiciary is wary of allowing private litigants to act as "private attorneys general" for regulatory purposes unless the legislature explicitly invites them to do so. By limiting the "teeth" of the MPWA to the Commissioner, the court ensures that the state maintains control over the punitive aspects of labor law, even if it limits the immediate financial windfall for the individual worker.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The implications of this decision for state wage acts are significant. It reinforces a bifurcated enforcement model: private plaintiffs can seek to be made whole, but only the state can "punish" the employer. This may diminish the incentive for private litigation in cases where the primary goal is to trigger a penalty rather than recover back pay. For employers, it provides a degree of protection; if a policy change occurs and the "wrongful conduct" is mitigated, the threat of a private penalty suit is curtailed.

Furthermore, the court’s brief detour into the MFLSA claim—where it vacated summary judgment due to potential mootness—adds another layer of complexity. The court suggested that if Consilio has already stopped the violative policy, Cohen might lack the "continuing interest" required for standing to seek a government penalty. This underscores a growing judicial trend: even when a private party *does* have a right to seek a penalty, that right may vanish the moment the underlying conduct ceases.

Ultimately, *Cohen* serves as a cautionary tale for plaintiffs' advocates. It clarifies that in the eyes of the court, the "appropriate relief" available to a private citizen is strictly bounded by the text of the statute. If the legislature wanted to give the people the power to punish, they had the tools to do so; if they chose not to use them, the courts will not provide them.



# Procedural Boundaries: Why New Remedies Cannot Be 'Briefed-In'

In the theater of civil litigation, one of the most fundamental principles is the "no surprises" rule. A defendant must know exactly what they are defending against, and a court must know exactly what it is being asked to decide. The Eighth Circuit’s recent decision in *Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC* serves as a sharp reminder of this principle, reinforcing the procedural boundary between a plaintiff's initial complaint and the arguments they are permitted to advance in a summary judgment brief. By refusing to grant injunctive relief that was not pleaded in the original complaint, the court reaffirmed that new remedies cannot simply be "briefed-in" to bypass the rigors of formal pleading.

The case at hand involved a dispute over overtime wages. Cohen, an attorney working for Consilio, alleged that the company’s new premium overtime policy violated several Minnesota wage acts. After Consilio paid Cohen the disputed wages and liquidated damages, the litigation shifted toward statutory penalties and injunctive relief. During the summary judgment phase, Cohen attempted to pivot, requesting an affirmative injunction to force Consilio to rescind its overtime policy. However, because this specific request was absent from his original complaint, the court declined to consider it.

The court’s reasoning rests on the distinction between the *substance* of a claim and the *scope* of the litigation. A motion for summary judgment is a mechanism to determine whether a case should proceed to trial based on the facts and legal theories presented at the outset. When a plaintiff introduces a new form of relief—such as an injunction—in a brief without first amending their complaint, they are essentially attempting to expand the scope of the lawsuit mid-stream. The Eighth Circuit correctly identified that the court is not a "shopping gallery" for remedies; if a plaintiff wants a new prayer for relief, they must follow the path laid out by Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a) and amend their pleadings.

This procedural gatekeeping is vital for judicial economy and fairness. If courts allowed plaintiffs to introduce new remedies in summary judgment briefs, defendants would be forced to defend against "moving targets." A defendant who has prepared to litigate a claim for monetary damages may be entirely unprepared to litigate the nuanced standards of a permanent injunction. By requiring that all sought remedies be pleaded in the complaint, the rules ensure that the defendant has a fair opportunity to develop a defense and that the court maintains a clear jurisdictional map of the issues before it.

The doctrinal implications of *Cohen* are clear: the summary judgment brief is a vehicle for argument, not a vehicle for new claims. While it might seem like a technicality, this distinction protects the integrity of the litigation process. It prevents the "piecemeal" expansion of lawsuits, where a plaintiff might wait until the final stages of a case to "tack on" a significant new request for relief. By upholding the requirement that new claims must be properly raised through amended complaints, the court ensures that the boundaries of a case remain stable, predictable, and, most importantly, fair.
