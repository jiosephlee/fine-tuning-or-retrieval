Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Bruce Cohen v. Consilio, LLC"

### How does the 'legislative inaction' argument work in statutory interpretation?
Question:
In Section II(A), the court denies Cohen's MPWA claim by saying that the legislature 'knew how' to grant him penalties because they did so in other sections (like § 181.13). I'm struggling to understand how a legislature's *failure* to include a specific power in one statute is used as evidence of their *intent* to exclude it. Isn't it possible they just forgot or didn't get to it yet?
Answer:
The "legislative inaction" argument—often referred to as the "know-how" principle—is a method of determining legislative intent by looking at what a legislature *did* do in similar contexts versus what it *omitted* doing in the specific statute at issue.

In statutory interpretation, courts generally operate under the presumption that a legislature is a deliberate body. When a legislature passes a complex scheme of laws, it is assumed that the inclusion or exclusion of specific powers is a conscious policy choice rather than a clerical error or an oversight.

In *Cohen v. Consilio*, the court applies this logic in Section II(A) to resolve whether an employee can seek "average daily wage penalties" under § 181.101 of the Minnesota Payment of Wages Act (MPWA). The court observes that while § 181.101 is silent on an employee's right to penalties (only mentioning the Commissioner's authority), other sections of the same act—specifically § 181.13 and § 181.14—explicitly grant employees the right to "charge and collect a penalty."

By pointing to these other sections, the court establishes a "blueprint" of what the legislature is capable of doing. The court reasons that because the legislature successfully drafted language to grant penalties to employees in sections 181.13 and 181.14, it "knew how" to accomplish that objective if it wanted to. Therefore, the failure to include that same language in § 181.101 is interpreted as a deliberate choice to limit the remedies available under that specific section.

To address your concern about the possibility of the legislature simply "forgetting": while it is theoretically possible for a legislature to make a mistake, courts are hesitant to assume incompetence. If a legislature has demonstrated a clear ability to grant a power in one part of a statutory scheme, the most logically consistent interpretation of its silence in a similar part of that same scheme is that the silence is intentional. In short, if the legislature had the "tools" to give you a penalty and chose not to use them in your specific section, the court infers that the lack of a penalty was the intended result.
