Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# The 'Listener' as a Recipient: Interpreting Statutory 'Receipt' in the Age of APIs

In the rapidly evolving landscape of digital commerce, a recurring friction point exists between 20th-century statutory language and 21st-century infrastructure. How does a court define "receipt" when the "recipient" is not a human clerk with a filing cabinet, but an automated web service "listening" for an API call? The United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit recently addressed this question in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, offering a pragmatic, technology-neutral interpretation that could have significant downstream implications for how courts treat automated notifications across various industries.

The case centered on Louisiana’s strict requirements for cancelling insurance policies financed by a third party. Under Louisiana law, when a premium finance company (in this case, APAC) exercises its power of attorney to cancel a policy due to nonpayment, it must provide the insurer with a "notice of cancellation" and a "statement certifying" specific facts. The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto failed to comply with these procedures because the "receipt" of the notice was automated and the "certification" lacked a physical signature. They contended that for a cancellation to be valid, a human being had to physically possess the document before the cancellation took effect.

The Fifth Circuit’s analysis begins with a restrained approach to statutory interpretation. Regarding the "certification" requirement, the court noted that while Louisiana law demands "strict adherence" to procedures, that adherence does not grant judges the license to add requirements not found in the text. Because the statute does not explicitly require a signature, the court found that a company can "certify" information through a corporate statement without a specific individual’s signature. This is a foundational principle of modern corporate law: the entity is the actor, and its systems are its agents.

However, the more innovative legal reasoning appears in the court's treatment of "receipt." The plaintiffs argued that "receipt" implies the physical taking of possession by a human agent. The court looked to *Black’s Law Dictionary*, which defines "receipt" as "taking physical possession" or "getting from some outside source." The court then mapped these definitions onto the reality of modern software architecture. GoAuto’s system functioned as a "listener"—a web service that waits for a "call" from APAC’s system. When APAC’s system triggered that call, the information was "received" by GoAuto’s infrastructure.

This "listener" model provides a compelling bridge between old-world law and new-world tech. By concluding that a computer system's intake of data constitutes "receipt," the court avoids the trap of demanding "human-in-the-loop" verification for every automated transaction. If the court had required a human to physically touch a piece of paper or an employee to manually acknowledge an email before a computer could execute a cancellation, it would have created a logistical bottleneck that would render high-frequency, automated commerce nearly impossible to manage under the current statutory framework.

The doctrinal implications of this ruling extend far beyond insurance premium financing. This case offers a roadmap for how courts should handle "receipt" in other areas of law, such as the service of process, the delivery of notices of default in real estate, or the notification of regulatory violations. By focusing on the functional transfer of information—where the "outside source" successfully delivers data into the "listener" system—the court ensures that the law remains technology-neutral. It suggests that as long as the information is accessible to the company’s systems and the company's systems are programmed to act upon it, the legal requirement of "receipt" has been satisfied. In the age of APIs, the "possession" of data is increasingly synonymous with its availability to an automated process.



# When 'Strict Adherence' Isn't a License for Judicial Invention

In many insurance and regulatory contexts, the phrase "strict adherence" serves as a warning to corporations: any minor procedural deviation can render an action void. Plaintiffs often seize upon these phrases as an invitation for courts to scrutinize every technicality, seeking to invalidate contracts or actions based on missing stamps, misplaced signatures, or "imperfect" workflows. However, the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit recently sent a clear message to those seeking to expand statutory duties through judicial imagination. In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the court held that while a statute may demand "strict adherence," that does not grant a judge the authority to insert requirements into the text that the legislature chose to omit.

The dispute centered on the mechanics of insurance policy cancellation. Under Louisiana law, when an insurance-premium finance company (in this case, APAC) determines that a policyholder has defaulted on payments, it can exercise a power of attorney to cancel the policy. To do so legally, the finance company must "certify" several facts—including the existence of a valid power of attorney and the fact that the default was not cured—and send that notice to the insurer. The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto’s procedures were invalid because the "certification" lacked a physical signature and because GoAuto’s computer system "received" the cancellation request before a human employee had physically handled the paperwork.

The Fifth Circuit’s analysis of the "certification" requirement provides a masterclass in textualist restraint. The plaintiffs argued that "certifying" inherently implies a signature. The court, however, pointed out that the Louisiana statute makes no explicit reference to a signature. While the court acknowledged that some past cases discussed signatures, it noted that none actually held that a signature was a prerequisite for a valid cancellation. The court observed that the statute identifies "the insurance premium finance company" as the entity performing the certification, not a specific individual. By refusing to read a signature requirement into the law, the court underscored a fundamental principle of statutory interpretation: "Strict adherence" to a procedure does not mean adherence to a court-constructed version of that procedure.

Similarly, the court addressed the modernization of "receipt." The plaintiffs contended that "receipt" required a human being to take physical possession of a notice before a policy could be canceled. They argued that because GoAuto’s automated system canceled the policy the moment it "heard" a call from APAC’s system, the "receipt" was premature. The court rejected this, leaning into a functionalist view of modern commerce. It noted that in a "modern statute set in current times," a computer system that is "always listening" and performs an action upon receiving an electronic signal satisfies the requirement of receipt. The court essentially ruled that the "how" of the technology does not change the "what" of the legal requirement.

The doctrinal implications of *Williams* are significant for both the insurance industry and the broader practice of statutory litigation. First, it reinforces the limits of "procedural" litigation. Plaintiffs who rely on "strict adherence" clauses must be prepared to point to a specific, textual violation of the law rather than a failure to meet a subjective standard of "proper" behavior. If the statute doesn't require a wet-ink signature, a missing signature is not a violation, no matter how much it might feel like a lapse in formality.

Second, the opinion signals a judicial willingness to accommodate automated systems in regulatory compliance. By equating a computer's "web service" call with "receipt," the court bridged the gap between 20th-century legal definitions and 21st-century infrastructure. For businesses, this provides a degree of certainty: if the automated system fulfills the functional goal of the statute, the court is unlikely to strike down the action based on the absence of a human intermediary. Ultimately, *Williams* serves as a reminder that while the law demands precision, it does not demand a return to manual relics unless the legislature explicitly commands it.



# The High Price of Certification: Why 'Undefined' Isn't 'Compelling'

In federal diversity jurisdiction cases, a recurring procedural friction arises when a district court or a circuit court reaches a conclusion on a state law issue that has never been explicitly adjudicated by the state’s highest court. For many litigants, this is the "golden ticket": an opportunity to move the needle of state law by asking the federal court to certify the question to the state supreme court. However, the Fifth Circuit’s recent decision in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* serves as a sobering reminder that the power to certify is not a right, but a privilege—one that the court views as a finite resource.

The case at hand involved a technical but high-stakes dispute over Louisiana’s insurance-premium finance statutes. The central question was whether GoAuto Insurance’s automated procedures for canceling policies—specifically, the use of an automated computer system to "receive" a cancellation notice and "certify" the compliance of that notice without a physical signature—met the state’s "strict adherence" requirements. The plaintiffs argued that the lack of a physical signature and the nuances of electronic "receipt" required a definitive ruling from the Louisiana Supreme Court. The Fifth Circuit, however, chose to settle the matter on the bench, declining to certify the issue.

The court’s analytical path was grounded in a pragmatic reading of the statute. Regarding the requirement to "certify" information, the court noted that the Louisiana statute specifies that the *insurance premium finance company* is the entity that certifies, not a specific individual. Because the notices in question clearly stated the required certifications, the court found no reason to invent a signature requirement where the legislature had remained silent. Similarly, the court addressed the "receipt" of notices by invoking a modern interpretation of the term. By pointing to *Black’s Law Dictionary* and acknowledging that the statute is a "modern statute set in current times," the court concluded that a computer system "listening" for a web service call constitutes possession or custody for the purposes of the law.

The real doctrinal interest, however, lies in the court’s refusal to certify these interpretations. The plaintiffs argued that because these terms were undefined by the Louisiana Supreme Court, the issue was of such importance to the state’s insurance industry that it demanded a referral. The Fifth Circuit flatly rejected this "certification-by-default" logic. Relying on the principle of judicial restraint, the court expressed a "chary" attitude toward certification, describing the power to do so as a "precious resource."

This "scarcity theory" of certification has significant downstream effects for federal litigation. It signals that a vacuum of state-level precedent is not, in itself, a "compelling reason" for a federal court to step aside and let a state court take the wheel. For litigants, this raises the bar significantly; to move a question of state law into the supreme court’s orbit, they must prove that the issue is not just unsettled, but fundamentally ambiguous or of such profound systemic importance that a circuit court’s interpretation would be inherently inadequate.

By affirming the district court’s summary judgment without certification, the Fifth Circuit underscored a preference for finality and efficient adjudication. The court is essentially saying that if a reasonable interpretation of the law can be derived from the text and modern context, the federal court will take it. While this may frustrate plaintiffs seeking to shape state law via the federal pipeline, it reinforces a standard of judicial economy that prevents federal courts from becoming a revolving door for every unsettled state-law nuance. In the eyes of the Fifth Circuit, "undefined" is merely an invitation for a good argument, not a mandate for a referral.
