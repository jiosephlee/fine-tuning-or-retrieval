Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# The 'Listener' as a Recipient: Interpreting Statutory 'Receipt' in the Age of APIs

In the rapidly evolving landscape of digital commerce, a recurring friction point exists between 20th-century statutory language and 21st-century infrastructure. How does a court define "receipt" when the "recipient" is not a human clerk with a filing cabinet, but an automated web service "listening" for an API call? The United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit recently addressed this question in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, offering a pragmatic, technology-neutral interpretation that could have significant downstream implications for how courts treat automated notifications across various industries.

The case centered on Louisiana’s strict requirements for cancelling insurance policies financed by a third party. Under Louisiana law, when a premium finance company (in this case, APAC) exercises its power of attorney to cancel a policy due to nonpayment, it must provide the insurer with a "notice of cancellation" and a "statement certifying" specific facts. The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto failed to comply with these procedures because the "receipt" of the notice was automated and the "certification" lacked a physical signature. They contended that for a cancellation to be valid, a human being had to physically possess the document before the cancellation took effect.

The Fifth Circuit’s analysis begins with a restrained approach to statutory interpretation. Regarding the "certification" requirement, the court noted that while Louisiana law demands "strict adherence" to procedures, that adherence does not grant judges the license to add requirements not found in the text. Because the statute does not explicitly require a signature, the court found that a company can "certify" information through a corporate statement without a specific individual’s signature. This is a foundational principle of modern corporate law: the entity is the actor, and its systems are its agents.

However, the more innovative legal reasoning appears in the court's treatment of "receipt." The plaintiffs argued that "receipt" implies the physical taking of possession by a human agent. The court looked to *Black’s Law Dictionary*, which defines "receipt" as "taking physical possession" or "getting from some outside source." The court then mapped these definitions onto the reality of modern software architecture. GoAuto’s system functioned as a "listener"—a web service that waits for a "call" from APAC’s system. When APAC’s system triggered that call, the information was "received" by GoAuto’s infrastructure.

This "listener" model provides a compelling bridge between old-world law and new-world tech. By concluding that a computer system's intake of data constitutes "receipt," the court avoids the trap of demanding "human-in-the-loop" verification for every automated transaction. If the court had required a human to physically touch a piece of paper or an employee to manually acknowledge an email before a computer could execute a cancellation, it would have created a logistical bottleneck that would render high-frequency, automated commerce nearly impossible to manage under the current statutory framework.

The doctrinal implications of this ruling extend far beyond insurance premium financing. This case offers a roadmap for how courts should handle "receipt" in other areas of law, such as the service of process, the delivery of notices of default in real estate, or the notification of regulatory violations. By focusing on the functional transfer of information—where the "outside source" successfully delivers data into the "listener" system—the court ensures that the law remains technology-neutral. It suggests that as long as the information is accessible to the company’s systems and the company's systems are programmed to act upon it, the legal requirement of "receipt" has been satisfied. In the age of APIs, the "possession" of data is increasingly synonymous with its availability to an automated process.
