Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# When 'Strict Adherence' Isn't a License for Judicial Invention

In many insurance and regulatory contexts, the phrase "strict adherence" serves as a warning to corporations: any minor procedural deviation can render an action void. Plaintiffs often seize upon these phrases as an invitation for courts to scrutinize every technicality, seeking to invalidate contracts or actions based on missing stamps, misplaced signatures, or "imperfect" workflows. However, the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit recently sent a clear message to those seeking to expand statutory duties through judicial imagination. In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the court held that while a statute may demand "strict adherence," that does not grant a judge the authority to insert requirements into the text that the legislature chose to omit.

The dispute centered on the mechanics of insurance policy cancellation. Under Louisiana law, when an insurance-premium finance company (in this case, APAC) determines that a policyholder has defaulted on payments, it can exercise a power of attorney to cancel the policy. To do so legally, the finance company must "certify" several facts—including the existence of a valid power of attorney and the fact that the default was not cured—and send that notice to the insurer. The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto’s procedures were invalid because the "certification" lacked a physical signature and because GoAuto’s computer system "received" the cancellation request before a human employee had physically handled the paperwork.

The Fifth Circuit’s analysis of the "certification" requirement provides a masterclass in textualist restraint. The plaintiffs argued that "certifying" inherently implies a signature. The court, however, pointed out that the Louisiana statute makes no explicit reference to a signature. While the court acknowledged that some past cases discussed signatures, it noted that none actually held that a signature was a prerequisite for a valid cancellation. The court observed that the statute identifies "the insurance premium finance company" as the entity performing the certification, not a specific individual. By refusing to read a signature requirement into the law, the court underscored a fundamental principle of statutory interpretation: "Strict adherence" to a procedure does not mean adherence to a court-constructed version of that procedure.

Similarly, the court addressed the modernization of "receipt." The plaintiffs contended that "receipt" required a human being to take physical possession of a notice before a policy could be canceled. They argued that because GoAuto’s automated system canceled the policy the moment it "heard" a call from APAC’s system, the "receipt" was premature. The court rejected this, leaning into a functionalist view of modern commerce. It noted that in a "modern statute set in current times," a computer system that is "always listening" and performs an action upon receiving an electronic signal satisfies the requirement of receipt. The court essentially ruled that the "how" of the technology does not change the "what" of the legal requirement.

The doctrinal implications of *Williams* are significant for both the insurance industry and the broader practice of statutory litigation. First, it reinforces the limits of "procedural" litigation. Plaintiffs who rely on "strict adherence" clauses must be prepared to point to a specific, textual violation of the law rather than a failure to meet a subjective standard of "proper" behavior. If the statute doesn't require a wet-ink signature, a missing signature is not a violation, no matter how much it might feel like a lapse in formality.

Second, the opinion signals a judicial willingness to accommodate automated systems in regulatory compliance. By equating a computer's "web service" call with "receipt," the court bridged the gap between 20th-century legal definitions and 21st-century infrastructure. For businesses, this provides a degree of certainty: if the automated system fulfills the functional goal of the statute, the court is unlikely to strike down the action based on the absence of a human intermediary. Ultimately, *Williams* serves as a reminder that while the law demands precision, it does not demand a return to manual relics unless the legislature explicitly commands it.
