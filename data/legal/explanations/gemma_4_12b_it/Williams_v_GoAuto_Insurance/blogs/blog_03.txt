Title: Blog about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

# The High Price of Certification: Why 'Undefined' Isn't 'Compelling'

In federal diversity jurisdiction cases, a recurring procedural friction arises when a district court or a circuit court reaches a conclusion on a state law issue that has never been explicitly adjudicated by the state’s highest court. For many litigants, this is the "golden ticket": an opportunity to move the needle of state law by asking the federal court to certify the question to the state supreme court. However, the Fifth Circuit’s recent decision in *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance* serves as a sobering reminder that the power to certify is not a right, but a privilege—one that the court views as a finite resource.

The case at hand involved a technical but high-stakes dispute over Louisiana’s insurance-premium finance statutes. The central question was whether GoAuto Insurance’s automated procedures for canceling policies—specifically, the use of an automated computer system to "receive" a cancellation notice and "certify" the compliance of that notice without a physical signature—met the state’s "strict adherence" requirements. The plaintiffs argued that the lack of a physical signature and the nuances of electronic "receipt" required a definitive ruling from the Louisiana Supreme Court. The Fifth Circuit, however, chose to settle the matter on the bench, declining to certify the issue.

The court’s analytical path was grounded in a pragmatic reading of the statute. Regarding the requirement to "certify" information, the court noted that the Louisiana statute specifies that the *insurance premium finance company* is the entity that certifies, not a specific individual. Because the notices in question clearly stated the required certifications, the court found no reason to invent a signature requirement where the legislature had remained silent. Similarly, the court addressed the "receipt" of notices by invoking a modern interpretation of the term. By pointing to *Black’s Law Dictionary* and acknowledging that the statute is a "modern statute set in current times," the court concluded that a computer system "listening" for a web service call constitutes possession or custody for the purposes of the law.

The real doctrinal interest, however, lies in the court’s refusal to certify these interpretations. The plaintiffs argued that because these terms were undefined by the Louisiana Supreme Court, the issue was of such importance to the state’s insurance industry that it demanded a referral. The Fifth Circuit flatly rejected this "certification-by-default" logic. Relying on the principle of judicial restraint, the court expressed a "chary" attitude toward certification, describing the power to do so as a "precious resource."

This "scarcity theory" of certification has significant downstream effects for federal litigation. It signals that a vacuum of state-level precedent is not, in itself, a "compelling reason" for a federal court to step aside and let a state court take the wheel. For litigants, this raises the bar significantly; to move a question of state law into the supreme court’s orbit, they must prove that the issue is not just unsettled, but fundamentally ambiguous or of such profound systemic importance that a circuit court’s interpretation would be inherently inadequate.

By affirming the district court’s summary judgment without certification, the Fifth Circuit underscored a preference for finality and efficient adjudication. The court is essentially saying that if a reasonable interpretation of the law can be derived from the text and modern context, the federal court will take it. While this may frustrate plaintiffs seeking to shape state law via the federal pipeline, it reinforces a standard of judicial economy that prevents federal courts from becoming a revolving door for every unsettled state-law nuance. In the eyes of the Fifth Circuit, "undefined" is merely an invitation for a good argument, not a mandate for a referral.
