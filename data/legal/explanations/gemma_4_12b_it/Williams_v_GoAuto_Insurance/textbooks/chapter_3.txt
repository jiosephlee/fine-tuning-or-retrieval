Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

Chapter 3: # The Requirement of 'Certification' vs. Physical Signatures

## Plaintiffs' Argument for Mandatory Signatures

The central dispute regarding the validity of the policy cancellations hinges on the timing and form of the "certification" required by Louisiana law. The plaintiffs argue that for a cancellation to be legally effective, the premium finance company must provide a "certified" statement to the insurer *before* the insurer acts upon that cancellation. 

Specifically, the plaintiffs point to GoAuto’s internal workflow as evidence of a procedural defect. In this case, GoAuto’s computer system automatically cancels a policy as soon as it receives an automated email from the finance company (APAC). However, a human employee from APAC does not affix a physical signature stamp to the printed version of that cancellation notice until *after* the computer system has already processed the cancellation. The plaintiffs contend that because the "certification" (the signature) was not present at the moment of cancellation, the procedure failed to comply with the strict statutory requirements, rendering the cancellation ineffective.

## Textual Analysis: Does 'Certifying' Require a Signature?

To resolve this issue, the court performs a textual analysis of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The statute requires that the premium finance company send a copy of the notice of cancellation "together with a statement certifying" four specific facts: the existence of a valid power of attorney, the status of the default, the fact that a notice was sent to the insured, and the fact that copies were sent to interested third parties.

The court observes that the statute is silent regarding the requirement of a physical signature. It notes that if the legislature had intended for a signature to be a prerequisite for a valid cancellation, the word "signature" would likely appear in the text. Instead, the statute uses the word "certifying." The court reasons that "certifying" refers to the content of the statement—confirming that the four required facts are true—rather than the physical method by which that statement is delivered or authenticated. Furthermore, the court points out that the statute identifies the "insurance premium finance company" as the entity that effects the cancellation by certifying the information, rather than requiring a specific individual employee to sign a document.

## Analysis of Louisiana Precedent (KMJ Servs., Hodges, Lewis, and Benitez)

The court examines existing Louisiana case law to see if any prior decisions have clarified what "certifying" means in the context of insurance-premium finance. The court finds that the judiciary has been largely unhelpful in defining this specific requirement.

In *KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood*, the court paraphrased the requirement as a "statement certifying compliance with" the statute, but it did not mention signatures. Similarly, *Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins.* allowed a cancellation based upon the "receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement," again omitting any requirement for a signature. The court finds *Lewis v. Julien* to be equally unhelpful on this point.

The plaintiffs point to *Benitez v. Elsayed*, where a court noted that a notice of cancellation was "not signed by any individual" and "does not purport to 'certify' any of the statements." However, the court clarifies that *Benitez* did not actually hold that the absence of a signature disqualified the cancellation; it merely listed it as a shortcoming of the notice. Because no clear authority exists requiring a signature, the court concludes that it cannot invent a signature requirement where the statute does not explicitly demand one.

## The Court’s Conclusion on Non-Signature Certifications

The court ultimately rejects the plaintiffs' argument, grounding its decision in the principle of "strict adherence" to the statute. While Louisiana law requires strict adherence to the procedures for cancellation, the court emphasizes that "strict adherence" does not grant the judiciary the authority to add extra requirements to the law that the legislature did not include.

The court concludes that GoAuto’s procedures satisfy the statute because the insurer received a statement certifying the necessary facts. The fact that the certification is delivered via an automated "web service" and that a physical signature is added later by a human employee is irrelevant to the legal validity of the cancellation. By interpreting "receipt" and "certifying" based on their plain meanings in the context of modern technology, the court finds that a computer-generated certification is sufficient to trigger a valid cancellation.
