Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Williams v. GoAuto Insurance"

Chapter 2: # Statutory Framework for Policy Cancellation

## Requirements of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv)

To understand the court's analysis, one must first look at the specific mechanics of Louisiana's insurance-premium finance laws. When an insured person finances their insurance premiums through a third-party company (in this case, American Premium Assistance Company, or APAC), that finance company typically holds a power of attorney to cancel the policy if the insured fails to make payments. Louisiana law provides a specific procedural pathway for how that finance company must communicate the cancellation to the insurance provider (GoAuto).

Under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a), the finance company does not simply notify the insurer of a default; it must provide a formal package. This package must include a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a statement "certifying" four specific elements. The statute requires the finance company to confirm that the premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney, that the agreement is in default and the default remains uncured, that a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured (including the date it was sent), and that copies of the notice were sent to all persons with an interest in any potential loss (such as governmental agencies or holders of security interests).

## The Four-Part Certification Requirement

The "certification" mentioned in the statute is the heart of the procedural requirement. In *Williams v. GoAuto Insurance*, the plaintiffs argued that the certification was insufficient because it lacked a physical signature from a specific individual. However, the court’s analysis highlights a key principle of statutory interpretation: courts generally will not add requirements to a statute that are not explicitly stated. 

The court noted that while the statute requires a "statement certifying" the four points listed above, it does not explicitly require a signature. The court looked to existing Louisiana case law and found that other opinions have allowed cancellations based on a "statement" from the finance company without specifying a signature. Because the statute identifies "the insurance premium finance company" as the entity effecting the cancellation by certifying the information, the court concluded that a company can certify information through its corporate processes without needing to identify a specific human employee by name or signature.

## The Doctrine of 'Strict Adherence' in Louisiana Insurance Law

A critical legal standard in this opinion is the doctrine of "strict adherence." Louisiana courts do not view the requirements for policy cancellation as mere suggestions or "best practices." Instead, they are mandatory procedures. As the court noted by citing *Delatte v. Lemotte* and *Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co.*, any defect in the process of exercising a power of attorney to cancel a policy results in an "ineffective cancellation."

For a law student, this means that when a statute provides a specific multi-step process for a high-stakes action—such as terminating insurance coverage—the court will scrutinize the "how" as much as the "what." If a finance company fails to include even one of the four required certifications, or fails to follow the delivery method prescribed by the statute, the cancellation is legally void, even if the insured actually failed to pay their premiums.

## The Purpose of Statutory Compliance: Timing and Cure Periods

To understand why the court insists on "strict adherence," one must look at the underlying policy goals of the statute. The court identified two primary functions for these rigid requirements. First, the strict procedures provide a "clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation." This is vital for determining when a company's liability ends and when an insured's coverage ceases.

Second, these procedures grant a "minimum time during which the default may be cured." By requiring the finance company to follow a specific notice-and-certification chain, the law ensures that the insured is given a fair opportunity to rectify the nonpayment before the policy is terminated. In the context of *Williams*, these goals informed the court’s decision that GoAuto’s automated computer system—which "listens" for an email and performs an automatic cancellation—satisfies the "receipt" requirement of the statute. In a modern context, the court held that electronic "receipt" and possession by a computer system fulfill the legislative intent of the law, provided the required certifications are included in the transmission.
