Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 2: # Trial Evidentiary Issues and the Harmless Error Doctrine

In reviewing a criminal conviction on appeal, a primary task for the court is to determine whether the trial judge committed any "abuse of discretion" regarding the admission or exclusion of evidence. In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals addressed several such objections, analyzing how specific rules of evidence—such as those governing propensity, hearsay, and prejudice—apply to the facts of a firearms possession case.

## Rule 404(b) and Intrinsic Evidence: Proving Possession through Prior Ownership

A common defense strategy is to object to evidence that suggests a defendant has a history of similar conduct, arguing that such evidence is "propensity" evidence. Under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b), evidence of a crime, wrong, or other act is generally not admissible to prove that a person has a character trait (such as being a "criminal") that would make them likely to have committed the crime in question. 

However, the court in *Cutbank* distinguished between propensity and "intrinsic" evidence. The government sought to introduce testimony from witnesses who had seen Cutbank in possession of the specific Marlin .22 caliber rifle prior to the night of the incident. The court held that this was not improper propensity evidence. Instead, it was intrinsic evidence relevant to the charge of possession. Because the prosecution had to prove that Cutbank possessed the firearm during the crime, showing that he owned or had control over that specific weapon "a handful of times" before the incident directly supports the element of possession. The court clarified that prior possession of a specific item helps establish ownership or continuous control, which is a necessary factual component of the offense.

## Hearsay and the Confrontation Clause: Requirements for Declarant Testimony

The defense also challenged the admission of statements made by the victim, D.F., to the police. Generally, out-of-court statements offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted are considered hearsay and are inadmissible. Furthermore, the Sixth Amendment Confrontation Clause requires that a defendant have the right to confront the witnesses against them. 

Cutbank argued that the court should have issued a limiting instruction preventing the jury from considering D.F.’s prior statements to police as substantive evidence until she testified in person. The court declined to find an abuse of discretion in the district court’s refusal to give this specific instruction. The court reasoned that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the hearsay declarant actually appears in court and testifies. Because D.F. did testify during the trial, the constitutional requirement was met. Additionally, the court noted that it is not required to provide a specific limiting instruction for every item of evidence at the moment of admission; the court may instead provide a general instruction at the close of the trial to clarify how the jury should weigh various pieces of evidence.

## Rule 403 Analysis: Balancing Probative Value against Unfair Prejudice in Cross-Examination

During the trial, the victim testified that her sister had been killed by a gun. This testimony came up during cross-examination when she was being questioned about inconsistent prior statements regarding her familiarity with firearms. The defense moved to strike this testimony, likely arguing that it was unfairly prejudicial to the defendant's case.

To evaluate such a motion, courts apply Federal Rule of Evidence 403, which allows the exclusion of relevant evidence if its "probative value" (its ability to prove a fact) is substantially outweighed by the danger of "unfair prejudice," confusion of the issues, or misleading the jury. In *Cutbank*, the court upheld the district court’s decision to admit the testimony. The court reasoned that the statement was a direct response to a question about the witness's inconsistent statements. It served to explain the witness’s state of mind and her reasons for her testimony. Because the testimony was a response to a specific line of questioning and did not appear to cause unfair prejudice or confuse the jury, it was properly admitted.

## Authentication Requirements: Admissibility of Extrinsic Evidence for Impeachment

The defense attempted to impeach a government witness, C.F., by seeking to have portions of a police report read into the record. The report contained statements C.F. had made to an investigator. While the government allowed the defense to ask C.F. questions about what she told the police, it objected to the defense actually reading the report into evidence.

The court's ruling here highlights the requirement for "authentication." To introduce a document (extrinsic evidence) into a trial, the proponent must provide a foundation showing that the document is what it purports to be. In this case, because the police report was not authored by C.F., the defense could not simply read it into the record without proper authentication. The court correctly maintained that while a witness can be impeached by their own prior inconsistent statements, the documents containing those statements must still be authenticated before they can be admitted as evidence.

## The Harmless Error Doctrine: The Threshold for Reversing a Conviction based on Trial Errors

Finally, the court addressed a fundamental principle of appellate review: the Harmless Error Doctrine. Even if a trial court makes an error in admitting or excluding evidence, an appellate court will not automatically overturn a conviction. Instead, the conviction is only reversed if the error was "prejudicial"—meaning the error was significant enough that it "may have substantially swayed" the jury’s verdict.

In *Cutbank*, the court applied this standard to all the evidentiary issues raised by the defendant. The court noted that the government’s evidence against Cutbank was "substantial," including both physical evidence (the gun with matching DNA) and multiple eyewitness accounts. Because the evidence was so overwhelming, the court concluded that any minor evidentiary errors—even if they were technically incorrect—did not influence the final verdict or, at most, had only a "slight influence." Consequently, the conviction remained valid despite the procedural and evidentiary challenges.
