Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### How does the 'substantial prejudice' standard for harmless error work in practice?
Question:
The court mentions that they won't reverse a conviction for an erroneous ruling if the error was 'harmless' and only reverse if the jury may have been 'substantially swayed.' I'm confused about what 'substantially swayed' actually looks like. If there is some evidence of a mistake, but the government also has DNA evidence and multiple witnesses, does that automatically mean the error is harmless? How do courts quantify that 'swing' in the jury's mind?
Answer:
To determine whether a trial error is "harmless," the court asks an objective question: Did the error have a sufficient impact on the proceedings to have prejudiced the defendant's right to a fair trial? In practice, as shown in *United States v. Cutbank*, this standard focuses on whether the error was significant enough to have "substantially swayed" the jury's ultimate verdict.

### The Relationship Between Evidence and Prejudice
The "substantial prejudice" standard is often viewed through the lens of the strength of the underlying evidence. In *Cutbank*, the defendant pointed to several evidentiary and procedural errors (such as the admission of propensity evidence and certain testimonies). However, the court refused to overturn the conviction because the government’s primary evidence was "substantial."

To answer your specific question: Yes, if the government possesses overwhelming evidence—such as the DNA evidence, multiple witnesses, and the physical evidence (the rifle and knife) described in *Cutbank*—a minor evidentiary error is much less likely to be considered "prejudicial." The court’s reasoning is that if the "substantial" evidence alone is enough to support a conviction, then a minor mistake that "did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict" cannot be the reason for the conviction.

### Quantifying the "Swing"
Courts do not typically provide a mathematical formula for a "swing," but they do perform a balancing test. They look at the weight of the improperly admitted evidence compared to the weight of the properly admitted evidence. 

In *Cutbank*, the court looked at the following factors to conclude the error was harmless:
1.  **The Totality of the Evidence:** The court noted that the government's evidence was both "testimonial and physical." Because the weight of this evidence was so heavy, it provided a "buffer" against minor errors.
2.  **The Magnitude of the Error:** The court examined the specific rulings (regarding Rule 404(b), hearsay, and the Confrontation Clause) and determined that despite the errors, the jury's final decision was supported by the core facts of the case.
3.  **Cumulative Effect:** The court also emphasized that even when there are multiple errors, the conviction will stand unless there is "substantial prejudice" resulting from the cumulative effect of those errors.

In summary, "substantially swayed" means that the error must be more than just a mistake; it must be a mistake significant enough that it could have tipped the scales of justice. If the government’s case is so robust that the error would not have changed the jury's mind from "not guilty" to "guilty," the error is deemed harmless.
