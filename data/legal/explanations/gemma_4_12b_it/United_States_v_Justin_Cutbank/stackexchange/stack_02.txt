Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### Why is testimony about prior possession of a gun considered 'intrinsic evidence' rather than 'propensity evidence' under Rule 404(b)?
Question:
The defendant argued that the witnesses testifying about seeing him with the gun in the past was propensity evidence (i.e., 'he had a gun before, so he is the type of person who would have a gun now'). However, the court ruled it was 'intrinsic evidence' relevant to proving current possession. Can you explain the distinction? Why does 'ownership or control' of a specific item bypass the usual 404(b) restrictions on character evidence?
Answer:
The distinction between "propensity" evidence and "intrinsic" evidence (in the context of ownership) centers on the **purpose** for which the evidence is being offered to the jury.

### Propensity Evidence vs. Rule 404(b)
Under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b), evidence of a defendant's past acts is generally inadmissible to prove "propensity"—that is, the idea that because a person acted a certain way in the past, they have a character trait that makes them likely to act that way again. If the testimony were admitted as propensity evidence, the jury would be asked to conclude: *"Because Cutbank owned a gun before, he is the type of person who would own a gun now."* This is exactly what Rule 404(b) seeks to prevent, as it invites the jury to convict based on character rather than the specific facts of the current charge.

### Intrinsic Evidence and "Ownership or Control"
The court in *United States v. Cutbank* distinguished this by classifying the testimony as "intrinsic evidence" relevant to the specific elements of the crime. The charge in this case was "felon in possession of a firearm." To secure a conviction, the government must prove that the defendant possessed the firearm at the time of the offense.

The court held that testimony regarding the defendant's prior possession of the **specific** weapon was admissible because it directly addresses the element of **ownership or control**. The reasoning is as follows:

1.  **Direct Relevance to Possession:** The court noted that "prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon." This is not a comment on the defendant's character; it is a factual statement about the history of the physical object in question.
2.  **Continuous Possession:** By testifying that they saw Cutbank with the specific sawed-off rifle "a handful of times" before the incident, the witnesses were providing evidence of "continuous possession." This helps the jury determine whether the gun found by police was the same one Cutbank owned previously, rather than a gun he might have acquired or been handed moments before the arrest.
3.  **Fact vs. Character:** Ownership is a matter of fact. If a witness identifies a specific object as belonging to the defendant in the past, that evidence helps establish the "nexus" between the defendant and the evidence. It moves the needle from *"What kind of person is he?"* (propensity) to *"Who did this specific gun belong to?"* (ownership).

### Summary of the Distinction
The reason "ownership or control" bypasses the 404(b) restriction is that it serves a purpose other than proving character. In *Cutbank*, the testimony was used to establish a fact essential to the government's burden of proof: that the defendant had a history of possessing that specific firearm. Because the evidence was offered to prove a specific fact (ownership/control) rather than a general trait (propensity), it was deemed admissible.
