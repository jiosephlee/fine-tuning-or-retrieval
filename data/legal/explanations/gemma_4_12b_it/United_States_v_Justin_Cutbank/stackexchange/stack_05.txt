Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### How can a sentence be 'within the guidelines' but still be argued as violating the 'sufficient, but not greater than necessary' principle of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)?
Question:
The defendant argues that the 4-level enhancement makes his sentence 'greater than necessary' under § 3553(a), even though the final sentence fell within the resulting guideline range. If the Guidelines are designed to be the starting point for a 'sufficient' sentence, how can a sentence stay inside those bounds but still be 'too much'? Is this a procedural argument about the calculation of the range, or a substantive argument about the judge's discretion?
Answer:
To address your question, it is helpful to distinguish between the **procedural** requirement of calculating the Guidelines range and the **substantive** requirement of balancing the § 3553(a) factors. 

The argument that a sentence is "greater than necessary" while still falling within the Guidelines range is a **substantive argument** regarding the appropriateness of the Guidelines calculation itself, rather than a procedural error in arithmetic.

### The Two-Step Sentencing Process
As noted in *United States v. Cutbank*, federal sentencing follows a specific sequence:

1.  **Procedural Calculation:** The court must first "correctly calculate the applicable Guidelines range." *Gall v. United States*, 552 U.S. 38, 49 (2007). This is a procedural prerequisite. If the court miscalculates the range (e.g., uses the wrong base level or fails to apply a required enhancement), the sentence may be vacated regardless of its reasonableness.
2.  **Substantive Reasonableness:** Once the range is correctly established, the court then considers whether the § 3553(a) factors "warrant a departure or variance from the advisory guidelines range sentence." *Rosales-Mireles v. United States*, 585 U.S. 129, 143-44 (2018).

### How a "Within-Range" Sentence Can Be "Too Much"
The defendant's argument in *Cutbank* centers on the idea that the **four-level enhancement** used to determine the range was substantively improper. Even if the math used to reach the 292–365 month range was "correct" (procedurally sound), the defendant argues that the resulting range is so high that it violates the "sufficient, but not greater than necessary" mandate of § 3553(a).

In essence, the defendant is arguing that the Guidelines range itself—created by the inclusion of that specific 4-level enhancement—is substantively disproportionate to the offense. 

### The Court’s Resolution
The court in *Cutbank* addresses this by relying on a presumption of reasonableness. The court explains that:
*   If a district court imposes a sentence **within** the correctly calculated guidelines range, that sentence is "presumed reasonable on appeal." *United States v. Goodhouse*, 81 F.4th 786, 793 (8th Cir. 2023).
*   Because the district court correctly calculated the range and chose a sentence within it, the appellate court presumes the district court "considered and rejected" the defendant’s arguments regarding whether the enhancement was "greater than necessary." *United States v. Masood*, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025).

Therefore, while a defendant can theoretically argue that a Guidelines calculation is substantively "too much," it is a difficult argument to win on appeal if the court performed the procedural calculation correctly. To succeed, the defendant would generally have to show that the district court failed to actually consider the § 3553(a) factors at all, rather than simply disagreeing with the judge's ultimate balancing of those factors.
