Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Case Background and Procedural History

## The November 2020 incident: Domestic violence and firearm possession

The facts of *United States v. Justin Cutbank* center on a violent domestic incident that occurred during the night of November 19-20, 2020. According to the government’s evidence, Cutbank—who had been consuming alcohol and methamphetamine—subjected his girlfriend, D.F., to a harrowing ordeal. Cutbank allegedly pointed a firearm at D.F.’s face, accused her of being monitored by "listening devices," and forced her into a closet. He held her there for several hours at gunpoint, during which time he threatened her life and struck her in the face with the weapon. 

The violence extended to other occupants of the home. Cutbank ordered D.F. to wake up her two teenage sons and a housemate, C.F. After this interaction, Cutbank fled the residence, taking D.F.’s cell phone and the firearm with him. This sequence of events established the primary basis for the criminal charges, highlighting a pattern of domestic abuse and the immediate presence of a firearm during the commission of these acts.

## Police intervention and the recovery of the sawed-off Marlin .22 rifle

Following Cutbank's flight from D.F.’s home, police were alerted to a man barricaded in a garage at a residence approximately half a mile away. During the subsequent standoff, Cutbank behaved erratically, claimed to possess a weapon, and resisted officers' attempts to detain him by biting an officer in the face and neck. 

During the police investigation, officers searched the area between the two residences. They recovered a butcher-style knife, D.F.’s cell phone, and a Marlin .22 caliber rifle with a sawed-off barrel and stock. Forensic evidence, specifically DNA on the rifle, linked the weapon to Cutbank. Furthermore, both D.F. and C.F. identified the sawed-off rifle as a weapon they had observed in Cutbank’s possession on previous occasions. These findings provided the physical and testimonial evidence necessary to support a charge of firearm possession.

## Criminal history and the specific charges under 18 U.S.C. §§ 922(g)(1) and 924(e)

The government charged Cutbank with being a felon in possession of a firearm, a violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Because Cutbank had a significant criminal history, the government also sought an enhanced penalty under 18 U.S.C. § 924(e). This specific statute applies to defendants who are convicted of a crime of possession of a firearm and have been previously convicted of at least three violent felonies. 

The interplay between Cutbank’s prior record and his current conduct is central to the sentencing issues in this case. The prosecution’s goal was to move beyond a standard possession charge to trigger the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), which imposes significant mandatory minimums and sentencing enhancements based on a defendant's history of violent and/or firearm-related offenses.

## The jury's verdict and the district court's sentencing decision

A jury ultimately convicted Cutbank of the charged felon in possession offense and found that he had three or more prior violent felony convictions, thereby triggering the enhanced penalties of § 924(e). Following the conviction, the district court calculated the advisory guidelines for sentencing. 

The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) recommended a high offense level (35) due to several factors: the sawed-off nature of the firearm, Cutbank’s prior convictions, the obliterated serial number on the gun, and the fact that he recklessly created a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing police. Combined with his high Criminal History Category (VI), the court arrived at an advisory guidelines range of 292 to 365 months. The district court sentenced Cutbank to 292 months, a term that fell within the calculated advisory range.

## The grounds for appeal: Evidentiary errors and sentencing enhancements

Cutbank appealed both his conviction and his sentence, raising two primary categories of error. First, he argued that the district court committed multiple evidentiary and procedural errors during the trial. Specifically, he challenged the admission of testimony regarding his prior possession of the rifle (arguing it was improper propensity evidence), the handling of hearsay and Confrontation Clause issues regarding D.F.'s statements to police, and the admission of testimony regarding a family member's death. 

Second, Cutbank challenged the sentencing phase, specifically the application of the USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement. This enhancement adds four levels to the offense level when a firearm is used or possessed in connection with another felony. Cutbank contended that applying this enhancement resulted in a sentence that violated the principles of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a), which requires that a sentence be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary." These arguments form the basis of the appellate review.

# Trial Evidentiary Issues and the Harmless Error Doctrine

In reviewing a criminal conviction on appeal, a primary task for the court is to determine whether the trial judge committed any "abuse of discretion" regarding the admission or exclusion of evidence. In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals addressed several such objections, analyzing how specific rules of evidence—such as those governing propensity, hearsay, and prejudice—apply to the facts of a firearms possession case.

## Rule 404(b) and Intrinsic Evidence: Proving Possession through Prior Ownership

A common defense strategy is to object to evidence that suggests a defendant has a history of similar conduct, arguing that such evidence is "propensity" evidence. Under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b), evidence of a crime, wrong, or other act is generally not admissible to prove that a person has a character trait (such as being a "criminal") that would make them likely to have committed the crime in question. 

However, the court in *Cutbank* distinguished between propensity and "intrinsic" evidence. The government sought to introduce testimony from witnesses who had seen Cutbank in possession of the specific Marlin .22 caliber rifle prior to the night of the incident. The court held that this was not improper propensity evidence. Instead, it was intrinsic evidence relevant to the charge of possession. Because the prosecution had to prove that Cutbank possessed the firearm during the crime, showing that he owned or had control over that specific weapon "a handful of times" before the incident directly supports the element of possession. The court clarified that prior possession of a specific item helps establish ownership or continuous control, which is a necessary factual component of the offense.

## Hearsay and the Confrontation Clause: Requirements for Declarant Testimony

The defense also challenged the admission of statements made by the victim, D.F., to the police. Generally, out-of-court statements offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted are considered hearsay and are inadmissible. Furthermore, the Sixth Amendment Confrontation Clause requires that a defendant have the right to confront the witnesses against them. 

Cutbank argued that the court should have issued a limiting instruction preventing the jury from considering D.F.’s prior statements to police as substantive evidence until she testified in person. The court declined to find an abuse of discretion in the district court’s refusal to give this specific instruction. The court reasoned that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the hearsay declarant actually appears in court and testifies. Because D.F. did testify during the trial, the constitutional requirement was met. Additionally, the court noted that it is not required to provide a specific limiting instruction for every item of evidence at the moment of admission; the court may instead provide a general instruction at the close of the trial to clarify how the jury should weigh various pieces of evidence.

## Rule 403 Analysis: Balancing Probative Value against Unfair Prejudice in Cross-Examination

During the trial, the victim testified that her sister had been killed by a gun. This testimony came up during cross-examination when she was being questioned about inconsistent prior statements regarding her familiarity with firearms. The defense moved to strike this testimony, likely arguing that it was unfairly prejudicial to the defendant's case.

To evaluate such a motion, courts apply Federal Rule of Evidence 403, which allows the exclusion of relevant evidence if its "probative value" (its ability to prove a fact) is substantially outweighed by the danger of "unfair prejudice," confusion of the issues, or misleading the jury. In *Cutbank*, the court upheld the district court’s decision to admit the testimony. The court reasoned that the statement was a direct response to a question about the witness's inconsistent statements. It served to explain the witness’s state of mind and her reasons for her testimony. Because the testimony was a response to a specific line of questioning and did not appear to cause unfair prejudice or confuse the jury, it was properly admitted.

## Authentication Requirements: Admissibility of Extrinsic Evidence for Impeachment

The defense attempted to impeach a government witness, C.F., by seeking to have portions of a police report read into the record. The report contained statements C.F. had made to an investigator. While the government allowed the defense to ask C.F. questions about what she told the police, it objected to the defense actually reading the report into evidence.

The court's ruling here highlights the requirement for "authentication." To introduce a document (extrinsic evidence) into a trial, the proponent must provide a foundation showing that the document is what it purports to be. In this case, because the police report was not authored by C.F., the defense could not simply read it into the record without proper authentication. The court correctly maintained that while a witness can be impeached by their own prior inconsistent statements, the documents containing those statements must still be authenticated before they can be admitted as evidence.

## The Harmless Error Doctrine: The Threshold for Reversing a Conviction based on Trial Errors

Finally, the court addressed a fundamental principle of appellate review: the Harmless Error Doctrine. Even if a trial court makes an error in admitting or excluding evidence, an appellate court will not automatically overturn a conviction. Instead, the conviction is only reversed if the error was "prejudicial"—meaning the error was significant enough that it "may have substantially swayed" the jury’s verdict.

In *Cutbank*, the court applied this standard to all the evidentiary issues raised by the defendant. The court noted that the government’s evidence against Cutbank was "substantial," including both physical evidence (the gun with matching DNA) and multiple eyewitness accounts. Because the evidence was so overwhelming, the court concluded that any minor evidentiary errors—even if they were technically incorrect—did not influence the final verdict or, at most, had only a "slight influence." Consequently, the conviction remained valid despite the procedural and evidentiary challenges.

# Sentencing Guidelines and Statutory Enhancements

To understand the sentencing of a federal defendant, one must look beyond the crime itself and examine the mathematical and statutory framework of the United States Sentencing Guidelines (USSG). In *United States v. Cutbank*, the court’s analysis of the defendant's sentence serves as a masterclass in how specific statutory triggers and guideline enhancements interact to produce a final advisory range.

## The Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) and its Application to Repeat Violent Offenders

The Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) is a federal statute that imposes significantly harsher penalties on individuals who commit new firearm offenses after having been convicted of certain "prior violent felonies." In Cutbank’s case, the jury found that he had been previously convicted of at least three violent felonies. This finding triggered the enhanced penalty provisions of 18 U.S.C. § 924(e).

When the ACCA is triggered, it dictates a specific sentencing guideline. For Cutbank, the ACCA provided a guideline that would have resulted in a total offense level of 34. The ACCA serves as a "floor" or a baseline for repeat offenders, ensuring that those with extensive criminal histories receive sentences that reflect the gravity of their recidivism. However, as the court demonstrates, the ACCA guideline is not always the final word on the offense level; it is one component of a larger calculation.

## USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B): The 'Connection to Another Felony' Enhancement

While the ACCA looks at the defendant's past, other sentencing enhancements look at the specific circumstances of the current crime. One such enhancement is USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), which applies if a defendant used or possessed a firearm in connection with another felony offense. 

This specific enhancement is powerful because of its broad scope. The court notes that the enhancement applies regardless of whether a criminal charge was actually brought or a conviction was obtained for that underlying felony. Furthermore, the "connection" can be to a federal, state, or local felony offense. In Cutbank's case, the district court adopted the factual findings in the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) showing that he possessed the sawed-off rifle in connection with another felony. This triggered a four-level increase to his offense level.

## Calculating the Guidelines Range: Offense Levels, Criminal History, and Specific Adjustments

Federal sentencing begins with the calculation of the "Offense Level," which is then combined with a "Criminal History Category" to determine an advisory range. The PSR for Cutbank provided a step-by-step accounting of these levels:

1.  **Base Offense Level:** The starting point was 26, based on the possession of a sawed-off firearm and the defendant's prior violent crimes.
2.  **Specific Adjustments:** The court added a three-level increase for an obliterated serial number, a two-level increase for recklessly creating a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing, and the four-level increase for the connection to another felony.
3.  **Total Offense Level:** These adjustments resulted in a total offense level of 35.
4.  **Criminal History Category:** Based on Cutbank's extensive record, he was placed in Category VI.

By plugging an offense level of 35 and a Category VI into the Guidelines table, the court arrived at an advisory range of 292 to 365 months.

## USSG § 4B1.4(b): Determining Which Offense Level Applies When Multiple Guidelines are Triggered

A critical procedural point in *Cutbank* involves how a court handles situations where two different guidelines yield two different offense levels. In this case, the ACCA produced an offense level of 34, while the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement resulted in an offense level of 35.

The court resolved this conflict by applying USSG § 4B1.4(b). This rule stipulates that when multiple guidelines apply, the court must use the greater offense level. Therefore, the court correctly moved from the ACCA-based level of 34 to the higher level of 35. This illustrates a key principle of federal sentencing: specific enhancements for the underlying conduct of the current crime can "stack" or supersede broader statutory categories like the ACCA if they result in a higher offense level.

## The Distinction Between Federal, State, and Local Felony Connections

Finally, it is important to note the jurisdictional breadth of the "connection to another felony" enhancement. Cutbank’s defense did not successfully argue that the court was limited only to federal felonies. Because USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) explicitly includes state and local felonies, the government can successfully argue for the four-level enhancement even if the underlying felony was a state-level crime that never reached a federal court. This broad definition ensures that the Guidelines account for a defendant's total conduct, regardless of which jurisdiction’s laws were violated during the commission of the underlying felony.

# Standards of Review and Sentencing Reasonableness

## Standard of Review: Procedural Correctness versus Substantive Reasonableness

When an appellate court reviews a criminal sentence, it does not simply ask if the sentence feels "fair." Instead, the court follows a structured two-step inquiry. The first step is a procedural check: the court must ensure the district court committed no significant procedural errors, such as failing to calculate the Sentencing Guidelines correctly or improperly calculating the Guidelines range. As established in *Rosales-Mireles v. United States*, this procedural correctness is a prerequisite before the court can move to the next step.

Once the court determines that the Guidelines were calculated correctly, it moves to the second step: evaluating the "substantive reasonableness" of the sentence. At this stage, the court examines whether the sentence imposed by the district court is appropriate under the broader statutory requirements of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit first confirmed that the district court properly adopted the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) and correctly applied the necessary sentencing enhancements to arrive at the advisory guidelines range.

## Presumption of Reasonableness: Sentences Falling Within the Advisory Guidelines Range

A key principle in federal sentencing is the "presumption of reasonableness." Because the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines are advisory rather than mandatory, the district court has discretion to deviate from them. However, if a district court imposes a sentence that falls within the advisory guidelines range, the appellate court generally presumes that the sentence is reasonable. 

In the case of Cutbank, the district court determined an advisory guidelines range of 292 to 365 months based on his extensive criminal history and the specific nature of his offense. Because the district court sentenced Cutbank to 292 months—the bottom of that range—the Eighth Circuit applied this presumption. The court noted that because the sentence was within the range, it is presumed reasonable on appeal unless the defendant can show a significant error in the underlying calculation or a total lack of consideration for the necessary sentencing factors.

## Evaluating § 3553(a) Factors: The Court's Duty to Consider vs. Mechanically Recite Factors

Even when a sentence falls within the guidelines range, the district court is still required to consider the factors set forth in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). These factors include the nature and circumstances of the offense, the history and characteristics of the defendant, and the need to avoid unwarranted sentence disparities. However, the law does not require the district court to provide a "mechanical recitation" of every single factor in its written order.

The Eighth Circuit clarifies that to uphold a sentence, it must simply be clear from the record that the district court actually *considered* these factors. In *Cutbank*, the court observed that the district court had reviewed the PSR and the sentencing memoranda, and was aware of Cutbank’s specific arguments for a lower sentence. By acknowledging these arguments and still selecting a sentence within the guidelines range, the district court demonstrated that it had weighed the § 3553(a) factors and reached a reasoned conclusion.

## Substantial Sentencing Discretion: The Court's Role in Balancing the Defendant's History and the Nature of the Offense

The ultimate goal of the sentencing process is to balance the defendant's individual circumstances against the gravity of their crimes. The court acknowledges that district courts possess "substantial sentencing discretion" to make this balance. In reviewing Cutbank's sentence, the Eighth Circuit looked at the severity of his actions: he was involved in an incident of domestic abuse, kidnapping, and assault in a home with children, and he injured a police officer while resisting arrest.

The court also weighed his history, noting that Cutbank had nearly a dozen prior felony convictions, many of which involved violence and firearms. By looking at both the brutal nature of the offense and the defendant's extensive history of violence, the appellate court concluded that the district court did not abuse its discretion. The sentence of 292 months was seen as a proportionate response to the facts of the case, even if it sat at the lower end of the calculated guidelines range.

## Final Disposition

Because the district court correctly calculated the guidelines range, the sentence fell within that range (triggering the presumption of reasonableness), and the record showed the court considered the necessary § 3553(a) factors, the Eighth Circuit found no reason to interfere. The court affirmed the judgment of the district court, upholding Cutbank's 292-month sentence.
