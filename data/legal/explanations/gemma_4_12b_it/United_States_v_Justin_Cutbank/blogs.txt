Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Ownership is Not Propensity: Navigating the Rule 404(b) Boundaries in Firearm Possession Cases

In the crucible of a criminal trial, the distinction between a defendant’s "character" and the "facts" of the crime can often blur. Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b) serves as the gatekeeper in this arena, prohibiting the introduction of evidence of a defendant's prior crimes or acts to prove a "propensity" to commit crimes. The logic is simple: a jury should not convict a man because he is a "bad person," but rather because he committed the specific act in question. However, as the Eighth Circuit recently clarified in *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the rule contains a significant carve-out for "intrinsic evidence"—facts that are relevant to the specific elements of the crime, such as ownership or control, rather than the defendant’s general moral fiber.

In *Cutbank*, the government sought to introduce testimony from the defendant’s girlfriend and housemate regarding the fact that they had seen Cutbank in possession of a specific sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle on several occasions prior to the night of the incident. Cutbank challenged this, arguing that such testimony constituted prohibited propensity evidence. His argument was that by showing he possessed the gun in the past, the prosecution was implicitly suggesting he had a propensity for firearm-related crimes. The Eighth Circuit rejected this framing, siding with the district court’s determination that the evidence was "intrinsic" to the charge of felon in possession of a firearm.

The court’s reasoning rests on the practical necessity of establishing ownership. To convict a defendant of possession under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1), the government must prove the defendant had the ability to control the firearm. By admitting testimony that the defendant had owned the weapon "a handful of times" before the incident, the court allowed the government to build a bridge from the discovery of the weapon by police to the defendant’s continuous control over it. As the court noted, citing *United States v. Battle*, prior possession is directly relevant to proving later possession because it establishes a history of ownership. This distinction is vital: the evidence does not say "he is a man who likes guns"; it says "this is his gun."

From a prosecutorial standpoint, this ruling provides a powerful tool for constructing a "narrative of continuity." In many weapons cases, the defense may attempt to argue that a firearm was "found" on a defendant by police or was planted, or that the defendant had no knowledge of the weapon's presence. By allowing "intrinsic" evidence of prior ownership, the government can effectively neutralize these defenses. It transforms the weapon from a piece of evidence found at a static scene into a persistent attribute of the defendant’s lifestyle. This narrative of continuity makes it significantly harder for a jury to view the possession as an isolated, accidental, or coerced event.

Doctrinally, the *Cutbank* decision reinforces a fact-specific approach to Rule 404(b). It suggests that the "propensity" prohibition is not a blanket ban on a defendant’s history, but a specific prohibition against using that history to fill in the blanks of the current crime. If the prior act is a "stepping stone" to proving an essential element of the offense—such as the identity of the weapon or the defendant's access to it—it remains admissible. This creates a subtle but important boundary: the evidence must be tied to the *object* of the crime (the rifle) rather than the *nature* of the defendant’s behavior.

While the defense may argue that this still invites a "bad man" inference, the court’s reliance on the "harmless error" standard provides a safety valve. Because the government also possessed physical evidence (the gun with Cutbank’s DNA) and corroborating testimony from multiple witnesses, any potential prejudice from the "intrinsic" evidence was deemed insufficient to sway the verdict. Moving forward, *Cutbank* serves as a reminder that in the eyes of the court, establishing the "who" and "how" of ownership can often override the "why" of a defendant's criminal history.



# When Guidelines Clash: The 'Greater Offense Level' Rule and the ACCA

Sentencing a career criminal in the federal system is often less about the specific facts of a single afternoon and more about a complex, mathematical interplay between different statutory "pathways." In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit offers a masterclass in the technical mechanics of federal sentencing, specifically how the court handles "stacking" enhancements when a defendant triggers both the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) and the standard firearm possession enhancements under the United States Sentencing Guidelines (USSG).

The facts of the case are stark. Justin Cutbank, a felon with an extensive violent history, held a woman at gunpoint in her own home, eventually barricading himself in a garage and resisting police with a sawed-off rifle. While the conduct was egregious, the legal significance of the case lies in the Eighth Circuit’s treatment of the "greater offense level" rule. The court had to reconcile two different ways of calculating Cutbank's sentencing range: the standard calculation for a felon in possession of a firearm and the specialized calculation mandated by the ACCA.

To understand the court’s reasoning, one must look at the "math" of the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR). Under the standard guidelines, the court started with a base offense level of 26 (reflecting the sawed-off weapon and Cutbank's prior crimes of violence). From there, the court added several "stackable" enhancements: three levels for an obliterated serial number, two levels for creating a risk of death during his flight from police, and a significant four-level increase for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony offense under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). This brought the total offense level to 35.

However, because Cutbank had three or more prior violent felonies, he also triggered the ACCA. The ACCA provides its own set of rules for calculating an offense level, which in this instance would have resulted in a total offense level of 34. This creates a potential conflict: which number does the court use to determine the sentencing range? 

The Eighth Circuit’s resolution of this conflict relies on USSG § 4B1.4(b), the "greater offense level" rule. The guidelines dictate that when a defendant is eligible for multiple sentencing guidelines—such as the standard possession guidelines and the ACCA—the court must apply the guideline that produces the greater offense level. By affirming the 292-month sentence (based on the level 35 calculation), the court confirmed that the standard enhancements—specifically the "connection to another felony" increase—can effectively "out-compete" the ACCA’s own calculation.

This is a critical doctrinal point for federal prosecutors and defense counsel alike. The § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement is particularly potent because it applies "regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained." This allows the government to reach back into a defendant's history to find a "connection" to another felony, thereby pushing the offense level higher than what the ACCA might otherwise dictate. 

The downstream effect of this reasoning is a reinforcement of the "maximum exposure" principle in federal sentencing. It ensures that a defendant’s sentence is not capped by the ACCA’s specific calculation if other, independent guidelines provide a higher ceiling. For career criminals, this means that the "stacking" of secondary enhancements—like the risk of death or the connection to other crimes—can be just as influential as the primary statutory triggers. By upholding this calculation, the Eighth Circuit underscored that the federal sentencing system is designed to be cumulative; when different rules provide different paths to a sentence, the law defaults to the path of greatest severity.



# Contextualizing Hearsay: Judicial Discretion and the Confrontation Clause in Violent Crime Trials

In the high-stakes theater of a violent crime prosecution, the "why" of a police officer's actions is often as important to the jury as the "what." To explain why an officer rushed into a building, brandished a weapon, or took a specific tactical risk, the government frequently seeks to introduce the victim’s initial statements to the police. However, these statements sit at the crossroads of two competing legal interests: the prosecution’s need to provide narrative context and the defendant’s right to a trial free from the "taint" of out-of-court hearsay.

In *United States v. Justin Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit addressed this friction, ultimately favoring a pragmatic approach to judicial discretion that prioritizes the government's ability to frame the narrative, provided the structural requirements of the Confrontation Clause are met.

## The Narrative of Context

The facts of *Cutbank* underscore why contextual evidence matters. Following a violent domestic incident involving a firearm, the defendant barricaded himself in a garage, resisted arrest, and bit an officer. To explain the police's response and the urgency of their actions, the government sought to admit statements made by the victim, D.F., to the police. 

The defendant moved for a limiting instruction, arguing that these out-of-court statements should not be considered substantive evidence of his actions until D.F. took the stand. Essentially, the defense wanted to prevent the jury from "pre-loading" their understanding of the crime based on hearsay before the victim had a chance to provide her own live testimony. The government countered that these statements were merely "context" for the officers' knowledge and actions at the time of their arrival.

## Deference to Judicial Discretion

The Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s refusal to grant the limiting instruction. The court’s reasoning rests on two primary pillars: the satisfaction of the Confrontation Clause and the flexibility of trial procedure.

First, the court noted that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the declarant—in this case, D.F.—actually appears in court to testify. Because she did testify, the constitutional hurdle was cleared. Second, the court emphasized that a trial judge is not required to provide a "prophylactic" limiting instruction at the moment an item of evidence is admitted. Instead, the court pointed to the established principle that a judge may provide an appropriate instruction at the close of the trial to clarify the weight or scope of the evidence.

By upholding this approach, the Eighth Circuit signaled a preference for judicial economy and narrative flow. The court essentially ruled that if a judge believes they can successfully "correct" the record with a final instruction, they are not obligated to interrupt the flow of the prosecution's case with a preliminary limiting instruction.

## Analytical Perspective: The Risk of "Narrative Bleed"

From an analytical standpoint, the court’s reasoning reflects a pro-prosecutorial tilt toward narrative coherence. While technically sound under existing precedents, this approach risks what might be called "narrative bleed." 

When a jury hears a victim's frantic out-of-court statement to a 911 operator or a first responder, that statement creates an immediate, visceral impression. Even if a judge later instructs the jury to only consider that statement as "context" for the officers' behavior, the psychological impact of the hearsay often lingers. The defense's argument—that the jury should not be allowed to hear that "context" until the victim is under cross-examination—is an attempt to preserve the integrity of the evidence's "substantive" weight. By allowing the context to enter the record early, the court permits the government to shape the jury's emotional and cognitive framework before the defense has a chance to challenge the declarant’s credibility.

## Downstream Implications

The *Cutbank* decision reinforces a standard of "harmless error" and broad judicial discretion that will likely persist in federal criminal trials. It tells defense counsel that unless they can demonstrate a specific, substantial prejudice or a clear violation of the Confrontation Clause, the court will likely defer to the government’s desire to present a complete story.

Furthermore, this underscores the importance of the "context" exception in modern evidence law. As police work becomes increasingly complex, the government will continue to rely on these contextual "bridges" to explain tactical decisions. The Eighth Circuit’s stance ensures that as long as the declarant eventually takes the stand, the government can use their prior statements as a narrative scaffold, even if that scaffold risks influencing the jury’s perception of the facts before the defense can intervene.



# The Shield of Substantial Evidence: The High Bar for Overturning Evidentiary Errors

In the American criminal justice system, there is a persistent tension between the pursuit of a "perfect" trial and the practical reality of human error. Appellate courts are tasked with policing these errors, but they are not tasked with overturning a conviction simply because a judge made a suboptimal ruling on a minor point of evidence. The Eighth Circuit’s recent decision in *United States v. Justin Cutbank* serves as a textbook illustration of the "harmless error" doctrine—a judicial philosophy that prioritizes the weight of the overall evidence over the technical perfection of the proceedings.

The case against Cutbank was factually heavy. A felon with a significant criminal history, Cutbank was accused of holding his girlfriend at gunpoint, kidnapping her and her children, and subsequently resisting arrest by biting a police officer. The government’s case was anchored by a "smoking gun" in the literal sense: a sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle with a sawed-off barrel and stock, which bore Cutbank’s DNA and was identified by witnesses as a weapon he had possessed previously. Because of the gravity of the crimes and Cutbank's prior record, he was sentenced under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), resulting in a nearly 292-month prison term.

On appeal, Cutbank attempted to dismantle the conviction by constructing a cumulative narrative of procedural and evidentiary failures. He challenged the admission of "propensity" evidence regarding his prior ownership of the rifle, the lack of specific limiting instructions regarding hearsay, the admission of testimony about a family member's death, and the authentication of police reports used for impeachment. While some of these points involve nuanced questions of the Federal Rules of Evidence, the Eighth Circuit’s treatment of them reveals a clear judicial priority: the "substantial prejudice" standard.

The court’s reasoning follows a two-step logic. First, it addressed the specific evidentiary rulings, largely upholding the district court’s discretion. For example, the court reaffirmed that prior possession of a firearm is "intrinsic evidence" relevant to proving current possession—it isn't merely showing a "propensity" for crime, but establishing the fact of ownership. When the court does find a potential error—such as the lack of a specific limiting instruction or a complex impeachment issue—it invokes the *DeMarce* and *Keys* standards. Under these precedents, a conviction is only reversed if the improperly admitted evidence was so prejudicial that it likely swayed the jury to a verdict they otherwise would not have reached.

This is where the "shield of substantial evidence" comes into play. The Eighth Circuit essentially argued that because the government’s evidence was so overwhelming—comprising both DNA evidence and consistent testimonial accounts from multiple witnesses—any individual procedural hiccups became "harmless." In the eyes of the court, the "haystack" of evidence was so large that a few "needles" of trial error could not possibly have moved the needle of the jury's ultimate decision. 

The doctrinal implication here is significant for defense strategy and appellate practice. It underscores the reality that in cases involving high-quality physical evidence (like DNA) and multiple corroborating witnesses, the threshold for reversal is extraordinarily high. To win on appeal in such a context, a defendant cannot simply point to a series of "incorrect" rulings; they must prove that those errors were of such magnitude that they poisoned the entire well of the trial.

Furthermore, the court’s treatment of the sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) reinforces this "totality" approach. By affirming a sentence that was within the guidelines—despite Cutbank's arguments about the "sufficiency" of the sentence—the court signaled its deference to the district court’s role in weighing the § 3553(a) factors. For the defense, *Cutbank* serves as a reminder that while the rules of evidence are the mechanics of a fair trial, the "substantial prejudice" test is the ultimate gatekeeper of appellate reversal. When the government’s facts are as stark as they were here, the gate remains firmly closed.
