Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Distinguishing 'Right-to-Control' from 'Fraudulent Inducement' in the Wake of *Ciminelli*

The landscape of federal fraud prosecutions has undergone a significant shift in the last year. With the Supreme Court’s recent decisions in *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States*, the judiciary has signaled a move toward a more formalistic, property-centric interpretation of the mail, wire, and securities fraud statutes. By narrowing the scope of what constitutes "fraud," the Court has effectively killed the "right-to-control" theory—the idea that a defendant commits fraud simply by depriving a victim of the information necessary to make a discretionary economic decision. However, as the Fifth Circuit’s recent decision in *United States v. Constantinescu* demonstrates, there remains a robust, albeit more narrowly defined, path for the government to prosecute sophisticated schemes like "pump and dump" social media frauds.

## The Death of the Right-to-Control Theory

To understand the Fifth Circuit’s reasoning, one must first look at the boundaries established in *Ciminelli*. In that case, the Supreme Court held that a fraud conviction cannot rest solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information. When the government attempts to argue that a defendant's lie caused a victim to make a poor choice with their own assets, the Court has demanded more: a traditional property interest must be at stake. Under the rejected "right-to-control" theory, the victim’s "property" was their right to control their assets based on accurate information. The Supreme Court rejected this, insisting that the federal fraud statutes are not designed to police every instance of bad information, but rather to protect against the theft or misappropriation of tangible property.

The challenge for federal prosecutors in the wake of *Ciminelli* is ensuring that indictments do not fall into this trap. If an indictment merely alleges that a defendant lied to a victim, and that lie caused the victim to make a "bad" investment, the case may be dismissed for failing to state an offense. To survive, the government must now be much more precise in describing the mechanics of the deception, moving away from general "economic harm" and toward specific "property transfers."

## Fraudulent Inducement as the Solution

The Fifth Circuit in *Constantinescu* navigated this minefield by distinguishing between the "right-to-control" theory and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. In the *Constantinescu* case, the defendants were accused of a classic social media "pump and dump": they allegedly used their large followings to post false information, inducing followers to purchase securities. The district court dismissed the indictment, arguing it only alleged a deprivation of economic information. 

The Fifth Circuit disagreed, noting that the indictment didn't just allege the defendants lied to the followers; it alleged they tricked the followers into *purchasing* the securities. This is the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. As the Court highlighted via *Kousisis*, fraudulent inducement occurs when a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract or transaction that requires the victim to hand over money or property. Unlike the right-to-control theory, this theory is squarely rooted in property. It doesn't matter if the victim's "choice" was bad; what matters is that the victim was tricked into the act of parting with their funds.

## The Irrelevance of "Net" Economic Loss

A secondary but equally important distinction made by the Fifth Circuit involves the concept of intent and harm. The defendants in *Constantinescu* argued that they didn't intend to "harm" anyone; they only intended to profit. They suggested that because the victims received securities in exchange for their money, they hadn't suffered a net pecuniary loss. 

The Fifth Circuit, leaning on *Kousisis*, dismissed this as a distinction without a difference. The Court clarified that the fraud statutes do not require the government to prove that a defendant sought to leave the victim "economically worse off." If a defendant induces a victim to part with property under false pretenses, the fraud is complete the moment that property is transferred. Even if the victim receives something of "equal value" in return, the fraudulent nature of the transaction remains. This is a critical nuance for high-frequency trading and social media schemes where the "loss" might be obscured by market fluctuations or the immediate acquisition of other assets.

## Doctrinal Implications and the Drafting Burden

The *Constantinescu* decision provides a roadmap for federal prosecutors who are still adjusting to the post-*Ciminelli* era. The takeaway is clear: the government must draft indictments that explicitly describe the "hand-off." It is no longer sufficient to allege a scheme to "deceive" or "mislead" in a general sense. Instead, the indictment must allege a specific transaction—a purchase, a sale, a contract—where the victim is induced to part with a traditional property interest.

This creates a higher hurdle for the government but provides a clearer rule of law. By forcing prosecutors to focus on the "fraudulent inducement" of property rather than the "deprivation of information," the courts are narrowing the scope of federal fraud to its traditional roots. For the securities markets, this means that while "pump and dump" schemes will still be prosecuted vigorously, the legal theory will lean less on the "bad advice" received by the public and more on the specific, fraudulent transactions that moved money from the followers' pockets into the hands of the fraudsters.
