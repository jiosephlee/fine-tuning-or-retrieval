Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Case Background: The Social Media 'Pump and Dump' Scheme

## Profiles of the defendants as social media influencers

The defendants in *United States v. Constantinescu*—Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight—were not merely casual investors; they were prominent social media influencers. According to the government's indictment, these individuals cultivated large followings across various social media platforms, where they marketed themselves as skilled stock traders. By frequently posting their trading activities and building a public persona around their investment expertise, they established a platform of influence that allowed them to reach a wide audience of potential investors.

## Mechanics of the 'pump and dump' scheme: purchasing, pumping, and dumping

The core of the government’s securities fraud allegation centers on a classic "pump and dump" scheme, adapted for the digital age. The indictment alleges that the defendants executed this scheme in three distinct phases. First, the defendants would purchase a particular security. Second, they would "pump" the price of that security by posting false and misleading information about it on social media. The goal of this deception was to induce their followers to purchase the security, which would artificially inflate the price. Finally, the defendants would "dump" the security by secretly selling their holdings at these inflated prices for a profit.

## The government's allegations: material misrepresentations and illicit profits

To support the charges of conspiracy to commit securities fraud and multiple counts of securities fraud, the government alleged that the defendants engaged in specific material misrepresentations. These included posts that misrepresented the defendants' actual trading positions and the potential future prices of securities. The indictment claims that these deceptive tactics were used to manipulate the market, ultimately allowing the defendants to profit from the scheme. The government alleges that the defendants' actions resulted in a total profit of approximately $114 million.

## The district court's dismissal based on the lack of a 'traditional property interest'

The initial procedural hurdle for the government arose when the district court dismissed the indictment. The district court’s reasoning turned on the specific nature of the harm alleged in the indictment. The court concluded that the indictment failed to state an offense because it merely alleged that the defendants sought to deprive their followers of "potentially valuable economic information" rather than a "traditional property interest." Under existing fraud jurisprudence, the mere deprivation of information—even if that information is valuable for making economic decisions—is often insufficient to sustain a fraud conviction unless it is tied to the misappropriation of tangible money or property.

## The government's appeal to the Fifth Circuit

Following the district court's dismissal, the government timely appealed the decision to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit. The government’s primary argument is that the district court misinterpreted the indictment. While the defendants argued that the indictment only alleged a deprivation of information, the government contends that the indictment actually alleges a "fraudulent-inducement theory." Under this theory, the defendants did not just withhold information; they used material misstatements to trick victims into a transaction—the purchase of securities—which required the victims to hand over their money or property. The Fifth Circuit must now determine whether the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud under these standards.

# Procedural Framework: Reviewing the Sufficiency of an Indictment

## The 'de novo' standard of review for indictments

When a district court dismisses an indictment and the government appeals that dismissal, the appellate court must determine the appropriate standard of review. In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit clarifies that it reviews the sufficiency of an indictment *de novo*. In legal procedure, a *de novo* review means the appellate court does not give any deference to the lower court’s legal conclusions. Instead, the appellate court looks at the issue with "fresh eyes," performing its own independent analysis of whether the indictment meets the legal requirements to proceed.

## The 'take as true' rule: evaluating allegations without weighing evidence

A critical component of reviewing an indictment’s sufficiency is the "take as true" rule. Because an indictment is merely a charging document—not a final judgment on the facts—the appellate court is not tasked with deciding whether the defendants actually committed the crimes. Rather, the court must accept every allegation in the indictment as factually true for the purpose of the analysis.

Under this rule, the court's only question is whether the facts, as alleged in the indictment, satisfy the legal elements of the crime. If the government alleges a set of facts that, if true, would constitute a violation of the law, the indictment is sufficient. The court does not weigh evidence, consider the credibility of witnesses, or determine the likelihood of conviction; it only determines if the government has stated a valid offense.

## The three primary requirements for a sufficient indictment

To ensure that a criminal prosecution is fair and legally sound, the court identifies three primary requirements that an indictment must satisfy to be considered sufficient. First, the indictment must enumerate each *prima facie* element of the charged offense. This means the document must explicitly state the basic facts required to prove the crime.

Second, the indictment must "fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him." This ensures the defendant knows exactly what conduct the government is accusing them of so they can prepare a defense. Third, the indictment must provide the defendant with a "double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions." This ensures that the government cannot repeatedly charge a defendant with the same conduct under different labels, protecting the defendant from being prosecuted twice for the same act.

## The requirement to allege each material element of the charged offense

The court summarizes these requirements into a core principle: to be sufficient, an indictment must allege each "material element" of the offense. A material element is a fundamental component of the crime's legal definition.

In the context of securities fraud, this means the indictment cannot simply allege a general "bad act." It must specifically allege the components that make up the fraud, such as a "scheme to defraud" and an "intent to defraud." If the indictment fails to describe the facts necessary to meet even one of these material elements, it is legally insufficient and must be dismissed. In *Constantinescu*, the focus of the review was specifically whether the indictment alleged the material elements of "scheme" and "intent" as required by the securities fraud statute.

# Statutory Framework: Elements of Securities Fraud

## Overview of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 2 (Securities Fraud)
In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the defendants were charged with securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 2. These statutes provide the federal government with the authority to prosecute individuals who engage in fraudulent schemes involving securities. In this specific case, the government alleged that the defendants participated in a “pump and dump” scheme. By using social media to post false and misleading information, the defendants allegedly induced followers to purchase securities, thereby artificially inflating prices so the defendants could sell their own holdings for a profit. To sustain a conviction under these statutes, the government must prove that the defendants engaged in a specific type of deceptive conduct aimed at obtaining a tangible benefit from others' investments.

## Parallels between securities fraud and mail/wire fraud statutes
While the Fifth Circuit noted that there is relatively "scant caselaw" specifically construing the securities fraud statute (18 U.S.C. § 1348), the court emphasized that this statute "borrows key concepts" from the more established mail and wire fraud statutes. Consequently, the court relies on the judicial treatment of mail and wire fraud to interpret the requirements of securities fraud. For a law student, this means that when analyzing a securities fraud charge, you must look beyond the specific text of the securities statute and consider the broader federal fraud framework. The core requirements derived from these parallels are the existence of a "scheme to defraud" and an "intent to defraud."

## Defining the 'scheme to defraud': material misrepresentations and obtaining value
The "scheme to defraud" element is the structural component of the offense. It requires more than a simple lie; it requires a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others for the purpose of obtaining something of value. In the context of securities fraud, the court looks for a plan where the defendant uses false pretenses to trick a victim into a transaction. In *Constantinescu*, the "scheme" was the "pump and dump" operation: the defendants allegedly misrepresented their trading positions and the potential price of securities to induce others to purchase them. The "object" of this fraud was the acquisition of money or property—specifically, the money the followers spent to buy the securities.

## Defining 'intent to defraud': the requirements of deception and resulting harm
To satisfy the "intent to defraud" element, the government must show that the defendant possessed two specific types of intent: (1) an intent to deceive, and (2) an intent to cause some harm to result from that deceit. The court clarified that "harm" in this context does not necessarily mean the victim must suffer a net pecuniary loss. Instead, the law focuses on the defendant’s objective. If the defendant’s goal was to obtain the victim’s money or property through deception, the intent to defraud is present. In this case, the defendants argued they only intended to profit, not to harm their followers. However, the court held that because the defendants' objective was to obtain money by inducing followers to part with their property, the intent to defraud was sufficiently alleged.

## The distinction between 'deception' and 'obtaining money or property' as the object of fraud
A critical distinction in this opinion is the difference between the "right-to-control" theory and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. Relying on the Supreme Court’s decision in *Ciminelli*, the court explained that federal fraud statutes do not protect "valuable economic information." Under the "right-to-control" theory, a defendant would be guilty of fraud simply for depriving a victim of information needed to make an economic decision. The Supreme Court rejected this because the right to information is not a "traditional property interest."

In contrast, *Constantinescu* applies the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. Under this theory, a defendant is liable if they use a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over money or property. This theory is distinct because it protects "money and property" rather than "information." The court held that because the indictment alleged the defendants induced followers to *purchase* securities—thereby handing over their money—the government was seeking to protect a traditional property interest. Furthermore, citing *Kousisis*, the court clarified that the government does not need to prove the victim was left "economically worse off." If the defendant fraudulently induces the deprivation of money or property, the fraud is complete, regardless of whether the victim received something of equal value in return.

# Legal Theory: Fraudulent Inducement vs. Right-to-Control

To determine whether a defendant has committed securities fraud, the court must identify the specific type of harm alleged in the indictment. In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit distinguishes between two theories of fraud: the "right-to-control" theory and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. This distinction is critical because federal fraud statutes—including the securities fraud statute (18 U.S.C. § 1348)—only protect traditional property interests. Understanding which theory the government is using determines whether the indictment is sufficient to proceed.

## The Supreme Court's decision in Ciminelli: rejecting the right-to-control theory

The distinction between these two theories was clarified by the Supreme Court in *Ciminelli v. United States*. In that case, the government attempted to prosecute a defendant for wire fraud based on the "right-to-control" theory. Under this theory, the government argues that fraud occurs when a defendant schemes to deprive a victim of "potentially valuable economic information" necessary to make discretionary economic decisions. Essentially, the government argues that the victim’s property is their "right to control" their own assets based on accurate information.

The Supreme Court rejected this theory. It held that the federal fraud statutes do not protect the right to receive accurate information or the right to make informed decisions. Because the "right to valuable economic information" is not a traditional property interest, the government cannot sustain a fraud conviction by showing that a defendant merely withheld or distorted information that would have helped a victim make a better financial choice.

## What constitutes a 'traditional property interest' in federal fraud law

The scope of federal fraud law is limited by the types of interests it is designed to protect. For a scheme to qualify as fraud under the statutes at issue in *Constantinescu*, it must involve the deprivation of a "traditional property interest." The court emphasizes that the law is not intended to criminalize the deprivation of information or the loss of potential profit.

A traditional property interest refers to tangible assets such as money, securities, real estate, or physical goods. In the context of securities fraud, the law protects the actual money or property a person holds. If a defendant’s conduct only results in a victim being "left out" of a good deal or making a bad decision because they lacked certain facts, that does not constitute a violation of the fraud statutes. The core of the inquiry is whether the defendant's scheme resulted in the victim parting with an actual asset.

## The fraudulent-inducement theory: tricking victims into contracts/transactions

While the "right-to-control" theory fails because it lacks a property interest, the "fraudulent-inducement" theory succeeds because it focuses on the transfer of property. Under this theory, a defendant commits fraud by using material misstatements or fraudulent pretenses to trick a victim into entering into a contract or transaction that requires them to hand over their money or property.

The Fifth Circuit notes that the Supreme Court has confirmed that fraudulent inducement is not just a "repackaging" of the right-to-control theory. Instead, it is a distinct doctrine. In fraudulent inducement, the "object" of the fraud is the money or property itself. A key component of this theory, as clarified in *Kousisis v. United States*, is that the government does not need to prove that the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss. Even if the victim receives something of equal value in return, the crime is complete once the defendant has successfully induced the victim to part with their property under false pretenses.

## Why the 'pump and dump' indictment constitutes fraudulent inducement

The defendants in *Constantinescu* argued that the indictment was insufficient because it only alleged they deprived their followers of valuable economic information (the "right-to-control" theory). They contended that because they were merely "pumping" the price of a stock, they were not taking property from anyone.

However, the Fifth Circuit rejected this argument by pointing to the specific language of the indictment. The government did not just allege that the defendants lied about the stock; it alleged that the defendants used those lies to *induce* their followers to *purchase* the securities. By purchasing the securities, the followers were parting with their money—a traditional property interest. This moves the conduct from the rejected "right-to-control" theory into the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. The defendants' goal of profiting from the scheme does not negate this; rather, it demonstrates that their "object" was to obtain money through the fraudulent inducement of others to part with theirs.

## Distinguishing the deprivation of information from the deprivation of property

The ultimate distinction between these two theories rests on what the victim loses. In a right-to-control scenario, the victim loses the *opportunity* to make a better decision based on accurate data; they keep their money, but they lose the "information" that would have helped them manage it. In a fraudulent-inducement scenario, the victim loses the *money or property* itself because they were tricked into a transaction.

In *Constantinescu*, the court held that the indictment properly alleged the latter. Because the "pump and dump" scheme involved inducing followers to purchase securities, the defendants were accused of a scheme to obtain money through fraudulent pretenses. This satisfies the requirement of a traditional property interest, distinguishing the case from *Ciminelli* and providing a sufficient basis for the securities fraud charges.

# The Harm Requirement: Intent and Economic Loss

In analyzing the sufficiency of a securities fraud indictment, a critical question arises regarding the "intent to defraud." The defendants in *United States v. Constantinescu* argued that because their actions did not result in a net economic loss for their followers—who were induced to purchase securities rather than being stripped of their funds entirely—the government failed to allege an intent to defraud. To resolve this, the court looked to recent Supreme Court jurisprudence to define the boundaries of what constitutes "harm" under federal fraud statutes.

## The Supreme Court's decision in Kousisis: the absence of a net loss requirement

The court’s analysis centers on the Supreme Court’s decision in *Kousisis v. United States*. In that case, the defendant obtained government contracts by making false representations about his company's ability to fulfill specific requirements. The government argued that even though the defendant might have provided something of value in return for the contracts, the act of obtaining those contracts through lies constituted fraud.

The Supreme Court agreed, establishing a vital principle for interpreting the mail, wire, and securities fraud statutes: a defendant does not need to seek to "hurt the victim’s bottom line" to be guilty of fraud. Instead, the statutes are satisfied if the defendant schemes to "obtain" the victim’s money or property. This means that the legal focus is on the *deception used to facilitate the transfer of assets*, not the final balance sheet of the victim after the transaction is complete.

## Distinguishing 'obtaining' property from 'harming' a victim's bottom line

To understand the court's reasoning, students must distinguish between the "right-to-control" theory and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. As discussed in the previous section of the opinion, the right-to-control theory (rejected in *Ciminelli*) suggests that fraud occurs when a victim is deprived of valuable economic information. Under that theory, the "property" being stolen is the victim's ability to make informed decisions.

In contrast, the "fraudulent-inducement" theory used in *Constantinescu* focuses on the actual transfer of money or property. Under this theory, the fraud is complete the moment the defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract or transaction that requires handing over assets. The court emphasizes that the law protects against the fraudulent *transfer* of property. Therefore, the government does not need to prove that the victim ended up "worse off" in a global sense; it only needs to prove that the victim was tricked into partaking in a transaction that moved their money or property into the hands of the defendant.

## The 'mirror-image' requirement: why a defendant's gain does not need to equal a victim's loss

The defendants argued that for a fraud to exist, there must be a "mirror-image" relationship between the defendant’s gain and the victim’s loss. Essentially, they suggested that if the followers received securities of value, there was no "loss" to mirror the defendants' "profit."

The court explicitly rejected this "mirror-image" requirement. Drawing on both the Supreme Court and other circuit decisions (such as *United States v. Baker*), the court clarified that the fraud statutes do not require the victim's loss of money or property to be the direct source of the defendant's gain in a way that leaves the victim with nothing. The fraud occurs at the point of inducement. If a defendant tricks a victim into a transaction, the fraud is complete, regardless of whether the victim receives something of value in return.

## Analyzing 'profit' as evidence of the intent to defraud

A secondary defense raised by the defendants was that they intended to "profit," not to "harm." They argued that since their goal was personal enrichment through a "pump and dump" scheme, they lacked the specific intent to cause harm to their followers.

The court dismissed this distinction as a "distinction without a difference." In the context of a fraud indictment, "intent to profit" through deception is functionally equivalent to "intent to defraud." Because the indictment alleges that the defendants' object was to obtain money by fraudulently inducing others to purchase securities, the allegation of "profit" serves as evidence of their intent. By seeking to profit from the deceptive transfer of their followers' money, the defendants demonstrated the requisite intent to defraud.

## Why the receipt of market-value securities by followers does not obviate fraud charges

Finally, the court addressed the specific fact that the followers received securities in exchange for their money. The defendants argued that because the followers received a product (the securities), the government failed to allege a scheme to deprive them of a protected property interest.

The court clarified that the receipt of market-value securities does not "obviate" (cancel out) the fraud charges. The core of the crime is the fraudulent inducement—the lie that moved the money from the follower's pocket to the defendants' control. Even if the followers received securities that were worth something, the fact that they were tricked into the purchase based on false information remains a violation of the securities fraud statute. The court concluded that the indictment sufficiently alleged that the defendants' object was to obtain money, and the followers' receipt of securities does not negate the fact that they were fraudulently induced to part with their property.

# Holding and Significance

## The Court’s Reversal and Remand

The Fifth Circuit reversed the district court’s dismissal of the indictment, holding that the government provided sufficient allegations to support a conviction for securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. The district court had originally dismissed the case on the narrow ground that the defendants were merely depriving their followers of "potentially valuable economic information"—a theory the court identified as a "right-to-control" theory, which does not constitute fraud under federal law. However, the Fifth Circuit clarified that the indictment did not rely on that theory. Instead, it alleged a "fraudulent-inducement" scheme, where the defendants used material misrepresentations to trick followers into parting with their own money and property to purchase securities.

Because the court found the indictment sufficient, it remanded the case for further proceedings. In a procedural sense, a remand means the case is sent back to the lower court to continue. Since the appellate court determined that the indictment meets the legal standard required to proceed, the district court must now move forward with the prosecution of the defendants, rather than dismissing the charges.

## The Fraudulent-Inducement Theory in Influencer Cases

This opinion is significant because it provides a clear roadmap for prosecuting "pump and dump" schemes conducted via social media. The court distinguished the current case from the Supreme Court’s decision in *Ciminelli*, where the government failed because it tried to prosecute a defendant for merely depriving a victim of the information needed to make a good economic decision. In the influencer context, the court clarifies that the "property" at stake is not the "right to choose" correctly, but the actual money the followers spend based on the influencers' lies.

By applying the "fraudulent-inducement" theory from *Kousisis*, the court established that influencers can be held liable even if the followers technically receive a "market-value" security in exchange for their money. The fraud occurs at the moment of the transaction: the influencer uses a lie to trick the victim into handing over their property. The court’s reasoning ensures that "pump and dump" schemes cannot escape prosecution simply because the victims "received" something of value in return for their money, as the crime is the fraudulent inducement to part with that money in the first place.

## Summary of Requirements for a Sufficient Securities Fraud Indictment

Based on the court's reasoning, a sufficient indictment for securities fraud under § 1348 must allege the following elements:

1.  **A Scheme to Defraud:** The indictment must allege a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others to obtain something of value, such as money or property.
2.  **Intent to Defraud:** The indictment must allege an intent to (1) deceive and (2) cause some harm to result from that deceit.
3.  **Fraudulent Inducement:** Rather than merely alleging a "right-to-control" violation (depriving a victim of information), the indictment must allege that the defendant used a misstatement to trick a victim into a transaction that requires handing over money or property.
4.  **Lack of a "Net Loss" Requirement:** The indictment does not need to allege that the victim suffered a "net pecuniary loss" or that the victim ended up "worse off" economically. The fraud is complete once the defendant induces the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses.
