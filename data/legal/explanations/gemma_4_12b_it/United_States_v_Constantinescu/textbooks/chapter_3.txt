Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 3: # Statutory Framework: Elements of Securities Fraud

## Overview of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 2 (Securities Fraud)
In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the defendants were charged with securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and 2. These statutes provide the federal government with the authority to prosecute individuals who engage in fraudulent schemes involving securities. In this specific case, the government alleged that the defendants participated in a “pump and dump” scheme. By using social media to post false and misleading information, the defendants allegedly induced followers to purchase securities, thereby artificially inflating prices so the defendants could sell their own holdings for a profit. To sustain a conviction under these statutes, the government must prove that the defendants engaged in a specific type of deceptive conduct aimed at obtaining a tangible benefit from others' investments.

## Parallels between securities fraud and mail/wire fraud statutes
While the Fifth Circuit noted that there is relatively "scant caselaw" specifically construing the securities fraud statute (18 U.S.C. § 1348), the court emphasized that this statute "borrows key concepts" from the more established mail and wire fraud statutes. Consequently, the court relies on the judicial treatment of mail and wire fraud to interpret the requirements of securities fraud. For a law student, this means that when analyzing a securities fraud charge, you must look beyond the specific text of the securities statute and consider the broader federal fraud framework. The core requirements derived from these parallels are the existence of a "scheme to defraud" and an "intent to defraud."

## Defining the 'scheme to defraud': material misrepresentations and obtaining value
The "scheme to defraud" element is the structural component of the offense. It requires more than a simple lie; it requires a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others for the purpose of obtaining something of value. In the context of securities fraud, the court looks for a plan where the defendant uses false pretenses to trick a victim into a transaction. In *Constantinescu*, the "scheme" was the "pump and dump" operation: the defendants allegedly misrepresented their trading positions and the potential price of securities to induce others to purchase them. The "object" of this fraud was the acquisition of money or property—specifically, the money the followers spent to buy the securities.

## Defining 'intent to defraud': the requirements of deception and resulting harm
To satisfy the "intent to defraud" element, the government must show that the defendant possessed two specific types of intent: (1) an intent to deceive, and (2) an intent to cause some harm to result from that deceit. The court clarified that "harm" in this context does not necessarily mean the victim must suffer a net pecuniary loss. Instead, the law focuses on the defendant’s objective. If the defendant’s goal was to obtain the victim’s money or property through deception, the intent to defraud is present. In this case, the defendants argued they only intended to profit, not to harm their followers. However, the court held that because the defendants' objective was to obtain money by inducing followers to part with their property, the intent to defraud was sufficiently alleged.

## The distinction between 'deception' and 'obtaining money or property' as the object of fraud
A critical distinction in this opinion is the difference between the "right-to-control" theory and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. Relying on the Supreme Court’s decision in *Ciminelli*, the court explained that federal fraud statutes do not protect "valuable economic information." Under the "right-to-control" theory, a defendant would be guilty of fraud simply for depriving a victim of information needed to make an economic decision. The Supreme Court rejected this because the right to information is not a "traditional property interest."

In contrast, *Constantinescu* applies the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. Under this theory, a defendant is liable if they use a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over money or property. This theory is distinct because it protects "money and property" rather than "information." The court held that because the indictment alleged the defendants induced followers to *purchase* securities—thereby handing over their money—the government was seeking to protect a traditional property interest. Furthermore, citing *Kousisis*, the court clarified that the government does not need to prove the victim was left "economically worse off." If the defendant fraudulently induces the deprivation of money or property, the fraud is complete, regardless of whether the victim received something of equal value in return.
