Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 5: # The Harm Requirement: Intent and Economic Loss

In analyzing the sufficiency of a securities fraud indictment, a critical question arises regarding the "intent to defraud." The defendants in *United States v. Constantinescu* argued that because their actions did not result in a net economic loss for their followers—who were induced to purchase securities rather than being stripped of their funds entirely—the government failed to allege an intent to defraud. To resolve this, the court looked to recent Supreme Court jurisprudence to define the boundaries of what constitutes "harm" under federal fraud statutes.

## The Supreme Court's decision in Kousisis: the absence of a net loss requirement

The court’s analysis centers on the Supreme Court’s decision in *Kousisis v. United States*. In that case, the defendant obtained government contracts by making false representations about his company's ability to fulfill specific requirements. The government argued that even though the defendant might have provided something of value in return for the contracts, the act of obtaining those contracts through lies constituted fraud.

The Supreme Court agreed, establishing a vital principle for interpreting the mail, wire, and securities fraud statutes: a defendant does not need to seek to "hurt the victim’s bottom line" to be guilty of fraud. Instead, the statutes are satisfied if the defendant schemes to "obtain" the victim’s money or property. This means that the legal focus is on the *deception used to facilitate the transfer of assets*, not the final balance sheet of the victim after the transaction is complete.

## Distinguishing 'obtaining' property from 'harming' a victim's bottom line

To understand the court's reasoning, students must distinguish between the "right-to-control" theory and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. As discussed in the previous section of the opinion, the right-to-control theory (rejected in *Ciminelli*) suggests that fraud occurs when a victim is deprived of valuable economic information. Under that theory, the "property" being stolen is the victim's ability to make informed decisions.

In contrast, the "fraudulent-inducement" theory used in *Constantinescu* focuses on the actual transfer of money or property. Under this theory, the fraud is complete the moment the defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract or transaction that requires handing over assets. The court emphasizes that the law protects against the fraudulent *transfer* of property. Therefore, the government does not need to prove that the victim ended up "worse off" in a global sense; it only needs to prove that the victim was tricked into partaking in a transaction that moved their money or property into the hands of the defendant.

## The 'mirror-image' requirement: why a defendant's gain does not need to equal a victim's loss

The defendants argued that for a fraud to exist, there must be a "mirror-image" relationship between the defendant’s gain and the victim’s loss. Essentially, they suggested that if the followers received securities of value, there was no "loss" to mirror the defendants' "profit."

The court explicitly rejected this "mirror-image" requirement. Drawing on both the Supreme Court and other circuit decisions (such as *United States v. Baker*), the court clarified that the fraud statutes do not require the victim's loss of money or property to be the direct source of the defendant's gain in a way that leaves the victim with nothing. The fraud occurs at the point of inducement. If a defendant tricks a victim into a transaction, the fraud is complete, regardless of whether the victim receives something of value in return.

## Analyzing 'profit' as evidence of the intent to defraud

A secondary defense raised by the defendants was that they intended to "profit," not to "harm." They argued that since their goal was personal enrichment through a "pump and dump" scheme, they lacked the specific intent to cause harm to their followers.

The court dismissed this distinction as a "distinction without a difference." In the context of a fraud indictment, "intent to profit" through deception is functionally equivalent to "intent to defraud." Because the indictment alleges that the defendants' object was to obtain money by fraudulently inducing others to purchase securities, the allegation of "profit" serves as evidence of their intent. By seeking to profit from the deceptive transfer of their followers' money, the defendants demonstrated the requisite intent to defraud.

## Why the receipt of market-value securities by followers does not obviate fraud charges

Finally, the court addressed the specific fact that the followers received securities in exchange for their money. The defendants argued that because the followers received a product (the securities), the government failed to allege a scheme to deprive them of a protected property interest.

The court clarified that the receipt of market-value securities does not "obviate" (cancel out) the fraud charges. The core of the crime is the fraudulent inducement—the lie that moved the money from the follower's pocket to the defendants' control. Even if the followers received securities that were worth something, the fact that they were tricked into the purchase based on false information remains a violation of the securities fraud statute. The court concluded that the indictment sufficiently alleged that the defendants' object was to obtain money, and the followers' receipt of securities does not negate the fact that they were fraudulently induced to part with their property.
