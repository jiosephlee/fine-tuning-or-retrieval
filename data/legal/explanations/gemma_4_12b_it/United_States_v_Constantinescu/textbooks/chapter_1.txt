Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

Chapter 1: # Case Background: The Social Media 'Pump and Dump' Scheme

## Profiles of the defendants as social media influencers

The defendants in *United States v. Constantinescu*—Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight—were not merely casual investors; they were prominent social media influencers. According to the government's indictment, these individuals cultivated large followings across various social media platforms, where they marketed themselves as skilled stock traders. By frequently posting their trading activities and building a public persona around their investment expertise, they established a platform of influence that allowed them to reach a wide audience of potential investors.

## Mechanics of the 'pump and dump' scheme: purchasing, pumping, and dumping

The core of the government’s securities fraud allegation centers on a classic "pump and dump" scheme, adapted for the digital age. The indictment alleges that the defendants executed this scheme in three distinct phases. First, the defendants would purchase a particular security. Second, they would "pump" the price of that security by posting false and misleading information about it on social media. The goal of this deception was to induce their followers to purchase the security, which would artificially inflate the price. Finally, the defendants would "dump" the security by secretly selling their holdings at these inflated prices for a profit.

## The government's allegations: material misrepresentations and illicit profits

To support the charges of conspiracy to commit securities fraud and multiple counts of securities fraud, the government alleged that the defendants engaged in specific material misrepresentations. These included posts that misrepresented the defendants' actual trading positions and the potential future prices of securities. The indictment claims that these deceptive tactics were used to manipulate the market, ultimately allowing the defendants to profit from the scheme. The government alleges that the defendants' actions resulted in a total profit of approximately $114 million.

## The district court's dismissal based on the lack of a 'traditional property interest'

The initial procedural hurdle for the government arose when the district court dismissed the indictment. The district court’s reasoning turned on the specific nature of the harm alleged in the indictment. The court concluded that the indictment failed to state an offense because it merely alleged that the defendants sought to deprive their followers of "potentially valuable economic information" rather than a "traditional property interest." Under existing fraud jurisprudence, the mere deprivation of information—even if that information is valuable for making economic decisions—is often insufficient to sustain a fraud conviction unless it is tied to the misappropriation of tangible money or property.

## The government's appeal to the Fifth Circuit

Following the district court's dismissal, the government timely appealed the decision to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit. The government’s primary argument is that the district court misinterpreted the indictment. While the defendants argued that the indictment only alleged a deprivation of information, the government contends that the indictment actually alleges a "fraudulent-inducement theory." Under this theory, the defendants did not just withhold information; they used material misstatements to trick victims into a transaction—the purchase of securities—which required the victims to hand over their money or property. The Fifth Circuit must now determine whether the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud under these standards.
