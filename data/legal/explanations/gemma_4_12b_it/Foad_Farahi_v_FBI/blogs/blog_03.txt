Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# The Secrecy Paradox: Ex Parte Declarations and the Erosion of FOIA Transparency

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) is built on the fundamental premise that government records belong to the public, and that the "sunlight" of disclosure is the best disinfectant for administrative overreach. However, a recent decision by the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* highlights a growing procedural paradox: the judiciary is increasingly relying on secret, *ex parte* declarations to determine exactly what information the public is forbidden from seeing. By treating this reliance as an "inherent" hurdle, the court risks transforming judicial oversight into a rubber stamp for executive secrecy.

In *Farahi*, the court addressed a massive FOIA request for the FBI’s records concerning an Iranian national with alleged ties to international terrorism. The FBI withheld the vast majority of the 10,750-page file, invoking Exemption 7(A), which protects records compiled for law enforcement purposes if their release could interfere with "pending or reasonably anticipated" proceedings. The litigation eventually centered on two questions: whether the investigations were still "ongoing" despite the passage of time, and whether the records were "reasonably segregable"—meaning, could the FBI provide the non-exempt parts while keeping the sensitive stuff hidden?

The court’s reasoning on the "ongoing" nature of the investigation provides a roadmap for how the judiciary handles long-term surveillance and counter-terrorism files. The government presented a series of declarations: a detailed public one from a former Section Chief (Hardy) and a much more "terse" subsequent update from an Acting Section Chief (Bender). Farahi argued that the brevity of the Bender declaration was insufficient to prove the investigations were still active. The court, however, adopted a cumulative approach, ruling that the Bender declaration did not need to stand alone; it merely had to confirm that the conditions established in the original, detailed Hardy declarations remained unchanged. This creates a significant hurdle for plaintiffs: once the government establishes a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" in a detailed initial filing, a mere confirmation of that status may suffice to maintain secrecy indefinitely.

The more troubling aspect of the opinion, however, lies in the court’s treatment of segregability. To determine whether the FBI could separate the "public" information from the "secret" information, the court relied heavily on *ex parte* submissions—documents filed under seal that the plaintiff was not permitted to see. The district court found that the FBI had provided a "detailed justification" in these secret filings for why the records were inextricably intertwined. The D.C. Circuit affirmed this, explicitly acknowledging that while *ex parte* declarations "handicap FOIA plaintiffs," this handicap is "inherent" in cases involving sensitive national security material.

This "inherent" handicap creates a profound procedural irony. The court is essentially tasked with deciding whether the government has followed the law in withholding information, but it is doing so by relying on evidence that the requester is legally barred from viewing. When the court accepts a secret declaration stating that "all records are intertwined," it removes the plaintiff’s ability to rebut that claim. In a standard FOIA dispute, the agency must show a "rational nexus" between the records and its duties; here, the nexus is established in a vacuum.

The downstream effect of this reasoning is a narrowing of judicial oversight. If the judiciary accepts the government’s word in a sealed envelope as the definitive reason for non-disclosure, the "reasonable steps" requirement of FOIA—which mandates that agencies must provide any reasonably segregable portion of a record—becomes nearly impossible to enforce. It allows for a "black box" of evidence where the government can claim that a document is unsegregable without ever having to prove it in a way that a third party can verify.

While the court was correct to note that some level of secrecy is necessary to protect active investigations, the *Farahi* decision suggests a trajectory where the "procedural necessity" of *ex parte* evidence begins to eclipse the transparency mandate of FOIA. By framing the plaintiff’s inability to see the evidence as an unavoidable byproduct of the law, the court may be inadvertently creating a system where the most sensitive government records are shielded not by the strength of the government's argument, but by the opacity of the process used to evaluate it.
