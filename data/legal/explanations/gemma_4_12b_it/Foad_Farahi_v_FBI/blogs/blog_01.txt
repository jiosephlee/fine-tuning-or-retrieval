Title: Blog about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# The 'Infinite Investigation': Testing the Temporal Limits of FOIA Exemption 7(A)

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) is built on the principle of government transparency, yet it contains vital "shields" to protect the integrity of law enforcement. One of the most significant of these is Exemption 7(A), which allows agencies to withhold records if their disclosure could "reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." However, for a proceeding to qualify for this protection, it must be "pending or reasonably anticipated." The central tension in FOIA litigation often lies in determining when a proceeding ceases to be "pending" and becomes a historical artifact. In *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation*, the D.C. Circuit explored the outer limits of this temporal requirement, raising significant questions about whether the gravity of an investigation can effectively immunize records from disclosure indefinitely.

The case at hand involved a massive request by Foad Farahi, an Iranian national, for his FBI file. The records in question—totaling over 10,000 pages—detailed Farahi’s alleged connections to high-level terrorists and organizations like the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood. Because the FBI specialized in these types of matters, the court granted it significant deference in determining that the records were indeed compiled for law enforcement purposes. The real battleground, however, was whether the investigation remained "ongoing" enough to justify a blanket withholding of the file after years of litigation and administrative delays.

Farahi’s primary argument was one of "staleness." He contended that the passage of time during the FOIA process rendered the FBI’s assertions of an ongoing investigation conclusory and vague. He pointed to a "terse" supplemental declaration from the FBI as evidence that the agency was merely asserting a continuing status without providing fresh evidence of active pursuit. In many FOIA contexts, a vague assertion that an investigation is "ongoing" can be insufficient to overcome a plaintiff's demand for transparency, especially when the litigation itself has dragged on for years.

The D.C. Circuit, however, took a different path. The court reasoned that the Bender declaration—the one Farahi attacked as conclusory—should not be viewed in a vacuum. Instead, the court looked at the broader context established by previous declarations that provided "considerable detail" about the scope of the investigation. By linking the records to Farahi’s ties to known Al-Qaeda members and other high-profile terrorists, the court found that the sheer *breadth* of the investigation provided a plausible basis for it to remain "ongoing." Essentially, the court suggested that if an investigation is sufficiently serious and sprawling, its "ongoing" status can be inferred to persist even if the specific updates provided by the agency are brief.

This reasoning introduces a potentially problematic doctrinal shift: the prioritization of the *gravity* of the subject matter over the *chronology* of the investigation. If the "breadth" of an investigation can serve as a proxy for its "pendency," it creates a pathway for what might be termed "infinite investigations." In such a framework, records regarding counterterrorism or high-level national security could remain shielded indefinitely, provided the agency can point to a sufficiently broad historical nexus to a security risk. This risks transforming Exemption 7(A) from a shield for active police work into a permanent vault for sensitive records that may no longer have a practical impact on current enforcement efforts.

Furthermore, the court’s treatment of segregability in this case highlights the inherent difficulties for FOIA plaintiffs. By relying on *ex parte* declarations to determine that the records were "inextricably intertwined," the court allowed the FBI to maintain the secrecy of the very material at the heart of the dispute. While the court correctly noted that such *ex parte* reviews are often necessary to protect secrets, the result is a litigation environment where the plaintiff is forced to accept the government's word on both the "ongoing" status of the investigation and the "unsavegability" of the information. For future litigants, *Farahi* serves as a reminder that in the realm of national security, the passage of time may not be a sufficient remedy for a lack of transparency.
