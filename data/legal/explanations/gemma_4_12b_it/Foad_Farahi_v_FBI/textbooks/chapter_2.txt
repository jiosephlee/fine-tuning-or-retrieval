Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 2: # Case Background and Procedural Posture

## The Plaintiff: Farahi’s immigration history and allegations of ties to terrorism

To understand the government’s interest in withholding the records in this case, one must first look at the history of the plaintiff, Foad Farahi. Farahi, an Iranian national who has resided in the United States since 1993, became the subject of Department of Homeland Security (DHS) removal proceedings in 2002 following a visa overstay. Throughout his immigration proceedings, Farahi sought various forms of legal relief, including asylum and protection under the Convention Against Torture. 

The government’s opposition to Farahi’s relief was rooted in allegations of serious national security threats. During these proceedings, DHS presented evidence and live testimony from an FBI agent who documented Farahi’s alleged connections to high-level terrorists. Specifically, the FBI’s public and sealed declarations alleged that Farahi acted as a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, was connected to known Al Qaeda members such as Khaled Sheikh Mohamed, and was linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists, including Jose Padilla. These allegations established the factual foundation for the FBI’s claim that the records in question were compiled for critical law enforcement purposes.

## The FOIA Request: Scope and volume of the FBI file (10,750+ pages)

The litigation began when Farahi submitted a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request specifically seeking his FBI file. The FBI's search for responsive records yielded a massive volume of information: approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. Because the FBI failed to meet the statutory deadline for responding to the request, Farahi filed a lawsuit to compel the disclosure of these records.

Over the following several years, the FBI conducted a comprehensive review of the material. While the agency disclosed some records to Farahi, it chose to withhold the vast majority of the files. The sheer scale of the request—exceeding 10,000 pages—created a complex administrative and legal task for the agency in determining which specific portions of the file could be released and which must remain confidential to protect ongoing investigations.

## Initial Litigation: The FBI’s first motion for summary judgment and the district court's request for updated status

In October 2019, the FBI moved for summary judgment, asking the court to rule that the withheld records were protected from disclosure. To support this motion, the FBI submitted a declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Hardy asserted that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their release could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that were either pending or reasonably anticipated.

However, the district court did not grant summary judgment in November 2022. While the court acknowledged that the records were likely compiled for law enforcement purposes, it expressed concern over the passage of time since the original declarations were filed. The court ordered the FBI to provide updated evidence to confirm that the enforcement proceedings were still ongoing. Additionally, the court indicated it required more information regarding "segregability"—whether the exempt information could be separated from the nonexempt information—but noted that it could not provide specific details on those concerns in the public record. Consequently, the court denied the motion without prejudice, allowing the FBI to provide supplemental evidence.

## The Supplemental Evidence: The role of the Hardy and Bender declarations (public vs. ex parte)

The FBI responded to the court’s request by submitting renewed motions supported by declarations from Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. These submissions included both public and *ex parte* declarations. In the public declaration, Bender noted that the agent overseeing Farahi's file confirmed that investigations remained ongoing. The *ex parte* declaration, which was filed under seal and not available for public viewing, provided the court with more granular details regarding the nature of the investigation and addressed the court's specific concerns about segregability.

The court treated these new declarations not as a brand-new basis for withholding the records, but as an update to the initial Hardy declarations. Because the original Hardy declarations had already established the existence and scope of the investigation, the Bender declarations served to confirm that the status of those investigations had not changed during the period of litigation. This procedural step was necessary to satisfy the requirement that the enforcement proceedings remain "pending or reasonably anticipated."

## District Court Resolution: The final grant of summary judgment and the grounds for non-segregability

Following the review of the supplemental evidence, the district court granted the FBI’s motion for summary judgment. The court credited the FBI's confirmation that the relevant proceedings remained ongoing and concluded that the agency had adequately explained why the exempt material could not be reasonably segregated from the rest of the file.

In reaching this conclusion, the court relied on the FBI's assertion that the protected information was inextricably intertwined with the nonexempt portions. Under FOIA, while agencies are generally required to release any reasonably segregable portions of a record, they may withhold a record in its entirety if the exempt and nonexempt portions cannot be separated without compromising the exempt information. The court found that the FBI had met its burden by showing that the records were so closely linked to the ongoing national security investigations that any meaningful disclosure would interfere with the FBI's law enforcement duties. This final ruling formed the basis of Farahi’s appeal to the D.C. Circuit.
