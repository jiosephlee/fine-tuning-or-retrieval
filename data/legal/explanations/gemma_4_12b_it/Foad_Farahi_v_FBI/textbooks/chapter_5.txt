Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

Chapter 5: # Segregability and Ex Parte Evidence

## The Segregability Standard: Distinguishing between 'reasonably segregable' and 'inextricably intertwined' material

Under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), the government’s power to withhold records is not absolute. Even when a record falls under a specific exemption—such as Exemption 7(A), which protects records compiled for law enforcement purposes—the agency is still mandated by statute to take "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." This means that if a document contains both exempt (secret) and nonexempt (public) portions, the agency must provide the nonexempt parts to the requester.

However, the court distinguishes between material that is merely "mixed" and material that is "inextricably intertwined." If the nonexempt portions of a record are so deeply woven into the exempt portions that they cannot be separated without compromising the protection of the exempt information, the agency may withhold the entire record. In *Farahi*, the court looked at the FBI’s assertion that the withheld records were not reasonably segregable and found that the district court correctly accepted the FBI’s justification. When the exempt and nonexempt portions cannot be cleanly partitioned, the "inextricably intertwined" standard allows the government to withhold the document in its entirety.

## Meaningful Information vs. Words: Why line-by-line interspersed nonexempt words do not constitute segregable information

A common point of contention in FOIA litigation is whether the government must "redact" every single nonexempt word within a larger exempt document. In *Farahi*, Farahi argued that because the records were so vast, there must be some segregable and releasable information contained within them. The court rejected this argument by clarifying the objective of FOIA: the focus is on the disclosure of *information*, not simply the disclosure of individual words that the government has written down.

The court explains that if a small amount of nonexempt material is "randomly interspersed line-by-line" throughout a document, that material is not "reasonably segregable." For a court to order the release of information, that information must be distinct enough to be meaningful to the requester. If the nonexempt material is merely scattered at a granular level, the burden of identifying and extracting every individual nonexempt word would be impractical and would not fulfill the purpose of the Act. Therefore, if the nonexempt portions lack "meaningful" independence from the exempt material, the record remains protected.

## The Role of Ex Parte Declarations: When secret evidence is necessary to resolve a FOIA dispute without compromising secrets

One of the most complex procedural aspects of FOIA litigation involves "ex parte" evidence—information submitted to the court under seal, meaning it is not shared with the plaintiff. In *Farahi*, the FBI submitted ex parte declarations to explain why the records were not segregable. Because the records were themselves classified or sensitive enough to warrant an Exemption 7(A) claim, the government argued that it could not show the court the specific reasons for non-segregability in open court without violating the very secrecy the exemption was designed to protect.

The court notes that the use of ex parte evidence is often "inherent" in these types of cases. If the court were required to review the exempt material in a public proceeding, the secret information would be disclosed to the plaintiff. To resolve the dispute without compromising the secret, the court must rely on the government's private assertions (the ex parte declarations) to determine if the agency’s claims of non-segregability are credible and specific enough to meet the legal standard.

## Judicial Reliance: Why the court can rely on ex parte evidence even if the plaintiff did not oppose its use

Farahi raised a procedural objection, complaining that the district court relied too heavily on the FBI's secret (ex parte) submissions to reach its conclusion. The appellate court dismissed this objection based on two primary points. First, Farahi did not oppose the initial motion to file these declarations ex parte; therefore, he could not later challenge the court's reliance on them. In legal procedure, if a party does not object to the method of evidence gathering, they generally cannot complain about the court's use of that evidence.

Second, the court emphasized that while ex parte declarations can be a hurdle for plaintiffs, they are a necessary tool when the dispute cannot be decided otherwise. Because the FBI's primary argument was that the records were not segregable, the only way for the court to verify that claim was to look at the specific documents in private. The court found that the FBI provided "reasonable specificity" in those secret filings to explain why the records could not be split, satisfying the legal burden even though those details remained hidden from Farahi.

## Conclusion: The finality of the appellate ruling on Exemption 7(A)

The appellate court affirmed the district court’s ruling, concluding that the FBI successfully met its burden under Exemption 7(A). The court's reasoning rested on three pillars: first, that the records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes" related to concrete terrorism investigations; second, that their release would "reasonably be expected to interfere" with those ongoing proceedings by "tipping off" targets; and third, that the records were not "reasonably segregable" because the nonexempt information was not distinct enough to be released. By upholding these findings, the court underscored that while FOIA favors transparency, it does not require the government to dismantle protected law enforcement records to provide a "line-by-line" accounting of nonexempt words.
