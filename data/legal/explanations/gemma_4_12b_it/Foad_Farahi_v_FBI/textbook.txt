Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

# Foundations of FOIA and Exemption 7(A)

## Overview of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) and the General Agency Duty to Disclose

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) establishes a foundational principle of transparency in government: federal agencies are generally required to release records to the public upon request. As the court notes in *Farahi*, this duty is the default state of the statute. However, this transparency is not absolute. The law provides a specific list of statutory exemptions that allow agencies to withhold certain types of information. When a requester like Farahi sues for records, the burden lies with the government to prove that the withheld information falls under one of these specific exemptions. If the government cannot meet this burden, it must disclose the records.

## The Specific Language of Exemption 7(A): 'Compiled for Law Enforcement Purposes'

One of the most common hurdles for FOIA requesters is Exemption 7(A), which protects records "compiled for law enforcement purposes." To successfully invoke this exemption, the government must show that the records in question were created as part of an actual law enforcement activity. In *Farahi*, the court emphasizes that because the FBI is a specialized law enforcement agency, its determination on whether a record was compiled for such purposes is entitled to a "fair measure of deference." 

However, this deference is not a blank check. The court requires that the government establish a "rational nexus" between the investigation and the agency’s law enforcement duties. This means the records must connect a specific individual or incident to a possible security risk or a violation of federal law. In this case, the FBI met this burden by demonstrating that Farahi’s file was compiled to investigate violations of national security and international terrorism, specifically linking him to known terrorist organizations and individuals.

## The 'Interference' Test: Determining if Disclosure Could Reasonably Be Expected to Interfere with Proceedings

The second prong of Exemption 7(A) requires the government to show that disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." This is a forward-looking test; the government must argue that releasing the information would harm the integrity or success of a law enforcement action. 

In *Farahi*, the court accepted the FBI’s argument that releasing the records could "tip off" the targets about the scope of the ongoing investigation. This is a widely recognized justification in counterterrorism cases. The court looks for a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding"—meaning the government must show that the investigation is not merely theoretical but is a real, active effort to gather evidence or identify threats.

## Defining 'Proceedings': Distinguishing Between Pending and Reasonably Anticipated Actions

A critical point of contention in *Farahi* was whether the "proceedings" were still active. Under FOIA, "proceedings" are defined as those that are either "pending" (currently in progress) or "reasonably anticipated" (likely to happen in the near future). The court clarifies that an ongoing criminal investigation counts as a "proceeding" for the purposes of Exemption 7(A). 

Even if a formal court case has not yet been filed, if the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future case and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of evidence, the exemption applies. Farahi argued that the passage of time suggested the investigation might have stalled. The court rejected this, looking at the breadth of the investigation—which involved high-level terrorist links—and concluding that as long as the FBI maintains that the investigation is "ongoing," the "pending or reasonably anticipated" standard is satisfied.

## The Government's Duty to Segregate Non-Exempt Information

Finally, the court addresses the agency's duty to "segregate" information. Even when a record falls under an exemption, the government is not permitted to withhold the entire document if only portions of it are exempt. Agencies must take "reasonable steps" to delete the exempt portions and release the rest. 

In *Farahi*, the court explained the limits of this duty through the concept of "inextricably intertwined" material. If exempt information is so woven into the document that it cannot be separated without destroying the meaning of the record, the government may withhold the entire file. The court clarifies a vital distinction for students: FOIA is about "information," not "words." While a plaintiff might argue that individual words or phrases are not secret, the government can withhold a document if the *meaningful information* it conveys is protected. Because the FBI demonstrated that the exempt material was not reasonably segregable, the court upheld the government's right to withhold the records.

# Case Background and Procedural Posture

## The Plaintiff: Farahi’s immigration history and allegations of ties to terrorism

To understand the government’s interest in withholding the records in this case, one must first look at the history of the plaintiff, Foad Farahi. Farahi, an Iranian national who has resided in the United States since 1993, became the subject of Department of Homeland Security (DHS) removal proceedings in 2002 following a visa overstay. Throughout his immigration proceedings, Farahi sought various forms of legal relief, including asylum and protection under the Convention Against Torture. 

The government’s opposition to Farahi’s relief was rooted in allegations of serious national security threats. During these proceedings, DHS presented evidence and live testimony from an FBI agent who documented Farahi’s alleged connections to high-level terrorists. Specifically, the FBI’s public and sealed declarations alleged that Farahi acted as a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, was connected to known Al Qaeda members such as Khaled Sheikh Mohamed, and was linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists, including Jose Padilla. These allegations established the factual foundation for the FBI’s claim that the records in question were compiled for critical law enforcement purposes.

## The FOIA Request: Scope and volume of the FBI file (10,750+ pages)

The litigation began when Farahi submitted a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request specifically seeking his FBI file. The FBI's search for responsive records yielded a massive volume of information: approximately 10,750 pages and 80 CDs. Because the FBI failed to meet the statutory deadline for responding to the request, Farahi filed a lawsuit to compel the disclosure of these records.

Over the following several years, the FBI conducted a comprehensive review of the material. While the agency disclosed some records to Farahi, it chose to withhold the vast majority of the files. The sheer scale of the request—exceeding 10,000 pages—created a complex administrative and legal task for the agency in determining which specific portions of the file could be released and which must remain confidential to protect ongoing investigations.

## Initial Litigation: The FBI’s first motion for summary judgment and the district court's request for updated status

In October 2019, the FBI moved for summary judgment, asking the court to rule that the withheld records were protected from disclosure. To support this motion, the FBI submitted a declaration from David Hardy, the Section Chief of the Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Hardy asserted that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their release could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings that were either pending or reasonably anticipated.

However, the district court did not grant summary judgment in November 2022. While the court acknowledged that the records were likely compiled for law enforcement purposes, it expressed concern over the passage of time since the original declarations were filed. The court ordered the FBI to provide updated evidence to confirm that the enforcement proceedings were still ongoing. Additionally, the court indicated it required more information regarding "segregability"—whether the exempt information could be separated from the nonexempt information—but noted that it could not provide specific details on those concerns in the public record. Consequently, the court denied the motion without prejudice, allowing the FBI to provide supplemental evidence.

## The Supplemental Evidence: The role of the Hardy and Bender declarations (public vs. ex parte)

The FBI responded to the court’s request by submitting renewed motions supported by declarations from Joseph Bender, the Acting Section Chief of RIDS. These submissions included both public and *ex parte* declarations. In the public declaration, Bender noted that the agent overseeing Farahi's file confirmed that investigations remained ongoing. The *ex parte* declaration, which was filed under seal and not available for public viewing, provided the court with more granular details regarding the nature of the investigation and addressed the court's specific concerns about segregability.

The court treated these new declarations not as a brand-new basis for withholding the records, but as an update to the initial Hardy declarations. Because the original Hardy declarations had already established the existence and scope of the investigation, the Bender declarations served to confirm that the status of those investigations had not changed during the period of litigation. This procedural step was necessary to satisfy the requirement that the enforcement proceedings remain "pending or reasonably anticipated."

## District Court Resolution: The final grant of summary judgment and the grounds for non-segregability

Following the review of the supplemental evidence, the district court granted the FBI’s motion for summary judgment. The court credited the FBI's confirmation that the relevant proceedings remained ongoing and concluded that the agency had adequately explained why the exempt material could not be reasonably segregated from the rest of the file.

In reaching this conclusion, the court relied on the FBI's assertion that the protected information was inextricably intertwined with the nonexempt portions. Under FOIA, while agencies are generally required to release any reasonably segregable portions of a record, they may withhold a record in its entirety if the exempt and nonexempt portions cannot be separated without compromising the exempt information. The court found that the FBI had met its burden by showing that the records were so closely linked to the ongoing national security investigations that any meaningful disclosure would interfere with the FBI's law enforcement duties. This final ruling formed the basis of Farahi’s appeal to the D.C. Circuit.

# The Legal Standard for Law Enforcement Exemptions

## The Burden of Proof: Establishing that the Government Must Justify the Invocation of an Exemption

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) is built on a foundational principle of transparency: federal agencies are required to release records to the public unless a specific statutory exemption applies. Because the default state of the Act is disclosure, the legal burden of proof rests squarely on the government. When an agency like the FBI seeks to withhold records, it must affirmatively demonstrate that the information falls within a recognized exemption. 

In the context of this case, the FBI invoked Exemption 7(A), which protects records "compiled for law enforcement purposes" if disclosure could "reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." To succeed, the FBI cannot merely claim the records are sensitive; it must provide evidence that the records were actually compiled for a specific law enforcement purpose and that their release would jeopardize a pending or reasonably anticipated proceeding. If the government fails to meet this burden, the court must order the release of the records.

## Agency Deference: Why the FBI's Expertise Earns it 'Fair Measure of Deference' in Exemption 7 Cases

While the government bears the burden of proof, the court does not review the government’s claims with a completely neutral eye. Instead, the judiciary grants a "fair measure of deference" to the FBI when it invokes Exemption 7. This deference stems from the FBI’s specialized role as the nation’s primary law enforcement agency. The court recognizes that the FBI is in a unique position to evaluate its own internal records and determine how their release might impact ongoing operations.

This deference means that if the FBI provides a plausible explanation for why a record is law enforcement-related, the court will generally accept that determination unless it is clearly unsupported by the facts. The court is not required to conduct an independent, exhaustive investigation into the FBI's internal processes; rather, it looks for a reasonable basis for the agency's assertion.

## The Nexus Requirement: Linking Investigations to Specific Security Risks or Violations of Federal Law

Deference is not a blank check. Even with the FBI's specialized expertise, the agency must still establish a "nexus"—a clear logical connection—between the records and its official duties. To satisfy this requirement, the FBI’s declarations must show two specific links. First, there must be a rational nexus between the investigation and one of the agency’s specific law enforcement duties. Second, there must be a connection between the specific individual or incident in the records and a possible security risk or a violation of federal law.

In practice, this prevents the government from withholding records simply because they are "interesting" or "sensitive" in a general sense. The FBI must point to a concrete law enforcement objective, such as an investigation into a specific crime or a specific threat to public safety, to justify the use of the exemption.

## National Security Considerations: Heightened Deference in Counterterrorism Contexts

The court’s standard of deference becomes even more pronounced when national security concerns are at issue. The judiciary has consistently reiterated that the executive branch should be given significant latitude when dealing with records related to counterterrorism investigations. In these cases, the court is particularly wary of interfering with the government's ability to protect the country.

When an Exemption 7(A) claim involves counterterrorism, the court’s deference to the FBI is "particularly appropriate." The reasoning is that the government has a compelling interest in maintaining the secrecy of its methods and the status of its investigations to prevent adversaries from learning about the scope of federal efforts to thwart terrorist activities.

## Application to Farahi: Analyzing the Public Allegations of Recruitment and Financing of Terrorism

In *Farahi*, the court applied these standards by weighing the FBI’s assertions against the public facts of the case. To satisfy the nexus requirement and justify the use of Exemption 7(A), the FBI had to show that Farahi's file was indeed compiled for law enforcement purposes. The court looked to the FBI's public allegations against Farahi to confirm this.

The court found that the FBI had carried its burden because it publicly alleged that Farahi was a recruiter for the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, was connected to known Al Qaeda members (including Khaled Sheikh Mohamed), and was linked to multiple convicted or indicted terrorists. Furthermore, the FBI alleged that Farahi supported charities that financed terrorism. These specific, high-level allegations provided the "rational nexus" required by the law: they linked Farahi’s file to specific violations of federal law and significant security risks. Because these facts supported the FBI's claim that the records were part of a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding," the court upheld the government’s right to withhold the records.

# Analyzing Pendency and the Interference Test

To prevail on a motion to withhold records under FOIA Exemption 7(A), the government must demonstrate that the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their disclosure "could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings." Central to this analysis is the requirement that such proceedings be either "pending" or "reasonably anticipated." In *Farahi*, the court’s analysis of these requirements focuses on the practical mechanics of law enforcement investigations and how a court should evaluate government assertions over time.

## Interference Logic: The 'Tipping Off' Justification

The first component of the interference test is whether disclosure would actually hamper the government’s work. The court identifies a "widely accepted justification" for this under Exemption 7(A): the risk of "tipping off" the targets of an investigation. If a subject learns the scope, methods, or specific evidence gathered by the FBI, they may take steps to obstruct justice, destroy evidence, or flee. 

In the *Farahi* case, the court accepted the FBI’s assertion that releasing the records would tip off the subjects regarding the scope of the investigation. Because the court views this as a standard and legitimate concern in counterterrorism cases, the government’s burden is met if it can provide a plausible explanation of how disclosure would jeopardize the integrity of the ongoing inquiry.

## Ongoing Criminal Investigations as 'Proceedings'

A common point of confusion in FOIA litigation is whether an "enforcement proceeding" requires a formal court date or an active indictment. The court clarifies this by noting that "ongoing criminal investigations" count as proceedings for the purposes of Exemption 7(A). 

The standard here is whether the investigation is still "gathering evidence for a possible future criminal case." If the release of information would jeopardize that future case by alerting the targets prematurely, the records remain protected. Therefore, the "proceeding" is not merely the trial itself, but the entire investigative process required to build the case for that trial.

## The 'Vague Assertion' Problem

A primary point of contention in *Farahi* was whether the FBI’s evidence was sufficiently specific. Farahi argued that the declaration provided by Joseph Bender was too "terse" and "conclusory" to prove that investigations were actually still happening. He pointed to the "vague nature" of such assertions as a reason to deny the exemption.

The court acknowledges that a standalone, brief assertion that an investigation is "ongoing" might be insufficient. However, the court distinguishes between an assertion made in a vacuum and one that serves as an update to a previously established record. This highlights a key pedagogical point in reading these opinions: the court often looks at the "totality of the record" rather than viewing every piece of evidence in isolation.

## Contextual Interpretation of Declarations

To resolve the issue of whether Bender’s declaration was too vague, the court employed a contextual interpretation. It refused to view the Bender declaration as a standalone document. Instead, the court looked at the entire history of the litigation:

1.  The initial **public Hardy declaration**, which established the existence of the investigation.
2.  The **ex parte Hardy declaration**, which provided "considerable detail" about the nature and scope of the investigation.
3.  The **district court’s prior finding**, which had already concluded the FBI "seemingly" met its burden.

The court reasoned that the Bender declaration was not intended to establish the pendency of the proceedings from scratch; it was merely an update to confirm that "nothing had changed" while the motion was pending. By linking the detailed earlier declarations with the brief update, the court found the government’s evidence sufficient.

## The 'Concrete Prospective Proceeding' Standard

Finally, the court addressed the "passage of time." Because Farahi had been in the system for years, he argued that the investigations could no longer be considered "reasonably anticipated." The court countered this by looking at the severity and breadth of the allegations.

The court noted the FBI’s public allegations against Farahi: his links to the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, Al Qaeda, and various "charities that financed terrorism." Given the gravity of these national security concerns, the court found it "plausible" that a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding" continues to exist. This suggests that for high-stakes national security cases, the court will allow a longer window of time for an investigation to be considered "ongoing" before it is deemed "stale."

# Segregability and Ex Parte Evidence

## The Segregability Standard: Distinguishing between 'reasonably segregable' and 'inextricably intertwined' material

Under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), the government’s power to withhold records is not absolute. Even when a record falls under a specific exemption—such as Exemption 7(A), which protects records compiled for law enforcement purposes—the agency is still mandated by statute to take "reasonable steps" to "segregate and release nonexempt information." This means that if a document contains both exempt (secret) and nonexempt (public) portions, the agency must provide the nonexempt parts to the requester.

However, the court distinguishes between material that is merely "mixed" and material that is "inextricably intertwined." If the nonexempt portions of a record are so deeply woven into the exempt portions that they cannot be separated without compromising the protection of the exempt information, the agency may withhold the entire record. In *Farahi*, the court looked at the FBI’s assertion that the withheld records were not reasonably segregable and found that the district court correctly accepted the FBI’s justification. When the exempt and nonexempt portions cannot be cleanly partitioned, the "inextricably intertwined" standard allows the government to withhold the document in its entirety.

## Meaningful Information vs. Words: Why line-by-line interspersed nonexempt words do not constitute segregable information

A common point of contention in FOIA litigation is whether the government must "redact" every single nonexempt word within a larger exempt document. In *Farahi*, Farahi argued that because the records were so vast, there must be some segregable and releasable information contained within them. The court rejected this argument by clarifying the objective of FOIA: the focus is on the disclosure of *information*, not simply the disclosure of individual words that the government has written down.

The court explains that if a small amount of nonexempt material is "randomly interspersed line-by-line" throughout a document, that material is not "reasonably segregable." For a court to order the release of information, that information must be distinct enough to be meaningful to the requester. If the nonexempt material is merely scattered at a granular level, the burden of identifying and extracting every individual nonexempt word would be impractical and would not fulfill the purpose of the Act. Therefore, if the nonexempt portions lack "meaningful" independence from the exempt material, the record remains protected.

## The Role of Ex Parte Declarations: When secret evidence is necessary to resolve a FOIA dispute without compromising secrets

One of the most complex procedural aspects of FOIA litigation involves "ex parte" evidence—information submitted to the court under seal, meaning it is not shared with the plaintiff. In *Farahi*, the FBI submitted ex parte declarations to explain why the records were not segregable. Because the records were themselves classified or sensitive enough to warrant an Exemption 7(A) claim, the government argued that it could not show the court the specific reasons for non-segregability in open court without violating the very secrecy the exemption was designed to protect.

The court notes that the use of ex parte evidence is often "inherent" in these types of cases. If the court were required to review the exempt material in a public proceeding, the secret information would be disclosed to the plaintiff. To resolve the dispute without compromising the secret, the court must rely on the government's private assertions (the ex parte declarations) to determine if the agency’s claims of non-segregability are credible and specific enough to meet the legal standard.

## Judicial Reliance: Why the court can rely on ex parte evidence even if the plaintiff did not oppose its use

Farahi raised a procedural objection, complaining that the district court relied too heavily on the FBI's secret (ex parte) submissions to reach its conclusion. The appellate court dismissed this objection based on two primary points. First, Farahi did not oppose the initial motion to file these declarations ex parte; therefore, he could not later challenge the court's reliance on them. In legal procedure, if a party does not object to the method of evidence gathering, they generally cannot complain about the court's use of that evidence.

Second, the court emphasized that while ex parte declarations can be a hurdle for plaintiffs, they are a necessary tool when the dispute cannot be decided otherwise. Because the FBI's primary argument was that the records were not segregable, the only way for the court to verify that claim was to look at the specific documents in private. The court found that the FBI provided "reasonable specificity" in those secret filings to explain why the records could not be split, satisfying the legal burden even though those details remained hidden from Farahi.

## Conclusion: The finality of the appellate ruling on Exemption 7(A)

The appellate court affirmed the district court’s ruling, concluding that the FBI successfully met its burden under Exemption 7(A). The court's reasoning rested on three pillars: first, that the records were "compiled for law enforcement purposes" related to concrete terrorism investigations; second, that their release would "reasonably be expected to interfere" with those ongoing proceedings by "tipping off" targets; and third, that the records were not "reasonably segregable" because the nonexempt information was not distinct enough to be released. By upholding these findings, the court underscored that while FOIA favors transparency, it does not require the government to dismantle protected law enforcement records to provide a "line-by-line" accounting of nonexempt words.
