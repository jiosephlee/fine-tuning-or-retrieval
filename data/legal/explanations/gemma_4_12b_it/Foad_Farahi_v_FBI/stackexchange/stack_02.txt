Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation"

### What is the specific threshold for an investigation to be considered 'reasonably anticipated' under Exemption 7(A)?
Question:
The court notes that 'ongoing criminal investigations' count as 'proceedings' and that the government may show this simply by attesting to their 'existence.' Does this mean that as long as an agent says they are 'looking into' something, the records are automatically exempt? At what point does a 'potential' investigation become 'reasonably anticipated' enough to trigger Exemption 7(A)?
Answer:
Under Exemption 7(A) of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), the threshold for an investigation to be considered "reasonably anticipated" is not met by a mere "potential" inquiry or a vague assertion of "looking into" a matter. Instead, the government must demonstrate that the records were compiled for a specific law enforcement purpose and that their disclosure would "reasonably be expected to interfere" with a "concrete prospective law enforcement proceeding."

The court in *Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation* clarifies the standard for these proceedings as follows:

### 1. The "Ongoing" Investigation Standard
The court affirms that "ongoing criminal investigations" qualify as "proceedings" under Exemption 7(A). While the government may satisfy this by "attesting to their existence," this attestation must be supported by a sufficient factual nexus. Specifically, Exemption 7(A) applies "so long as the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case, and that case would be jeopardized by the premature release of that evidence."

### 2. The Requirement of a "Nexus"
To move beyond a "potential" investigation to a "reasonably anticipated" one, the government must establish:
*   A rational **nexus** between the investigation and one of the agency’s specific law enforcement duties; and
*   A connection between an individual or incident and a **possible security risk or violation of federal law**.

In *Farahi*, the court found the FBI met this burden because the records were compiled to investigate violations of national security and international terrorism, specifically linked to the subject’s documented associations with known terrorists and foreign terrorist organizations.

### 3. Context vs. Conclusory Statements
The court addresses the "vague nature" of government assertions. While a "terse" or "conclusory" statement that an investigation is "ongoing" might not suffice in isolation, the court will look at the context of the government's declarations. 

In this case, the court held that a simple update confirming an investigation is "ongoing" was sufficient because it was an update to a previously established, detailed record. The government had already provided "considerable detail about the nature and scope of the investigation" in prior declarations. Therefore, the threshold is met when the government provides enough detail to show that a "concrete prospective" proceeding exists, even if the specific status update provided to the court is brief.

### Summary of the Threshold
An investigation is "reasonably anticipated" when the government can show that:
1.  The records were compiled for a specific law enforcement duty (e.g., counterterrorism).
2.  The investigation is actively gathering evidence for a potential future criminal case.
3.  Disclosure would jeopardize that case (e.g., by "tipping off" the targets).
4.  There is a "concrete" link between the subject and a violation of federal law or a security risk.
