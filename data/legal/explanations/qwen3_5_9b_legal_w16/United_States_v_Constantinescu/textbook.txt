# Chapter 1: Factual Background and Procedural History

## The Nature of the ‘Pump and Dump’ Scheme

Understanding the factual allegations at the heart of *United States v. Constantinescu* requires a clear definition of the specific criminal conduct under scrutiny. At issue was a scheme described by the government as a "pump and dump" scheme involving securities fraud. In the context of this opinion, a pump and dump involves two distinct stages of manipulation: the "pump" and the "dump." The government alleged that the defendants would first purchase a specific security. Once they owned the asset, they would then "pump the price of that security" by broadcasting false and misleading information to the public. The purpose of this inflation was to induce other investors to purchase the security, which would artificially increase the market price. The final step, the "dump," involved the defendants secretly selling the security at this artificially inflated price to realize a profit. While this is a common term in financial markets, the opinion notes that these actions were alleged to violate federal criminal statutes by constituting a scheme to defraud investors in connection with a security.

## Defendants' Social Media Operations and Allegations

To analyze how the scheme was executed, it is necessary to understand the defendants' role in the capital markets as they alleged by the indictment. The accused included Edward Constantinescu, Perry "PJ" Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight. The indictment alleges that these individuals shared a network of large social media followings across various online platforms. Beyond their user status, the defendants are described as having held themselves out to be skilled stock traders. This representation was allegedly key to the operation because it lent credibility to the information they shared. The government alleged that these defendants engaged in a recurring pattern where they posted their trading activities on social media. The falsification involved their posts allegedly misrepresenting their trading positions and the potential price trajectories of specific securities. By trusting these personas, their social-media followers were allegedly misled to view the defendants' posts as legitimate investment advice, which led to increased participation in the fraudulent scheme.

## The Original Indictment and Criminal Statutes Involved

When these allegations were filed, the defendant collectively faced specific charges that required them to face the U.S. justice system. The indictment was a superseding one, meaning it updated or replaced prior charges against the specific defendant. The defendants were indicted for conspiracy to commit securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, as well as multiple counts of securities fraud in violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 and § 2. Securities fraud is a federal crime distinct from general fraud because it specifically targets deceptive practices connected to the purchase or sale of securities. The scale of the alleged operation was significant; the indictment claims the defendants profited approximately $114 million from their scheme. At the time of the proceedings, one defendant, Knight, pleaded guilty. However, the remaining defendants, including the one central to the appeal, moved to dismiss the indictment, leading the District Court to enter a ruling before the case reached this Circuit Court. This challenge was not to the facts initially, but to the legal sufficiency of the document serving as the basis for trial.

## District Court Rationale for Dismissal

The procedural history turned on a specific legal interpretation regarding what constitutes a protected property interest under the federal fraud statutes. The District Court dismissed the indictment and concluded that it failed to state an offense. The judge’s reasoning focused on the nature of the harm the indictment alleged was intended. The District Court noted that the indictment failed to allege that the defendants sought to deprive their victims of any "traditional property interest." Instead, the District Court characterized the defendants' alleged intent as the deprivation of followers of "potentially valuable economic information." In the eyes of the District Court, this distinction was fatal; the court reasoned that while economic information is valuable, it is not necessarily a property interest that the federal deception statutes are designed to protect in the same manner as money. Consequently, the court held that describing a scheme to deprive victims of information, rather than property, was legally insufficient to support a conviction for securities fraud under the relevant statutes.

# Chapter 2: Standard of Review and Statutory Framework

## De Novo Review of Indictment Sufficiency
In *United States v. Constantinescu*, the court first addressed the procedural standard it applied to evaluate the district court’s dismissal of the indictment. Because the indictment’s sufficiency is a question of law, the Fifth Circuit reviews such challenges de novo. This means the appellate judges do not defer to the district court’s assessment of the document; instead, they independently examine the text of the indictment. Under this approach, the court must take the allegations within the indictment as true and determine solely whether an offense has been stated. This de novo standard ensures that the constitutional protections afforded to criminal defendants are enforced strictly, as the indictment is the first document filed in a criminal case. In *Constantinescu*, the district court had concluded the indictment was insufficient, yet the appellate court treated the dismissal as an error requiring fresh analysis, a step necessary to ensure that the procedural bars do not swallow the substantive merits of the charge.

## The Three-Prong Requirement for Sufficient Indictments
Beyond the standard of review, the *Constantinescu* court outlined the specific functional requirements a federal indictment must satisfy to be constitutionally adequate. Relying on established Fifth Circuit precedent, the opinion identifies three non-exhaustive prongs that govern the sufficiency of an indictment. First, the indictment must enumerate each prima facie element of the charged offense; without this, the jury lacks the factual basis required by the Constitution. Second, the document must fairly inform the defendant of the charges filed against him, allowing the accused to prepare a defense. Third, a sufficient indictment must provide the defendant with a double jeopardy defense against future prosecutions for the same offense. These requirements collectively ensure that the grand jury and the court do not arbitrarily define crimes. The *Constantinescu* opinion notes that the appellate court must determine if the indictment alleges each material element of 18 U.S.C. § 1348 to satisfy these constitutional mandates, specifically noting that while it is a friendly review, the court accepts the face-value of the indictment's allegations.

## Text and Scope of 18 U.S.C. § 1348
The substantive legal analysis in *Constantinescu* centers on the Firearms Fraud statute, 18 U.S.C. § 1348. The court quoted the text of the relevant sections, which prohibits schemes to "defraud any person in connection with . . . any security." The statute explicitly criminalizes two types of fraudulent conduct: schemes to defraud a person in connection with a security, and schemes to obtain "money or property" by means of false or fraudulent pretenses in connection with the purchase or sale of a security. The district court in *Constantinescu* had focused heavily on the notion that the defendants deprived their followers of "valuable economic information" rather than "traditional property." This narrowing view required the appellate court to define the scope of the statute more expansively, as the text does not explicitly exclude frauds related to information, provided a property interest is implicated. The opinion required the students or readers to look closely at the phrasing "in connection with," which serves as the bridge between general deception and specific economic markets.

## Borrowing Doctrine from Mail and Wire Fraud Statutes
To interpret the limits of section § 1348, the court looked outside the statute itself to the federal mail and wire fraud statutes. Because there is scant caselaw in the Fifth Circuit specifically construing § 1348 independently, the court relied on the "borrowing doctrine." Under this approach, courts have given terms similar treatment across these fraud statutes, often relying on mail and wire fraud cases to analyze securities fraud charges. This borrowing doctrine is pragmatic, allowing the judiciary to leverage a rich body of law already established for other types of fraud to illuminate the meaning of securities-specific provisions. The opinion explains that by looking to similar statutory schemes, the Fifth Circuit can determine what constitutes a "scheme to defraud" under § 1348. This borrowing is critical in *Constantinescu* because it allows the appellate court to apply the reasoning from Supreme Court cases like *Kousisis* and *Ciminelli*, which, though decided in different contexts, clarify the boundaries of "property" and "scheme" in fraud statutes generally.

## Core Elements: Scheme to Defraud and Intent to Defraud
The court further disassembled § 1348 into its core elements, which a prosecution must establish: a "scheme to defraud" and an "intent to defraud." For a scheme to defraud, the indictment must allege a material misrepresentation intended to deceive others to obtain something of value, such as money. The opinion emphasizes that deception must be coupled with a desire to obtain value; it is not enough to simply lie. Regarding intent to defraud, the court clarified two necessary components: an intent to deceive and an intent to cause harm from the deceit. However, *Constantinescu* grappled significantly with the definition of "harm" and "property." The court distinguished between the "right-to-control theory," which was rejected by the Supreme Court in *Ciminelli* because "valuable economic information" is not a property interest, and the "fraudulent-inducement theory," which is accepted. Under the fraudulent-inducement theory, a defendant violates the fraud statutes by using a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over money or property. In *Constantinescu*, the defendants intended to "pump" prices by lying to followers, inducing them to part with money. The court held that because the intent was to obtain the victim's money or property—specifically the money used to buy the securities—the indictment met the high bar for a scheme to defraud, regardless of whether the victims suffered a net loss. Furthermore, the court ruled that the defendant's intent to profit, rather than cause harm, does not negate the intent to defraud, as derived from *Kousisis*. The district court’s dismissal was reversed because the indictment alleged that the object of the fraud was to obtain money, even if the victims received securities equal in market value.

# Chapter 3: The 'Scheme to Defraud' Element: Protected Property Interests

In *United States v. Constantinescu*, 2025 WL ______, the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit addressed the sufficiency of an indictment charging defendants with securities fraud pursuant to a “pump and dump” scheme. A critical issue before the court was whether the district court properly dismissed the indictment under the reasoning that it lacked allegations of a deprivation of a protected “property interest.” The appeals court clarified that while fraud statutes do not permit convictions based solely on depriving victims of valuable economic information, they do support convictions where the scheme involves a fraudulent inducement to part with actual money or property. This chapter analyzes the Fifth Circuit’s reasoning to explain how recent Supreme Court precedent distinguishes between prohibited theories of fraud and those that adequately allege a protected property interest.

## Defense Argument Against the Right-to-Control Theory

The district court declined to issue the indictment against the defendants, ultimately concluding that the indictment failed to state an offense under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. At the motion stage, the court reasoned that the defendants sought to deprive their social media followers of potentially valuable economic information, rather than scheming to deprive victims of a traditional property interest. The defense articulation of this point centers on the nature of the alleged harm. Specifically, the defendants argued that the district court properly dismissed the indictment because the indictment did not allege a scheme to deprive their followers of a protected property interest. This argument rests on the premise that the fraud was targeted at information rather than tangible assets. In other words, the defense contended the indictment merely alleged an intent to deceive to manipulate stock prices, without alleging that the victims were tricked into parting with their own money or property in the manner necessary for securities fraud. The defendants conceded the indictment adequately alleged deception but maintained that the deception did not implicate a protected property interest because the fraud targeted the followers' access to financial data rather than the funds themselves.

## Limitations Established in Ciminelli v. United States

To understand the Fifth Circuit’s rejection of the defense argument, one must consider the Supreme Court’s decision in *Ciminelli v. United States*, which is control the statutes mentioned in the opinion. In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court held that a fraud conviction cannot be based solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information. The rejection of the “right-to-control” theory is central to this limitation. Under that theory, a government charges fraud by showing that the defendant schemed to deprive a victim of potentially valuable economic information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions. The Supreme Court determined this theory did not work because the federal fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests. Under the right-to-control theory, the Court found that the victim’s property was merely “the right to control [his] assets,” rather than a protected property interest itself. Consequently, the Court ruled that the right-to-control theory could not form the basis for a fraud conviction because the right to information alone does not qualify as a traditional property interest under the statutory scheme.

## The Fraudulent-Inducement Theory Explained

While the right-to-control theory was rejected in *Ciminelli*, the Second Circuit opinion in *United States v. Constantinescu* identifies a permissible alternative known as the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. This theory is derived from Supreme Court decisions in *Kousisis* and *Ciminelli*. The Court clarified that this theory involves the use of a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property. This concept fundamentally shifts the focus from the victim's access to information to the victim's transfer of assets. Under the fraudulent-inducement view, a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property’.” This creates a bright-line rule that the victim need not suffer an economic loss from the transaction; rather, the intent to obtain money or property regardless of potential loss to the victim is sufficient to constitute a fraud scheme. This theory is distinct from the right-to-control theory because it protects money and property directly, rather than the informational capacity of the victim.

## Distinction Between Deprivation of Information and Property

The *Constantinescu* case turns on the distinction between the injury caused to the followers. The Fifth Circuit analyzed the indictment under the False Pretenses elements of securities fraud statutes. The opinion notes that defendants must show a material misrepresentation intended to deceive. While Ciminelli stands for the principle that depriving individuals of potentially valuable economic information alone is not enough, the nature of the transaction determines which protection applies. The indictment in *Constantinescu* did not mention the right-to-control theory; rather, it alleged a fraudulent-inducement theory. Unlike the right-to-control theory rejected in Ciminelli, the fraudulent-inducement theory advanced here is said to “protect money and property.” The distinction is critical because it limits the scope of fraud to tangible property transfers or contract obligations involving property, rather than mere informational advantages. If the alleged harm was purely informational, the indictment would fail, but if the alleged harm was financial (i.e., giving up money for securities), it satisfies the property interest requirement despite the Supreme Court’s cautions in *Ciminelli*.

## Application of the Fraudulent-Inducement Theory to the Indictment

Applying the fraudulent-inducement theory to the specific charges in *Constantinescu*, the appeals court found the indictment sufficient because it alleged a scheme to induce followers to purchase securities. The Supreme Court further relevance in *Kousisis* indicates that this covers schemes where a defendant provides something in return. In *Kousisis*, the Court concluded a defendant may be liable even when the scheme provides something of equal value. This reinforces the appellate court's decision that the fraudulent-inducement theory is complete when the defendant is liable in fraud even when he “provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return.” Under the indictment, defendants posted false and misleading information regarding a security's potential price so that investors were induced to purchase the security. Because the fraud statutes do not require actual economic harm, the alleged receipt of market-value securities does not obviate the fraud charges, provided the object was to obtain money. The indictment’s allegation that defendants pumped and dumped to gain a profit showed they “had money in mind,” satisfying the requirement that they possessed an intent to defraud rather than simply harm the victims' bottom line. Thus, the court reversed the dismissal and remanded the case for proceedings consistent with this opinion.

# Chapter 4: The 'Intent to Defraud' Element and Economic Harm

## Defense Argument Against Harm Requirement
The central dispute in this appeal concerned whether the stolen indictment met the standard for stating a criminal offense under the securities fraud statutes. The district court had dismissed the indictment, concluding that it lacked the necessary factual allegations to establish a crime. The court determined that the indictment failed to allege that the defendants schemed to deprive investors of a traditional property interest. Instead, the district court interpreted the indictment as alleging that defendants merely sought to deprive their followers of valuable economic information, which the court found insufficient to constitute a "scheme to defraud." This legal distinction is critical because federal fraud statutes, including those applicable to securities, protect traditional property interests. While it is true that a defendant cannot be convicted of fraud for simply depriving an individual of potentially valuable economic information alone, this opinion clarifies that the indictment in this proceeding went further than a mere deprivation of information. The appellate court recognized that the indictment actually alleged a scheme to defraud followers of money through the specific mechanism of inducing them to purchase securities, thereby distinguishing the case from prior dismissals based on non-property harms.

## Precedent from Kousisis v. United States
To understand the appellate court’s rejection of the district court’s reasoning, students must understand the binding precedent set by recent Supreme Court decisions, specifically *Kousisis* and *Ciminelli*. In *Ciminelli*, the Supreme Court held that a fraud conviction cannot be based solely on the deprivation of valuable economic information. The *Ciminelli* Court rejected the "right-to-control theory," which posits that a victim's property is the right to control their assets. The Supreme Court clarified that the federal fraud statutes protect only traditional property interests; therefore, the right-to-control theory cannot form the basis of a conviction. In *Constantinescu*, the court applied this distinction by noting that the indictment did not rely on the rejected right-to-control theory. Instead, it relied on the "fraudulent-inducement theory," under which a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over money or property. By confirming that *Ciminelli* protects money and property rather than just information, the *Constantinescu* court utilized recent precedent to validate the indictment's focus on the flow of funds rather than the state of investor knowledge.

## Profit Motive vs. Economic Loss to Victim
The second major barrier to conviction often raised in fraud cases is the inquiry into whether the defendant intended to cause economic loss to the victim. The defendants in this case argued that the indictment failed to allege an intent to defraud because it described an intent to profit from their scheme rather than an intent to harm any specific victim’s bottom line. They contended that the fraud statutes required the victim to suffer economic loss. However, the court in *Constantinescus* relied on the Supreme Court’s decision in *Kousisis* to reject this view. In *Kousisis*, the Court held that the fraud statutes do not require a victim to suffer an economic loss from a defendant’s scheme. The Court reasoned that a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he "schem[es] to obtain the victim's money or property, regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." In the context of securities fraud, this means that the defendant's goal to generate profit for themselves is legally sufficient to establish the intent to defraud, provided that profit motive is achieved through deception that induces the victim to part with money.

## Intent to Cause Harm via Inducement
The application of *Kousisis* to the facts of this case turns on how the deception was executed. The opinion explains that a fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses, regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss. In this scheme, the defendants posted false and misleading information about securities so that other investors were induced to purchase the security. This specific factual allegation aligns with the fraudulent-inducement theory validated in *Kousisis*. Under this theory, a crime is committed when the defendant seeks to obtain the victim's money or property, often in exchange for something of value, even if the provided value is illusory. The court noted that defendants' object was to obtain money, and they did so by fraudulently inducing their followers to purchase securities. Therefore, the indictment’s allegation that defendants "pumped and dumped" to gain a profit shows that they "had money in mind," satisfying the intent requirement without needing to prove that the investors paid more than the securities were worth in a broader economic sense.

## Holding and Reversal of Dismissal
Based on the analysis of statutory elements and the Supreme Court's recent precedent, the Fifth Circuit concluded that the district court’s dismissal was legally erroneous. The indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme and intent to defraud because it describes a failure to obtain a specific property interest (money) through deception. Consequently, the appellate court reversed the district court's dismissal of the indictment and remanded the case for proceedings consistent with this opinion. This ruling reinforces the principle that federal fraud statutes punish the deceptive act of inducing victims to part with property, rather than punishing specific outcomes regarding the victim's net wealth. By reversing the dismissal, the court ensured that the defendants could face trial for securities fraud charges where the core allegation is the manipulative scheme to purchase assets through lies, rather than whether the scheme resulted in a unique property deprivation or a measurable financial loss in the victims' portfolios.
