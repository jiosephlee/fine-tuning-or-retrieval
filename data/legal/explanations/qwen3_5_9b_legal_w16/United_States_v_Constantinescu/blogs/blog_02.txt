Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# The Death of the 'Loss' Requirement: Integrating Kousisis Into 5th Circuit Fraud Jurisprudence

## Introduction: The Post-Ciminelli Landscape

The Supreme Court’s decisions in *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States* (the latter appearing in the just-released 2025 Term opinion) fundamentally altered the landscape of fraud litigation, striping away requirements that favored broad litigation of information-based harms. Nowhere is this jurisprudential shift more tangible than in *United States v. Constantinescu*, a recent Fifth Circuit decision that serves as a roadmap for lower courts enforcing these mandates. In a unanimous opinion, Judge Kurt Engelhardt reversed a district court’s dismissal of a securities fraud indictment, cementing the "fraudulent-inducement" theory while explicitly rejecting the argument that victims must suffer measurable financial loss to sustain a conviction. This matters significantly because it shrinks the safe harbor for those engaged in "pump and dump" schemes, removing the defense that their followers received securities that helped them profit or that the defendants' schemes were merely profitable rather than harmful.

## The Information vs. Money Distinction

The crux of the controversy in *Constantinescu* centered on the nature of the property interest at stake in online investment fraud. The district court had grounded its dismissal on the reasoning that the indictment failed to allege a scheme depriving victims of a "traditional property interest" because the harm described was the deprivation of "potentially valuable economic information." The defendant argued this fell squarely under the "right-to-control theory" that the Supreme Court squarely rejected in *Ciminelli*. However, the Fifth Circuit’s analysis in *Constantinescu* neatly distinguishes this from the category of cases *Ciminelli* intended to bar.

Judge Engelhardt’s opinion highlights that the indictment here was not charged on a theory of control over information, but rather on a "fraudulent-inducement theory." To allege a scheme to defraud under this theory, the government must show that the defendant used material misstatements to trick a victim into a contract requiring the handing over of money or property. Here, the alleged misrepresentations induced followers to actually purchase securities. The Court emphasizes that the followers parted with money they could have otherwise kept. By framing the scheme as one that steals money rather than merely depriving followers of the "right to be informed," the Fifth Circuit distinguishes the *Constantinescu* facts from the policy considerations underpinning *Ciminelli*. This is a critical distinction; it allows courts to criminalize trading manipulation that relies on misrepresentations to extract capital from victims, provided those representations are used to secure that transaction conduct.

## Rejection of the Economic Loss Defense

Beyond the nature of the property interest, perhaps the most contentious doctrinal point is the requirement of victim loss. The Supreme Court in *Kousisis* recently clarified that the fraudulent-inducement theory applies regardless of whether the defendant sought to leave the victim economically worse off. The defendants in *Constantinescu* argued that the indictment failed because the scheme aimed to enrich the defendants rather than harm the followers. The Fifth Circuit readily adopts the reasoning of *Kousisis* to reject this defense.

The logic rests on the notion that fraud statutes protect against the deprivation of money or property itself, not a victim's "bottom line" at any given second. If a defendant tricks a victim into selling a stock for five dollars when it is actually worth ten dollars, or perhaps in this specific scenario, into buying shares at an inflated price to offload them, the money has changed hands based on deceit. The Fifth Circuit rejects the idea that the victim's receipt of securities constitutes a neutral transaction. Even if the securities provided were of equal "market value," the conviction relies on the fact that the victim entrusted their capital based on deception. This line of reasoning eliminates the need for prosecutors to prove that the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss, focusing instead on the deception that facilitated the transfer of funds. The "intent to profit" of the defendant is a sufficient indicator of intent to defraud, as long as it is linked to the fraudulent acquisition of funds from the victim.

## Doctrinal Implications for Digital Assets and Funding

The immediate doctrinal implication of *Constantinescu* is to clarify the boundaries of federal fraud statutes in contexts where property transfer is digital. The decision effectively closes the door on the "right-to-control" defense when a fraudulent transaction results in an actual transfer of assets. For the Fifth Circuit, this harmonizes its securities fraud jurisprudence with the Supreme Court’s broader fraud interpretation. However, the broader implications for investors and marketplace platforms are significant. By ruling that a strict economic loss requirement is unnecessary for a securities fraud indictment, the court expands the scope of exposure for online influencers and platforms that rely on social proof and investment advice.

This also raises practical concerns regarding the "safe" strategies of high-frequency traders or schemes that pass value through but use intermediaries to obscure the flow of fraud. The Fifth Circuit in *Constantinescu* notes that the defendants sought to profit, and in a "pump and dump" scheme, the fraud is complete when the victims are induced to part with money for the defendant's gain. This suggests that even if a trader's own portfolio does not suffer a net loss, their manipulation of the market for illicit gain could be the crime. The Fifth Circuit's reliance on *Baker* and *Smith* to show that the fraud statutes do not impose a "mirror image" requirement further solidifies this aggressive stance. If a court adopts this reasoning, the defense of inflated but profitable transactions for followers becomes perilously thin, shifting the focus entirely to the authenticity of the information imparted to the market.

## Conclusion

*Constantinescu* is a critical refinement of how federal fraud statutes apply to modern securities markets. By explicitly rejecting the "information deprivation" defense in favor of the "property transfer" theory, the Fifth Circuit ensures that *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* are interpreted as limiting claims of control over intangible rights, not shielding asset inflations. The ruling confirms that the intent to defraud is satisfied by the intent to acquire money through deception, regardless of whether the victim ultimately incurs a net economic loss. For defense attorneys, this signals that the viability of a challenge to securities fraud indictments now rests almost entirely on the materiality of the misrepresentation, not on the complicated math of the victim’s transactional outcome. As the circuit courts begin to apply this framework, we can expect a wave of prosecutions to focus heavily on the mechanics of inducement rather than the aftermath of financial performance.
