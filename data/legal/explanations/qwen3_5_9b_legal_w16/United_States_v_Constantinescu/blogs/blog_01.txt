Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Beyond Valuable Information: Construing the Property Element of Fraud Statutes After Ciminelli

The Supreme Court’s 2023 decision in *Ciminelli* fundamentally altered the landscape of federal fraud prosecutions by rejecting the “right-to-control” theory. In doing so, the Justines clarified that fraud statutes protect traditional property interests, not merely valuable economic information. Just a couple of weeks ago, in *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit applied this new boundary data to a high-profile modern issue: social media “pump and dump” schemes. The ruling matters because it reassures prosecutors that digital influencers charging followers with stock fraud do not need to prove plaintiffs suffered a net economic loss after the stock was sold. Conversely, the defendants lost a powerful shield, and now know that guiding investors through a deceptive transaction is sufficient for a property deprivation charge, even if the information they gained was arguably “valuable” without the scheme.

## The Reasoning: Distinguishing Information from Exchange

The crux of the *Constantinescu* dispute centered on whether a superseding indictment claiming a securities fraud conspiracy sufficiently stated a crime. The district court had dismissed the indictment, reasoning that the government failed to allege a scheme to deprive victims of a “traditional property interest.” Since social-media followers were misled about trading positions rather than told to part with cash directly, the district court framed the alleged deprivation as purely informational. Judge Engelhardt recognized that the prosecution option for this type of data was legally narrow.

The Fifth Circuit noted that the securities fraud statute borrows heavily from mail and wire fraud precedents, requiring a “scheme to defraud” with an “intent to defraud.” The court emphasized that the scheme must involve obtaining something of value, such as money, from the victims. Here, the indictment specifically alleged that defendants purchased a security and then posted false and misleading information on social media to induce followers to purchase the same security. This created an artificial price inflation allowing the defendants to sell at a profit. Judge Engelhardt found that because the followers were induced to actually purchase securities—a transfer of money—the indictment satisfied the property interest requirement.

This distinction is vital because it avoids the *Ciminelli* trap. The court explicitly noted that *Ciminelli* barred convictions based solely on depriving victims of potentially valuable economic information. However, *Ciminelli* was rooted in a relationship where the victim was denied information necessary to control their assets (the “right-to-control” theory). In contrast, the *Constantinescu* defendants did not just tell followers what to do; the indictment alleged they actively sought to trick them into buying stocks to part with their money. The court correctly identified that the scheme did not rely on denying subscribers access to information, but rather using that information to initiate a transaction where money changed hands.

## The Kousisis Distinction and Intent to Defraud

The second major hurdle the defendants faced was the "intent to defraud" element. Defendant attorneys argued that the intent was to enrich themselves, not to harm any victim. The Fifth Circuit relied on the Supreme Court’s recent decision in *Kousisis v. United States* to dismantle this argument. In *Kousisis*, the Court clarified that fraud statutes do not require evidence of actual economic loss for a victim. A defendant can be liable under fraudulent inducement theory even if the victim receives something of equal value.

The Fifth Circuit applied this logic to the indictment, which alleged that the defendants intended to obtain followers’ money via misrepresentations. Since the fraud statutes protect the "money or property" of the victim, the goal of the fraud is the transfer of that property, not the subsequent impact of that transfer. As long as the deception caused the victims to part with money, the fraud was complete. Judge Engelhardt noted that the defendants intended to profit, and the indictment alleged the fraudulently induced followers to purchase securities. This proved the object was to obtain money, not to harm.

The court rejected the notion that a "mirror image" requirement exists where the victim's loss must supply the defendant's gain. In *Constantinescu*, while the investors might have purchased stocks at fair market value (or potentially gained while the pump was active), the district court dismissal was for the reason that the scheme relied on fraudulent inducement ("money in mind") rather than just economic harm. The Fifth Circuit held that the statutory prohibition against property deprivation was satisfied by the scheme to obtain money, regardless of the net pecuniary result for the investor.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The *Constantinescu* decision offers important doctrinal clarity for prosecutors, defense attorneys, and compliance officers alike. By cementing the distinction between the "right-to-control" theory and "fraudulent-inducement" theory in the context of securities fraud, the Fifth Circuit signals a specific path forward for criminal charges in the age of digital finance. Prior to *Ciminelli*, defense lawyers could argue that influencers giving financial advice was merely information sharing. While *Constantinescu* is a criminal indictment sufficiency ruling, it underscores the fifth circuit’s view that settlement, disclosure, and promotion strategies built on deception to generate transactions are violative of the core "property" interests protected by the criminal fraud statutes.

The decision effectively removes the "net loss" defense from the mixed bag of litigation tools available to defense counsel. In civil securities actions, investors often argue that the loss in equity equals the gain for the wrongdoer. *Constantinescu* makes clear that in a criminal context, the moment the deception induces a transaction, the crime is consummated. This limits the ability of downstream corporate fraud cases to argue that the fraud was "socially neutral" or net-zero for the victim.

Furthermore, the ruling supports the expansion of federal fraud statutes to cover intangible assets without giving rise to new Article III standing problems. If the fraud statute protects property, and stocks are property, inducing investors to buy based on lies is fraud even if the "price" reflects valid market data at the moment of purchase. This is particularly relevant for crypto exchanges and social media platforms where the asset class is speculative and the loss potential is often high.

In sum, *Constantinescu* serves as a bridge between the *Ciminelli* era and the practical realities of digital market manipulation. It affirms that while information alone is not property, information that converts into money through deception is the subject of a fraud conviction. This provides a strong tool for the government to pursue this new class of high-frequency, high-volume social media fraud, ensuring that the "pump and dump" scheme remains a viable topic for criminal prosecution.
