Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Pumping the Indictment: Prosecutorial Strategy for Social Media Securities Fraud

# United States v. Constantinescu

## The Supreme Court's New Framework for Fraud

Social media "pump and dump" schemes have been a staple of white-collar enforcement for decades, but the landscape shifts dramatically when the Supreme Court clarifies the boundaries of fraud liability. In *United States v. Constantinescu*, a 2025 Fifth Circuit decision, the privacy of the indictment—a technical line of attack that not uncommonly shuts down cases—is resolved in light of the Supreme Court’s recent forays into fraud theory. While the case concerns the standard of a federal indictment, its doctrinal reliance on *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* offers a roadmap for future prosecutions of digital-era financial misconduct. The core takeaway is clear: prosecutors can charge fraud financial advice without needing to prove the victim suffered net pecuniary loss, provided the indictment plausibly alleges a fraudulent inducement to exchange money for property.

The reason *Constantinescu* matters extends beyond the specific defendants on the stand-pops. The district court below had dismissed the indictment because the government allegedly only sought to deprive followers of "valuable economic information" rather than a "traditional property interest." This ruling creates a specific precedent for how lower courts interpret the scope of Section 1348 (securities fraud). By affirming that the deception caused a purchaser to transfer funds, the Fifth Circuit effectively dismantles a defense common in modern financial crimes where the victim walks away with something of value, but the transaction is tainted by false pretenses.

## The Doctrinal Shift: From Information to Inducement

To understand the 5th Circuit's reasoning, one must appreciate the Supreme Court's recent bifurcation of fraud theories. As Judge Engelhardt notes, the case rests heavily on the distinction between the "right-to-control" theory and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. The *Ciminelli* decision explicitly rejected the right-to-control theory, holding that depriving a victim of valuable economic information is not enough to constitute fraud under federal statutes because the right itself isn't a "traditional property interest." In *Ciminelli*, the Court refused to extend federal fraud protections to the mere denial of information necessary for decision-making.

This left prosecutors and courts searching for a viable lawsuit model. That search concluded with *Kousisis* in 2025, which the Fifth Circuit relies on heavily here. *Kousisis* validated the fraudulent-inducement theory, holding that a defendant can violate fraud statutes if they use material misrepresentations to trick a victim into a transaction requiring money, regardless of whether the fraudster sought to cause the victim a net pecuniary loss.

## Applying the Framework to Social Media Fraud

The Fifth Circuit's application of this new Supreme Court jurisprudence in *Constantinescu* is where the practical utility lies. The defendants argued that the indictment was defective because it failed to allege they intended to harm their followers; they intended to profit from the sale of inflated securities. This argument rests on the premise that fraud requires intent to injure the victim. Rejecting this view, the court points to the indictment's specific language: that defendants posted false information "so that other investors were induced to purchase the security."

Scrutinizing the opinion closely reveals that the deficit of the indictment was not the absence of harm allegations, but rather the absence of transaction allegations. The district court focused on the deprivation of knowledge. The Fifth Circuit corrected the record by focusing on the acquisition of the stock. The indictment alleges followers were tricked into spending money through false pretenses. Because *Kousisis* clarifies that the goal of the fraud is the "obtaining of money or property" rather than the inflicting of harm, the defendants' own profit motive satisfies the intent-to-fraud element without needing to allege a forfeiture on the victim's end. This was key in distinguishing the current case from a right-to-control scenario where the victim might have received a "deal" that was simply an information void.

## Implications for Litigation Strategy

From a practical standpoint, *Constantinescu* offers relief to district judges who had been using broad "right-to-control" arguments to dismiss indictments, but it also sets a higher bar for how allegations are drafted. Prosecutors can no longer rely on vague claims of deception without explicitly linking that deception to the transfer of funds or property. As demonstrated here, the indictment must allege a scheme that induced the victim to "part with their money."

This is particularly significant given the nature of the race between influencers and regulators. Misleading claims are common in the tails of trading like pump and dump. While some instances may be treated as civil regulatory violations, they don't always rise to the level of crimes without a financial victim. This ruling suggests that the government can now proceed with criminal charges as long as the defendant sold the story of their trade using false pretenses.

The defense's argument that they didn't seek to harm the victim was rejected as a distinction without a difference, provided the transaction was fraudulent. This could open the door to broader enforcement actions against financial influencers who exaggerate holdings. If the prosecution can prove the followers bought stock based on lies, the followers met the definition of victims, regardless of whether they profited or lost money on a subsequent sale.

## The "Net Loss" Defense and Downstream Effects

A broader doctrinal implication of *Constantinescu* is the severance of the link between actual economic loss and criminal liability in fraud. Historically, defendants often argue that because the victim was compensated (e.g., they received equal value in return) or even profited from the transaction, the crime is negated. The court here points to *Kousisis* to underscore that the fraud statutes protect the integrity of the transaction itself, not the economic outcome of the transaction.

In future proceedings, defense counsel will need to focus intensely on whether the indictment alleges a genuine inducement to purchase rather than a right-to-control deprivation, or whether the scheme actually involved a transaction. The Ripple effect of *Constantinescu* means that district courts must ensure indictments explicitly describe the quid pro quo nature of the fraud. If the government alleges that the innocent follower bought a security, the defense cannot argue that this was merely a deprivation of information. This ruling also signals to the SEC and DOJ that they don't need to prove the "victim" lost money to secure an indictment; the objective of defrauding them out of their funds is enough.

## Conclusion

United States v. Constantinescu is a procedural win for prosecutors, but also a doctrinal cleaving of the wire and mail fraud huddle. By aligning 5th Circuit practice with the 2025 Supreme Court landscape, the court protects the indicts based on fraudulent inducement. For regulators and prosecutors looking into the increasingly opaque social media financial landscape, this case means their ammunition is potent, provided they frame the allegations correctly. The damage done to the integrity of the market doesn't have to be economic loss; it just has to be the cost of the lie.
