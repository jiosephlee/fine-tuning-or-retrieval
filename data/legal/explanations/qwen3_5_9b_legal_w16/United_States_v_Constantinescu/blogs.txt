Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

# Beyond Valuable Information: Construing the Property Element of Fraud Statutes After Ciminelli

The Supreme Court’s 2023 decision in *Ciminelli* fundamentally altered the landscape of federal fraud prosecutions by rejecting the “right-to-control” theory. In doing so, the Justines clarified that fraud statutes protect traditional property interests, not merely valuable economic information. Just a couple of weeks ago, in *United States v. Constantinescu*, the Fifth Circuit applied this new boundary data to a high-profile modern issue: social media “pump and dump” schemes. The ruling matters because it reassures prosecutors that digital influencers charging followers with stock fraud do not need to prove plaintiffs suffered a net economic loss after the stock was sold. Conversely, the defendants lost a powerful shield, and now know that guiding investors through a deceptive transaction is sufficient for a property deprivation charge, even if the information they gained was arguably “valuable” without the scheme.

## The Reasoning: Distinguishing Information from Exchange

The crux of the *Constantinescu* dispute centered on whether a superseding indictment claiming a securities fraud conspiracy sufficiently stated a crime. The district court had dismissed the indictment, reasoning that the government failed to allege a scheme to deprive victims of a “traditional property interest.” Since social-media followers were misled about trading positions rather than told to part with cash directly, the district court framed the alleged deprivation as purely informational. Judge Engelhardt recognized that the prosecution option for this type of data was legally narrow.

The Fifth Circuit noted that the securities fraud statute borrows heavily from mail and wire fraud precedents, requiring a “scheme to defraud” with an “intent to defraud.” The court emphasized that the scheme must involve obtaining something of value, such as money, from the victims. Here, the indictment specifically alleged that defendants purchased a security and then posted false and misleading information on social media to induce followers to purchase the same security. This created an artificial price inflation allowing the defendants to sell at a profit. Judge Engelhardt found that because the followers were induced to actually purchase securities—a transfer of money—the indictment satisfied the property interest requirement.

This distinction is vital because it avoids the *Ciminelli* trap. The court explicitly noted that *Ciminelli* barred convictions based solely on depriving victims of potentially valuable economic information. However, *Ciminelli* was rooted in a relationship where the victim was denied information necessary to control their assets (the “right-to-control” theory). In contrast, the *Constantinescu* defendants did not just tell followers what to do; the indictment alleged they actively sought to trick them into buying stocks to part with their money. The court correctly identified that the scheme did not rely on denying subscribers access to information, but rather using that information to initiate a transaction where money changed hands.

## The Kousisis Distinction and Intent to Defraud

The second major hurdle the defendants faced was the "intent to defraud" element. Defendant attorneys argued that the intent was to enrich themselves, not to harm any victim. The Fifth Circuit relied on the Supreme Court’s recent decision in *Kousisis v. United States* to dismantle this argument. In *Kousisis*, the Court clarified that fraud statutes do not require evidence of actual economic loss for a victim. A defendant can be liable under fraudulent inducement theory even if the victim receives something of equal value.

The Fifth Circuit applied this logic to the indictment, which alleged that the defendants intended to obtain followers’ money via misrepresentations. Since the fraud statutes protect the "money or property" of the victim, the goal of the fraud is the transfer of that property, not the subsequent impact of that transfer. As long as the deception caused the victims to part with money, the fraud was complete. Judge Engelhardt noted that the defendants intended to profit, and the indictment alleged the fraudulently induced followers to purchase securities. This proved the object was to obtain money, not to harm.

The court rejected the notion that a "mirror image" requirement exists where the victim's loss must supply the defendant's gain. In *Constantinescu*, while the investors might have purchased stocks at fair market value (or potentially gained while the pump was active), the district court dismissal was for the reason that the scheme relied on fraudulent inducement ("money in mind") rather than just economic harm. The Fifth Circuit held that the statutory prohibition against property deprivation was satisfied by the scheme to obtain money, regardless of the net pecuniary result for the investor.

## Doctrinal Implications and Downstream Effects

The *Constantinescu* decision offers important doctrinal clarity for prosecutors, defense attorneys, and compliance officers alike. By cementing the distinction between the "right-to-control" theory and "fraudulent-inducement" theory in the context of securities fraud, the Fifth Circuit signals a specific path forward for criminal charges in the age of digital finance. Prior to *Ciminelli*, defense lawyers could argue that influencers giving financial advice was merely information sharing. While *Constantinescu* is a criminal indictment sufficiency ruling, it underscores the fifth circuit’s view that settlement, disclosure, and promotion strategies built on deception to generate transactions are violative of the core "property" interests protected by the criminal fraud statutes.

The decision effectively removes the "net loss" defense from the mixed bag of litigation tools available to defense counsel. In civil securities actions, investors often argue that the loss in equity equals the gain for the wrongdoer. *Constantinescu* makes clear that in a criminal context, the moment the deception induces a transaction, the crime is consummated. This limits the ability of downstream corporate fraud cases to argue that the fraud was "socially neutral" or net-zero for the victim.

Furthermore, the ruling supports the expansion of federal fraud statutes to cover intangible assets without giving rise to new Article III standing problems. If the fraud statute protects property, and stocks are property, inducing investors to buy based on lies is fraud even if the "price" reflects valid market data at the moment of purchase. This is particularly relevant for crypto exchanges and social media platforms where the asset class is speculative and the loss potential is often high.

In sum, *Constantinescu* serves as a bridge between the *Ciminelli* era and the practical realities of digital market manipulation. It affirms that while information alone is not property, information that converts into money through deception is the subject of a fraud conviction. This provides a strong tool for the government to pursue this new class of high-frequency, high-volume social media fraud, ensuring that the "pump and dump" scheme remains a viable topic for criminal prosecution.



# The Death of the 'Loss' Requirement: Integrating Kousisis Into 5th Circuit Fraud Jurisprudence

## Introduction: The Post-Ciminelli Landscape

The Supreme Court’s decisions in *Ciminelli v. United States* and *Kousisis v. United States* (the latter appearing in the just-released 2025 Term opinion) fundamentally altered the landscape of fraud litigation, striping away requirements that favored broad litigation of information-based harms. Nowhere is this jurisprudential shift more tangible than in *United States v. Constantinescu*, a recent Fifth Circuit decision that serves as a roadmap for lower courts enforcing these mandates. In a unanimous opinion, Judge Kurt Engelhardt reversed a district court’s dismissal of a securities fraud indictment, cementing the "fraudulent-inducement" theory while explicitly rejecting the argument that victims must suffer measurable financial loss to sustain a conviction. This matters significantly because it shrinks the safe harbor for those engaged in "pump and dump" schemes, removing the defense that their followers received securities that helped them profit or that the defendants' schemes were merely profitable rather than harmful.

## The Information vs. Money Distinction

The crux of the controversy in *Constantinescu* centered on the nature of the property interest at stake in online investment fraud. The district court had grounded its dismissal on the reasoning that the indictment failed to allege a scheme depriving victims of a "traditional property interest" because the harm described was the deprivation of "potentially valuable economic information." The defendant argued this fell squarely under the "right-to-control theory" that the Supreme Court squarely rejected in *Ciminelli*. However, the Fifth Circuit’s analysis in *Constantinescu* neatly distinguishes this from the category of cases *Ciminelli* intended to bar.

Judge Engelhardt’s opinion highlights that the indictment here was not charged on a theory of control over information, but rather on a "fraudulent-inducement theory." To allege a scheme to defraud under this theory, the government must show that the defendant used material misstatements to trick a victim into a contract requiring the handing over of money or property. Here, the alleged misrepresentations induced followers to actually purchase securities. The Court emphasizes that the followers parted with money they could have otherwise kept. By framing the scheme as one that steals money rather than merely depriving followers of the "right to be informed," the Fifth Circuit distinguishes the *Constantinescu* facts from the policy considerations underpinning *Ciminelli*. This is a critical distinction; it allows courts to criminalize trading manipulation that relies on misrepresentations to extract capital from victims, provided those representations are used to secure that transaction conduct.

## Rejection of the Economic Loss Defense

Beyond the nature of the property interest, perhaps the most contentious doctrinal point is the requirement of victim loss. The Supreme Court in *Kousisis* recently clarified that the fraudulent-inducement theory applies regardless of whether the defendant sought to leave the victim economically worse off. The defendants in *Constantinescu* argued that the indictment failed because the scheme aimed to enrich the defendants rather than harm the followers. The Fifth Circuit readily adopts the reasoning of *Kousisis* to reject this defense.

The logic rests on the notion that fraud statutes protect against the deprivation of money or property itself, not a victim's "bottom line" at any given second. If a defendant tricks a victim into selling a stock for five dollars when it is actually worth ten dollars, or perhaps in this specific scenario, into buying shares at an inflated price to offload them, the money has changed hands based on deceit. The Fifth Circuit rejects the idea that the victim's receipt of securities constitutes a neutral transaction. Even if the securities provided were of equal "market value," the conviction relies on the fact that the victim entrusted their capital based on deception. This line of reasoning eliminates the need for prosecutors to prove that the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss, focusing instead on the deception that facilitated the transfer of funds. The "intent to profit" of the defendant is a sufficient indicator of intent to defraud, as long as it is linked to the fraudulent acquisition of funds from the victim.

## Doctrinal Implications for Digital Assets and Funding

The immediate doctrinal implication of *Constantinescu* is to clarify the boundaries of federal fraud statutes in contexts where property transfer is digital. The decision effectively closes the door on the "right-to-control" defense when a fraudulent transaction results in an actual transfer of assets. For the Fifth Circuit, this harmonizes its securities fraud jurisprudence with the Supreme Court’s broader fraud interpretation. However, the broader implications for investors and marketplace platforms are significant. By ruling that a strict economic loss requirement is unnecessary for a securities fraud indictment, the court expands the scope of exposure for online influencers and platforms that rely on social proof and investment advice.

This also raises practical concerns regarding the "safe" strategies of high-frequency traders or schemes that pass value through but use intermediaries to obscure the flow of fraud. The Fifth Circuit in *Constantinescu* notes that the defendants sought to profit, and in a "pump and dump" scheme, the fraud is complete when the victims are induced to part with money for the defendant's gain. This suggests that even if a trader's own portfolio does not suffer a net loss, their manipulation of the market for illicit gain could be the crime. The Fifth Circuit's reliance on *Baker* and *Smith* to show that the fraud statutes do not impose a "mirror image" requirement further solidifies this aggressive stance. If a court adopts this reasoning, the defense of inflated but profitable transactions for followers becomes perilously thin, shifting the focus entirely to the authenticity of the information imparted to the market.

## Conclusion

*Constantinescu* is a critical refinement of how federal fraud statutes apply to modern securities markets. By explicitly rejecting the "information deprivation" defense in favor of the "property transfer" theory, the Fifth Circuit ensures that *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* are interpreted as limiting claims of control over intangible rights, not shielding asset inflations. The ruling confirms that the intent to defraud is satisfied by the intent to acquire money through deception, regardless of whether the victim ultimately incurs a net economic loss. For defense attorneys, this signals that the viability of a challenge to securities fraud indictments now rests almost entirely on the materiality of the misrepresentation, not on the complicated math of the victim’s transactional outcome. As the circuit courts begin to apply this framework, we can expect a wave of prosecutions to focus heavily on the mechanics of inducement rather than the aftermath of financial performance.



# Pumping the Indictment: Prosecutorial Strategy for Social Media Securities Fraud

# United States v. Constantinescu

## The Supreme Court's New Framework for Fraud

Social media "pump and dump" schemes have been a staple of white-collar enforcement for decades, but the landscape shifts dramatically when the Supreme Court clarifies the boundaries of fraud liability. In *United States v. Constantinescu*, a 2025 Fifth Circuit decision, the privacy of the indictment—a technical line of attack that not uncommonly shuts down cases—is resolved in light of the Supreme Court’s recent forays into fraud theory. While the case concerns the standard of a federal indictment, its doctrinal reliance on *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* offers a roadmap for future prosecutions of digital-era financial misconduct. The core takeaway is clear: prosecutors can charge fraud financial advice without needing to prove the victim suffered net pecuniary loss, provided the indictment plausibly alleges a fraudulent inducement to exchange money for property.

The reason *Constantinescu* matters extends beyond the specific defendants on the stand-pops. The district court below had dismissed the indictment because the government allegedly only sought to deprive followers of "valuable economic information" rather than a "traditional property interest." This ruling creates a specific precedent for how lower courts interpret the scope of Section 1348 (securities fraud). By affirming that the deception caused a purchaser to transfer funds, the Fifth Circuit effectively dismantles a defense common in modern financial crimes where the victim walks away with something of value, but the transaction is tainted by false pretenses.

## The Doctrinal Shift: From Information to Inducement

To understand the 5th Circuit's reasoning, one must appreciate the Supreme Court's recent bifurcation of fraud theories. As Judge Engelhardt notes, the case rests heavily on the distinction between the "right-to-control" theory and the "fraudulent-inducement" theory. The *Ciminelli* decision explicitly rejected the right-to-control theory, holding that depriving a victim of valuable economic information is not enough to constitute fraud under federal statutes because the right itself isn't a "traditional property interest." In *Ciminelli*, the Court refused to extend federal fraud protections to the mere denial of information necessary for decision-making.

This left prosecutors and courts searching for a viable lawsuit model. That search concluded with *Kousisis* in 2025, which the Fifth Circuit relies on heavily here. *Kousisis* validated the fraudulent-inducement theory, holding that a defendant can violate fraud statutes if they use material misrepresentations to trick a victim into a transaction requiring money, regardless of whether the fraudster sought to cause the victim a net pecuniary loss.

## Applying the Framework to Social Media Fraud

The Fifth Circuit's application of this new Supreme Court jurisprudence in *Constantinescu* is where the practical utility lies. The defendants argued that the indictment was defective because it failed to allege they intended to harm their followers; they intended to profit from the sale of inflated securities. This argument rests on the premise that fraud requires intent to injure the victim. Rejecting this view, the court points to the indictment's specific language: that defendants posted false information "so that other investors were induced to purchase the security."

Scrutinizing the opinion closely reveals that the deficit of the indictment was not the absence of harm allegations, but rather the absence of transaction allegations. The district court focused on the deprivation of knowledge. The Fifth Circuit corrected the record by focusing on the acquisition of the stock. The indictment alleges followers were tricked into spending money through false pretenses. Because *Kousisis* clarifies that the goal of the fraud is the "obtaining of money or property" rather than the inflicting of harm, the defendants' own profit motive satisfies the intent-to-fraud element without needing to allege a forfeiture on the victim's end. This was key in distinguishing the current case from a right-to-control scenario where the victim might have received a "deal" that was simply an information void.

## Implications for Litigation Strategy

From a practical standpoint, *Constantinescu* offers relief to district judges who had been using broad "right-to-control" arguments to dismiss indictments, but it also sets a higher bar for how allegations are drafted. Prosecutors can no longer rely on vague claims of deception without explicitly linking that deception to the transfer of funds or property. As demonstrated here, the indictment must allege a scheme that induced the victim to "part with their money."

This is particularly significant given the nature of the race between influencers and regulators. Misleading claims are common in the tails of trading like pump and dump. While some instances may be treated as civil regulatory violations, they don't always rise to the level of crimes without a financial victim. This ruling suggests that the government can now proceed with criminal charges as long as the defendant sold the story of their trade using false pretenses.

The defense's argument that they didn't seek to harm the victim was rejected as a distinction without a difference, provided the transaction was fraudulent. This could open the door to broader enforcement actions against financial influencers who exaggerate holdings. If the prosecution can prove the followers bought stock based on lies, the followers met the definition of victims, regardless of whether they profited or lost money on a subsequent sale.

## The "Net Loss" Defense and Downstream Effects

A broader doctrinal implication of *Constantinescu* is the severance of the link between actual economic loss and criminal liability in fraud. Historically, defendants often argue that because the victim was compensated (e.g., they received equal value in return) or even profited from the transaction, the crime is negated. The court here points to *Kousisis* to underscore that the fraud statutes protect the integrity of the transaction itself, not the economic outcome of the transaction.

In future proceedings, defense counsel will need to focus intensely on whether the indictment alleges a genuine inducement to purchase rather than a right-to-control deprivation, or whether the scheme actually involved a transaction. The Ripple effect of *Constantinescu* means that district courts must ensure indictments explicitly describe the quid pro quo nature of the fraud. If the government alleges that the innocent follower bought a security, the defense cannot argue that this was merely a deprivation of information. This ruling also signals to the SEC and DOJ that they don't need to prove the "victim" lost money to secure an indictment; the objective of defrauding them out of their funds is enough.

## Conclusion

United States v. Constantinescu is a procedural win for prosecutors, but also a doctrinal cleaving of the wire and mail fraud huddle. By aligning 5th Circuit practice with the 2025 Supreme Court landscape, the court protects the indicts based on fraudulent inducement. For regulators and prosecutors looking into the increasingly opaque social media financial landscape, this case means their ammunition is potent, provided they frame the allegations correctly. The damage done to the integrity of the market doesn't have to be economic loss; it just has to be the cost of the lie.
