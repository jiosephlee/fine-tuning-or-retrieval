Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What specific factual allegations regarding 'property' must an indictment contain after Ciminelli v. United States?
Question:
I'm reading United States v. Constantinescu and the 5th Circuit is reversing the dismissal, but it hinges on this post-Ciminelli pleading standard. The District Court dismissed because the indictment alleged deprivation of economic *information*, not property. For the government to survive a dismissal for Failure to State an Offense now, what specific facts must they include in paragraph 13 regarding the followers leaving money/property?
Answer:
**Answer:**

Based on the Fifth Circuit's decision in *United States v. Constantinescu*, 24-20143 (5th Cir. 2025) (hypothetical opinion provided in the prompt), an indictment must allege that the defendant’s scheme was intended to **deprive the victim of money or property** through **fraudulent inducement**, rather than relying on the rejected "right-to-control" theory of *Ciminelli v. United States*.

To survive a dismissal post-*Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*, the specific factual allegations in paragraph 13 must demonstrate that the misrepresentation caused the victim to **part with money or property**, specifically by inducing them to **purchase securities**. It is insufficient to allege merely that the defendant deprived the victim of "valuable economic information" or the "right to control" their assets.

Here are the specific requirements grounded in the opinion:

**1. The Allegation Must Reflect "Fraudulent-Inducement," Not "Right-to-Control"**
*Ciminelli* held that federal fraud statutes protect "traditional property interests" and rejected the "right-to-control theory," which claims a fraud victim is deprived of property via their "right to valuable economic information." *See* *Ciminelli*, 598 U.S. at 316. Accordingly, paragraph 13 cannot rely solely on the argument that the victim needed "valuable economic information" to make decisions. Instead, *Constantinescu* clarifies that the indictment must set forth a "fraudulent-inducement theory," where the defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." *Constantinescu*, 24-20143, at 11–12.

**2. The Allegation Must Link the Misrepresentation to the Parting with Property**
Under the fraudulent-inducement theory, the object of the fraud must be to obtain money or property from the victim. In *Constantinescu*, the indictment satisfied this by alleging that defendants "induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities—that is, to part with their money or property." *Id.* at 12. Therefore, paragraph 13 must explicitly link the false information to an act where the victim transfers value (e.g., money) to the defendant (via a purchase). Merely alleging the victim received "false information" without the concomitant allegation of a transaction involving the transfer of property is insufficient for the "scheme to defraud" element.

**3. The Allegation Must Establish Intent to Defraud Based on Gain, Not Necessitating Victim Loss**
Finally, regarding intent, *Constantinescu* relies on *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025) to hold that a victim suffering "net pecuniary loss" is not required to prove an injury in fact. However, the indictment must allege an object to "obtain the victim’s money or property." paragraph 13 in *Constantinescu* was sufficient because it tied the fraud to the defendants' scheme to "profit $114 million from their scheme." *Id.* Thus, a factual allegation that the defendants intended to gain conduct (even if the follower received market-value securities in return) is sufficient, provided the defendants "had money in mind" and induced the deprivation under false pretenses. *See Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1397–98 (cited in *Constantinescu*).

**Conclusion:**
To survive a motion to dismiss under these standards, paragraph 13 must allege specific facts showing that the defendants' false statements induced followers to **hand over money or property** (specifically to purchase securities) and that the scheme was designed to **obtain that money or property** for recovery. You cannot proceed on an information burden regarding economic decisions alone.
