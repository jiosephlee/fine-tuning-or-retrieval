Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### How does 18 U.S.C. § 1348 'borrowing concepts' from mail fraud affect the 'property' definition for securities fraud?
Question:
The opinion states that § 1348 'borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes' and courts often rely on those cases. Since Ciminelli was a wire fraud case, does this statutory borrowing mean securities fraud has the exact same 'property' elements as wire fraud, or is there a legislative intent that links them differently? Why does the Supreme Court's wire fraud logic apply to this securities statute so directly?
Answer:
**Does § 1348 “Borrow” the Wire Fraud Definition of Property, or Does It Define It Differently?**

The Fifth Circuit's decision in *United States v. Constantinescu*, 24-20143 (5th Cir. Oct. 2, 2025), clarifies that 18 U.S.C. § 1348 (securities fraud) incorporates the property and fraud definitions from 18 U.S.C. §§ 1341–1346 (including mail and wire fraud) as a matter of statutory interpretation and judicial practice.

The short answer is that securities fraud does not have a distinct "property" element that deviates from wire fraud; instead, § 1348 explicitly borrows the legal framework, meaning the *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* standards apply directly. However, the indictment in *Constantinescu* survived because it relied on the **fraudulent-inducement theory** (inducing a victim to buy securities for money), rather than the **right-to-control theory** (depriving a victim of economic information), which *Ciminelli* rejected.

Here is the breakdown of why the wire fraud logic applies directly to § 1348 based on the opinion:

### 1. Statutory Borrowing and Judicial Consistency
The opinion explicitly states that while there is "scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit," § 1348 "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes," such that "courts have given the terms similar treatment, often relying on mail and wire fraud cases in analyzing securities fraud charges." *Constantinescu* at p. 338.

This indicates legislative and judicial intent to align the "property" definition across the statutes. Consequently, there is no separate proprietary interest unique to securities fraud that insulates it from the *Ciminelli* holding that fraud must protect "only traditional property interests."

### 2. The *Ciminelli* Property Standard Applies to § 1348
The core legal doctrine within the "borrowed" concepts is the definition of "property" under federal fraud statutes.
*   **The Rejected Theory:** The "right-to-control" theory, where property is the "right to control [his] assets" (valuable information), was rejected in *Ciminelli v. United States*, 598 U.S. 306 (2023). The Supreme Court held that "the right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest." *Constantinescu* at p. 316; citing *Ciminelli*.
*   **The Application:** In *Constantinescu*, Defendants argued the indictment failed because it did not allege a scheme to deprive followers of "a protected property interest." The Court noted that the government precedent for the right-to-control theory is invalid.
*   **The Valid Theory:** Instead, the Court found the indictment sufficient under the "fraudulent-inducement theory." As explained in *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), this theory "protects money and property" where a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." *Constantinescu* at p. 338.

Because § 1348 borrows these concepts, the *Kousisis*/Ciminelli distinction is mandatory for securities fraud cases.

### 3. Intent to Defraud Requires Intent to Obtain, Not Harm
A second element under the borrowed framework is "intent to defraud." Defendants argued that because their followers received securities of market value and were not economically harmed, there was no intent to defraud.
*   **The Doctrine:** *Kousisis* rejected the argument that fraud statutes require the victim to "suffer an economic loss from a defendant’s scheme."
*   **The Application:** The Supreme Court held that a defendant violates fraud statutes by "scheming to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." *Constantinescu* at p. 339 (quoting *Kousisis*).
*   **The Holding:** The Fifth Circuit in *Constantinescu* found this sufficient because the indictment alleged defendants "had money in mind" and intended to "profit from their scheme," which aligns with the requirement to obtain property via deception, regardless of whether the victim suffers a net loss.

### Conclusion
Your assumption that the statutes might link "differently" is incorrect as a matter of § 1348 interpretation. The opinion confirms that because § 1348 "borrows key concepts," the Supreme Court’s interpretation of mail and wire fraud statutes (specifically regarding the definition of "property" and the requirements for intent) applies **directly** to securities fraud charges. The *Constantinescu* court allowed the indictment to stand precisely because it applied the wire fraud-derived "fraudulent-inducement theory" rather than the rejected "right-to-control" theory.
