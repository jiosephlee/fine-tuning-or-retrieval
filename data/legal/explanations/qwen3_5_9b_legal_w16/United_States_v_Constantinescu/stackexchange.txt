Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Constantinescu"

### What specific factual allegations regarding 'property' must an indictment contain after Ciminelli v. United States?
Question:
I'm reading United States v. Constantinescu and the 5th Circuit is reversing the dismissal, but it hinges on this post-Ciminelli pleading standard. The District Court dismissed because the indictment alleged deprivation of economic *information*, not property. For the government to survive a dismissal for Failure to State an Offense now, what specific facts must they include in paragraph 13 regarding the followers leaving money/property?
Answer:
**Answer:**

Based on the Fifth Circuit's decision in *United States v. Constantinescu*, 24-20143 (5th Cir. 2025), an indictment must allege that the defendant’s scheme was intended to **deprive the victim of money or property** through **fraudulent inducement**, rather than relying on the rejected "right-to-control" theory of *Ciminelli v. United States*.

To survive a dismissal post-*Ciminelli* and *Kousisis*, the specific factual allegations in paragraph 13 must demonstrate that the misrepresentation caused the victim to **part with money or property**, specifically by inducing them to **purchase securities**. It is insufficient to allege merely that the defendant deprived the victim of "valuable economic information" or the "right to control" their assets.

Here are the specific requirements grounded in the opinion:

**1. The Allegation Must Reflect "Fraudulent-Inducement," Not "Right-to-Control"**
*Ciminelli* held that federal fraud statutes protect "traditional property interests" and rejected the "right-to-control theory," which claims a fraud victim is deprived of property via their "right to valuable economic information." *See* *Ciminelli*, 598 U.S. at 316. Accordingly, paragraph 13 cannot rely solely on the argument that the victim needed "valuable economic information" to make decisions. Instead, *Constantinescu* clarifies that the indictment must set forth a "fraudulent-inducement theory," where the defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." *Constantinescu*, 24-20143, at 11–12.

**2. The Allegation Must Link the Misrepresentation to the Parting with Property**
Under the fraudulent-inducement theory, the object of the fraud must be to obtain money or property from the victim. In *Constantinescu*, the indictment satisfied this by alleging that defendants "induced their followers, through misrepresentations, to purchase securities—that is, to part with their money or property." *Id.* at 12. Therefore, paragraph 13 must explicitly link the false information to an act where the victim transfers value (e.g., money) to the defendant (via a purchase). Merely alleging the victim received "false information" without the concomitant allegation of a transaction involving the transfer of property is insufficient for the "scheme to defraud" element.

**3. The Allegation Must Establish Intent to Defraud Based on Gain, Not Necessitating Victim Loss**
Finally, regarding intent, *Constantinescu* relies on *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025) to hold that a victim suffering "net pecuniary loss" is not required to prove an injury in fact. However, the indictment must allege an object to "obtain the victim’s money or property." paragraph 13 in *Constantinescu* was sufficient because it tied the fraud to the defendants' scheme to "profit $114 million from their scheme." *Id.* Thus, a factual allegation that the defendants intended to gain conduct (even if the follower received market-value securities in return) is sufficient, provided the defendants "had money in mind" and induced the deprivation under false pretenses. *See Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. at 1397–98 (cited in *Constantinescu*).

**Conclusion:**
To survive a motion to dismiss under these standards, paragraph 13 must allege specific facts showing that the defendants' false statements induced followers to **hand over money or property** (specifically to purchase securities) and that the scheme was designed to **obtain that money or property** for recovery. You cannot proceed on an information burden regarding economic decisions alone.

### How does the 'fraudulent-inducement' theory differ from the rejected 'right-to-control' theory in Ciminelli?
Question:
Section III.A of the opinion tries to distinguish Constantinescu from Ciminelli by relying on 'fraudulent-inducement' vs 'right-to-control.' In Ciminelli, the Court rejected 'depriving a victim of potentially valuable economic information' as not being a property interest. If the protection is 'deceiving a victim into handing over money,' does that mean if the victim *accepts* the security (the money), no harm was done? I can't reconcile why the court finds this a valid scheme if the followers are buying the stock, not just declining a future transaction.
Answer:
Here is a breakdown of the distinction between these two theories as applied in *United States v. Constantinescu*, along with an explanation of why the defendant's lack of economic loss to the victim does not defeat the fraud charges.

### The "Right-to-Control" vs. "Fraudulent-Inducement" Distinction

The central distinction relied upon in *Constantinescu* lies in **what constitutes the victim's property interest** that is being deprived.

*   **The "Right-to-Control" Theory (Rejected in *Ciminelli*):** Under the theory rejected in *Ciminelli*, the victim’s property interest is conceptually defined as the "right to control [his] assets" based on the possession of valuable economic information. As the opinion notes, *Ciminelli* held that "the right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest." Therefore, a scheme that merely deprives a victim of information or the right to control their assets via that information is insufficient for fraud under the statute. (See *Constantinescu*, § III.A).
*   **The "Fraudulent-Inducement" Theory (Sustained in *Constantinescu*):** In contrast, the fraudulent-inducement theory focuses on the defendant tricking the victim into a transaction where they actually **part with money or property**. The opinion clarifies that, unlike the right-to-control theory, this theory "protects money and property" because it alleges the defendant used misrepresentations to induce the victim to hand over the requested funds. Here, the property interest is the cash paid to purchase the securities, not the information about the securities themselves. (See *Constantinescu*, § III.A).

### Addressing the "No Harm" Argument

Your question regarding the harm requirement is foresighted: because the followers accepted the security (the money was given for an asset), they did not suffer an immediate net loss to their bottom line. *Constantinescu* addresses this directly by clarifying the statutory focus is on the **intent to obtain** money, rather than the **result of economic harm** to the victim.

The court notes that the fraud statutes "do not require actual economic harm" and that "a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he 'schemes[] to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." (See *Constantinesc*[u]*, § III.B, quoting *Kousisis*).

Therefore, the fact that the followers purchased securities rather than simply declining a transaction does not absolve the defendants. The "violation" is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money under false pretenses. The court rejects the argument that the defendant’s gain in profit mirrors the victim’s loss (the "mirror image" requirement), holding that fraud is complete upon the inducement to part with money, regardless of whether the victim suffers a net pecuniary loss. (See *Constantinescu*, § III.B, citing *Baker* and *Smith*).

**Summary:**
The indictment survives because it alleges the **deprivation of money** (fraudulent-inducement) rather than the **deprivation of information or control** (right-to-control). Furthermore, the fraud statute is violated by the intent to obtain money through deception, even if the victim receives an asset of value in exchange and suffers no net economic loss in the transaction.

### Does securities fraud require the victim to suffer a net pecuniary loss, according to the Kousisis holding?
Question:
The opinion explicitly cites United States v. Kousisis (2025) to say that defendants need not intend to cause economic *harm* to the victim; intended profit is enough. In this pump-and-dump scheme, the investors bought the stock based on false info. If the stock value partially recovers or the investors aren't forced out, is that still fraud? I'm confused about how 'obtaining money' counts as 'injury' if the victims received a thing of value (the stock) in exchange for their money.
Answer:
No. According to the *Kousisis* holding as applied in the *Constantinescu* opinion, securities fraud does **not** require a victim to suffer a net pecuniary loss.

The Fifth Circuit in *United States v. Constantinescu* clarifies that fraud under the fraudulent-inducement theory focuses on the defendant’s intent to obtain money or property from the victim through deception, rather than the intent to cause economic harm or leave the victim "economically worse off." As the opinion explains, citing *Kousisis*, 145 S. Ct. 1382, the fraud statutes protect against the deprivation of money or property obtained via false pretenses, regardless of subsequent market conditions or the value of the consideration provided to the victim.

Specifically, the *Constantinescu* opinion addresses your confusion regarding the exchanges in a scheme where victims receive securities:

> "The fraud statutes do not require actual economic harm, defendants' followers' receipt of market-value securities does not obviate the fraud charges... A fraud is complete when the defendant has induced the deprivation of money or property under materially false pretenses—regardless of whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss."

Furthermore, the court notes that the defendant’s provision of value to the victim—even if of equal market value—does not negate liability:

> "[Kousisis] concluded defendant may be liable in fraud even when he 'provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return.'"

Consequently, under the standard set forth in *Kousisis* as interpreted in *Constantinescu*, the existence of the fraud is determined by the inducement of the transaction via material misrepresentations and the resulting transfer of funds, not by the ultimate financial recovery or loss of the investor after the scheme concludes (or whether the stock value partially recovers). The injury is the loss of the specific property transferred under the deception, not necessarily a net reduction in the victim's overall wealth.

### How does 18 U.S.C. § 1348 'borrowing concepts' from mail fraud affect the 'property' definition for securities fraud?
Question:
The opinion states that § 1348 'borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes' and courts often rely on those cases. Since Ciminelli was a wire fraud case, does this statutory borrowing mean securities fraud has the exact same 'property' elements as wire fraud, or is there a legislative intent that links them differently? Why does the Supreme Court's wire fraud logic apply to this securities statute so directly?
Answer:
**Does § 1348 “Borrow” the Wire Fraud Definition of Property, or Does It Define It Differently?**

The Fifth Circuit's decision in *United States v. Constantinescu*, 24-20143 (5th Cir. Oct. 2, 2025), clarifies that 18 U.S.C. § 1348 (securities fraud) incorporates the property and fraud definitions from 18 U.S.C. §§ 1341–1346 (including mail and wire fraud) as a matter of statutory interpretation and judicial practice.

The short answer is that securities fraud does not have a distinct "property" element that deviates from wire fraud; instead, § 1348 explicitly borrows the legal framework, meaning the *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* standards apply directly. However, the indictment in *Constantinescu* survived because it relied on the **fraudulent-inducement theory** (inducing a victim to buy securities for money), rather than the **right-to-control theory** (depriving a victim of economic information), which *Ciminelli* rejected.

Here is the breakdown of why the wire fraud logic applies directly to § 1348 based on the opinion:

### 1. Statutory Borrowing and Judicial Consistency
The opinion explicitly states that while there is "scant caselaw construing the securities fraud statute in this circuit," § 1348 "borrows key concepts from the mail and wire fraud statutes," such that "courts have given the terms similar treatment, often relying on mail and wire fraud cases in analyzing securities fraud charges." *Constantinescu* at p. 338.

This indicates legislative and judicial intent to align the "property" definition across the statutes. Consequently, there is no separate proprietary interest unique to securities fraud that insulates it from the *Ciminelli* holding that fraud must protect "only traditional property interests."

### 2. The *Ciminelli* Property Standard Applies to § 1348
The core legal doctrine within the "borrowed" concepts is the definition of "property" under federal fraud statutes.
*   **The Rejected Theory:** The "right-to-control" theory, where property is the "right to control [his] assets" (valuable information), was rejected in *Ciminelli v. United States*, 598 U.S. 306 (2023). The Supreme Court held that "the right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest." *Constantinescu* at p. 316; citing *Ciminelli*.
*   **The Application:** In *Constantinescu*, Defendants argued the indictment failed because it did not allege a scheme to deprive followers of "a protected property interest." The Court noted that the government precedent for the right-to-control theory is invalid.
*   **The Valid Theory:** Instead, the Court found the indictment sufficient under the "fraudulent-inducement theory." As explained in *Kousisis v. United States*, 145 S. Ct. 1382 (2025), this theory "protects money and property" where a defendant "uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property." *Constantinescu* at p. 338.

Because § 1348 borrows these concepts, the *Kousisis*/Ciminelli distinction is mandatory for securities fraud cases.

### 3. Intent to Defraud Requires Intent to Obtain, Not Harm
A second element under the borrowed framework is "intent to defraud." Defendants argued that because their followers received securities of market value and were not economically harmed, there was no intent to defraud.
*   **The Doctrine:** *Kousisis* rejected the argument that fraud statutes require the victim to "suffer an economic loss from a defendant’s scheme."
*   **The Application:** The Supreme Court held that a defendant violates fraud statutes by "scheming to 'obtain' the victim's 'money or property,' regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off." *Constantinescu* at p. 339 (quoting *Kousisis*).
*   **The Holding:** The Fifth Circuit in *Constantinescu* found this sufficient because the indictment alleged defendants "had money in mind" and intended to "profit from their scheme," which aligns with the requirement to obtain property via deception, regardless of whether the victim suffers a net loss.

### Conclusion
Your assumption that the statutes might link "differently" is incorrect as a matter of § 1348 interpretation. The opinion confirms that because § 1348 "borrows key concepts," the Supreme Court’s interpretation of mail and wire fraud statutes (specifically regarding the definition of "property" and the requirements for intent) applies **directly** to securities fraud charges. The *Constantinescu* court allowed the indictment to stand precisely because it applied the wire fraud-derived "fraudulent-inducement theory" rather than the rejected "right-to-control" theory.


### Does the reversal of the indictment affect the conspiracy charge under § 1349?
Question:
The court reverses the dismissal of the indictment for 'conspiracy to commit securities fraud, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349' in addition to the fraud charges. The reasoning focuses on the 'scheme' and 'intent' elements of the substantive fraud. Does the logic applied to § 1348 (Kousisis/Ciminelli) automatically cure the § 1349 deficiency, or should conspiracy charges be analyzed differently regarding the intent to deprive property?
Answer:
Yes, under the reasoning in *United States v. Constantinescu*, the logic applied to § 1348 securities fraud serves as the governing standard for the sufficiency of the conspiracy charge under § 1349, thereby curing the alleged deficiency.

The court treats the sufficiency of the indictment regarding the conspiracy charge and the substantive fraud charges inextricably. While § 1348 (securities fraud) is the substantive offense detailed in the opinion, § 1349 (conspiracy to commit securities fraud) is predicated on an agreement to commit the substantive violation. The district court dismissed the entire indictment for failing to allege a "traditional property interest" in violation of the fraud statutes. The Fifth Circuit reversed this dismissal, applying the Supreme Court’s standards from *Ciminelli* and *Kousisis* to the underlying fraud allegations, rather than applying any separate "conspiracy-specific" intent analysis.

The opinion clarifies that the critical element resisting dismissal was not a distinction between conspiracy and substantive fraud, but rather the nature of the "property" alleged to be the object of the scheme. Applying *Ciminelli’s* rejection of the "right-to-control theory" and *Kousisis’s* adoption of the "fraudulent-inducement theory," the court found that the indictment successfully alleged a scheme to deprive victims of "money or property" by inducing them to purchase securities, rather than merely valuable economic information. Because the conspiracy charge requires the intent to commit the underlying fraud (§ 1348), and the court found the intent to defraud purchasers of securities (money/property) was sufficiently alleged, the logic for § 1348 validates the charge of conspiracy to commit securities fraud. The court reverses the dismissal of the indictment generally, indicating that no separate analysis regarding "intent to deprive property" was necessary to sustain the § 1349 count as long as the substantive elements of the fraud are met.
