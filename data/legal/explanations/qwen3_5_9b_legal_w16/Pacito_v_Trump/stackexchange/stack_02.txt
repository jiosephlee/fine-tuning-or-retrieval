Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

### How does the "likely to succeed on the merits" factor apply when the Court splits on the two different statutory challenges?
Question:
The opinion states that the government is likely to succeed on the challenge to Executive Order 14163 (refugee admission suspension) but is *not* likely to succeed on the challenge regarding the defunding of USRAP under the APA. Since the standards for issuing a stay (Nken factors) weigh heavily on the showing of success on the merits, how did the panel balance these contradictory likelihoods to justify staying the District Court's introductory injunctions in their entirety?
Answer:
This is a great question that highlights the complexity of applying *Nken* factor analysis when there are multiple distinct statutory challenges within a single injunction.

To answer your question: The panel did not balance the contradictory likelihoods of success by granting a "stay in its entirety" in the traditional sense. Rather, they **applied the "likely to succeed on the merits" factor specifically to each statutory challenge**, using the stay order to suspend proceedings generally while carving out an exception for the challenge where the government lacked a meritorious claim.

Here is how the panel reconciled the split likelihoods of success to issue the stay order:

### 1. The "Merits" Factor was Applied Claim-Specific
The order relies on the *Nken* standard but explicitly separates the government's prospects for the two types of injunctions.

*   **Executive Order 14163 (Refugee Admission Suspension):** The panel found the government **likely to succeed** on the merits of this challenge. The order states: "We cannot engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications" under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and cites *Trump v. Hawaii*. Because the Executive Order functions under a deferential standard where the President's "finding[] that the entry... would be detrimental" is the "sole prerequisite," the panel granted a stay pending appeal *even though* the plaintiffs were requesting this specific part of the injunction to be enforced immediately.
*   **USRAP Funding (§ 1522):** Conversely, the panel found the government **not likely to prevail** on the APA challenge regarding the defunding. The court noted the statutory mandate to provide "reception and placement services" and highlighted "the government’s uncertainty regarding its ability to provide" these mandated services, including an admission it was unaware of progress toward securing new agencies.

### 2. The "Except as Noted Below" Carve-out Solved the Tension
You asked how the court justified staying the injunctions "in their entirety." While the order text states the district court's orders are "stayed in their entirety except as specified in this Order," this language provided the necessary bridging mechanism.

*   Because the government was **likely to prevail** on the EO challenge, the court stayed the injunction blocking the suspension, allowing the suspension to proceed pending the final merits opinion.
*   Because the government was **not likely to prevail** on the funding challenge, the court did not let the "stay in entirety" protect the government's defunding. Instead, the court utilized the exception clause to direct the government to **reinstate** the cooperative agreements immediately ("In light of the government’s uncertainty... the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements").

### Summary of the Logical Balance
The panel avoided a pure application of a "global stay" that would have frozen the injunctions regardless of success likelihood. Instead, they treated the "stay" order as a procedural stay of the District Court's orders *pending final resolution*, but modified the *substantive effect* based on the merits:

1.  **EO Challenge:** Governed by a **Stay** on the injunction because the Gov is **likely to succeed** (merits satisfied under *Trump v. Hawaii*).
2.  **Funding Challenge:** Governed by an **Immediate Mandate** (Reinstatement) because the Gov is **not likely to succeed** (statutory duty to fund).

Thus, the "balance" was effectively kept within the exception clause. The court stayed the *orders* generally to keep the issue pending on appeal, but carved out an immediate directive that respected the judgment that the government could not succeed on the funding merits.
