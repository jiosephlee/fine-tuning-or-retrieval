Title: Blog about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# The Split Shield: How *Pacito* Separates Presidential Power to Bar Entry from the Mandate to Support Those Already Inside

In the immigration litigation landscape of 2025, the September 12, 2025 panel order in *Pacito v. Trump* presented a singular and complex jurisprudential moment. The Ninth Circuit’s interim stay order effectively bifurcates the judicial review of a President’s sweeping refugee policy: while it affords broad deference to the Executive’s power to prevent outsiders from entering the country, it simultaneously reasserts that once individuals are admitted, the obligations Congress attaches to that status are non-negotiable. This distinction matters profoundly because it establishes a floor of civil liability for the government that cannot be eroded by the same weapons used to build the walls of the foreign policy bar. By carving the refugee program into a "gate" and a "floor," the panel signals that statutory mandates for resettlement services operate in a legal continuum distinct from the plenary power the Court recognized in *Trump v. Hawaii*.

## The Presidential Shield and Entrant Policy

The order’s reasoning regarding the suspension of new admissions is straightforward, relying heavily on the deference standards established in *Trump v. Hawaii*. The panel acknowledged the Ninth Circuit’s earlier jurisprudence that the authority vested in the President under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend entry "exude[s] deference." The court reasoned that the sole statutory prerequisite for such a suspension is that the President find entry would be detrimental to the national interest—and the President has made that finding regarding the Refugee Admission Program.

This creates a predictable outcome regarding the *foreign* side of immigration: the likelihood of the government prevailing on the merits of the EO 14163 suspension is high, which is one of the critical factors for a stay of a preliminary injunction. By folding the analysis of § 1182(f) back into the *Hawaii* framework, the court avoids a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s factual justification at this stage. This suggests that while the Trojan Horse litigation is escalating, the judiciary remains reluctant to intervene in the President’s foreign policy determinations regarding who may cross the border in the first place. However, the court is effectively saying that while the entry gate can remain shut temporarily, the President cannot use the administrative machinery of that suspension to dismantle the interior support systems for those who have legally entered.

## The Statutory Safety Net: Why 1522 Changes the Game

The doctrinal pivot of this order lies in the Court’s handling of the plaintiffs' challenge regarding the suspension of funding for domestic resettlement services. Here, the court explicitly rejects the idea that the § 1182(f) suspension justifies a freeze on all refugee infrastructure. The stay order directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services, even as the entry ban under § 1182(f) remains on the table pending further briefing.

The Court distinguishes the "gray zone" of § 1522 from the "amber zone" of § 1182(f). While § 1182(f) grants broad discretion to restrict entry, § 1522 contains a substantive directive for once an alien is "admitted." The injunction regarding the funding for admits is not stayed in full; rather, the court orders the government to reinstate funds that were terminated by the State Department in February 2025. The rationale is practical as well as legal: the government terminated all cooperative agreements to provide resettlement services in the United States, yet they were unsure whether they had secured a replacement agency. The Court noted that the termination of these contracts created a vacuum of legal responsibility that the Executive branch could simply not fill by administrative fiat while litigation was in progress.

This creates a significant separation of powers dynamic. The President has the power to stop the river of migration before it hits the dam, but the legislation constrains whether that dam is to be overloaded. By prioritizing the statutory duty to provide services for those *already* admitted (or conditionally approved) over the executive's desire for a blanket funding pause, the panel is signaling that statutory authorizations for interior protection are not imperiled by the power to exclude at the border. The decision effectively contextualizes immigration law as having both an exclusionary dimension and an internal civil rights component that, for purposes of the stay, cannot be paused en bloc.

## Judge Lee and the Writhing Text of Authorization

The nuance of the decision is sharpened by the partial dissent authored by Judge Lee, who argues that the majority fails to see the statutory language as truly mandatory. Judge Lee contends that § 1522 is best read as an authorization rather than a mandate, noting that Congress often uses "shoves" like "to the extent of available appropriations" or defines Director actions as "authorized" rather than "required." He argues that the "shall" in § 1522(a) applies to conditions and considerations *if* the government chooses to fund the programs, rather than imposing an absolute duty to spend money regardless of appropriations.

This dissent highlights a perennial tension in administrative law within the Ninth Circuit: does the text of a statute demand action, or does it grant authority to choose? Judge Lee’s interpretation suggests that without clear appropriation or specific appropriation language, the Executive retains discretion over the very existence of the services. For the majority opinion in the pending full opinion, this view is dangerous; it implies that the government can force the Executive into a box of paperwork and appropriations to produce the legal form of the service. However, by ordering the reinstatement of agreements and referencing the specific obligations for unaccompanied minors in § 1522(d), the panel order suggests that when the Executive plumbs the depths of the statute’s text, it must justify *why* it is withholding statutory duties, not just why it is withholding policy.

## Implications for the Separation of Powers

Even as an interim stay, *Pacito* discusses a doctrinal fissure that could reshape immigration litigation for years to come. It suggests that the separation of powers regarding immigration is not a monolith of Executive supremacy. There is a boundary where statutory obligations—vested in refugees by virtue of their admission—clash with the plenary power to exclude aliens.

The practical downstream effect is visible. The government cannot enjoy the benefits of a policy that "resets" the refugee program to zero without honoring the existing promises made to the individuals processed under the previous regime. If the court were to allow the funding suspension to continue alongside the entry ban, it would amount to a retroactive revocation of rights based on a policy change that was effectively overturned by the court below. The Ninth Circuit’s refusal to let the funding stay fully dormant creates a "lifeline" for current refugee processing systems.

Ultimately, the *Pacito* panel order serves as a reminder that immigration law involves two distinct sets of actors and rights: the new entrant and the existing one. While the political winds have favored the government on the latter point—granting the stay of the injunction against the entry ban—the shadow of § 1522 remains a protective shield over those already inside. The full opinion to come may refine this distinction, but for now, the precedent stands as a boundary: you can control whose boots are on American soil, but you cannot simply take away the boots' shoes while they are inside the house.



# Authorization Versus Mandate: C.J. Lee's Textualist Dissent on the Nature of Refugee Resettlement Funding

In district court litigation often defined by raw numbers and sweeping policy impacts, the nuances of statutory text can sometimes feel secondary to the political realities. However, in *Pacito v. Trump*, currently pending before the Ninth Circuit, the court has rendered a critical interim order that forces a high-stakes re-examination of just how tightly statutory text binds the executive branch in times of immigration crisis. As the court navigates the waters of Executive Order No. 14163—a sweeping suspension of the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program—the panel has created a procedural split. While the majority is accepting the government’s claim of broad discretionary power to suspend entry under the Immigration and Nationality Act, they carve out a strict judicial mandate regarding the aftercare and funding of refugees who have already been admitted. In a partial concurrence and dissent, Circuit Judge Lee provides a deeply textualist critique of this carveout, challenging the majority’s reading of 8 U.S.C. § 1522.

## The Tension Between Entry and Aftercare

The core tension in this dispute lies in the difference between the power to stop people from arriving and the statutory obligation to support those who are already admitted. For the vast majority of the immigration doctrine established since *Trump v. Hawaii*, the President’s discretion to suspend entry under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) exudes a high level of deference. In this interlocutory phase of *Pacito*, the Ninth Circuit’s panel appears to accept that deference on the question of whether the entry ban is valid. Consequently, the District Court’s preliminary injunctions blocking the entry ban are essentially put on hold pending a full opinion on the merits.

However, the panel establishes a crucial limitation on that discretionary power. The Order directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees admitted before the suspension took effect. On the surface, this looks like a victory for the plaintiffs regarding funding, even as the court acknowledges the President’s right to lock the doors. Judge Lee, in his in-part dissents, challenges this distinction. He argues that by imposing a specific duty on the executive to fund specific services while the President has the unilateral power to suspend the program entirely, the court is being inconsistent in its interpretation of the statutes it purports to enforce. If the executive has the power to suspend the entry class that generates the funding need, the logic of the funding mandate becomes shaky if that mandate is not truly mandatory.

## Lee’s Textualist Hammer

C.J. Lee’s dissent drills into the linguistic weeds of 8 U.S.C. § 1522 to dismantle the majority's reading of the statute as a strict mandate. The majority relies on the use of "shall" throughout the sub-chapter to imply obligation. However, Lee points out that the title of the specific section is "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees." In Lee’s view, the legislature did not use the more binding term "Mandate for services," instead choosing the permissive terminology of "Authorization." He argues that this is not a semantic quibble but a deliberate legislative choice indicating permission rather than requirement.

Lee’s argument further expands on the specific language within the statute, noting that subsections granting the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement authority to assist use the phrase "Director is authorized." While the majority interprets these clauses in conjunction with "shall" language found in subsection (a), Lee reads "shall" as a condition precedent strictly limited by a qualifying phrase: "to the extent of available appropriations." To him, this defines the funding requirement as conditional upon Congress actually writing the money into the budget, rather than a court-orderable obligation independent of the purse. In this reading, the directives to provide services are not hard mandates that the court can enforce; they are discretionary instructions on how funds should be spent if Congress gives the administrator permission to spend them.

## Doctrinal Implications for Administrative Law

The implications of resolving this textual battle extend well beyond the specific refugee population in question. If the majority’s reading of § 1522 holds water, it creates a precedent that administrative courts can identify "safe zones" within an agency's purview where agency discretion is limited by mandatory spending duties, even when the head of the executive branch is exercising broad discretion elsewhere. Specifically, it suggests that certain statutory sub-divisions can be interpreted as mandatory spending bills that survive political shifts or policy coups.

Conversely, if Judge Lee’s textualism prevails in a future full opinion or subsequent circuit court development, it would gravely limit judicial oversight of agency discretion regarding enforcement priorities and funding allocation. It would push the line where the court can step in to ensure agency commitment, shifting the balance of power back toward the executive branch to manage its own spending obligations without judicial second-guessing. For administrative lawyers, *Pacito* will likely be litigated as a template for challenges to funding cuts: does the statute say "shall" or "is authorized"? Does the court enforce the spending words or the authorization words?

In the interim, the Ninth Circuit’s order marks a fragile cease-fire in what could become a years-long legal battle. The court has acknowledged the President’s power to withdraw the welcome mat, but it suggests the judiciary still holds the keys to the house for those already inside the doors. While the majority believes this is a statutory mandate, Judge Lee remains unconvinced that the text demands such judicial intervention against the executive's historical prerogative to manage resettlement logistics, leaving the door open for a sharper textualist majority opinion to come.



# The Predictive Stay: What *Pacito*'s Interim Order Reveal About the 'Likely to Prevail' Factor in Immigration Litigation

**## A Preview of the Merits in a Procedural Order**

The Ninth Circuit’s recent language in *Pacito v. Trump* signals a significant evolution in the procedural architecture of immigration litigation. Typically, the "likely to prevail" factor introduced in *Nken v. Holder* serves as a threshold inquiry to determine whether a stay pending appeal is warranted, writ up solely in the context of the four-part test without necessarily dissecting the merits in depth. However, in this September 2025 panel order, the court took an unusual step by explicitly weighing the likelihood of success on the merits for distinct components of the government’s case well before issuing a formal opinion on the substantive claims. This order vacates a series of previous stays and ultimately enforces a temporary injunction regarding the failure to fund certain refugee services. This bifurcated decision—that the government will likely prevail on admission restrictions but face hurdles regarding funding obligations—sends a stark message to litigants about the stability of the executive’s wartime powers versus its statutory service mandates.

**## The Split Analysis of Admission and Support**

The court’s reasoning hinges on the critical distinction it draws between the power to admit and the power to support. Regarding the executive order suspending refugee admissions, the panel relied heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii*. The majority noted that the president’s authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to “suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens” is broad and that the court cannot engage in a “searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” In this context, the stay factors all favored the government, allowing the administration to enforce its moratorium on admitting new refugees.

The analysis shifts significantly when addressing defunding the resettlement program. Here, the court applied a stricter standard, concluding the government is not likely to prevail on at least one challenge under the Administrative Procedure Act. The order directs the reinstatement of cooperative agreements for reception and placement services because the State Department had terminated them while claiming to seek new providers. This distinction suggests a judicial consensus that the入れる (entry) power is political and broad, but the establishment of support services for those already granted status falls within a more constrained statutory framework involving appropriations and contractual obligations.

**## Strategic Implications of the Interim Stay Order**

The most impactful doctrinal shift here is the move away from purely backward-looking discretion to forward-looking certainty in stay orders. By predicting the outcome of the merits challenge in the stay order itself, the court has effectively allowed the "likely to succeed" factor to drive the procedural posture of the case. From a litigation strategy standpoint, this creates a "predictive stay" phenomenon where parties must litigate the merits of their case even before a suppression hearing is concluded, in hopes of securing a final disposition on the broader appeal. This accelerates the timeline for resolution but increases the pressure on parties to frame their arguments around specific statutory limitations rather than broad statutory immigration authority.

Furthermore, the strategic vacating of prior stays indicates a procedural desire to centralized control. In the months leading up to the September order, the court oscillated between stays of district court injunctions and stays of those stays. By vacating these and issuing a unified stay pending final resolution, the court signals that the issue of whether existing refugees should be supported via contract is too urgent to be left in limbo. However, this highlights a lingering vulnerability in the "funding" arguments. If the primary statutory provisions for refugee supports are read as "authorizations" rather than "mandates," as suggested by the dissent, the court may revert to a more deferential posture once the opinion issue is resolved.

**## Doctrinal Tensions and the Path Forward**

The dissent by Judge Lee serves as a crucial read for future immigration litigation indicating that the statutory duty to support refugees is not mandatory. The dissent argues that 8 U.S.C. § 1522 is an "authorization" for the government to act if appropriations exist, rather than a command. This creates a doctrinal wedge that protects executive discretion over spending while limiting judicial oversight over whether the discretion is exercised. If the court ultimately adopts this reasoning in the full opinion, it would imply that the district court's order compelling the significant resources allow fundraising is the only source of funding for the services described.

This tension between the "likely to prevail" factors and the public interest prong remains a dominant theme. While the government argued public interest favored a stay of the stoppage of existing refugee services—owing to national security or consistency—this shifted to the second factor regarding irreparable injury and the specific funding obligations. The court’s tentative finding that the government will struggle to show successful challenges under the APA regarding services suggests it views the distinction between entering a person and maintaining an existing legal status before the court differently than the Justice Department typically argues.

**## Conclusion**

The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Pacito v. Trump* is more than an administrative pivot; it is a political signal. By signaling that while the President retains the power to shut the door on new applications, the Executive also maintains a contractual obligation to open it for the time those refugees might actually pass through. For future litigants, the lesson is clear: the "likely to prevail" inquiry is no longer a backchannel analysis. Instead, it shapes the procedural landscape immediately. The full opinion arriving in due course may solidify this split, confirming that immigration law is simultaneously a realm of potent executive discretion and a zone of statutory constraint on how that power is administered through funding mechanisms.
