Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 3: # Chapter 3: Judicial Deference in Refugee Admissions Challenges

## Statutory Authority: 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and Presidential Power

To understand the Ninth Circuit's reasoning in *Pacito v. Trump*, one must first grapple with the statutory foundation of the President's action. This opinion concerns the President's reliance on 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), a statute within the Immigration and Nationality Act. The district court had issued a preliminary injunction staying the enforcement of Executive Order 14163, which sought to suspend the Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) under the authority of § 1182(f). The panel order explains that this statutory provision grants the President broad power to manage entry restrictions. Specifically, 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) permits the President to suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants if the President finds that such entry would be detrimental to the interests of the United States. The text emphasizes that this authority represents a significant grant of discretion to the executive branch, allowing the President to impose restrictions not otherwise enumerated in the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In the context of appellate procedure, the Ninth Circuit views statutory grants of broad discretion differently than statutory mandates. Here, the court analyzes the challenge to the suspension of refugee admissions. When addressing challenges to the President's use of § 1182(f), the court does not treat the statute as requiring a specific justification that it can independently review for fact-accuracy in the administrative context. Instead, the court recognizes that the statute vests the President with exemption immunity from ordinary judicial override regarding the determination of national interest. This deference shapes the entire standard of review for the stay.

## The Sole Prerequisite: Finding Entry Detrimental to Interests

A critical element in reading this opinion is identifying what the statute explicitly requires from the President. The text highlights that the "sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f)" is a finding that entry would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States." This phrasing is deliberate. The court notes that only two conditions must exist under the statute: first, the President must make a finding, and second, that finding must relate to detriments to U.S. interests. The court does not impose additional requirements on Presidential authority under this section beyond what the text of the statute provides.

When applying this principle, the court notes that the President has undoubtedly fulfilled this statutory prerequisite. The opinion indicates that the Executive Order relied upon this statutory authority to direct the suspension of refugee admissions. For a reader, understanding this means viewing the President's action not necessarily through the lens of whether the suspension was "good policy" regarding refugees, but whether the President met the minimal statutory threshold of making a finding. Because the § 1182(f) text does not require a hearing or specific evidence while the finding is made, the court's analysis focuses heavily on whether the authority itself is valid, rather than interrogating the specific facts backing the finding. Consequently, the court assumes the President met the statutory burden, moving the analysis to the standard of review applied to the Executive's authority to claim national interests were at risk.

## Precedent from Trump v. Hawaii (585 U.S. 667)

The panel's reasoning relies heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii*, the Supreme Court case that addressed the merits of a similar Presidential entry restriction. The order quotes *Hawaii* extensively to establish the level of deference Due Process and other constitutional challenges must receive in this context. In the earlier case, the Supreme Court reasoned that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and "vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions."

In analyzing *Pacito*, the Ninth Circuit finds that the Supreme Court in *Hawaii* clarified the boundaries of this power. A key takeaway for a student of administrative law is how the Court interprets the "sole prerequisite." Post-*Hawaii*, the inquiry shifts away from the necessity of the restriction to the President's ability to make the finding. The court observes that the language of § 1182(f) is clear and that the Executive Order does not exceed any textual limits. This analysis teaches that when the Supreme Court interprets a provision as granting the President "ample power," appellate courts limit independent scrutiny of the underlying policy justification. The opinion uses *Hawaii* to illustrate that statutory interpretation and constitutional challenges in this realm often defer to the Executive Branch's invocation of national security or national interest powers exclusively.

## Evaluation of President's Justifications for Suspension

The opinion discusses the standard for the stay, which involves evaluating four factors, including whether the government is likely to succeed on the merits. In this specific context, the court delineates the line between a "searching inquiry" and a deferential standard. While courts generally scrutinize government claims, this panel notes that regarding the President's invocation of § 1182(f), the standard is highly deferential. The text states that the court cannot engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications."

This distinction is vital for understanding the scope of judicial review in immigration and national security matters. The majority in *Pacito* cites *Hawaii* to support the proposition that the President has "undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement." Instead of analyzing the specific reasons given in the Executive Order for why refugee entry might be detrimental, the court applies the legal standard that the President's finding alone satisfies the statute. If the President makes the finding, the legal inquiry essentially ends at the statutory level because the text grants the power. This analysis serves as a cautionary note to law students: while courts can apply general administrative law principles like the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), they often apply distinct, highly deferential standards when Congress has explicitly granted the President special authority by statute.

## Conclusion of the Majority: Government Likely to Prevail

With the statutory authority and deference standard established, the court turns to the four-part test for issuing a stay of the district court's preliminary injunction. The text identifies the two most critical factors: the applicant's likelihood of success on the merits and the potential for irreparable injury. In analyzing whether the government is likely to prevail on the suspension of refugee admissions, the court concludes that it is.

The reasoning rests on the premises established in the previous sections. Because § 1182(f) "exudes deference" and the President has made the requisite finding, the government meets the second most critical factor for the stay. While the district court had enjoined the enforcement of the Executive Order, the Ninth Circuit panel reasoned that given the statutory grant of power, the government would succeed in its challenge to the validity of the suspension under § 1182(f). This determination is significant because it governs the issuance of the stay. In the context of interlocutory appeals, overwhelming deference to the President on the merits allows the court to find that the government is likely to prevail, which then weighs heavily against the necessity of a stay for the plaintiffs. This reasoning demonstrates how statutory language can effectively dictate the outcome of a stay motion before the merits are fully briefed or argued.

## Result: Granting Stay on Refugee Suspension Injunction

The immediate procedural outcome in this chapter is the vacating of prior stays and the issuance of a new stay pending appeal regarding the suspension of refugee admissions. The panel directs that the district court's February 28 and March 24, 2025, preliminary injunctions are stayed "in their entirety" with respect to the suspension, except as modified for other issues. This disposition means the Government is allowed to proceed with restraining the refugee suspension while the appeal is ongoing.

It is important to note that this partial stay order is an interim measure. The opinion explicitly states, "An opinion on the merits will issue in due course." However, the reasoning provided in this section establishes the majority's view on the deference applicable to Presidential actions under § 1182(f). The instruction to the panel signaling that the "government is likely to prevail" reaffirms that, at this stage of the litigation, the judiciary will remain subordinate to the President's authority under this specific statute. The final holding on the merits regarding suspension will likely follow this deferential logic, provided the President continues to demonstrate the statutory finding. This section of the chapter exemplifies how judicial review can balance deferential interpretations of statutes granting broad executive power against the procedural right of litigants to challenge the enforcement of government orders.
