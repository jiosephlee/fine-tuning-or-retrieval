Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 5: # Chapter 5: Concurrence and Dissent on Statutory Interpretation

## Majority's Interpretation of § 1522 as Mandatory
In the context of *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit’s panel addressed not only the suspension of refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) but also the second-order issue regarding the defunding of refugee reception and placement services. While an opinion on the merits was scheduled for a later date, the panel issued an administrative stay of the district court’s preliminary injunctions pending final resolution. Crucially, the majority explained its reasoning for why the rebate to the district court’s injunction on funding defunding was necessary. The court noted that the government failed to promptly replace the terminated cooperative agreements despite knowing that such agreements are necessary for state resettlement services. The majority interpreted 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing obligations on the government to provide such services to refugees admitted to the United States. Consequently, the court directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who had been admitted to the United States prior to the suspension. This interpretation rested on the court’s assessment that the government was not likely to prevail on the plaintiffs’ Administrative Procedure Act challenge regarding the defunding of these services, effectively treating the statute as creating a duty that the executive branch could not abrogate unilaterally.

## Judge Lee's Textual Analysis of 'Authorization' and 'Shall' Language
Circuit Judge Lee, writing a concurrence in part and dissent in part, disagreed fundamentally with the majority’s conclusion that the government must be directed to reinstate funding. Her dissent focused primarily on the statutory text of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, applying a textualist approach that emphasizes the precise language used by Congress. While the majority viewed the statute as imposing a mandatory requirement, Judge Lee observed that the section is titled “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” She argued that the word “Authorization” is inherently permissive, distinct from a mandate. Furthermore, she examined the operative language within the text of the statute. The majority relied on specific subsections that use the word “shall,” which typically signals a mandatory obligation. However, Judge Lee pointed out that other subsections use the language “is authorized,” which she characterized as statutory signals that the executive branch retains discretion over funding and implementation. By focusing on the interplay between these terms, Judge Lee asserted that the statute does not compel the executive branch to provide these services, but rather supplies permission if the funding exists.

## Distinguishing Mandatory 'Conditions' from Discretionary Programs
A central tension in the dissent appears in the interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522(a), which outlines “Conditions and considerations” for providing assistance. Judge Lee distinguished this mandatory language from the discretionary programs described in subsections (b) through (e). She argued that § 1522(a) imposes mandatory conditions and considerations *only if* the government chooses to fund refugee programs. For example, she noted that the Director “shall make available sufficient resources,” but she contextualized this phrase under the umbrella of the discretionary funding provisions found in subsequent subsections. In her view, the statutory language functions to regulate how funds are used if spent, rather than mandating that specific programs (like the initial relocation or cash assistance programs) must be created in the first place. Judge Lee emphasized that the executive branch has a choice regarding whether to operate under these conditions, meaning that the statute does not strip the executive of the power to determine whether to pursue these programs.

## The 'Necessary' and 'As Needed' Qualifiers in Statute
Beyond the specific verbs and titles, Judge Lee focused on qualifying language within 8 U.S.C. § 1522 that she read as reinforcing discretion. Specifically, she highlighted clauses that use terms like “if necessary” and “as needed.” For instance, § 1522(d)(2)(B) states that the program’s Director shall assume legal responsibility for unaccompanied children only “if such responsibility is necessary.” She argued that even when the word “shall” appears, the section drips with deference to the executive branch. Similarly, § 1522(e)(7)(A) directs the Secretary to develop projects for support as they are “needed.” Judge Lee reasoned that these qualifiers leave the determination of the scope and timing of programs up to executive discretion. Therefore, the record did not support a reading that forced the government to act in a specific manner if it determined it was not "necessary" or "as needed" under its own assessment. In the dissent, she noted the uncertainty regarding unaccompanied children itself, suggesting that the law depends on factual determinations the executive branch is mandated to make.

## Limitation of Executive Branch Mandate Without Fiscal Appropriation Requirement
Beyond the textual qualifiers, Judge Lee grounded her interpretation in the separation of powers and the executive branch’s lack of mandate for spending without a specific fiscal appropriation. She noted that the statute is qualified by the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations.” This phrase, she argued, is a clear congressional signal that such programs are subject to spending constraints. While the majority cited $3.178 billion in FY 2025 appropriations alongside a reduction of $800 million, Judge Lee read this as confirmation that the statutes allow the executive to operate within appropriated limits rather than a mandate to allocate those funds specifically to refugee services before they are permitted by statute. Her logic posits that if the law does not demand that the executive branch provide these services, the court cannot mandate them. She contended that the power to appropriate the funds resides with Congress, and without a mandatory appropriation command in § 1522, the court’s order to reinstate cooperative agreements effectively forced the executive to spend money that the statute did not require to be spent.

## Impact of Dissent on Future Rulings and Congressional Power
The divergence between the majority and Judge Lee highlights a significant doctrinal split regarding the scope of judicial review vis-à-vis executive implementation of immigration policy. Judge Lee’s dissent raises the prospect that future rulings interpreting similar statutory mandates must carefully distinguish between “needs” that trigger specific duties and policy choices left to the executive. By framing the statute as an authorization rather than a mandate, the dissent suggests that courts should be hesitant to issue non-discretionary orders in the context of federal funding allocations that are tied to appropriations power. If Congress intended to create a mandatory obligation under § 1522, Judge Lee argues it should have done so without the permissive language of authorization. This creates a precedent for future courts to view similar regulations in areas where the statute uses permissive language as discretionary rather than binding. Ultimately, the dissent serves as a reminder that strong textualist readings can constrain judicial mandates on spending and policy implementation, thereby preserving agency discretion and respecting the legislative authority to appropriate funds.
