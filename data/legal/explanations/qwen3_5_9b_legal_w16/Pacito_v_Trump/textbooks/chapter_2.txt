Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Standard for Granting Stays Pending Appeal

In federal appellate practice, the court must manage its docket by determining whether to maintain or alter interim relief while a case is pending before the mandate. This is particularly critical in immigration cases, where executive policies may be in flux. In *Pacito v. Trump*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit engaged in a comprehensive review of the procedural posture regarding a refugee admissions suspension and the subsequent defunding of resettlement programs. The panel issued an expedited order to stay the district court’s preliminary injunctions pending the final disposition of the appeal. To do so, the court applied the well-established standards for granting stays pending appeal, analyzing the likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable injury, and the balance of public interests. The following sections detail how the Fourth Circuit, as reflected in this Ninth Circuit opinion, applies the staying standards in the context of immigration administrative law.

## Nken v. Holder Factors: Likelihood of Success and Irreparable Injury

The Supreme Court established in *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), that a party seeking a stay pending appeal must satisfy four factors: (1) whether the applicant has made a strong showing that he is likely to succeed on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure the other parties interested in the proceeding; and (4) where the public interest lies. In *Pacito*, the Ninth Circuit panel explicitly adopted this framework, noting that while all four factors are considered, the first two are the most critical to the decision-making process. In the context of this appeal, the Government sought a stay because the district court's injunction prevented the enforcement of President Trump's Executive Order regarding refugee admissions. To decide whether to stay the injunction, the court had to weigh the Government’s likelihood of success on the merits of the challenge to Executive Order 14163 against the plaintiffs’ potential irreparable harm from the suspension of admissions. The court’s analysis demonstrated that the balance of these factors heavily influenced the interim relief granted.

## Public Interest and Injury to Other Parties

The assessment of the public interest and the injury to other parties is closely intertwined with the statutory authority granted to the Executive Branch. The Ninth Circuit observed that considerations touching upon both domestic and foreign interests favor the United States, particularly due to the “broad discretion” conferred on the President within the context of national foreign policy. The panel emphasized that the Government’s position on these factors was strong because the underlying executive actions concerned international relations and national security interests. However, the public interest analysis presented a complication regarding funding. While the United States had strong interests in maintaining control over refugee admissions, the public interest in ensuring that admitted refugees received statutorily mandated support remained a potent consideration. This tension between national security interests and administrative statutory obligations led the panel to issue a stay of the district court’s injunctive orders while simultaneously directing the reinstatement of specific cooperative agreements.

## Criteria for Denying a Stay in Immigration Contexts

Understanding the criteria for denying a stay clarifies why the court proceeded with the restriction on the refugees’ right to admission. If the applicants met the threshold for likelihood of success, the court would typically forbid the government from enforcing the policy. In *Pacito*, the court effectively denied Plaintiffs the relief they sought by finding that the Government was likely to prevail on the refugee admission portion of the challenge. The panel relied heavily on the deference accorded to the President's factual determinations regarding entry restrictions. Consequently, the panel concluded that the Plaintiffs did not make a strong showing of likelihood of success on the merits regarding the validity of the admissions suspension. This finding allowed the court to maintain the status quo of the evacuation and admissions process while the appeal was pending, illustrating how the standards function to defer to executive prerogative in immigration classification challenges.

## The 'Most Critical' Factors Analysis

While the four *Nken* factors provide the statutory framework, the Ninth Circuit highlighted that only two of them are truly dispositive in determining the outcome. These are the likelihood of success on the merits and the threat of irreparable injury. The opinion makes clear that the court exercises a specific threshold of probative value on these two points before moving to the more discretionary factors of public interest and injury. In *Pacito*, the court's license to stay the injunction without engaging in a deeper partisan analysis of the Plaintiff's potential irreparable harm was justified by the presumption that these first two factors favored the Government on the entry suspension. The argument relied on the idea that if the Executive is likely to win on the interpretation of the entry statutes—specifically 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)—then the remaining factors do not necessitate denying the stay regardless of the severity of the plaintiff's injury.

## Agency Discretion and Searching Inquiry Limitations

The legal reasoning in *Pacito* regarding Agency Discretion anchors the stay decision in constitutional and statutory deference. Citing *Trump v. Hawaii*, the Ninth Circuit explained that the authority under § 1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and “vests [him] with ample power.” Because the sole prerequisite for § 1182(f) is that the President finds entry would be detrimental to the interests of the United States, the court found that the President had undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement. In this context, the court explicitly refused to engage in a "searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications." The rule is that a stay is denied in this specific context unless the Executive’s finding of detrimency is arbitrary or exceeds textual limits on authority. The opinion concluded that the language of the statute was clear and did not exceed these textual limits, reinforcing the Government's position in the first two *Nken* factors.

## Application of Stay Standards to the Trump Administration

The application of these standards was not uniform across all claims in the brief opinion, revealing a nuanced approach to the Administration’s policies. The court distinguished between the suspension of refugee admissions and the defunding of resettlement services. On the admission side, the *Nken* standards favored the Government due to deference under the Immigration and Nationality Act. However, on the funding side (APA challenge), the court found that the Government might not prevail. This distinction arose because 8 U.S.C. § 1522 relates to specific statutory mandates for providing reception and placement services, which involved distinct congressional intent compared to § 1182(f). Consequently, the court directed the Government to reinstate cooperative agreements to provide these services, even as it stayed the injunctions against the admission suspension. Judge Lee’s partial dissent further highlighted the ambiguity of whether § 1522 acts as a mandatory obligation or merely an authorization, demonstrating how close reading of the statute can shift the balance of the stay analysis under the *Nken* framework.
