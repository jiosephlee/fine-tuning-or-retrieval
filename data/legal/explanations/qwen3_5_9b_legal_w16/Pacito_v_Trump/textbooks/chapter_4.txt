Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

Chapter 4: # Chapter 4: Statutory Obligations for Refugee Services Funding

## Chapter Description
This chapter addresses the second major challenge involving the Suspension of cooperative agreements, analyzing the court's finding on the government's duty under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 to provide reception services.

## Content of 8 U.S.C. § 1522: Reception and Placement Services
To understand the scope of the district court’s injunctive relief and the subsequent panel order, it is necessary to examine the specific statutory framework governing the treatment of refugees already admitted to the United States. While Executive Order No. 14163 focuses on suspending *entry* into the country, 8 U.S.C. § 1522 regulates the period *after* admission. This section, titled "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees," mandates specific reception and placement services for refugees who have already entered the United States or are in transit. The statute imposes obligations that go beyond general funding; it requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to ensure economic self-sufficiency through employment training and language acquisition. Specifically, § 1522 includes requirements to assure that women have equal opportunities for training and that cash assistance is made available in a way that does not discourage self-sufficiency. Additionally, the statute imposes unique obligations for unaccompanied minor refugees, requiring the Director to assume legal and financial responsibility for their immediate care during transit or interim placement. These provisions distinguish the government's statutory duties regarding established refugees from the broader discretionary power to control entry at the border.

## Distinction Between Financial Appropriations and Legal Mandates
A core legal concept emerging from this opinion is the difference between a statutory mandate and a discretionary authorization, particularly when conditioned on funding levels. While the statute uses the word "shall" in its mandate to ensure the quality of assistance under the Program of initial resettlement and subsequent assistance, this court interprets the statute in light of specific funding limitations. The statutory language establishes qualifying conditions, stating that the Director shall provide resources "to the extent of available appropriations." This phrasing signals to the judiciary that while the *duty* to provide specific services is statutory, the *source* of funding is tied to congressional appropriations. The opinion notes that for fiscal year 2025, Congress ultimately appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, although this figure was reduced by $800 million in the Rescissions Act of 2025. The court uses this context to explain that the government did not terminate its obligation to provide services merely because funding was adjusted, provided that the statutory mechanisms to deliver those services remained intact. This distinction is pivotal when analyzing a claim that the government has acted unlawfully by cutting off the operational agreements necessary to fulfill these statutory duties.

## Citing 45 C.F.R. and Migration Assistance Appropriations
In its analysis of the refugee services framework, the court relies on specific administrative regulations and funding statutes to flesh out the meaning of the congressional mandate. The panel order cites 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq., which provide the regulatory framework for the federal funding available for these refugee services. These regulations operationalize the statutory mandate by defining the various categories of assistance the government is authorized to make available. Furthermore, the court references Pub. L. No. 119-4, which adopted the FY 2025 appropriations, alongside the Rescissions Act of 2025, to establish the financial reality facing the agency. Citing these documents serves a specific pedagogical purpose: it demonstrates how a court bridges the gap between broad statutory language (the Refugee Act of 1980 codified in the INA) and the specific administrative reality of agency implementation. By referencing both the Code of Federal Regulations and the Public Law appropriations, the court grounds its decision in the existing administrative law hierarchy, ensuring that the injunction ordering the reinstatement of services is based on a comprehensive understanding of the government's authorized functions.

## Analysis of Cooperative Agreement Terminations
The procedural history and factual record of this case turn on the specific actions taken by the Department of State regarding cooperative agreements before and during the litigation process. On February 26, 2025, the Department terminated every cooperative agreement providing reception and placement services in the United States and terminated almost all agreements providing processing support abroad. The plaintiffs sued, alleging that this termination was arbitrary and violated the Refugee Act of 1980 and the Administrative Procedure Act. During the stay proceedings, the government advised the district court that it was actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency. However, this representation is contrasted with later testimony at oral argument, where the government admitted it was unaware whether progress had been made toward securing a new agency. This factual shift is critical for the legal analysis; it transforms the government's claim from a temporary administrative bottleneck into a potential statutory violation of its ongoing duty to provide reception and placement services to refugees admitted to the country.

## Government's Failure to Demonstrate Future Agency Selection
The court's reasoning regarding the government's ability to provide services relies heavily on the evidence concerning the selection of future service providers. The panel order highlights that despite the government's earlier assurance to the district court that it was preparing a request for proposals to secure a new resettlement agency, the implementation of the suspension left a gap in the legal framework for providing services. The court observes that the government advised the panel during oral argument that it was unaware of whether progress had been made toward securing a new agency capable of providing reception and placement services. In legal terms, this failure to demonstrate a viable mechanism for future agency selection supports the conclusion that the government has failed to meet the statutory requirements of 8 U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). This subsection specifically requires that services be provided through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies. Without evidence that such a mechanism was in motion, the court finds the statutory duty unfulfilled, distinguishing this situation from the discretionary decision to suspend admission.

## Holding: Directive to Reinstate Cooperative Agreements Pending Review
Based on the statutory obligations and the government's current position on the status of replacement contracts, the panel issues a specific directive as the primary holding of the order. The court orders the government to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States. Importantly, the court clarifies that these services shall include the "usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements." This holding is provisional and issued in the context of a stay pending appeal; it functions as an injunctive order to preserve the status quo regarding service delivery while the merits of the APA claim are argued. It distinguishes the court's ability to compel the maintenance of reception services from its current posture regarding the suspension of refugee admissions, where the court found deference to the President's justification under § 1182(f) left the government more likely to prevail.

## Interpretation of APA Claims in Refugee Funding Context
Finally, this chapter emphasizes the procedural posture and legal standards for interpreting Administrative Procedure Act claims in this specific context. In the discussion section of the order, the court explicitly states that while the government is likely to prevail on the constitutional challenge to the suspension of refugee admissions, the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of the plaintiffs' challenges under the APA regarding the defunding of services. This interpretation appears necessary to maintain the standard equilibrium of power between the executive and judicial branches. In the context of the immigration entry ban, the court cites precedent establishing that the President's authority under § 1182(f) "exudes deference." However, regarding the reception services (§ 1522 claim), the court treats the statutory obligation as a non-discretionary duty that survives executive policy changes regarding entry. This separation of the two claims allows the court to stay enforcement of the cooperation agreement terminations even while it leaves the broad discretionary powers regarding admission intact. This creates a legal standard for students to note: the APA claim is viable where the government's action frustrates a specific statutory duty (reception), unlike challenges to broad statutory authorization regarding exclusion.

## Judge Lee's Concurrence and Dissent
Although the primary focus of this chapter is the panel's directive, it is necessary to understand the dissenting views to appreciate the statutory interpretation debate underlying the order. Circuit Judge Lee, concurring in part and dissenting in part, argues that § 1522 should be read as an authorization rather than a mandate. The dissent relies on the statutory title "Authorization for programs" and notes that the specific subsections use the phrase "Director is authorized." Judge Lee contends that § 1522(a)'s mandatory language applies only if the government chooses to fund the programs in the first place. By framing the statute as permissive, Judge Lee would stay the district court's injunction in its entirety, including the suspension of refugee admissions. Understanding this dissent highlights that the panel's directive to reinstate agreements is a compromise interpretation—the court accepts the government's deference under admission law but finds a statutory reading that binds the government to its partnership obligations under the resettlement statute to ensure no admitted refugees are left without required reception and placement services.
