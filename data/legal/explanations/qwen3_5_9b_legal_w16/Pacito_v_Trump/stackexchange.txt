### Why did the panel order vacate the March 25, 2025 stay and issue a new stay pending appeal instead?
Question:
I'm trying to chart the procedural history of the stay in this case. The panel issued a stay on March 25, then another on April 21, May 9, and then July 18. The latest order on September 12 vacates all of those prior stays before announcing a new stay pending final resolution. Why did the court decide to clear the deck on the stay orders and machinery rather than just letting the first one stand? Does this change the practical effect of the injunction?
Answer:
**Answer**

**Why the Court Vacated the Prior Stays and Issued a New One**

The panel’s decision to vacate the March 25, 2025, stay (along with the subsequent July 18, 2025, order) was procedural in nature, intended to establish a single, comprehensive stay pending the final merits opinion. As the opinion explains, the panel had issued a patchwork of interlocutory orders—including stays and limited carveouts—following briefing and oral argument. In light of the oral argument on September 3, 2025, the court determined that the case was "submitted for decision" with an opinion to follow.

To avoid confusion during this interim period, the panel exercised its discretion to clear the stay log completely. Rather than trying to manage multiple conflicting stays (the original March 25 order, its amendments, and the administrative stay of July 18), the court issued a new "stay pending final resolution of this appeal." This superseding order serves to replace the earlier machinery with a current directive that reflects the court's decision regarding the underlying injunctions *pending* the final ruling.

**Impact on the Practical Effect of the Injunction**

Yes, the issuance of this new stay changes the practical effect of the injunction compared to the prior orders. The changes are twofold:

1.  **Broadening of the Stay:**
    The prior stay issued on March 25, 2025, included a "limited carveout." Under the March 25 order, the government denied the motion for a stay "to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025." This meant that for certain refugees, the district court’s preliminary injunction would have continued to operate or enforcement was allowed.
    
    By contrast, the September 12 order stays the district court’s preliminary injunctions "in their entirety." By suppressing the carveout, the new order grants broad relief to plaintiffs, staying the enforcement of the Executive Order against all relevant individuals rather than taking a class-based approach that arguably allowed the injunction to enforce the suspension of entry for early-conditionally approved refugees.

2.  **Specific Directives on Funding Protocols:**
    The September 12 order introduces affirmative duties regarding the execution of government funds under the Immigration and Nationality Act ("8 U.S.C. § 1522”). The September 12 order issues a directive to reinstate cooperative agreements: "the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States."
    
    While the earlier stays were primarily focused on issuing interim relief regarding the district court’s injunction on the Entry Order (Executive Order 14163), the September 12 order uses the stay period to address the practical implementation of funding. It specifically counters the "government’s uncertainty regarding its ability to provide the reception and placement services statutorily mandated" by ordering the reinstatement of existing agreements immediately. This is an additional compliance order not explicitly detailed in the March 25 public order, shifting the government’s obligations from merely accommodating the entry restriction to actively rebuilding the foreign resettlement infrastructure.

**In summary**, the court vacated the prior stays to reset the procedural framework for a final decision, and by doing so, it expanded the scope of the injunction’s stay to apply in its totality while adding a specific mandate to reinstate state department cooperative agreements under 8 U.S.C. § 1522.

### How does the "likely to succeed on the merits" factor apply when the Court splits on the two different statutory challenges?
Question:
The opinion states that the government is likely to succeed on the challenge to Executive Order 14163 (refugee admission suspension) but is *not* likely to succeed on the challenge regarding the defunding of USRAP under the APA. Since the standards for issuing a stay (Nken factors) weigh heavily on the showing of success on the merits, how did the panel balance these contradictory likelihoods to justify staying the District Court's introductory injunctions in their entirety?
Answer:
This is a great question that highlights the complexity of applying *Nken* factor analysis when there are multiple distinct statutory challenges within a single injunction.

To answer your question: The panel did not balance the contradictory likelihoods of success by granting a "stay in its entirety" in the traditional sense. Rather, they **applied the "likely to succeed on the merits" factor specifically to each statutory challenge**, using the stay order to suspend proceedings generally while carving out an exception for the challenge where the government lacked a meritorious claim.

Here is how the panel reconciled the split likelihoods of success to issue the stay order:

### 1. The "Merits" Factor was Applied Claim-Specific
The order relies on the *Nken* standard but explicitly separates the government's prospects for the two types of injunctions.

*   **Executive Order 14163 (Refugee Admission Suspension):** The panel found the government **likely to succeed** on the merits of this challenge. The order states: "We cannot engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications" under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and cites *Trump v. Hawaii*. Because the Executive Order functions under a deferential standard where the President's "finding[] that the entry... would be detrimental" is the "sole prerequisite," the panel granted a stay pending appeal *even though* the plaintiffs were requesting this specific part of the injunction to be enforced immediately.
*   **USRAP Funding (§ 1522):** Conversely, the panel found the government **not likely to prevail** on the APA challenge regarding the defunding. The court noted the statutory mandate to provide "reception and placement services" and highlighted "the government’s uncertainty regarding its ability to provide" these mandated services, including an admission it was unaware of progress toward securing new agencies.

### 2. The "Except as Noted Below" Carve-out Solved the Tension
You asked how the court justified staying the injunctions "in their entirety." While the order text states the district court's orders are "stayed in their entirety except as specified in this Order," this language provided the necessary bridging mechanism.

*   Because the government was **likely to prevail** on the EO challenge, the court stayed the injunction blocking the suspension, allowing the suspension to proceed pending the final merits opinion.
*   Because the government was **not likely to prevail** on the funding challenge, the court did not let the "stay in entirety" protect the government's defunding. Instead, the court utilized the exception clause to direct the government to **reinstate** the cooperative agreements immediately ("In light of the government’s uncertainty... the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements").

### Summary of the Logical Balance
The panel avoided a pure application of a "global stay" that would have frozen the injunctions regardless of success likelihood. Instead, they treated the "stay" order as a procedural stay of the District Court's orders *pending final resolution*, but modified the *substantive effect* based on the merits:

1.  **EO Challenge:** Governed by a **Stay** on the injunction because the Gov is **likely to succeed** (merits satisfied under *Trump v. Hawaii*).
2.  **Funding Challenge:** Governed by an **Immediate Mandate** (Reinstatement) because the Gov is **not likely to succeed** (statutory duty to fund).

Thus, the "balance" was effectively kept within the exception clause. The court stayed the *orders* generally to keep the issue pending on appeal, but carved out an immediate directive that respected the judgment that the government could not succeed on the funding merits.

### Can someone explain the statutory interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522—specifically how to reconcile "shall" with "Authorization"?
Question:
There seems to be a massive split between the majority and the dissent (Judge Lee) regarding Section 1522 of the INA. The majority reads the language requiring services as mandatory, while the dissent argues the title "Authorization for programs" and the use of "authorized" in subsections (b)-(e) make it permissive. How does the majority's reliance on "shall" in subsection (a) overcome the dissent's argument about subsections (b)-(e) using "authorized"? Does "authorization" inherently mean it's discretionary for funding purposes?
Answer:
In *Pacito v. Trump*, the statutory conflict centers on whether 8 U.S.C. § 1522 creates a mandatory duty to provide refugee services or merely grants discretionary authority to do so. The majority resolves this tension by prioritizing the operative language in § 1522(a)—specifically the use of "shall"—over the statutory title and the permissive language in subsections (b)-(e).

Here is the breakdown of how the majority reconciles these terms against the dissent’s argument:

### 1. The Majority’s Interpretation: "Shall" Imposes Mandatory Obligations
The majority’s reasoning rests on the distinction between the **subject matter** of the statute and the **impulse to act** to create programs.

*   **The Title ("Authorization"):** The dissent correctly points out the title: "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement." However, the majority treats this as descriptive of the statute’s purpose rather than a limitation on agency discretion regarding the *execution* of already-appropriated funds.
*   **The Text ("Shall"):** The majority locates the duty in § 1522(a)(1)(A), which directs the Director "to the extent of available appropriations" to "make available sufficient resources." The panel also explicitly cites § 1522(d)(2)(B) and § 1522(e)(7)(A), which similarly use "shall" to impose duties on the Director/Secretary regarding unaccompanied minors and interim cash/medical support.
*   **The Appropriations:** Crucially, the majority notes that for FY 2025, Congress appropriated specific funds ($3.178 billion). This factual predicate transforms the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" from a standard of discretion into a cap on a mandatory obligation. The Court reasons that once appropriations exist, the "shall" command triggers, and the government cannot simply terminate the cooperative agreements without violating the statutory mandate to provide services.

### 2. The Dissent’s Interpretation: "Authorization" Implies Discretion
Judge Lee’s dissent argues that the statutory framework as a whole signals discretion.
*   **Title vs. Mandate:** The dissent emphasizes that "Authorization" is permissive language, meaning permission to do something, not an obligation.
*   **The "Conditions" Qualifier:** The dissent reads the language in § 1522(a) as: "In providing assistance under this section... shall make available..." The dissent argues the "In providing assistance" phrase creates a condition precedent: the government only *chooses* to provide assistance. If they do not provide assistance, the "shall" provisions never activate.
*   **Discretionary Mechanisms:** Citing subsections (b)-(e), the dissent argues that phrases like "as needed" and "to make necessary decisions" (in § 1522(d)(2)(B)) reinforce that Congress intended broad executive discretion over the timing and scope of funding.

### 3. Reconciling the Two
The majority’s reconciliation of "shall" with "Authorization" relies on the following canon of construction principles evident in the Order's text:

1.  **Specific vs. General:** The majority reads § 1522(a) as the specific command establishing conditions for assistance, whereas subsections (b)-(e) (containing "authorized") describe the mechanisms for implementation. Thus, the "shall" in (a) controls the existence of the duty; the "authorized" language in (b)-(e) controls the means of fulfillment.
2.  **Appropriations as Trigger:** The majority treats the existence of appropriations ($3.178 billion for FY 2025) as satisfying the condition "to the extent of available appropriations." This interpretation neutralizes the "Availability" defense. If Congress has appropriated funds, the Office of Refugee Resettlement is directed "to the extent" of those funds to fulfill the "shall" mandate.
3.  **Obligation to Move Forward:** Unlike the dissent's view that the agency can decide *not* to proceed, the majority views the order to terminate agreements as a failure to act "consistent with" the prior cooperative agreements, thereby violating the statutory requirement to "provide certain reception and placement services."

**Bottom Line**
The majority concludes that while the section is technically titled an "Authorization," the "shall" clauses combined with actual congressional appropriations convert that authorization into a statutory duty for the current fiscal period. The dissent relies on the "Authorization" title to argue for agency autonomy, but the majority relies on the "shall" clauses to argue for judicial enforcement of the duty to utilize appropriated funds.

### Why does the panel direct the reinstatement of cooperative agreements in a "Stay Order" rather than an opinion on the merits?
Question:
The court states that an opinion will issue on the merits in due course. However, in this interim order, it directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services. Why is the court issuing substantive mandates on statutory obligations within a Stay Order? Does this not constitute a ruling on the merits that should wait for the formal opinion, or does the stay mechanism allow for this specific clarification now?
Answer:
### Answer

The panel’s decision to direct reinstatement in a stay order rather than an opinion on the merits rests on the panel’s preliminary application of the stay factors—specifically, the distinction between the court’s deference regarding the President’s restrictions on *entry* versus its interpretation of the statutory obligation to provide services to *admitted* refugees.

Under the standard articulated in *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009), a panel must consider four factors, with the most critical being a showing of likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable injury absent a stay. In *Pacito*, the panel explicitly recognized a split in the legal analysis:
1.  **Deferral on Entry:** The panel acknowledged the government is likely to prevail on the refugee admission restrictions under Executive Order No. 14163. Citing *Trump v. Hawaii*, the panel noted that 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) “‘exudes deference’ to the President,” and the President has fulfilled the sole prerequisite to suspend entry of a class of aliens.
2.  **Limitation on Services:** Conversely, the panel found the government is not likely to prevail on at least one challenge under the Administrative Procedure Act regarding domestic services. The opinion cites 8 U.S.C. § 1522, stating the government “must provide certain reception and placement services to refugees after their admission.”

Because the panel concluded the government would likely lose the statutory challenge regarding the *mandate* to provide services for those already admitted, but would likely win the broader challenge regarding entry restrictions, the panel directed specific relief to address the specific area of statutory liability.

The reinstatement of cooperative agreements within the stay order serves two procedural purposes consistent with the opinion:
*   **Preventing Irreparable Injury:** By terminating cooperation agreements, the State Department halted placement services for refugees admitted prior to the suspension. The panel implies that under the APA challenge, the government is legally bound to provide these services to those individuals regardless of the entry restrictions. Failing to institute this relief immediately would cause injury to the plaintiffs that could not be remedied by a merits ruling once the entry restrictions are ultimately upheld.
*   **Preserving the Status Quo:** The panel vacates its previous stays of the district court’s preliminary injunctions regarding June/July orders. By reinstating the cooperative agreements in the interim stay order, the court ensures that the plaintiffs’ claim regarding specific statutory obligations under § 1522 is not mooted by the timing of the final opinion.

Thus, the stay order acts as an expedited partial ruling on the factors of likelihood of success and irreparable injury, allowing the court to safeguard the specific statutory duty under § 1522 while reserving a formal opinion on the deference due to the President’s broader immigration policies. This distinction allows the panel to address the specific statutory mandate immediately without rendering a final judgment on the broader merits of the entry ban until the full briefing and argument concluded.

### What is the practical legal effect of the March 25, 2025 "limited carveout" now that the March 25 stay is vacated?
Question:
In the March 25 order, the panel established a carveout for individuals "conditionally approved for refugee status" before January 20, 2025. The current September 12 order says they "vacate our prior stay issued March 25." Does this mean the carveout is still in effect for those individuals, or does vacating the March 25 stay imply that *those* individuals are now subject to the suspension like everyone else? Who has the burden of determining current status?
Answer:
**The March 25 carveout is no longer in effect, and individuals conditionally approved for refugee status before January 20, 2025, are now subject to the suspension of the USRAP program.**

**Legal Doctrine: Stay and Vacatur**
In this context, a "stay" from this Court—such as the March 25 order—serves to suspend the enforcement of the underlying District Court orders (the preliminary injunctions) pending appeal. When the Court *vacates* a stay (as it did here on September 12), it reverses the legal effect of that intermediate order. Consequently, the underlying District Court orders take on the effect prescribed by the *new* stay issued in the current order (*Pacito v. Trump*, Order of Sept. 12, 2025).

**Analysis of the September 12 Order**
To determine the current legal status, one must look not to the March 25 order—which is now expressly "vacat[ed]"—but to the Scope of the *new* stay issued September 12. The September 12 order states that "The district court’s orders of February 28... and March 24... are stayed in their entirety except as specified in this Order."

In the context of this litigation, the District Court’s orders were preliminary injunctions that *prohibited* the enforcement of Executive Order No. 14163 (which suspended the entry of refugees). Therefore, when the September 12 order states that these District Court orders (and their injunctions) are "stayed," it means the injunctions are effectively paused or suspended, allowing the Executive Orders and their underlying restrictions to resume enforcement.

The September 12 order provides a specific exception to this general suspension: "the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been **admitted** to the United States."

This exception distinguishes between "admitted" refugees and "conditionally approved" individuals from outside the country. The plaintiffs in this case were defined as "refugees who were previously **admitted** to the United States or who have been **approved for resettlement**... but remain outside the country." The March 25 carveout specifically protected individuals "approved for refugee status... [before] January 20." However, the September 12 order does not re-eablish this protection; it limits its mandatory reinstatement directive to those who have already been **admitted** to the United States.

**Practical Effect and Burden**
Because the September 12 order vacates the March 25 stay without incorporating the March 25-specific language regarding the "conditionally approved" class, and because it explicitly limits its new protections to those "admitted to the United States," the carveout effectively expires. Individuals conditionally approved prior to the cutoff date fall outside the exception for reinstatement of services and remain subject to the suspension of the USRAP program.

Regarding the burden of determining status, the September 12 order does not delegate this determination to the applicants or mandate a specific evidentiary burden for the "conditionally approved" class. However, under the March 25 language which was vacated, the Court noted that these individuals had been approved by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS). Without a specific order reinstating that framework, the burden of establishing one's status under the new regime generally falls to the individual applicant or is determined by the relevant agency under standard administrative procedures, as the September 12 order directs the government to act on the basis of those who were already "admitted."

### How does the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" factor into the court's decision regarding the $800 million funding reduction?
Question:
The majority mentions that FY 2025 appropriated funds were reduced by $800 million in the Rescissions Act of 2025. However, the court also mentions that the funding language in § 1522 uses "to the extent of available appropriations." If Congress has cut the funding, does that Bankruptcy Act/Reduced Funding mean the Secretary is explicitly released from the § 1522 duty in the year of the reduction? Or can the court require reinstatement of agreements regardless of whether the money exists in the account?
Answer:
Based on the **Panel Order** issued in *Pacito v. Trump* (Sept. 12, 2025), the phrase "to the extent of available appropriations" does not currently release the Secretary from the statutory duty to reinstate the agreements, despite the $800 million funding reduction in the Rescissions Act of 2025.

Under the interim confines of this Order, the Court has characterized the compliance requirements of 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as "statutorily mandated" rather than merely "authorized," even while acknowledging the reduction in available funds.

### 1. Interim Authority: Order vs. Merits Opinion
First, it is critical to distinguish between the **interim stay order** and the final **merits opinion**. The text of *Pacito* states: "An opinion on the merits will issue in due course." This specific document constitutes a "stay pending appeal" directing the government to take action while the case is pending. At this stage, the Panel has dictated the outcome regarding the funding obligation for the interim period.

### 2. Mandatory Duty Despite Funding Limitations
The specific question you raise regarding the funding cut turns on how the Court interpreted the "to the extent of available appropriations" clause.

*   **The Funding Reduction:** The Order explicitly notes that the FY 2025 appropriation of $3.178 billion was reduced by $800 million in the *Rescissions Act of 2025*.
*   **The Statutory Language:** Section 1522(a)(1)(A) contains the "to the extent of available appropriations" limitation.
*   **The Court's Interpretation:** The Panel Order states that Section 1522 "requires the government to provide certain reception and placement services" and refers to these services as "**statutorily mandated**."

Crucially, the Panel did not treat the funding reduction as a release from these obligations. Instead, the Order mandates specific action: "In light of the government’s uncertainty regarding its ability to provide the reception and placement services... the government is hereby directed to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services."

This directive suggests that the "to the extent" language limits the **scope** or **method** of utilizing the remaining funds, but does not excuse the Secretary from the underlying obligation to enter into the agreements necessary to support the mandate. The Court is effectively ordering the reinstatement of the mechanisms (agreements), placing the burden on the agency to ensure those agreements are executed within the statutory limits of the available appropriation.

### 3. The Dissent’s Counter-Argument
The partial dissent by Judge Lee provides the necessary counter-weight for a law student’s understanding of the statutory interpretation challenge. Judge Lee argues that the "authorization" language in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 ("The Director is authorized") is distinct from the "shall" language in subsection (a).

> "Section 1522’s title is 'Authorization for programs... AUTHORIZATION is permissive, not mandatory... [and] it does not require that those programs operate."

Judge Lee asserts that subsection (a) imposes "conditions and considerations" only **"if the government chooses to fund refugee programs."** This highlights why the funding phrase is so critical: the Majority's interpretation (stated in the Order) treats § 1522 as requiring the availability of services regardless of fiscal shortfall, whereas the Dissent reads the funding cut as a potential execution barrier that negates the mandate.

### Conclusion for the Law Student
For the specific purpose of this **Panel Order**, the "to the extent of available appropriations" phrase **does not** release the Secretary from the duty to reinstate agreements. The Court has interpreted the mandate as operative and the funding reduction merely as a logistical constraint on the implementation, not a legal defense to the injunction itself. However, given the *Pacito* brief's conflict between the "mandate" and "authorization" interpretations of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the final merits decision will likely clarify whether the funding cut provides an absolute statutory "get out of jail free" card for the Executive Branch or simply constrains the scale of service delivery once the agreements are reinstated.

Until the merits opinion is issued, the staying Order requires the Secretary to comply fully with the reinstatement directive, regardless of the funding reduction's impact on the account balance.

### Why does the court cite Trump v. Hawaii to defer to the President on § 1182(f) but seemingly deny deference on the APA challenge to funding?
Question:
The opinion relies on Trump v. Hawaii to establish that the President has broad discretion under § 1182(f) to suspend entry. However, it simultaneously concludes the Government is not likely to prevail on the APA/Funding challenge by pointing to specific mandatory language of § 1522. Is there a doctrinal limit to how far the Trump deference command extends? Did the court find that the "detrimental to the interests" finding doesn't cover the domestic statutory obligations of § 1522, or does it violate the Refugee Act's intent?
Answer:
The court distinguishes between the two challenges based on a clear textual split between 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The "deference" command from *Trump v. Hawaii* applies to the President's unconditional discretion to exclude aliens (the power to admit), whereas the APA challenge to funding implicates specific statutory duties incumbent upon the government once aliens are admitted (the power to support).

### 1. Broad Discretion Under § 1182(f)
For the suspension of refugee admissions, the court grants the President full deference consistent with *Trump v. Hawaii*. The opinion describes § 1182(f) as granting the President "ample power to impose entry restrictions" and a "broad discretion" that prevents the court from engaging in a "searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications." The opinion notes that the "sole prerequisite" is the President's finding that entry would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States," a requirement the court deems the President has undoubtedly fulfilled. Here, the statute itself vests the power in the executive to decide the destiny of entry without specific congressional mandates that bind the President's policy choice.

### 2. Mandatory Obligations Under § 1522
In contrast, regarding the administrative challenge to defunding, the court finds the government is "not likely to prevail on at least one of plaintiffs' challenges under the APA." The opinion explicitly distinguishes the nature of this authority from § 1182(f) by citing the mandatory language of § 1522. The court states: "Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government **must** provide certain reception and placement services to refugees after their admission into the United States."

The opinion grounds this conclusion in specific statutory mandates:
*   **§ 1522(a)(1):** The Director "shall" make available resources for employment training and English language training "as required."
*   **§ 1522(e)(7)(A):** The Secretary "shall develop and implement alternative projects" for medical and support services "as needed."
*   **Factual Context:** The court notes that these statutory services are standards by which funding is permitted ("to the extent of available appropriations") and that Congress had already appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance for FY 2025.

### Synthesis
The doctrinal limit the court identifies is the difference between **unconditional exclusion** and **conditional resettlement**. *Trump v. Hawaii* deference applies to the former because § 1182(f) creates a discretionary "safety valve" for the President. However, once the admission process has occurred, the statutory scheme shifts. The court treats the provisions of § 1522 as "mandatory language" that limits the President's discretion to simply withdraw funding for established resettlement obligations. While the dissent (Lee, C.J.) argues § 1522 is merely an authorization, the plurality opinion limits the deference of *Trump* in this specific instance by viewing the *post-admission* funding as a statutory mandate requiring "cooperative agreements" that the government cannot unilaterally suspend without violating the APA.

**Summary:** The court allows *Trump* deference for exclusion because § 1182(f) grants broad discretion, but denies it (or rather, finds the government unlikely to prevail on the APA claim) regarding funding because § 1522 imposes specific obligations to admitants that § 1182(f) does not.
