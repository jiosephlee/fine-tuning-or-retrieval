Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "Pacito v. Trump"

# Chapter 1: Case Background and Procedural History

## Executive Order No. 14163: Suspending USRAP and Funding

To understand the litigation and the procedural history in *Pacito v. Trump*, students must first examine the factual predicate established in January 2025. On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, entitled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” This order relied on the authority of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the Statutory Detriment Clause. Specifically, President Trump determined that entry into the United States of refugees under the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) would be detrimental to the interests of the United States. Consequently, the Order suspended such entry pending further findings.

Pursuant to a different executive order, Executive Order No. 14169 dated January 20, 2025, the Department of State suspended all funding for the USRAP program on January 24, 2025. This suspension of funding purportedly was under authority of “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” Notably, at the time this funding was suspended, it covered domestic resettlement services for refugees who had already been admitted to the United States. These specific factual predicates—the suspension of admissions and the suspension of funding—are the central actions challenged in this case.

## Plaintiffs' Identity and Claims Under the Refugee Act and APA

This chapter establishes the parties and their specific legal claims. The plaintiffs comprise two groups: refugees who were previously admitted to the United States or approved for resettlement but remained outside the country at the time of the lawsuit; and three organizations holding cooperative agreements with the State Department for overseas processing and resettlement services filed in February 2025.

Regarding legal standards, the plaintiffs alleged that Executive Order 14163 violated the Refugee Act of 1980, codified in various provisions of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The plaintiffs' claim regarding the defunding of USRAP violations of the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. The Refugee Act of 1980 is referenced as a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons. Furthermore, the case has implications for 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which addresses domestic resettlement frameworks.

## District Court Rulings: Preliminary Injunctions (February - March 2025)

The procedural history begins in February 2025 when the plaintiffs filed suit in the Western District of Washington. Shortly after filing, on February 25, 2025, the district court orally issued a preliminary injunction. This order prohibited the enforcement of specific sections (§§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4) of Executive Order No. 14163. While the court initially granted this relief orally, the government terminated cooperative agreements for reception and placement services on February 26, 2025, following the oral ruling.

By February 28, 2025, the district court issued a written order to clarify the scope of the preliminary injunction. This written order specified that it also prohibited the suspension of USRAP funding. On March 24, 2025, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction. Unlike the first order, this order was more specific to the plaintiff organizations; it required the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements that had been terminated after the February 25 ruling. The government filed notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025, initiating the appellate process.

## Procedural Timeline of Appeals and Prior Stays

Once the notices of appeal were filed, the Ninth Circuit engaged in a series of interlocutory stays. On March 25, 2025, a motions panel granted a stay pending appeal in part. However, that panel denied the motion for a stay to the extent the injunction applied to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status before January 20, 2025. Following this, the Ninth Circuit issued clarifying orders on April 21 and May 9 regarding this "limited carveout from the stay."

The procedural timeline accelerated significantly after oral argument. In July 2025, the district court ordered a magistrate judge to oversee an enforcement framework for the limited carveout. Meanwhile, the Ninth Circuit noted the government filed an emergency motion on July 17 to enforce, clarify, or amend the stay order. In response, the Ninth Circuit issued an administrative stay on July 18, 2025, staying the district court’s July 14 order. Prior to the current panel's decision on September 12, 2025, the court had vacated the previous administrative and stays of March 25.

## Expedited Oral Argument and Expedited Order

Following the filings and briefing, the Ninth Circuit expedited the process for this matter. Parties were directed to briefing on the limited carveout before hearing. Oral argument was scheduled for an expedited basis on September 3, 2025. Following argument, the court found that the case was submitted for decision and would issue a full opinion in due course.

This current Panel Order serves as an "interim" remedy. The court decided to vacate the administrative stay issued on July 18 and vacate the stay of March 25, as amended on April 21 and May 9. Instead of relying on previous stays, the court issued this current order as a stay pending the final resolution of the appeal. This order illustrates a common appellate procedure where an earlier interlocutory order is superseded to ensure clarity as the case approaches a final disposition.

## Scope of the District Court Orders Subject to Appeal

The critical procedural outcome of this opinion is the scope of the stay it issues vis-à-vis the district court orders. The autumn order specifies that the district court’s orders of February 28, 2006, and March 24, 2025 are stayed in their entirety. A stay of a preliminary injunction generally means that the injunction does not apply, allowing the government to enforce the actions originally prohibited (the suspension of the REFRESAP program and funding).

However, the Order contains an explicit exception to the stay regarding the service mandates. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the text notes that the government is obligated to provide reception and placement services. Because the government was not secured a new agency or agencies to provide these services and the mandate was statutory, the Order directs the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary for such services. This command stands despite the broader stay of the district court's injunctions. The distinction highlights the court's procedural focus: while the general suspension of the refugee program and the validity of the Executive Order are stayed pending the final opinion, the specific statutory obligation to reinstate agreements for admitted refugees is enforced through this interim order. This leads to a situation where the injunction is technically stayed, but the litigation is temporarily paused except for the specific reinstatement of funding agreements.

# Chapter 2: Standard for Granting Stays Pending Appeal

In federal appellate practice, the court must manage its docket by determining whether to maintain or alter interim relief while a case is pending before the mandate. This is particularly critical in immigration cases, where executive policies may be in flux. In *Pacito v. Trump*, the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit engaged in a comprehensive review of the procedural posture regarding a refugee admissions suspension and the subsequent defunding of resettlement programs. The panel issued an expedited order to stay the district court’s preliminary injunctions pending the final disposition of the appeal. To do so, the court applied the well-established standards for granting stays pending appeal, analyzing the likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable injury, and the balance of public interests. The following sections detail how the Fourth Circuit, as reflected in this Ninth Circuit opinion, applies the staying standards in the context of immigration administrative law.

## Nken v. Holder Factors: Likelihood of Success and Irreparable Injury

The Supreme Court established in *Nken v. Holder*, 556 U.S. 418 (2009), that a party seeking a stay pending appeal must satisfy four factors: (1) whether the applicant has made a strong showing that he is likely to succeed on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure the other parties interested in the proceeding; and (4) where the public interest lies. In *Pacito*, the Ninth Circuit panel explicitly adopted this framework, noting that while all four factors are considered, the first two are the most critical to the decision-making process. In the context of this appeal, the Government sought a stay because the district court's injunction prevented the enforcement of President Trump's Executive Order regarding refugee admissions. To decide whether to stay the injunction, the court had to weigh the Government’s likelihood of success on the merits of the challenge to Executive Order 14163 against the plaintiffs’ potential irreparable harm from the suspension of admissions. The court’s analysis demonstrated that the balance of these factors heavily influenced the interim relief granted.

## Public Interest and Injury to Other Parties

The assessment of the public interest and the injury to other parties is closely intertwined with the statutory authority granted to the Executive Branch. The Ninth Circuit observed that considerations touching upon both domestic and foreign interests favor the United States, particularly due to the “broad discretion” conferred on the President within the context of national foreign policy. The panel emphasized that the Government’s position on these factors was strong because the underlying executive actions concerned international relations and national security interests. However, the public interest analysis presented a complication regarding funding. While the United States had strong interests in maintaining control over refugee admissions, the public interest in ensuring that admitted refugees received statutorily mandated support remained a potent consideration. This tension between national security interests and administrative statutory obligations led the panel to issue a stay of the district court’s injunctive orders while simultaneously directing the reinstatement of specific cooperative agreements.

## Criteria for Denying a Stay in Immigration Contexts

Understanding the criteria for denying a stay clarifies why the court proceeded with the restriction on the refugees’ right to admission. If the applicants met the threshold for likelihood of success, the court would typically forbid the government from enforcing the policy. In *Pacito*, the court effectively denied Plaintiffs the relief they sought by finding that the Government was likely to prevail on the refugee admission portion of the challenge. The panel relied heavily on the deference accorded to the President's factual determinations regarding entry restrictions. Consequently, the panel concluded that the Plaintiffs did not make a strong showing of likelihood of success on the merits regarding the validity of the admissions suspension. This finding allowed the court to maintain the status quo of the evacuation and admissions process while the appeal was pending, illustrating how the standards function to defer to executive prerogative in immigration classification challenges.

## The 'Most Critical' Factors Analysis

While the four *Nken* factors provide the statutory framework, the Ninth Circuit highlighted that only two of them are truly dispositive in determining the outcome. These are the likelihood of success on the merits and the threat of irreparable injury. The opinion makes clear that the court exercises a specific threshold of probative value on these two points before moving to the more discretionary factors of public interest and injury. In *Pacito*, the court's license to stay the injunction without engaging in a deeper partisan analysis of the Plaintiff's potential irreparable harm was justified by the presumption that these first two factors favored the Government on the entry suspension. The argument relied on the idea that if the Executive is likely to win on the interpretation of the entry statutes—specifically 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)—then the remaining factors do not necessitate denying the stay regardless of the severity of the plaintiff's injury.

## Agency Discretion and Searching Inquiry Limitations

The legal reasoning in *Pacito* regarding Agency Discretion anchors the stay decision in constitutional and statutory deference. Citing *Trump v. Hawaii*, the Ninth Circuit explained that the authority under § 1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and “vests [him] with ample power.” Because the sole prerequisite for § 1182(f) is that the President finds entry would be detrimental to the interests of the United States, the court found that the President had undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement. In this context, the court explicitly refused to engage in a "searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications." The rule is that a stay is denied in this specific context unless the Executive’s finding of detrimency is arbitrary or exceeds textual limits on authority. The opinion concluded that the language of the statute was clear and did not exceed these textual limits, reinforcing the Government's position in the first two *Nken* factors.

## Application of Stay Standards to the Trump Administration

The application of these standards was not uniform across all claims in the brief opinion, revealing a nuanced approach to the Administration’s policies. The court distinguished between the suspension of refugee admissions and the defunding of resettlement services. On the admission side, the *Nken* standards favored the Government due to deference under the Immigration and Nationality Act. However, on the funding side (APA challenge), the court found that the Government might not prevail. This distinction arose because 8 U.S.C. § 1522 relates to specific statutory mandates for providing reception and placement services, which involved distinct congressional intent compared to § 1182(f). Consequently, the court directed the Government to reinstate cooperative agreements to provide these services, even as it stayed the injunctions against the admission suspension. Judge Lee’s partial dissent further highlighted the ambiguity of whether § 1522 acts as a mandatory obligation or merely an authorization, demonstrating how close reading of the statute can shift the balance of the stay analysis under the *Nken* framework.

# Chapter 3: Judicial Deference in Refugee Admissions Challenges

## Statutory Authority: 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and Presidential Power

To understand the Ninth Circuit's reasoning in *Pacito v. Trump*, one must first grapple with the statutory foundation of the President's action. This opinion concerns the President's reliance on 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), a statute within the Immigration and Nationality Act. The district court had issued a preliminary injunction staying the enforcement of Executive Order 14163, which sought to suspend the Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) under the authority of § 1182(f). The panel order explains that this statutory provision grants the President broad power to manage entry restrictions. Specifically, 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) permits the President to suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants if the President finds that such entry would be detrimental to the interests of the United States. The text emphasizes that this authority represents a significant grant of discretion to the executive branch, allowing the President to impose restrictions not otherwise enumerated in the Immigration and Nationality Act.

In the context of appellate procedure, the Ninth Circuit views statutory grants of broad discretion differently than statutory mandates. Here, the court analyzes the challenge to the suspension of refugee admissions. When addressing challenges to the President's use of § 1182(f), the court does not treat the statute as requiring a specific justification that it can independently review for fact-accuracy in the administrative context. Instead, the court recognizes that the statute vests the President with exemption immunity from ordinary judicial override regarding the determination of national interest. This deference shapes the entire standard of review for the stay.

## The Sole Prerequisite: Finding Entry Detrimental to Interests

A critical element in reading this opinion is identifying what the statute explicitly requires from the President. The text highlights that the "sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f)" is a finding that entry would be "detrimental to the interests of the United States." This phrasing is deliberate. The court notes that only two conditions must exist under the statute: first, the President must make a finding, and second, that finding must relate to detriments to U.S. interests. The court does not impose additional requirements on Presidential authority under this section beyond what the text of the statute provides.

When applying this principle, the court notes that the President has undoubtedly fulfilled this statutory prerequisite. The opinion indicates that the Executive Order relied upon this statutory authority to direct the suspension of refugee admissions. For a reader, understanding this means viewing the President's action not necessarily through the lens of whether the suspension was "good policy" regarding refugees, but whether the President met the minimal statutory threshold of making a finding. Because the § 1182(f) text does not require a hearing or specific evidence while the finding is made, the court's analysis focuses heavily on whether the authority itself is valid, rather than interrogating the specific facts backing the finding. Consequently, the court assumes the President met the statutory burden, moving the analysis to the standard of review applied to the Executive's authority to claim national interests were at risk.

## Precedent from Trump v. Hawaii (585 U.S. 667)

The panel's reasoning relies heavily on *Trump v. Hawaii*, the Supreme Court case that addressed the merits of a similar Presidential entry restriction. The order quotes *Hawaii* extensively to establish the level of deference Due Process and other constitutional challenges must receive in this context. In the earlier case, the Supreme Court reasoned that § 1182(f) "exudes deference" to the President and "vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions."

In analyzing *Pacito*, the Ninth Circuit finds that the Supreme Court in *Hawaii* clarified the boundaries of this power. A key takeaway for a student of administrative law is how the Court interprets the "sole prerequisite." Post-*Hawaii*, the inquiry shifts away from the necessity of the restriction to the President's ability to make the finding. The court observes that the language of § 1182(f) is clear and that the Executive Order does not exceed any textual limits. This analysis teaches that when the Supreme Court interprets a provision as granting the President "ample power," appellate courts limit independent scrutiny of the underlying policy justification. The opinion uses *Hawaii* to illustrate that statutory interpretation and constitutional challenges in this realm often defer to the Executive Branch's invocation of national security or national interest powers exclusively.

## Evaluation of President's Justifications for Suspension

The opinion discusses the standard for the stay, which involves evaluating four factors, including whether the government is likely to succeed on the merits. In this specific context, the court delineates the line between a "searching inquiry" and a deferential standard. While courts generally scrutinize government claims, this panel notes that regarding the President's invocation of § 1182(f), the standard is highly deferential. The text states that the court cannot engage in "a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President's justifications."

This distinction is vital for understanding the scope of judicial review in immigration and national security matters. The majority in *Pacito* cites *Hawaii* to support the proposition that the President has "undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement." Instead of analyzing the specific reasons given in the Executive Order for why refugee entry might be detrimental, the court applies the legal standard that the President's finding alone satisfies the statute. If the President makes the finding, the legal inquiry essentially ends at the statutory level because the text grants the power. This analysis serves as a cautionary note to law students: while courts can apply general administrative law principles like the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), they often apply distinct, highly deferential standards when Congress has explicitly granted the President special authority by statute.

## Conclusion of the Majority: Government Likely to Prevail

With the statutory authority and deference standard established, the court turns to the four-part test for issuing a stay of the district court's preliminary injunction. The text identifies the two most critical factors: the applicant's likelihood of success on the merits and the potential for irreparable injury. In analyzing whether the government is likely to prevail on the suspension of refugee admissions, the court concludes that it is.

The reasoning rests on the premises established in the previous sections. Because § 1182(f) "exudes deference" and the President has made the requisite finding, the government meets the second most critical factor for the stay. While the district court had enjoined the enforcement of the Executive Order, the Ninth Circuit panel reasoned that given the statutory grant of power, the government would succeed in its challenge to the validity of the suspension under § 1182(f). This determination is significant because it governs the issuance of the stay. In the context of interlocutory appeals, overwhelming deference to the President on the merits allows the court to find that the government is likely to prevail, which then weighs heavily against the necessity of a stay for the plaintiffs. This reasoning demonstrates how statutory language can effectively dictate the outcome of a stay motion before the merits are fully briefed or argued.

## Result: Granting Stay on Refugee Suspension Injunction

The immediate procedural outcome in this chapter is the vacating of prior stays and the issuance of a new stay pending appeal regarding the suspension of refugee admissions. The panel directs that the district court's February 28 and March 24, 2025, preliminary injunctions are stayed "in their entirety" with respect to the suspension, except as modified for other issues. This disposition means the Government is allowed to proceed with restraining the refugee suspension while the appeal is ongoing.

It is important to note that this partial stay order is an interim measure. The opinion explicitly states, "An opinion on the merits will issue in due course." However, the reasoning provided in this section establishes the majority's view on the deference applicable to Presidential actions under § 1182(f). The instruction to the panel signaling that the "government is likely to prevail" reaffirms that, at this stage of the litigation, the judiciary will remain subordinate to the President's authority under this specific statute. The final holding on the merits regarding suspension will likely follow this deferential logic, provided the President continues to demonstrate the statutory finding. This section of the chapter exemplifies how judicial review can balance deferential interpretations of statutes granting broad executive power against the procedural right of litigants to challenge the enforcement of government orders.

# Chapter 4: Statutory Obligations for Refugee Services Funding

## Chapter Description
This chapter addresses the second major challenge involving the Suspension of cooperative agreements, analyzing the court's finding on the government's duty under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 to provide reception services.

## Content of 8 U.S.C. § 1522: Reception and Placement Services
To understand the scope of the district court’s injunctive relief and the subsequent panel order, it is necessary to examine the specific statutory framework governing the treatment of refugees already admitted to the United States. While Executive Order No. 14163 focuses on suspending *entry* into the country, 8 U.S.C. § 1522 regulates the period *after* admission. This section, titled "Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees," mandates specific reception and placement services for refugees who have already entered the United States or are in transit. The statute imposes obligations that go beyond general funding; it requires the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement to ensure economic self-sufficiency through employment training and language acquisition. Specifically, § 1522 includes requirements to assure that women have equal opportunities for training and that cash assistance is made available in a way that does not discourage self-sufficiency. Additionally, the statute imposes unique obligations for unaccompanied minor refugees, requiring the Director to assume legal and financial responsibility for their immediate care during transit or interim placement. These provisions distinguish the government's statutory duties regarding established refugees from the broader discretionary power to control entry at the border.

## Distinction Between Financial Appropriations and Legal Mandates
A core legal concept emerging from this opinion is the difference between a statutory mandate and a discretionary authorization, particularly when conditioned on funding levels. While the statute uses the word "shall" in its mandate to ensure the quality of assistance under the Program of initial resettlement and subsequent assistance, this court interprets the statute in light of specific funding limitations. The statutory language establishes qualifying conditions, stating that the Director shall provide resources "to the extent of available appropriations." This phrasing signals to the judiciary that while the *duty* to provide specific services is statutory, the *source* of funding is tied to congressional appropriations. The opinion notes that for fiscal year 2025, Congress ultimately appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, although this figure was reduced by $800 million in the Rescissions Act of 2025. The court uses this context to explain that the government did not terminate its obligation to provide services merely because funding was adjusted, provided that the statutory mechanisms to deliver those services remained intact. This distinction is pivotal when analyzing a claim that the government has acted unlawfully by cutting off the operational agreements necessary to fulfill these statutory duties.

## Citing 45 C.F.R. and Migration Assistance Appropriations
In its analysis of the refugee services framework, the court relies on specific administrative regulations and funding statutes to flesh out the meaning of the congressional mandate. The panel order cites 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq., which provide the regulatory framework for the federal funding available for these refugee services. These regulations operationalize the statutory mandate by defining the various categories of assistance the government is authorized to make available. Furthermore, the court references Pub. L. No. 119-4, which adopted the FY 2025 appropriations, alongside the Rescissions Act of 2025, to establish the financial reality facing the agency. Citing these documents serves a specific pedagogical purpose: it demonstrates how a court bridges the gap between broad statutory language (the Refugee Act of 1980 codified in the INA) and the specific administrative reality of agency implementation. By referencing both the Code of Federal Regulations and the Public Law appropriations, the court grounds its decision in the existing administrative law hierarchy, ensuring that the injunction ordering the reinstatement of services is based on a comprehensive understanding of the government's authorized functions.

## Analysis of Cooperative Agreement Terminations
The procedural history and factual record of this case turn on the specific actions taken by the Department of State regarding cooperative agreements before and during the litigation process. On February 26, 2025, the Department terminated every cooperative agreement providing reception and placement services in the United States and terminated almost all agreements providing processing support abroad. The plaintiffs sued, alleging that this termination was arbitrary and violated the Refugee Act of 1980 and the Administrative Procedure Act. During the stay proceedings, the government advised the district court that it was actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency. However, this representation is contrasted with later testimony at oral argument, where the government admitted it was unaware whether progress had been made toward securing a new agency. This factual shift is critical for the legal analysis; it transforms the government's claim from a temporary administrative bottleneck into a potential statutory violation of its ongoing duty to provide reception and placement services to refugees admitted to the country.

## Government's Failure to Demonstrate Future Agency Selection
The court's reasoning regarding the government's ability to provide services relies heavily on the evidence concerning the selection of future service providers. The panel order highlights that despite the government's earlier assurance to the district court that it was preparing a request for proposals to secure a new resettlement agency, the implementation of the suspension left a gap in the legal framework for providing services. The court observes that the government advised the panel during oral argument that it was unaware of whether progress had been made toward securing a new agency capable of providing reception and placement services. In legal terms, this failure to demonstrate a viable mechanism for future agency selection supports the conclusion that the government has failed to meet the statutory requirements of 8 U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). This subsection specifically requires that services be provided through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public or private nonprofit agencies. Without evidence that such a mechanism was in motion, the court finds the statutory duty unfulfilled, distinguishing this situation from the discretionary decision to suspend admission.

## Holding: Directive to Reinstate Cooperative Agreements Pending Review
Based on the statutory obligations and the government's current position on the status of replacement contracts, the panel issues a specific directive as the primary holding of the order. The court orders the government to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States. Importantly, the court clarifies that these services shall include the "usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements." This holding is provisional and issued in the context of a stay pending appeal; it functions as an injunctive order to preserve the status quo regarding service delivery while the merits of the APA claim are argued. It distinguishes the court's ability to compel the maintenance of reception services from its current posture regarding the suspension of refugee admissions, where the court found deference to the President's justification under § 1182(f) left the government more likely to prevail.

## Interpretation of APA Claims in Refugee Funding Context
Finally, this chapter emphasizes the procedural posture and legal standards for interpreting Administrative Procedure Act claims in this specific context. In the discussion section of the order, the court explicitly states that while the government is likely to prevail on the constitutional challenge to the suspension of refugee admissions, the government is not likely to prevail on at least one of the plaintiffs' challenges under the APA regarding the defunding of services. This interpretation appears necessary to maintain the standard equilibrium of power between the executive and judicial branches. In the context of the immigration entry ban, the court cites precedent establishing that the President's authority under § 1182(f) "exudes deference." However, regarding the reception services (§ 1522 claim), the court treats the statutory obligation as a non-discretionary duty that survives executive policy changes regarding entry. This separation of the two claims allows the court to stay enforcement of the cooperation agreement terminations even while it leaves the broad discretionary powers regarding admission intact. This creates a legal standard for students to note: the APA claim is viable where the government's action frustrates a specific statutory duty (reception), unlike challenges to broad statutory authorization regarding exclusion.

## Judge Lee's Concurrence and Dissent
Although the primary focus of this chapter is the panel's directive, it is necessary to understand the dissenting views to appreciate the statutory interpretation debate underlying the order. Circuit Judge Lee, concurring in part and dissenting in part, argues that § 1522 should be read as an authorization rather than a mandate. The dissent relies on the statutory title "Authorization for programs" and notes that the specific subsections use the phrase "Director is authorized." Judge Lee contends that § 1522(a)'s mandatory language applies only if the government chooses to fund the programs in the first place. By framing the statute as permissive, Judge Lee would stay the district court's injunction in its entirety, including the suspension of refugee admissions. Understanding this dissent highlights that the panel's directive to reinstate agreements is a compromise interpretation—the court accepts the government's deference under admission law but finds a statutory reading that binds the government to its partnership obligations under the resettlement statute to ensure no admitted refugees are left without required reception and placement services.

# Chapter 5: Concurrence and Dissent on Statutory Interpretation

## Majority's Interpretation of § 1522 as Mandatory
In the context of *Pacito v. Trump*, the Ninth Circuit’s panel addressed not only the suspension of refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) but also the second-order issue regarding the defunding of refugee reception and placement services. While an opinion on the merits was scheduled for a later date, the panel issued an administrative stay of the district court’s preliminary injunctions pending final resolution. Crucially, the majority explained its reasoning for why the rebate to the district court’s injunction on funding defunding was necessary. The court noted that the government failed to promptly replace the terminated cooperative agreements despite knowing that such agreements are necessary for state resettlement services. The majority interpreted 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing obligations on the government to provide such services to refugees admitted to the United States. Consequently, the court directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who had been admitted to the United States prior to the suspension. This interpretation rested on the court’s assessment that the government was not likely to prevail on the plaintiffs’ Administrative Procedure Act challenge regarding the defunding of these services, effectively treating the statute as creating a duty that the executive branch could not abrogate unilaterally.

## Judge Lee's Textual Analysis of 'Authorization' and 'Shall' Language
Circuit Judge Lee, writing a concurrence in part and dissent in part, disagreed fundamentally with the majority’s conclusion that the government must be directed to reinstate funding. Her dissent focused primarily on the statutory text of 8 U.S.C. § 1522, applying a textualist approach that emphasizes the precise language used by Congress. While the majority viewed the statute as imposing a mandatory requirement, Judge Lee observed that the section is titled “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” She argued that the word “Authorization” is inherently permissive, distinct from a mandate. Furthermore, she examined the operative language within the text of the statute. The majority relied on specific subsections that use the word “shall,” which typically signals a mandatory obligation. However, Judge Lee pointed out that other subsections use the language “is authorized,” which she characterized as statutory signals that the executive branch retains discretion over funding and implementation. By focusing on the interplay between these terms, Judge Lee asserted that the statute does not compel the executive branch to provide these services, but rather supplies permission if the funding exists.

## Distinguishing Mandatory 'Conditions' from Discretionary Programs
A central tension in the dissent appears in the interpretation of 8 U.S.C. § 1522(a), which outlines “Conditions and considerations” for providing assistance. Judge Lee distinguished this mandatory language from the discretionary programs described in subsections (b) through (e). She argued that § 1522(a) imposes mandatory conditions and considerations *only if* the government chooses to fund refugee programs. For example, she noted that the Director “shall make available sufficient resources,” but she contextualized this phrase under the umbrella of the discretionary funding provisions found in subsequent subsections. In her view, the statutory language functions to regulate how funds are used if spent, rather than mandating that specific programs (like the initial relocation or cash assistance programs) must be created in the first place. Judge Lee emphasized that the executive branch has a choice regarding whether to operate under these conditions, meaning that the statute does not strip the executive of the power to determine whether to pursue these programs.

## The 'Necessary' and 'As Needed' Qualifiers in Statute
Beyond the specific verbs and titles, Judge Lee focused on qualifying language within 8 U.S.C. § 1522 that she read as reinforcing discretion. Specifically, she highlighted clauses that use terms like “if necessary” and “as needed.” For instance, § 1522(d)(2)(B) states that the program’s Director shall assume legal responsibility for unaccompanied children only “if such responsibility is necessary.” She argued that even when the word “shall” appears, the section drips with deference to the executive branch. Similarly, § 1522(e)(7)(A) directs the Secretary to develop projects for support as they are “needed.” Judge Lee reasoned that these qualifiers leave the determination of the scope and timing of programs up to executive discretion. Therefore, the record did not support a reading that forced the government to act in a specific manner if it determined it was not "necessary" or "as needed" under its own assessment. In the dissent, she noted the uncertainty regarding unaccompanied children itself, suggesting that the law depends on factual determinations the executive branch is mandated to make.

## Limitation of Executive Branch Mandate Without Fiscal Appropriation Requirement
Beyond the textual qualifiers, Judge Lee grounded her interpretation in the separation of powers and the executive branch’s lack of mandate for spending without a specific fiscal appropriation. She noted that the statute is qualified by the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations.” This phrase, she argued, is a clear congressional signal that such programs are subject to spending constraints. While the majority cited $3.178 billion in FY 2025 appropriations alongside a reduction of $800 million, Judge Lee read this as confirmation that the statutes allow the executive to operate within appropriated limits rather than a mandate to allocate those funds specifically to refugee services before they are permitted by statute. Her logic posits that if the law does not demand that the executive branch provide these services, the court cannot mandate them. She contended that the power to appropriate the funds resides with Congress, and without a mandatory appropriation command in § 1522, the court’s order to reinstate cooperative agreements effectively forced the executive to spend money that the statute did not require to be spent.

## Impact of Dissent on Future Rulings and Congressional Power
The divergence between the majority and Judge Lee highlights a significant doctrinal split regarding the scope of judicial review vis-à-vis executive implementation of immigration policy. Judge Lee’s dissent raises the prospect that future rulings interpreting similar statutory mandates must carefully distinguish between “needs” that trigger specific duties and policy choices left to the executive. By framing the statute as an authorization rather than a mandate, the dissent suggests that courts should be hesitant to issue non-discretionary orders in the context of federal funding allocations that are tied to appropriations power. If Congress intended to create a mandatory obligation under § 1522, Judge Lee argues it should have done so without the permissive language of authorization. This creates a precedent for future courts to view similar regulations in areas where the statute uses permissive language as discretionary rather than binding. Ultimately, the dissent serves as a reminder that strong textualist readings can constrain judicial mandates on spending and policy implementation, thereby preserving agency discretion and respecting the legislative authority to appropriate funds.
