Title: Blog about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Admitting the Past: The ‘Battle’ Test for Intrinsic Firearm Evidence

#United States v. Justin Cutbank may look on its face like a standard criminal appeal—specifically, one where the defendant loses on the merits. But beneath the surface of this Eighth Circuit decision lies a critical reinforcement of how the federal courts handle evidentiary challenges in felony firearm convictions. At the heart of the litigation was the Federal Rules of Evidence’s prohibition on character evidence, specifically Rule 404(b). Justin Cutbank, a felon facing a life sentence for a life sentence, argued that witness testimony regarding his prior possession of the same weapon was essentially "propensity evidence" banned by the rule. The Eighth Circuit rejected this argument entirely, anchoring its reasoning in a single, controlling precedent: *United States v. Battle*. The takeaway for criminal practitioners is clear: in a felon-in-possession case, showing that a defendant had a specific gun "earlier than tonight" is often admissible, not because it proves bad character, but because it proves continuity of control.

## The Battle Framework: Propensity Versus Possession

The legal friction in *Cutbank* centers on Section II of the opinion, where the Eighth Circuit addresses Cutbank’s pretrial motion to exclude testimony from his girlfriend and housemate regarding prior sightings of the sawed-off Marlin rifle. Cutbank argued this was propensity evidence forbidden by Rule 404(b). The government countered that this was "intrinsic evidence" necessary to prove the charged offense. The district court agreed, a ruling the appellate court affirmed without hesitation.

The opinion explicitly relies on *United States v. Battle* to bridge the evidentiary gap. In *Battle*, the Eighth Circuit established that if the defendant is charged with possession of a specific firearm, testimony that they possessed that *same* firearm at another time is directly relevant to establishing ownership or control. As the court notes in *Cutbank*: "Prior possession of a firearm is directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon." This distinction is vital for criminal defense. Usually, Rule 404(b) exists to prevent the jury from hearing, "I like these prior crimes." But possession is continuous; if the state proves the item is already in their hand, it attacks the "second vessel" theory that the gun was a chaperone of the night rather than the defendant indefinitely. The court held that showing the defendant had the weapon "a handful of times" before the charged incident was not "prior bad acts" in spirit, but rather proof of the physical element of the crime itself.

## Analyzing the Rationale: Is "Intrinsic" Too Broad?

While the Eighth Circuit has codified this distinction for years, *Cutbank* reinforces its application in a way that narrows the ability of defense counsel to suppress evidence in federal firearm cases. The reasoning rests on the premise that without this continuity, a defendant could claim the gun was a gift or Robbery the night of the raid, leaving them with no link between the defendant and the weapon. However, this logic potentially sets a low bar for the prosecution. If the jury is told a witness saw the defendant with the gun "a handful of times," the information is highly prejudicial to a layperson. It establishes a narrative of danger regardless of the defendant’s defense of, say, borrowing the gun or being innocent of possession.

The *Cutbank* opinion acknowledges the sting of this evidence—Cutbank claimed the gun was stolen by police or innocent parties. But the court dismisses this as an escape route. The Eighth Circuit’s strict adherence to *Battle* means that unless the defense can argue the evidence is technically irrelevant because it concerns a *different* firearm or a non-violent possession incident, this type of testimony is largely inadmissible to exceeding the jury’s perceived scope. This creates a doctrinal environment where the distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic evidence in firearm cases is razor-thin, favoring the state’s ability to prove possession over the defendant’s ability to limit the scope of the weapon's origin.

## Harmless Error and the Limits of Appeal

Beyond the evidentiary rulings, *Cutbank* illustrates the court’s skepticism regarding appellate review of factual errors when physical evidence exists. Cutbank appealed on several fronts, including the admission of her sister’s death to explain why the witness changed her story. When the court noted these rulings did not result in abuse of discretion, it did so with a heavy dose of the "harmless error" doctrine. The opinion states, "Cutbank cannot clear this hurdle. The government’s evidence, both testimonial and physical, was substantial."

This reflects a broader trend in appellate criminal law, particularly regarding the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) and sentencing enhancements. The Eighth Circuit takes a wide view of "substantial prejudice" to the defendant. If the state proves DNA matching on a sawed-off rifle, suppression of testimonial continuity evidence arguably won't change the verdict. The court emphasizes that she cannot "clear this hurdle," effectively signalling that in felon-in-possession cases, the focus of scrutiny on appellate review should shift from evidentiary rulings to substantive sentencing or constitutional violations. It is a reminder that while defense counsel fights to stop the evidence from entering, appellate courts are generally "unconstrained in their discretion" of the error's impact on the great mass of a jury's decision.

## Conclusion

*United States v. Justin Cutbank* may not offer the typical doctrinal innovation seen in Supreme Court opinions, but it offers a pragmatic reality check for criminal defense practitioners. The Eighth Circuit’s refusal to second-guess the admission of prior possession testimony demonstrates the judiciary's preference for the "Battle" framework as the default in firearm possession cases. By classifying evidence of prior sightings as intrinsic rather than propensity, the court ensures that possession counts as a continuous state of being. This does not fix the potential for prejudice in the jury room, but it does provide the legal armor for the prosecution to ensure evidence that might prove the gun is indeed the government's witness: the gun-and the previous possession of that gun.



# Guideline Triaging: The Intersection of ACCA and USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B)

## The Arithmetic of Advisability
In the post-*Booker* advisory guidelines regime, technical sentencing disputes often reveal more about the stakes of judicial discretion than the raw merits of the underlying conduct. United States v. Justin Cutbank, a recent Eighth Circuit decision, provides a clear empirical lesson on how courts navigate the mathematical conflicts between the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) and specific enhancement provisions in the United States Sentencing Guidelines. While the court’s summary of the evidentiary record (primarily addressing Rule 404(b) admissibility and hearsay) is perfunctorily dismissed, the sentencing analysis offers a necessary checkpoint for defense counsel and appellate courts alike. At the heart of Cutbank lies a complex question of calculation: When a defendant triggers ACCA statutory enhancements through a history of violent felonies but also faces the Guideline enhancement for possession in connection with another felony, which level controls? The Eighth Circuit’s handling of this intersection reinforces the doctrine that procedural correctness in the Guidelines calculation trumps substantive challenges to the § 3553(a) sentencing factors.

## The Mathematical Arbitration of ACCA vs. Firearm Enhancements
The core technical issue in Cutbank is resolved through the application of USSG § 4B1.4(b), a provision often overlooked in high-level discussions about sentencing. In a case like this, where the defendant is a career offender with a significant prior record, the ACCA status itself dictates a specific offense level—here, a total offense level of 34. Simultaneously, the underlying conduct involves the possession of a sawed-off firearm in connection with another criminal offense, which triggers a four-level increase under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). The PSR calculated a total offense level of 35 based on the latter, which fell within the higher range simply because the specific enhancements and Criminal History Category (Category VI in this instance) pushed the calculation above the baseline ACCA level. Cutbank argued that applying the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement created a sentence that conflicted with the core principle of § 3553(a) that a sentence should be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary."

The Eighth Circuit rejected this argument on procedural grounds, relying heavily on the rule that the greater offense level applies when distinct statutory or guideline calculations result in conflicting levels. By citing USSG § 4B1.4(b), the court confirmed that the Guidelines themselves provide the mechanism to resolve this mathematical hierarchy. The court noted that the jury had already established the factual basis for the prior violent felonies and the connection of the firearm to the charged offense; therefore, the calculation error or lower ACCA guideline level was superseded by the more favorable-to-Government enhanced level of 35. This ensures that the Guidelines do not penalize the specific dangerous nature of the firearm (the enhanced level) less than the general recidivism profile. The technical hierarchy prevents a defendant from effectively "stepping down" from a specific enhancement to the lower floor of the ACCA calculation when both yield distinct offense levels.

## The Presumption of Reasonableness for Within-Range Sentences
Perhaps the more significant implication of Cutbank for lower courts is its rigid application of the substantive reasonableness standard. Cutbank’s argument regarding the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement was essentially that the sentence was unnecessarily harsh given the ACCA penalties were already on the books. However, the Eighth Circuit reiterated a recent line of authority that stiffens the requirements for a variance. The Court, citing *Goodhouse* (2023) and *Masood* (2025), held that because the district court imposed a sentence within the applicable Guidelines range, the sentence is presumed to be reasonable.

This presumption is critical for defending against claims that "enhancements" inherently violate the § 3553(a) proportionality factors. The Eighth Circuit reasoned that once a court correctly calculates the Guidelines range—accounting for greater levels and prior felonies—the appellate court presumes the judge considered and weighed the § 3553(a) factors, even if the judge did not mechanically recite them on the record. This approach creates a significant barrier for post-conviction claims that rely on the assertion that specific enhancements like § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) are inherently punitive rather than rehabilitative in a way that violates statutory constraints. The decision implies that as long as the court starts with the correct base and enhancement level, the likelihood of a reversal for a substantive reasonableness error diminishes rapidly.

## Downstream Effects on Sentencing Strategy
For defense practitioners, Cutbank signals that procedural precision is likely the only avenue for relief in ACCA-plus-felony-possession cases. The Eighth Circuit’s decision to affirm based on a within-range sentence, even amidst extensive prior criminal history, suggests that challenging the substantive weight of the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement at the appellate level is a high-risk strategy. Future defendants would be better served focusing their appeals on whether the enhancement was triggered at all (i.e., whether the underlying conduct actually constituted a "felony offense under state or local law") or whether the § 3553(a) factors warrant a downward variance outside the range, rather than challenging the ability to stack the ACCA and the enhancement vertically.

Moreover, the opinion clarifies that the "greater offense level" rule is not just a calculation aid but a mandate against undercounting conduct. By adhering to this strict procedural hierarchy, the Eighth Circuit ensures that the sentencing enhances for specific dangerous conduct are not rendered moot by the general recidivism penalty. This creates a more predictable sentencing landscape but perhaps leaves little room for judicial creativity to tailor sentences downward based on individual circumstances once the guidelines have been correctly calculated upward by statute. As sentencing reform debates continue, cases like Cutbank will likely serve as the de facto precedent for how the federal judiciary reconciles multiple layers of mandatory and advisory enhancements without sacrificing the structural integrity of the Sentencing Guidelines.



# The Duty of Briefing: Limited Discretion When Defense Counsel Offers No Authority

## The Procedural Floor for Appellate Arguments

The conviction and sentencing of Justin Cutbank, a felon in possession of a firearm who orchestrated domestic violence and fled police in a sawed-off gun, were separable enough details to warrant standard scrutiny of the merits. Yet, the Eighth Circuit’s opinion in *United States v. Cutbank* offers a more instructive lesson than the specifics of his 292-month sentence. For practitioners appearing before federal appellate courts, the opinion serves as a stark reminder that appellate advocacy carries an implicit duty to provide legal support for every assertion. When defense counsel files an appeal "providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his arguments," the court does not merely decline to reverse based on those arguments; it affirms the proceedings of record, effectively treating the lack of legal grounding as a forfeiture of the issue itself.

In *Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit’s primary friction point was not the evidentiary rulings themselves, but the defense’s failure to articulate the legal theory behind them during the appellate process. The opinion notes at the outset of its analysis of evidentiary issues that Cutbank raised a series of contentions while providing no cases and almost no analysis to support his argument. The court then proceeds to dismiss each point—it is not that the rulings were erroneous, but that the arguments lacked the legal infrastructure necessary for appellate review to function. This is a case about the boundary of judicial discretion: a judge’s lenient reception of error in the lower court ends where the lack of persuasive brief begins in the upper court.

## The Mechanics of Dismissal

The opinion illustrates how quickly a court can disengage when faced with "no-case" arguments, even in a case where the underlying facts were contested. Cutbank challenged the district court’s refusal to allow testimony regarding his prior possession of the rifle, arguing it violated Rule 404(b) as propensity evidence. Rather than engaging in a deep dive into Rule 404(b) analysis, the panel simply noted it had affirmed the district court. The court cited *United States v. Battle*, stating prior possession helps establish ownership or control. When application of this rule was made clear in the briefings, the merits of the appeal were moot.

The court applied this same methodology to claims regarding hearsay instructions and witness testimony. Cutbank argued he could not consider statements from D.F. substantively until she testified. The court acknowledged this raised Confrontation Clause concerns but dismissed the issue based on the record showing D.F. did testify and the district court provided appropriate instructions. This approach reflects a pragmatic reality of federal appellate jurisprudence. The Court has not reached an error rate decision unless there is substantial showing of failure to follow the law at trial. If the briefing does not identify the specific error or provide legal support to prove the jury might be misled, the review defaults to finding no abuse of discretion.

The 8th Circuit even invoked authority to police the issues scope when Cutbank raised potential claims during cross-examination of government witness C.F. regarding police reports. Although the issue was not raised as a formal claim by the defense brief, the government raised it to expand the scope. The court rejected this expansion, stating "We do not appreciate appellees who unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal." This line of reasoning implies that even if an appellate court hears a claim, the duty of briefing dictates the frame of the discussion. Without proper citation or objection, the appellate record does not support the issue, resulting in a judgment that the record's evidence meets the requirements, regardless of the defense's internal confidence in the error.

## Sentencing Reasonableness and the Guidelines Range

Despite the procedural focus, the court did address the substantive sentencing appeal, particularly regarding U.S.S.G. § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), the enhancement for possession in connection with another felony. Cutbank argued that applying this enhancement while the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) penalty was the governing factor created a conflict with 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)'s requirement that a sentence be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary." The district court imposed a sentence within its advisory guidelines range, calculated based on both ACCA and Guidelines provisions. The Appeals Court explained that nothing in the record suggested the sentence was outside of the advisory range and the court considered § 3553(a) factors.

The opinion highlights the presumption of reasonableness when a sentence is within the advisory guidelines and the trial court has considered the issues post-sentence. Under *Gall v. United States* and later cases in the 8th Circuit like *Masood*, the standard of review for substantive reasonableness is de novo, but the burden remains on the appellee to overturn a presumption of reasonableness in the lower court's discretion. Even if the appeal paired with the briefs had been its strongest rebuttal, the appellate court assumes that the trial court made a careful and considered decision absent a showing of procedural error.

## Downstream Effects on Defense Strategy

The practical implication of *Cutbank* for defense counsel and public defenders is a call for more rigorous briefing in the post-trial phase. The court's use of stereotyping language—"providing no cases," "none of the contentions has merit"—radiates frustration with the service rendered to appellate advocacy. If a defense brief fails to accompany assertions with citations, the appellate court treats any potential prejudice as unlikely because the record cannot support the burden of proof for reversal of error. The harmless error standard, which governs most criminal convictions, only functions properly if the appellate court has a legal basis to determine whether an error was harmless.

In the interest of procedural efficiency, the Eighth Circuit has drawn a clear line between arguments that are worth hearing and those that are mere complaints. A defense counsel who assumes an adverse trial outcome requires appeal does not assume an appellate court will simply hear their grievances. If they do not provide the findings to support the appeal, they are asking for leniency without a basis for relief. When the speech is unmoored from the law, the hearing is too short or nonexistent.

## Conclusion

The *Cutbank* opinion is not about the nature of domestic violence or the possession of firearms. It is about the expectations of appellate litigation. In a jurisdiction with limited judicial resources, the duty of briefing protects the court from having to act on arguments that cannot sustain themselves on their own. While *Cutbank* offers no new precedent on Rule 404(b) or sentencing enhancements, it reaffirms that appellate advocacy must be disciplined. A defense team can argue the bare merits of a case in the briefing, but they cannot afford to omit any legal authority. The opinion stands as a warning that "lack of analysis" is not just a procedural step, but the primary factor in the dismissal of appeals where the law is not proffered with the proper force.
