Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

### When does prior possession of a firearm qualify as 'intrinsic' evidence rather than propensity evidence under Rule 404(b)?
Question:
I'm reading the 8th Circuit opinion here regarding *United States v. Cutbank*. The district court allowed testimony that the victim had seen the defendant with the sawed-off rifle prior to the incident. The opinion distinguishes this as 'intrinsic evidence' relevant to 'establish ownership' rather than improper propensity character evidence. Could someone explain what makes something 'intrinsic' in a felon-in-possession case? How do we determine the line between proving ownership versus just suggesting a bad character?
Answer:
In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit addresses the tension between Rule 404(b) “other acts” evidence and the concept of “intrinsic” evidence within the context of a felon-in-possession charge. The core distinction lies in whether the evidence is necessary to prove a specific element of the crime (ownership or control) or if it is merely offered to show the defendant’s general criminal character.

Under the standard articulated in *Cutbank*, prior possession of a firearm qualifies as intrinsic evidence when it is "directly relevant to proving later possession of that same weapon because it helps establish ownership or control of the weapon." The opinion relies on *United States v. Battle* to support this proposition, noting that testimony from the victim that she had seen the defendant with the sawed-off rifle "a handful of times" before the charged incident was admissible.

The line between intrinsic and propensity evidence is drawn as follows:
*   **Propensity Evidence:** Prior acts alone are inadmissible to suggest the defendant has a "bad character" or is likely to have acted in conformity therewith.
*   **Intrinsic Evidence:** Prior acts are admissible when they form part of the same transaction or are essential to prove an element of the crime. In *Cutbank*, proving that the defendant had the weapon previously established his initial control over the specific instrumentality, preventing a factual dispute over whether he possessed it at the time of the incident.

Therefore, the distinction turns on whether the evidence proves **continuity of control** (ownership/control) versus **character** (habit/propensity). If the testimony helps establish *who* has the gun and *ownership*, it is intrinsic. If it is admitted solely to demonstrate the defendant is aggressive or dangerous, it violates Rule 404(b) unless it passes the intrinsic test.

### Can a Confrontation Clause challenge be cured by a jury instruction given at the close of trial?
Question:
On page 6 of the opinion, the court suggests that a district court 'need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted' if they provide an instruction 'at the close of trial.' I thought the Confrontation Clause requires the defendant to have the opportunity to cross-examine the declarant *at the time of the statement* or right away. The opinion cites *United States v. Charboneau* saying the declarant must actually appear in court, but doesn't explain delaying the instruction. Is there a specific nuance I'm missing about when the Confrontation Clause violation occurs?
Answer:
***Direct Answer: No, a jury instruction at the close of trial does not cure a Confrontation Clause violation.*

You are correctly identifying the distinction between evidentiary fairness and constitutional requirements. The court’s language regarding the timing of jury instructions applies to the district court's **discretion** over jury instructions, whereas the Confrontation Clause analysis turns on the **appearance** of the declarant.

**1. Jury Instructions are Discretionary (The Instruction Rule)**
The sentence you cite—"need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time the evidence is admitted if the court provides an appropriate instruction at the close of trial"—is an evidentiary standard regarding **limiting instructions** (see *United States v. Bennett*, 765 F.3d 887, 899 (8th Cir. 2014)). This rule allows courts to defer giving jury directions on how to treat certain evidence until the trial concludes, provided the jury is properly instructed on the record. This relates to whether the court abused its discretion in jury management, not whether the defendant was deprived of a constitutional right.

**2. The Confrontation Clause Requires Appearance (The Constitutional Rule)**
The court explicitly distinguishes this discretionary instruction rule from the Confrontation Clause in section II(B). The opinion states that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied only "when the hearsay declarants . . . actually appear in court and testify in person" (citing *United States v. Charboneau*, 613 F.3d 860, 861 (8th Cir. 2010)). 

This means the right to confront a witness is secured by the witness's **actual physical presence** for cross-examination, not by a jury instruction clarifying how to use out-of-court statements.

**3. Why the Opinion Notes Both**
In the *Cutbank* opinion, the court cites the instruction rule (*Bennett*) to determine that the district court did not abuse its discretion regarding the *timing* of the jury instruction. However, it simultaneously notes that there was no Confrontation Clause deficiency because "D.F. testified." 

Because the declarant (D.F.) actually appeared and testified, the Confrontation Clause was satisfied independently of any instruction. If the declarant had not appeared, a close-of-trial instruction would not have cured the constitutional violation; the objection would have hinged on the lack of voir dire opportunity (*Charboneau*), which an instruction cannot fix.

**Summary**
The instruction timing is a rule of evidence procedure regarding the management of the jury; the Confrontation Clause is a constitutional hurdle requiring the declarant to be available for cross-examination. The *Cutbank* opinion confirms that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied by testimony itself, while the instruction timing is an ancillary matter for appellate review of discretion.

### What is the legal nexus required for a USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement when charged with ACCA felonies?
Question:
The defendant was found guilty of being a felon in possession AND had three prior violent felonies (triggering ACCA). The PSR applied a 4-level enhancement for 'possession in connection with another felony.' The opinion says the Guidelines would have produced a higher level (35) than just the ACCA level (34), but the 4B1.4(b) rule says the greater level applies. I'm confused how the enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) interacts mathematically with the ACCA sentencing enhancements found by the jury. Does the 'another felony' in § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) mean the felony used as perjury on another charge, or the possession itself?
Answer:
The *Cutbank* opinion clarifies that the "another felony" required for a USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement is a **separate offense distinct from the current possession charge**, and the mathematical interaction between ACCA guidelines and this enhancement is governed by the "greater level" rule.

Here is the breakdown of the nexus and the calculation based on the opinion:

**1. The Legal Nexus**
The user asks whether the "another felony" means the felony used as perjury or the possession itself. **The opinion does not mention perjury, and the "another felony" is not the possession charge itself.**

In *Cutbank*, the nexus is established when the firearm is used or possessed "in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense." As the opinion states:
> "The enhancement applies if a defendant used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense 'regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.'" (Part III)

The district court adopted the PSR’s factual statements regarding Cutbank’s **prior felony convictions** to satisfy this requirement. The court reaffirmed that the facts established the gun was used "in connection with another felony," requiring no separate conviction for that other felony on this specific charge. Thus, the "other felony" is the distinct offense connected to the possession, often satisfied by the underlying criminal activity that predicates the defendant's status (in this context, the violent felonies).

**2. Mathematical Interaction and USSG § 4B1.4(b)**
You correctly identified that USSG § 4B1.4(b) dictates which guideline level controls. The confusion often lies in whether ACCA enhances the sentence *or* if a specific guideline enhancement applies *over* the ACCA calculation.

The *Cutbank* opinion explicitly addresses the off-chain calculation:
> "Though the jury found that Cutback had three or more prior violent felony convictions... the Armed Career Criminal Guideline would have produced a total offense level of 34. The Guidelines provide that the greater offense level applies. See USSG § 4B1.4(b)."

The district court calculated the offense level using § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) as a **four-level increase**. This brought the total offense level to **35**. Without this specific enhancement, the ACCA-guideline calculation would have only resulted in an offense level of **34**.

Because the § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) calculation resulted in a higher total (35) than the ACCA-specific guideline calculation (34), the court applied § 4B1.4(b) to mandate that the **greater level applies**. The enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) is not mutually exclusive with the ACCA statutory penalty; rather, it modifies the *advisory* guidelines range. However, if the § 2K2.1 enhancement results in a lower range within the ACCA guidelines, the ACCA guidelines generally control. Conversely, if the specific enhancement pushes the level higher (as it did here, 35 vs 34), the higher level governs the sentence calculation.

**Conclusion**
In *Cutbank*, the sentence of 292 months was within the calculated advisory range of 292–365 months. The enhancement under § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) was valid because the PSR established a nexus between the possession and another felony, and the resulting offense level (35) superseded the ACCA guideline level (34) under § 4B1.4(b).

### Why does the court presume the district court considered § 3553(a) factors even if they aren't explicitly recited in the record?
Question:
Under the 8th Circuit's reasoning, the court states 'we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected' the arguments because the defendant argued them at sentencing. However, *Gall* says 'Before a court of appeals can consider the substantive reasonableness of a sentence, it must first ensure that the district court committed no significant procedural error.' If the judge just didn't write the words 'I considered this', is that enough to say the appellate court won't check for 'procedural error'? What is the standard if the judge mentions it but doesn't explain *why* the specific factors weight the sentence up, but not by a 'greater than necessary' amount?
Answer:
In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit clarifies that while the District Court must follow *Gall* to ensure no significant procedural error occurs before substantive review, this does not mandate a comprehensive transcript of every factor's weight.

**The Legal Standard: Procedure vs. Mechanics**
Under *Gall v. United States*, 552 U.S. 38 (2007), a court of appeals must first ensure the District Court committed no procedural error—such as failing to calculate the Guidelines range or failing to consider *§ 3553(a)* factors—before considering whether the sentence is substantively reasonable. In *Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit explains that satisfying this procedural requirement does not require a "mechanical recitation" of the factors weighed for the specific sentencing. Citing *United States v. Maluoth*, 121 F.4th 1158, 1164 (8th Cir. 2024), *Cutbank* states:

> "To uphold the sentence, we do not require a district court to provide a mechanical recitation of the factors it weighed; it simply must be clear from the record that the district court *actually considered* the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence."

**Presumption of Consideration**
Because the appellate court relies on "what is clear from the record," it looks for evidence that the District Court was aware of the defendant's arguments and the relevant *§ 3553(a)* factors. In *Cutbank*, the record showed the District Court explicitly noted its review of the PSR and sentencing memoranda and that it "reviewed § 3553(a) factors that could warrant a variance." Because the District Court demonstrated it was aware of the specific arguments (which the opinion notes it was "aware of"), the Eighth Circuit applied a presumption regarding the consideration of those factors.

Citing *United States v. Masood*, 133 F.4th 799, 809 (8th Cir. 2025), the opinion explains:

> "Thus, the district court was aware of [Cutbank's] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them."

**Application to Your Question**
Your hypothesis is exactly why *Cutbank* matters. If the judge fails to mention any review of these factors—essentially providing no record to affirm the consideration—*Gall* dictates a procedural error finding is required. However, once the record shows the court acknowledged the arguments and the relevant statutory factors (as it did in *Cutbank* by noting it reviewed the PSR and factors that could warrant a variance), the District Court is presumed to have properly considered them.

Therefore, a judge not writing the words "I considered this" is not enough to trigger a reversal; conversely, a judge need not explain *why* the sentencing weight was determined as it was. The standard is met if the record suggests actual consideration, not mechanical recitation.

### What is the actual standard for finding 'substantial prejudice' regarding cumulative evidentiary error?
Question:
The court dismisses the appeal on the ground that any evidentiary errors were harmless. It quotes *DeMarce* and says we reverse only if the jury was 'substantially swayed.' But the opinion mentions 'cumulative effect of trial errors.' Is there a specific set of questions the appellate court asks before deciding the 'cumulative effect' was so minor that prejudice was absent? If I have dozens of technical R. Evid. errors and the evidence against the defendant is strong, does the court simply stack them and say 'it doesn't matter,' or does each error need to matter individually to proceed?
Answer:
The opinion in *United States v. Justin Cutbank* does not provide a specific, enumerated checklist of questions for determining "cumulative effect" errors. Instead, the court relies on the established "substantial prejudice" standard cited from *United States v. DeMarce* and *United States v. Keys* to resolve whether the errors should lead to reversal.

Regarding your question about "stacking" errors versus requiring individual weight: The *Cutbank* opinion supports the approach that an appellate court assesses the cumulative impact of errors to determine if they resulted in "substantial prejudice" (*Keys*). It does not appear to require that each isolated error be individually prejudicial to the point of reversal, but rather that their combined influence must not have "substantially swayed" the jury. The court dismissed the appeal because it concluded that the district court’s evidentiary rulings, even when viewed cumulatively, did not influence the verdict. Specifically, the *Cutbank* opinion states that because the government’s evidence was "substantial," the rulings "did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict."

In practice, this means the appellate court focuses on the totality of the evidence. As long as the conviction stands or the government's evidence regarding the facts (like possession of the firearm) is strong, the cumulative errors are deemed harmless and do not meet the threshold for "substantial prejudice" (*DeMarce*). You should expect that if the proof of guilt remains overwhelming despite multiple technical errors, the appeal will fail regardless of whether the errors technically happened, so long as the jury was not "substantially swayed" (*DeMarce*).

### Why does the police report require authentication before a witness can be cross-examined on its contents?
Question:
The defendant tried to impeach a witness (C.F.) about statements she made to a police investigator. The court allowed questioning about what she said but sustained an objection when counsel tried to read the report into the record because it 'was not authored by [the witness].' The opinion says extrinsic evidence 'must still be authenticated.' I understand hearsay rules, but I thought if we are just using a report to make a witness lie, we don't need to authenticate the *report* itself, just establish the witness's perception. Why is the authentication requirement violated if the officer didn't testify or sign off on the report?
Answer:
In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit clarifies that while a witness may be questioned on out-of-court statements intrinsically (via testimony on the stand), introducing the actual written police report into the record requires authentication because the report itself constitutes "extrinsic evidence."

### Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Impeachment

To understand the requirement, it is necessary to distinguish between how a witness is questioned versus how the document is introduced. 

1.  **Intrinsic (Direct Cross-Examination):** A lawyer may ask a witness if they previously made a statement to the police. This does not require authentication of a document because the witness testifies to their own recollection of what was said. The court permitted questioning regarding what C.F. said to police because it relates directly to her testimony, establishing her "perception" or knowledge.
2.  **Extrinsic (Introducing the Document):** If defense counsel attempts to read *portions of the police report* into the record, they are introducing a document into evidence. This is considered extrinsic evidence. According to *Cutbank*, even for impeachment purposes: "Although extrinsic evidence may be used for impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence must still be authenticated."

### The Legal Standard

The Eighth Circuit relied on *United States v. Cumbie*, 28 F.4th 907, 914 (8th Cir. 2022), which mandates authentication for extrinsic evidence. The report is a separate piece of evidence from the witness's recollection. To ensure the jury isn't misled by a document that may be irrelevant, obstructed, or fabricated without verification, the document must be authenticated to show what it is and that it is what was exhibits it claims to be.

In *Cutbank*, the objection was sustained specifically because the report "was not authored by C.F." This indicates the government could not establish the necessary foundation that the document accurately reflected the witness's content or process without a higher standard of reliance than the witness’s own testimony. Because the report was not authored by the witness but was instead an external record, the government could not simply assume the document’s content without verifying the document's integrity and relevance through authentication.

### Conclusion

Authentication is required not to protect the government from the witness's lie, but to protect the fact-finder from the unverified document. As the court in *Cutbank* implied, while you can't block a witness from talking about their statements, you cannot offer the physical report as evidence without ensuring the report actually belongs to the case and accurately records the events.
