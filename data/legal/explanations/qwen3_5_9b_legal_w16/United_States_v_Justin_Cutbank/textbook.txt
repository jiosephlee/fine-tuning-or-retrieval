Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

# Chapter 1: Procedural Posture and Factual Background

## The Factual Basis of the Firearms Possession

Understanding the factual background is the first step in reading any appellate opinion, as these facts serve as the foundation upon which the legal issues turn. In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals begins by summarizing the events underlying the conviction in Section I, titled "Background." According to this section, the core incident occurred during the night of November 19-20, 2020. The defendant, Justin Cutbank, was involved in a domestic dispute involving a girlfriend, identified in the opinion as D.F. During this encounter, which involved alcohol and methamphetamine use, Cutbank smuggled a sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle and held D.F. at gunpoint. D.F. testified that Cutbank forced her into a closet for hours, pointed the weapon at her face, and threatened to strike her. Eventually, Cutbank fled with the gun and D.F.'s cell phone.

For a law student, the duty of an appellate court here is to respect the trial court's findings of historical fact unless clear error is shown. The appellate court relies heavily on D.F.'s testimony and corroborating physical evidence, including DNA on the firearm matching Cutbank. The physical record supports the narrative that Cutbank fled to a neighboring house and attempted to resist arrest in a garage, eventually leading to the seizure of the weapon found on the path of travel. The prosecution secured a conviction based on the defendant's control over the firearm *after* the incident, but the facts establish the timeline of possession immediately preceding the incident. This chronology is critical because the defendent’s conviction rests on the theory that he possessed the firearm at the time of the gunpoint incident, a fact disputed during the trial regarding continuous possession of the weapon.

## Statutory Charges and the Armed Career Criminal Act

The legal framework surrounding *Cutbank* is defined by specific federal statutes, which inform both the conviction and the sentencing calculation. The opinion introduces the relevant laws in the introduction and discusses them in the fact sections. Specifically, Cutbank was convicted of violating 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) by being a felon in unlawful possession of a firearm. This statute makes it a crime for any person who "has been convicted of a crime punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year" to own or carry a firearm. Because Cutbank was prosecuted for a "felony" prior to this incident, the charges were anchored in his criminal history rather than the specifics of the gunpoint incident.

Furthermore, the sentencing ranges were driven by the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), codified at 18 U.S.C. § 924(e). The opinion notes that the jury found Cutbank had previously been convicted of at least three violent felonies. This finding triggered the ACCA’s enhanced penalty provisions, allowing for a mandatory minimum sentence that fell within the Federal Sentencing Guidelines. In the context of *Cutbank*, the gun itself had an obliterated serial number, and the possession occurred while he was recklessly creating a risk of bodily injury while fleeing police. These specific statutory factors meant that the base sentencing guidelines started high, resulting in an advisory sentencing range of 292 to 365 months. Understanding these statutes is essential because the appellate court reviews both the legal application of the felon-in-possession statute and the calculation of the ACCA enhancement to ensure the district court applied the law correctly.

## Procedural History: Conviction, Sentencing, and Record

Moving from the facts to the legal procedure requires understanding the distinction between the District Court and the Court of Appeals. The procedural history in *Cutbank* outlines that the District Court heard the crime, convicted the defendant, and sentenced him to 292 months of imprisonment. The opinion states this sentence was within the "advisory guidelines range as determined by the district court." Following conviction, Cutbank raised multiple issues regarding his conviction and the sentence, leading to an appeal to the Eighth Circuit.

In reading the opinion, students must recognize that the "Procedural History" section serves several functions. First, it establishes that the District Court was the "trial court," which meant it acted as the arbiter of evidence and fact at trial. The District Court handled motions to suppress or limit evidence, and rulings on those motions are appealed under an "abuse of discretion" standard. Second, the opinion details that the district court adopted the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) without objection. This is significant because it establishes a record for the appellate court to review. When the district court adopts a PSR's factual statements undisputedly, the appellate court can rely on those facts for sentencing review unless there was a clear error. This procedural posture clarifies that the appellate court is not retrying the facts but reviewing the trial court's application of the law to the established record.

## Scope of Appeal: Evidentiary Challenges and Sentencing Enhancements

The final element of the procedural landscape is the specific scope of the appeal. While the opinion mentions Cutbank argued there were multiple "evidentiary and procedural errors," the appellate court identifies these as specific challenges to the trial process. The introduction of the case clarifies that Cutbank’s primary claims focused on the admission of prior instances where he possessed the firearm and the acknowledgment of hearsay.

The appellate court in *Cutbank* limits its review to the arguments raised in the lower court's briefs and the record. Specifically, Cutbank argued the district court should not have admitted evidence of D.F. and C.F. previously identifying him with the gun, claiming it violated Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b) propensity rules. He also argued against the sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), claiming the court improperly applied the four-level increase for "possession in connection with another felony offense." The Eighth Circuit structured its opinion to address these specific challenges one by one. In Section II, the court reviews each evidentiary ruling (rulings A through E), determining if the record shows the district court abused its discretion. In Section III, the court isolates the sentencing enhancement argument. A student reading the opinion must recognize that the appellate court will not consider issues not raised in the Brief on Appeal if the defense failed to properly object at trial. The scope of the appeal is therefore defined by the specific legal errors Cutbank preserved during his trial and preserved on appeal.

# Chapter 2: Evidentiary Rulings, Admissibility, and Harmless Error

## Rule 404(b) Analysis: Prior Possession as Intrinsic Evidence vs. Propensity Evidence

The district court faced a challenge regarding the admissibility of evidence showing that the victim and a witness had previously observed the defendant’s possession of the firearm. Justin Cutbank filed a motion prior to trial to prevent the neighbors from testifying that they had seen him with the sawed-off rifle, arguing this would constitute propensity evidence violating Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The appellate court explains that this argument rests on a distinction between credibility-altering tendencies versus evidence of ownership or control. While Rule 404(b) generally prohibits evidence of other crimes or bad acts to show a defendant’s character, the court clarifies that prior possession of a firearm is considered "intrinsic evidence." This type of evidence is directly relevant to the charged offense because it establishes ownership or control of the weapon at the time of the alleged crime.

In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s ruling by relying on the precedent set in *United States v. Battle*. The court reasoned that prior possession helps establish continuous possession of the firearm before the specific incident at issue. Therefore, testimony from victims that they saw Cutbank with the gun in the past was not improperly excluded propensity evidence. This distinction is vital for law students to understand when evaluating evidentiary rulings: evidence is not admissible to suggest a defendant has a criminal disposition, but it is admissible if it proves a specific element of the crime or an essential fact regarding the weapon's ownership or the context of the offense.

## Confrontation Clause and Hearsay: Instructing the Jury on Victim Testimony Against Officers

Cutbank raised a specific procedural issue concerning how the jury was instructed regarding the statements made by the victim, D.F. He argued that the district court should have instructured the jury that it could not consider D.F.’s statements to police as substantive evidence until she herself testified in court. This request was designed to avoid hearsay violations and potential Confrontation Clause issues. The government countered that the statements would provide context for police actions and that the officer’s testimony would not violate hearsay rules. Furthermore, a limiting instruction at the close of trial, if necessary, would be sufficient.

The appellate court reviewed the district court’s decision to refrain from giving a limiting instruction during the trial by the standard of abuse of discretion. The court noted that it need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time it is admitted if an appropriate instruction is provided at the close of trial. Crucially, the Confrontation Clause was not violated in this specific instance because the victim appeared in court and testified in person. Citing *United States v. Charboneau*, the court clarified that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when hearsay declarants actually appear in court to testify. Because D.F. testified at the trial, the court concluded there was no abuse of discretion in denying the specific instruction Cutbank requested during the trial.

## Impeachment and Authentication: Admissibility of Prior Consistent/Inconsistent Statements regarding the Gun

The record shows two distinct issues regarding witness testimony and authentication. First, during cross-examination, D.F. mentioned that a relative had been killed by a gun to explain her unfamiliarity with firearms or inconsistent prior statements. The court denied the motion to strike this testimony. On appeal, Cutbank offered no authority contending that this memory of an unrelated death caused unfair prejudice or confusion. The court applied Federal Rule of Evidence 403, which balances probative value against danger of unfair prejudice, and found the district court acted within its broad discretion.

Second, Cutbank sought to undermine the government’s witness, C.F., regarding her prior use of the gun. Defense counsel attempted to cross-examine C.F. about prior statements made to a police investigator and sought to read portions of the resulting police report into evidence. The district court sustained the government’s objections because the report was not authored by C.F. On appeal, the court referenced *United States v. Cumbie*, noting that while extrinsic evidence may be used to impeach a witness for a prior inconsistent statement, that extrinsic evidence must be authenticated. The appellate court found the district court did not abuse its discretion by requiring proper authentication of the report before it could be read to the jury.

## Harmless Error Standard: Reviewing Whether Evidence Swayed the Verdict or Had Cumulative Effect

Even if the appellate court identified an error in a trial ruling, it will not necessarily reverse the conviction. The Eighth Circuit applies a harmless error standard in these contexts. The court explained that it will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the evidence that was improperly admitted. This is often phrased as asking whether the error was harmless. In *United States v. Cutbank*, the court noted that Cutbank could not clear this hurdle because the government’s evidence was substantial, including both testimonial and physical evidence.

The appellate court also addressed the cumulative effect of trial errors. It quoted *United States v. Keys*, stating that errors will not overturn a conviction unless there is substantial prejudice to the defendant. Because the government presented significant physical evidence of the gun and matching DNA, along with other testimony, the court found that the cumulative effect of the cited evidentiary rulings did not influence the verdict. This standard teaches law students that isolated evidentiary errors do not automatically result in a new trial when the overall record demonstrates strong proof of the underlying facts.

## Treatment of Unsubstantiated Appellant Arguments

Finally, the appellate court emphasizes the burden placed on the appellant to support their arguments. In this chapter of the opinion, the court notes that Cutbank raised a series of evidentiary issues without providing specific cases or analysis to support his claims. In legal appellate practice, an appellant is generally expected to cite authority or provide substantive reasoning to support a claim; if none is provided, the court may determine the contentions lack merit.

The court further noted that Cutback attempted to raise claims regarding cross-examination efforts on the police report that were not clearly preserved. The court expressed frustration with appellees who "unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal," noting that the circuit court generally does not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless. Because the cumulative evidence against the defendant was overwhelming, the court held that it did not need to reach the merits of unpreserved issues or claims lacking citation to relevant authority. This demonstrates the importance of proper preservation of error during trial and the necessity of articulating clear legal arguments on appeal.

# Chapter 3: Sentencing Guidelines: Calculation and Specific Enhancements

## Determining the Base Offense Level: Sawed-off Firearm and Creating Risk of Death

When reviewing the applicable sentencing guidelines, the Sentencing Commission follows a specific mathematical process to determine the appropriate range of imprisonment. In *Cutbank*, the appellate court explained that the starting point is the base offense level, which is then adjusted by specific enhancements found in the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR). In this case, the PSR recommended a base offense level of 26 because the offense involved the possession of a sawed-off firearm, as defined by 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a). From this base, the calculation was increased by adding levels for specific conduct. The district court applied a three-level increase because the firearm had an altered or obliterated serial number. Additionally, the court applied a two-level increase because Cutbank recklessly created a risk of death or serious bodily injury while fleeing from police. Finally, a four-level increase was applied for possessing the firearm in connection with another felony offense. This resulted in a total offense level of 35. Students must note that the district court is required to begin all sentencing proceedings by correctly calculating the applicable Guidelines range, and failing to do so is considered a significant procedural error that courts of appeals review strictly.

## USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B): Application of the 'Connection to Another Felony' Enhancement

A critical component of the sentencing calculation in *Cutbank* is the application of the United States Sentencing Guidelines Amendment § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B). This provision applies four offense levels to a defendant's sentence if they used or possessed any firearm in connection with another federal, state, or local felony offense. The Guidelines Commentary, specifically note (n.14(C)), clarifies that this enhancement applies regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought for the connection offense or a conviction was obtained. In the district court, this enhancement was adopted without objection from Cutbank. Because the court adopted the PSR's factual statements regarding Cutbank's prior felony convictions, the appellate court determined there was no error in finding that the facts established that the gun was used in connection with another felony. On appeal, the standard of review for these discretionary findings is lenient; the court assumes the district court "[a]ctually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence" unless the record indicates otherwise, and the Guidelines range is presumed reasonable if the sentence falls within it.

## ACCA Eligibility: Finding Three Prior Violent Felony Convictions

The *Cutbank* opinion illustrates how the presence of prior violent felonies can function differently under the statutory enhancement versus the Guidelines suggestion. The jury found that Cutbank had at least three prior violent felony convictions. This factual finding triggered the Armed Career Criminal Act's enhanced penalty provisions under 18 U.S.C. § 924(e), which imposes a mandatory minimum sentence. While this statutory enhancement is separate from the calculation of the Guidelines base offense level, the record notes that an application of the "Armed Career Criminal Guideline" would have produced a total offense level of 34. This indicates that the Guidelines provide a specific method for calculating offense levels based on the accumulation of violent felony priors. Understanding this distinction is vital: while the ACCA penalty is a statutory mandate based on prior convictions, the Guidelines calculation serves to determine the specific sentencing range. In this case, the district court's sentencing determination involved this calculation alongside specific offense enhancements, demonstrating how multiple layers of prior conviction data feed into the final advisory range.

## Conflict Resolution: When the Guidelines Exceed Other Possible Calculations

Prudential application of the Guidelines requires resolving conflicts when multiple calculation paths result in different totals. The *Cutbank* court clarified that when the Guidelines indicate that one calculation method proceeds differently than another—such as the specific offense characteristics versus the Armed Career Criminal Account—specific provisions govern which number controls. The court cited USSG § 4B1.4(b), noting that the Guidelines provide that the greater offense level applies. In *Cutbank*, the PSR calculated a total offense level of 35, whereas the ACCA-specific guideline calculation would have resulted in a level of 34. Because the PSR calculation relied on the "greater offense level" rule, the 35 count governed the final calculation. This approach ensures that procedural deference to the sentencing court is maintained only after the court has correctly calculated the applicable range. Finally, because the district court imposed a sentence within the applicable advisory Guidelines range, the appellate court applied a presumption of reasonableness, focusing on whether the burden of the § 3553(a) factors warranted a departure from the calculated range.

# Chapter 4: Substantive Reasonableness and Sentencing Discretion

## The Four-Step Review Process: Calculated Range to Reasonableness

The appellate review of a criminal sentence follows a distinct sequence designed to ensure that both procedural compliance and substantive fairness have been met before a sentence is deemed valid. The court begins by assessing whether the district court correctly calculated the applicable Guidelines range. In *Cutbank*, the jury found the defendant guilty of felon in possession, and the law applied a specific procedure to determine the sentencing trajectory. The district court adopted the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) recommendations, which included a base offense level of 26 adjusted for various enhancements, such as the nature of the weapon and prior convictions. This calculation established the advisory range. The Supreme Court established in *Gall v. United States* that a district court should begin all sentencing proceedings by correctly calculating the applicable Guidelines range. If the district court fails to calculate the range or does so incorrectly, the appellate court may identify significant procedural error before it can evaluate the reasonableness of the sentence itself. As noted in *Rosales-Mireles*, the court of appeals cannot consider the substantive reasonableness of a sentence until it ensures the Guidelines range was calculated properly. This structural requirement ensures that the sentencing baseline is objective before policy factors under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) are considered.

## Presumption of Reasonableness for Within-Guidelines Sentencing

Once the court calculates the correct range, the next step is to determine whether the sentence imposed was reasonable within that framework. The *Cutbank* opinion illustrates the deference appellate courts show to district courts when a sentence falls within the calculated Guidelines range. Because the district court in *Cutbank* imposed a sentence of 292 months, which fell within the advisory calculation of 292 to 365 months, the appellate court began with a presumption of reasonableness. This means the sentence is presumed correct unless the defendant can prove otherwise. In determining whether to reverse an erroneous evidentiary or sentencing ruling, the appellate court applies an abuse of discretion standard. When the sentence is within the range, the burden shifts to the appellant to show that the sentence was substantively unreasonable. Conversely, if a court imposes an outside-of-range sentence, it must explicitly address the justifications for the variance. In *Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit specifically noted that it will not reverse a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is substantial prejudice, and a within-range sentence is significant to this analysis. The district court is presumed to have considered the § 3553(a) factors, and appellate review requires a finding of significant procedural error to disturb the sentence.

## Analysis of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) Factors (Purposes of Sentencing)

When reviewing a sentence for substantive reasonableness, the focus turns to the statutory purposes of punishment outlined in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). A defendant in *Cutbank* argued that the district court's use of the USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement resulted in a sentence that conflicted with the principle that a sentence must be "sufficient, but not greater than necessary." The appellate court acknowledges this principle but emphasizes that the district court is not limited to a mechanical recitation of these factors. Instead, the court looks for a record demonstrating that the district court actually considered the factors in determining the sentence. The district court in *Cutbank* reviewed the PSR, sentencing memoranda, and the § 3553(a) factors. The argument against § 3553(a) compliance relied on the calculation of the enhancement rather than the actual conduct required by the statute. However, the Court noted that the district court imposed a sentence within the advisory range, implying that the statutory purposes were served by that punishment. By comparing the Criminal History Category VI and the severity of the offense—domestic abuse, kidnapping, and police confrontation—the appellate court found the sentence aligned with the goals of the statute. The mere fact that the defendant was convicted of three or more violent felonies triggering ACCA penalties supports the severity of the sentence when viewed in the context of the statutorily calculated guideline range.

## The District Court's Consideration Record and Abuse of Discretion Review

A critical component of reviewing a sentence on appeal is the record of the district court's consideration of the arguments presented. In *Cutbank*, the defendant argued that the district court had applied the sentencing enhancement in a way that was inconsistent with the statutory purpose of 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). However, during the sentencing hearing, the defense gave notice of these arguments, and the district court noted the review of the PSR and memoranda. The appellate court applied the rule from *United States v. Masood*, which presumes that if a defendant raised an argument at sentencing, the district court considered and rejected it unless indicated otherwise. This preserves the trial record's integrity by avoiding the need for a mechanical recitation of factors if the record is clear. Furthermore, the court noted *United States v. Maluoth*, where it held that the court simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors. Because the record showed the district court was aware of the arguments and the sentencing factors where the sentencing occurred, the appellate court deferred to the sentencing decision. There is no abuse of discretion if the record supports the conclusion that the district court considered the statutory factors and the enhancement was applied correctly under the rules.

## Final Disposition: Affirming the Sentence and Procedural Boundaries

The final disposition of the appellate review confirms the stability of the district court's decision when the procedural and substantive reviews hold up. In *Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the judgment of the district court. This affirmation rests on two pillars: the calculation of the Guidelines range was correct, and the sentence imposed was within that range. Because the sentence fell within the advisory Guidelines range, the appellate court affirmed the sentence under the well-established rule of deference. The record demonstrated that the defendant had nearly a dozen prior felony convictions involving violence and firearms, and the offense of conviction involved specific harms against a domestic victim. The court concluded that the district court did not abuse its substantial sentencing discretion. This case demonstrates that procedural compliance with *Gall* and *Rosales-Mireles*, combined with the presumption of reasonableness for within-range sentences, usually results in an affirmation unless the § 3553(a) factors dictate a clear variance which is absent in the record.
