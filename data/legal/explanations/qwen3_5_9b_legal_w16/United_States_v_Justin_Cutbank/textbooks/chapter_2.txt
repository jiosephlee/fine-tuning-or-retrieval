Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Evidentiary Rulings, Admissibility, and Harmless Error

## Rule 404(b) Analysis: Prior Possession as Intrinsic Evidence vs. Propensity Evidence

The district court faced a challenge regarding the admissibility of evidence showing that the victim and a witness had previously observed the defendant’s possession of the firearm. Justin Cutbank filed a motion prior to trial to prevent the neighbors from testifying that they had seen him with the sawed-off rifle, arguing this would constitute propensity evidence violating Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). The appellate court explains that this argument rests on a distinction between credibility-altering tendencies versus evidence of ownership or control. While Rule 404(b) generally prohibits evidence of other crimes or bad acts to show a defendant’s character, the court clarifies that prior possession of a firearm is considered "intrinsic evidence." This type of evidence is directly relevant to the charged offense because it establishes ownership or control of the weapon at the time of the alleged crime.

In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit affirmed the district court’s ruling by relying on the precedent set in *United States v. Battle*. The court reasoned that prior possession helps establish continuous possession of the firearm before the specific incident at issue. Therefore, testimony from victims that they saw Cutbank with the gun in the past was not improperly excluded propensity evidence. This distinction is vital for law students to understand when evaluating evidentiary rulings: evidence is not admissible to suggest a defendant has a criminal disposition, but it is admissible if it proves a specific element of the crime or an essential fact regarding the weapon's ownership or the context of the offense.

## Confrontation Clause and Hearsay: Instructing the Jury on Victim Testimony Against Officers

Cutbank raised a specific procedural issue concerning how the jury was instructed regarding the statements made by the victim, D.F. He argued that the district court should have instructured the jury that it could not consider D.F.’s statements to police as substantive evidence until she herself testified in court. This request was designed to avoid hearsay violations and potential Confrontation Clause issues. The government countered that the statements would provide context for police actions and that the officer’s testimony would not violate hearsay rules. Furthermore, a limiting instruction at the close of trial, if necessary, would be sufficient.

The appellate court reviewed the district court’s decision to refrain from giving a limiting instruction during the trial by the standard of abuse of discretion. The court noted that it need not instruct the jury regarding each item of evidence at the time it is admitted if an appropriate instruction is provided at the close of trial. Crucially, the Confrontation Clause was not violated in this specific instance because the victim appeared in court and testified in person. Citing *United States v. Charboneau*, the court clarified that the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when hearsay declarants actually appear in court to testify. Because D.F. testified at the trial, the court concluded there was no abuse of discretion in denying the specific instruction Cutbank requested during the trial.

## Impeachment and Authentication: Admissibility of Prior Consistent/Inconsistent Statements regarding the Gun

The record shows two distinct issues regarding witness testimony and authentication. First, during cross-examination, D.F. mentioned that a relative had been killed by a gun to explain her unfamiliarity with firearms or inconsistent prior statements. The court denied the motion to strike this testimony. On appeal, Cutbank offered no authority contending that this memory of an unrelated death caused unfair prejudice or confusion. The court applied Federal Rule of Evidence 403, which balances probative value against danger of unfair prejudice, and found the district court acted within its broad discretion.

Second, Cutbank sought to undermine the government’s witness, C.F., regarding her prior use of the gun. Defense counsel attempted to cross-examine C.F. about prior statements made to a police investigator and sought to read portions of the resulting police report into evidence. The district court sustained the government’s objections because the report was not authored by C.F. On appeal, the court referenced *United States v. Cumbie*, noting that while extrinsic evidence may be used to impeach a witness for a prior inconsistent statement, that extrinsic evidence must be authenticated. The appellate court found the district court did not abuse its discretion by requiring proper authentication of the report before it could be read to the jury.

## Harmless Error Standard: Reviewing Whether Evidence Swayed the Verdict or Had Cumulative Effect

Even if the appellate court identified an error in a trial ruling, it will not necessarily reverse the conviction. The Eighth Circuit applies a harmless error standard in these contexts. The court explained that it will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the evidence that was improperly admitted. This is often phrased as asking whether the error was harmless. In *United States v. Cutbank*, the court noted that Cutbank could not clear this hurdle because the government’s evidence was substantial, including both testimonial and physical evidence.

The appellate court also addressed the cumulative effect of trial errors. It quoted *United States v. Keys*, stating that errors will not overturn a conviction unless there is substantial prejudice to the defendant. Because the government presented significant physical evidence of the gun and matching DNA, along with other testimony, the court found that the cumulative effect of the cited evidentiary rulings did not influence the verdict. This standard teaches law students that isolated evidentiary errors do not automatically result in a new trial when the overall record demonstrates strong proof of the underlying facts.

## Treatment of Unsubstantiated Appellant Arguments

Finally, the appellate court emphasizes the burden placed on the appellant to support their arguments. In this chapter of the opinion, the court notes that Cutbank raised a series of evidentiary issues without providing specific cases or analysis to support his claims. In legal appellate practice, an appellant is generally expected to cite authority or provide substantive reasoning to support a claim; if none is provided, the court may determine the contentions lack merit.

The court further noted that Cutback attempted to raise claims regarding cross-examination efforts on the police report that were not clearly preserved. The court expressed frustration with appellees who "unnecessarily expand the issues on appeal," noting that the circuit court generally does not reverse an erroneous evidentiary ruling if the error was harmless. Because the cumulative evidence against the defendant was overwhelming, the court held that it did not need to reach the merits of unpreserved issues or claims lacking citation to relevant authority. This demonstrates the importance of proper preservation of error during trial and the necessity of articulating clear legal arguments on appeal.
