Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "United States v. Justin Cutbank"

Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Procedural Posture and Factual Background

## The Factual Basis of the Firearms Possession

Understanding the factual background is the first step in reading any appellate opinion, as these facts serve as the foundation upon which the legal issues turn. In *United States v. Cutbank*, the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals begins by summarizing the events underlying the conviction in Section I, titled "Background." According to this section, the core incident occurred during the night of November 19-20, 2020. The defendant, Justin Cutbank, was involved in a domestic dispute involving a girlfriend, identified in the opinion as D.F. During this encounter, which involved alcohol and methamphetamine use, Cutbank smuggled a sawed-off Marlin .22 caliber rifle and held D.F. at gunpoint. D.F. testified that Cutbank forced her into a closet for hours, pointed the weapon at her face, and threatened to strike her. Eventually, Cutbank fled with the gun and D.F.'s cell phone.

For a law student, the duty of an appellate court here is to respect the trial court's findings of historical fact unless clear error is shown. The appellate court relies heavily on D.F.'s testimony and corroborating physical evidence, including DNA on the firearm matching Cutbank. The physical record supports the narrative that Cutbank fled to a neighboring house and attempted to resist arrest in a garage, eventually leading to the seizure of the weapon found on the path of travel. The prosecution secured a conviction based on the defendant's control over the firearm *after* the incident, but the facts establish the timeline of possession immediately preceding the incident. This chronology is critical because the defendent’s conviction rests on the theory that he possessed the firearm at the time of the gunpoint incident, a fact disputed during the trial regarding continuous possession of the weapon.

## Statutory Charges and the Armed Career Criminal Act

The legal framework surrounding *Cutbank* is defined by specific federal statutes, which inform both the conviction and the sentencing calculation. The opinion introduces the relevant laws in the introduction and discusses them in the fact sections. Specifically, Cutbank was convicted of violating 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) by being a felon in unlawful possession of a firearm. This statute makes it a crime for any person who "has been convicted of a crime punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year" to own or carry a firearm. Because Cutbank was prosecuted for a "felony" prior to this incident, the charges were anchored in his criminal history rather than the specifics of the gunpoint incident.

Furthermore, the sentencing ranges were driven by the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), codified at 18 U.S.C. § 924(e). The opinion notes that the jury found Cutbank had previously been convicted of at least three violent felonies. This finding triggered the ACCA’s enhanced penalty provisions, allowing for a mandatory minimum sentence that fell within the Federal Sentencing Guidelines. In the context of *Cutbank*, the gun itself had an obliterated serial number, and the possession occurred while he was recklessly creating a risk of bodily injury while fleeing police. These specific statutory factors meant that the base sentencing guidelines started high, resulting in an advisory sentencing range of 292 to 365 months. Understanding these statutes is essential because the appellate court reviews both the legal application of the felon-in-possession statute and the calculation of the ACCA enhancement to ensure the district court applied the law correctly.

## Procedural History: Conviction, Sentencing, and Record

Moving from the facts to the legal procedure requires understanding the distinction between the District Court and the Court of Appeals. The procedural history in *Cutbank* outlines that the District Court heard the crime, convicted the defendant, and sentenced him to 292 months of imprisonment. The opinion states this sentence was within the "advisory guidelines range as determined by the district court." Following conviction, Cutbank raised multiple issues regarding his conviction and the sentence, leading to an appeal to the Eighth Circuit.

In reading the opinion, students must recognize that the "Procedural History" section serves several functions. First, it establishes that the District Court was the "trial court," which meant it acted as the arbiter of evidence and fact at trial. The District Court handled motions to suppress or limit evidence, and rulings on those motions are appealed under an "abuse of discretion" standard. Second, the opinion details that the district court adopted the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) without objection. This is significant because it establishes a record for the appellate court to review. When the district court adopts a PSR's factual statements undisputedly, the appellate court can rely on those facts for sentencing review unless there was a clear error. This procedural posture clarifies that the appellate court is not retrying the facts but reviewing the trial court's application of the law to the established record.

## Scope of Appeal: Evidentiary Challenges and Sentencing Enhancements

The final element of the procedural landscape is the specific scope of the appeal. While the opinion mentions Cutbank argued there were multiple "evidentiary and procedural errors," the appellate court identifies these as specific challenges to the trial process. The introduction of the case clarifies that Cutbank’s primary claims focused on the admission of prior instances where he possessed the firearm and the acknowledgment of hearsay.

The appellate court in *Cutbank* limits its review to the arguments raised in the lower court's briefs and the record. Specifically, Cutbank argued the district court should not have admitted evidence of D.F. and C.F. previously identifying him with the gun, claiming it violated Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b) propensity rules. He also argued against the sentencing enhancement under USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B), claiming the court improperly applied the four-level increase for "possession in connection with another felony offense." The Eighth Circuit structured its opinion to address these specific challenges one by one. In Section II, the court reviews each evidentiary ruling (rulings A through E), determining if the record shows the district court abused its discretion. In Section III, the court isolates the sentencing enhancement argument. A student reading the opinion must recognize that the appellate court will not consider issues not raised in the Brief on Appeal if the defense failed to properly object at trial. The scope of the appeal is therefore defined by the specific legal errors Cutbank preserved during his trial and preserved on appeal.
