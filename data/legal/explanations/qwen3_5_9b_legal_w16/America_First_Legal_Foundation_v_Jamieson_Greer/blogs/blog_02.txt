Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Speculative Causation as the New Redressability Standard: Learning from FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine

For the plaintiffs challenging agency inaction, the road to the federal courts has just become significantly narrower. In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the D.C. Circuit offered a stinging rejection of Article III standing that serves as a cautionary tale for would-be litigants relying on procedural triggers to access judicial review. America First Legal Foundation (AFL) sought to compel the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate the Justice Department’s arbitrary handling of FOIA requests. While the D.C. Circuit explicitly declined to decide the merits of OSC’s alleged statutory error, the court disposed of the entire case on standing grounds, utilizing a particularly tight reading of *Steel Co.* and a new, post-*FDA* reliance on redressability doctrine.

The decision matters because it tightens the "speculative causation" stringency in the administrative context. For years, plaintiffs have argued that procedural violations of statutes like FOIA create standing if they are sufficiently tied to a concrete interest. The district court had already found OSC made a legal error by declining to investigate, but the appellate court summarized the sweep of administrative injuries by declaring the causal chain too risky. By pivoting heavily on *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, the court signaled that any remedial path relying on another branch of government remains unbound by the court’s power.

## The Speculative Chain of Lay Outcomes

The crux of the opinion lies in how the court analyzed the first prong of AFL’s standing claim: the desire to receive the DOJ records they sought in the first place. AFL argued that without an OSC investigation, there would be no subsequent disciplinary action, and without discipline, DOJ might not release the documents. This created a three-step chain of potential events: OSC investigates, the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) sanctions employees, and DOJ then releases the records.

The D.C. Circuit dismantled this chain using a recent holding from *FDA*. In *FDA*, the Supreme Court had emphasized that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." Judge Katsas applied this to the administrative dance between agency inaction and congressional enforcement mechanisms. An order against OSC does not bind the MSPB, nor does it bind DOJ. Because the ultimate goal (getting the records) depends on the choices of two independent actors not before the court—the MSPB and DOJ—the redressability requirement fails.

This reliance on *FDA* is significant. The court did not just note that other actors were involved; it treated their "unfettered choices" as a jurisdictional cutoff. Even if OSC is legally bound to start a § 1216 investigation, the heating of facts required to make the final redress compelling cannot be guessed. The court wrote that AFL asks the court to speculate "about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel." In a procedural standing case, this creates a double barrier. The violation of the procedural right must be linked to a concrete injury, but then the redress of that right cannot bounce off another agency that isn't bound by the judgment.

## Doctrinal Implications for Procedure-Based Claims

Beyond the statutory mechanics of the OSC case, the doctrinal reach of this decision concerns the "procedural right" strand of standing. *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* established that violating a procedural right without a concrete interest is insufficient. AFL argued that the statutory requirement for an OSC investigation created the procedural right that could save its standing claim.

Judge Katsas confirmed that *Summers* still applies in the formative sense that ":". Of course, he noted that mere violations of a procedural rule don't count. However, the court noted that the informational interest supposed to underpin that right—notice of investigation status—was not even guaranteed by the statutory text. This leaves litigants with a conceptual bottleneck: they need a concrete interest to be protected, but they cannot raise that interest if the only defendant before the court (OSC) cannot control the outcome of that interest.

This creates a particularly troubling下游 effect for administrative law lawyers. In cases where statutes grant investigative powers to agencies but leave the ultimate remedy to a board or another agency, standing becomes nearly impossible to plead. The court's refusal to reframe the statutory scheme implies that plaintiffs must have a direct line of causation from the defendant to the injury, or the court refuses to receive the case. This disallows the court to act as a review forum for "programmatic" grievances where the agency is simply one link in a bureaucracy.

## Downstream Effects on Agency Accountability

The ramifications of *AFL v. Jamieson Greer* extend beyond the OSC and FOIA world. If the D.C. Circuit holds that redressability depends on "independent actors not before the court," it severely impacts plaintiffs who want to hold mid-level agencies accountable for broader patterns of misconduct. In *FDA v. Alliance*, the court found that federal judges are often powerless to dictate the outcome of executive actions when the remedy flows through other agencies. That precedent now sweeps broadly across the administrative state. It suggests that without a direct mandate to the defendant agency, claims must rely on the defendant agency's own leverage over their clients, supervisors, or witnesses.

It also suggests that a court's inability to write a judgment against an independent actor (like MSPB in this case) is not a textual issue but a constitutional one. The court here said the "redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties." Because the judgment would not physically bind the MSPB or DOJ, the redressability is zero. If you are trying to sue an individual or agency for how they handle documents and you want an investigation to prompt a third party to act, you will lose if you frame your claim as "negligence by the primary actor." You are stuck fighting the penalties of the real harm being caused by the other side.

In *AFL v. Jamieson Greer*, the court used Article III standing to draw a sidewall in the fight against information withholding. While not deciding whether OSC should investigate, the decision limits the judicial toolbox to reach these violations. The court forced the dismissal not because OSC was too powerful, but because it was too distant from the成果转化 that plaintiffs claimed to need.
