Title: Blog about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer: The 'Procedural Right in a Vacuum' Doctrine in Action

Katsas Circuit Judge’s decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* offers a potent reminder that statutory mandates for administrative oversight do not bypass the rigors of Article III standing. The D.C. Circuit has long served as a balking gatekeeper for agencies and plaintiffs, particularly in cases where the plaintiff seeks relief from a federal agency based on the actions of another. Here, the D.C. Circuit held that the America First Legal Foundation (AFL) lacked Article III standing to compel the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate the Department of Justice’s handling of a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request. While the litigation involves the OSC’s statutory mandate to investigate arbitrary withholdings under 5 U.S.C. § 1216, the D.C. Circuit concluded that the plaintiffs’ claims failed because the relief sought was too conjectural. This decision underscores a strict application of the *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* principle that a "procedural right in vacuo" is insufficient to confer standing, effectively dampening third-party efforts to police agency transparency through judicial enforcement of administrative procedures.

## The Statutory Mandate and the Standing Barrier

The case arose from a dispute over how the DOJ handled emails regarding attorney recusal. AFL first sought these records under FOIA. While the DOJ eventually produced the records, AFL remained dissatisfied with the agency’s stated policy of not searching employee emails without consent. Consequently, AFL turned to the Office of Special Counsel, the independent agency tasked with ensuring a fair Civil Service. Under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), OSC is empowered to investigate alleged arbitrary or capricious withholdings under FOIA.

Influenced by the lack of disciplinary action in the FOIA proceedings, AFL argued that OSC had an affirmative duty to launch an investigation under § 1216, regardless of whether the court ordered production in the underlying FOIA suit. The district court agreed that OSC may have acted incorrectly in refusing to investigate but dismissed AFL’s suit, remanding for reconsideration. The District Court essentially held that while OSC might have erred, the decision to investigate remained within OSC’s discretion. When OSC declined to investigate again, AFL returned to court. While the D.C. Circuit did not reach the merits of whether OSC had a statutory duty to investigate under § 1216, it concluded that AFL lacked the constitutional standing to bring the case at all.

The court applied the strictures of *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env't*, emphasizing that standing is strictly jurisdictional. Judge Katsas noted the two pathologies at play: first, that AFL claimed the OSC violated a procedural right, invoking the *Summers* doctrine of procedural standing; second, that the relief sought relied on speculative chains of causation where OSC’s decision would influence the choices of independent actors. The court identified the core constitutional hurdle as the "injury in fact" requirement, insisting that the injury must be concrete and particularized, not conjectural.

## Procedural Rights and Redressability

Central to the opinion is the rejection of AFL’s primary argument that the violation of a statutory right to an investigation constituted a concrete injury. The opinion relies on the *Summers* test, which recognizes that a procedural right alone is insufficient without a concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation. The court characterized AFL’s claim as a "procedural right in a vacuo," a phrase that did the heavy lifting for the court’s rejection of the standing claims.

Judge Katsas analyzed two specific theories AFL might use to establish standing. The first claimed that OSC’s failure to investigate prevented AFL from receiving the DOJ records in the first place. The court dismantled this theory based on causation and redressability principles, noting that the informational injury regarding the withheld records was traceable to the DOJ, not OSC. Furthermore, even if OSC had investigated, there would be a speculative link between OSC’s investigation and DOJ eventually complying with FOIA. Any causal chain requiring speculation about the "unfettered choices" of the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) or the DOJ, or how OSC exercises enforcement discretion, is generally insufficient to create standing. The opinion cites *Heckler v. Chaney*, reminding readers that administrative discretion rarely supports judgments that can redress private grievances absent a clear entitlement.

The second theory relied on the fruits of the OSC investigation itself. AFL argued that an investigation would provide them with notices required under § 1214, which AFC claims is a procedural right. However, the court found that § 1216 investigations do not create an entitlement to follow the procedural notice requirements mandated for personnel practice investigations under § 1214. Consequently, the court found no concrete injury because OSC was never statutorily obligated to provide those specific notifications in a section 1216 context.

## Doctrinal Implications and Future Litigation

The holding in *Jamieson Greer* has significant downstream effects for transparency advocates and organizations relying on OSC’s watchdog function. By treating procedural violations without concrete injuries as jurisdictionally barred, the D.C. Circuit forecloses a specific litigation strategy where plaintiffs bypass direct agency accountability by pushing OSC investigations. It forces plaintiffs to seek relief directly against the withholding agency—often the DOJ in cases like this—rather than relying on OSC as an intermediary enforcement mechanism. This suggests that claims based purely on the violation of a statute’s procedural mandate, even one that sounds mandatory like "shall conduct an investigation," may be too attenuated to satisfy the rigorous *Lujan* causation inquiry.

Practically, this decision will likely curb the ability of third-party organizations to enforce institutional transparency mandates through procedural standing alone. Litigants attempting to compel administrative agencies to "work harder" on policy issues or withholdings will find themselves at the mercy of standing doctrine if they cannot demonstrate a direct, non-speculative injury. The court’s reliance on *Summers* and *All. v. Hippocratic Med.* reinforces a high bar for redressability where the injury is derivative of another agency’s non-participation or independent decision-making.

Ultimately, *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* reasserts the separation of powers' boundaries on judicial enforcement of agency discretion. While OSC possesses broad powers under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 to investigate civil service misconduct, the D.C. Circuit confirms that this power cannot be weaponized by third parties through the courts unless a concrete injury is directly tied to the OSC’s refusal to act. For the doctrine of standing, this leaves a nagging question: how much power does Congress intend to grant OSC if the agency can ignore the public interest? Yet, until standing is resolved, such questions remain purely political rather than judicial. The D.C. Circuit has drawn the line: Article III limits access even to the most robust enforcement mechanisms.
