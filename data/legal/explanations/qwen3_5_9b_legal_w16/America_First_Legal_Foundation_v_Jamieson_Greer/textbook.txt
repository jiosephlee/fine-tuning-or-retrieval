Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

# Statutory Framework and Factual Genesis

## Overview of the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) and 5 U.S.C. § 1216 Authority

To understand the dispute in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, a student must first comprehend the institutional role of the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) and the specific statutory machinery that empowers it. The OSC operates as an investigative and prosecutorial agency established to ensure a "fair, efficient, and lawfully-conducted Civil Service." This mandate is rooted in the Civil Service Reform Act, which grants the OSC specific duties regarding the oversight of federal personnel. Central to this role is the authority to investigate allegations of misconduct by government employees and to seek corrective action where necessary.

The OSC’s authority to handle misconduct is codified under the Civil Service Reform Act, which distinguishes between different categories of violations and specifies the remedies available. The relevant mechanisms for these investigations are contained within Chapter 12, specifically sections 1214 and 1216 of the United States Code. Specifically, 5 U.S.C. § 1216 empowers the OSC to investigate allegations of arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited by the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). The statute explicitly states that OSC "shall . . . conduct an investigation" of such allegations. This framework positions the OSC as an intermediary body that does not simply decide cases but investigates the underlying warrants for potential enforcement actions or disciplinary proceedings against specific employees. Because the OSC is a federal agency, its activity is subject to judicial review only if the plaintiff can demonstrate a justiciable case or controversy, which in this litigation context led the appellate court to focus on standing. However, to assess the underlying legal conflict regarding OSC’s power, the court first analyzes the specific statutory provisions at play.

## OSC Enforcement Powers Under the Civil Service Reform Act for Prohibited Personnel Practices

The Civil Service Reform Act outlines two primary paths for OSC intervention: correcting prohibited personnel practices and addressing other forms of misconduct. While these are often discussed in tandem, their statutory origins differ, and the distinction is critical for determining the scope of OSC authority. Section 1214 of the statute governs procedures for addressing "prohibited personnel practices." This section sets forth specific provisions, including the requirement to notify the complaining party about the progress of the investigation and the eventual potential for corrective action. This mechanism is distinct from the mandate in 5 U.S.C. § 1216, which deals with other forms of misconduct.

For the purposes of the dispute before the Court, 5 U.S.C. § 1216 is the operative statute. It expands the OSC’s reach beyond traditional personnel protection to include FOIA violations. Under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(c), if a "prohibited personnel practice" is involved, OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 and disciplinary action under section 1215 in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." The Court notes that OSC declined to investigate the case because the specific predicates required by § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were not satisfied. Yet, AFL’s argument hinges on the text of § 1216(a)(3), which appears to mandate an investigation of any allegation concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under the FOIA, regardless of specific court findings. Thus, the first layer of the inquiry is whether OSC is legally entitled to investigate allegations of FOIA violations under this broad statutory provision, or whether the agency's discretion is constrained by the specific enforcement triggers found elsewhere in the federal code.

## FOIA Enforcement Provisions Under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) and Its Relationship to OSC Authority

While the OSC derives general investigatory authority from the Civil Service Reform Act, a separate provision imposes specific procedural triggers for OSC enforcement in FOIA disputes. Section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) of the FOIA creates a specific mandate for the OSC: it directs OSC to "promptly initiate a proceeding to determine whether disciplinary action is warranted" against responsible officers or employees. However, this is not an open-ended authority. The statute imposes three strict predicates for this action: (1) a court must have ordered production of agency records improperly withheld, (2) the court must award attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) the court must specifically question whether the agency "acted arbitrarily or capriciously with respect to the withholding." These elements distinguish the OSC’s role in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) from its general authority under the CSRA.

In this case, the OSC argued that its statutory authority under this provision was triggered only when these specific judicial conditions were met. Consequently, the OSC disclaimed authority to investigate based on a personal FOIA request or a policy dispute absent a lawsuit resulting in the specific requested court findings regarding arbitrary and capricious withholding. This defense was central to the OSC’s refusal to investigate the Department of Justice’s email search policy. The legal tension in the case, therefore, rests on whether the mandate in 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3)—the CSRA provision—enforces a broader duty to investigate than is permitted by the specific, conditional mandates found in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). The Department of Justice and OSC characterized the statutory demand as coinciding perfectly with the specific judicial triggers required for action, whereas AFL argued that the request was clearly within OSC’s mandate to investigate alleged statutory violations independently.

## Factual Background: AFL's Request for DOJ Emails and the Initial Lawsuit

The genesis of the dispute began when America First Legal Foundation (AFL) sought investigative records from the Department of Justice (DOJ) to understand how judicial recusal policies are managed. AFL requested specific emails exchanged between an outside attorney and the DOJ’s Civil Division. According to AFL’s account, the civil division initially denied the request, citing a dubious internal policy that it does not search employee emails for responsive records without the employee's consent. This initial refusal motivated AFL to file a suit directly against the DOJ under FOIA. This initial litigation resulted in the DOJ producing—and continuing to produce—records that were responsive to the request. The sufficiency of this production became less relevant to the dispute, as AFL’s grievances shifted from the withholding of emails to the underlying policy regarding the search of those emails in the future.

Once the substantive FOIA production had begun, AFL sought to intervene against the highest level of civil service protection. It filed a complaint against the OSC, asking the agency to investigate the DOJ’s alleged policy of not searching employee emails without consent. AFL explicitly invoked 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), asserting that because the withholding was arbitrary or capricious under the FOIA, OSC was statutorily obligated to investigate the issue. By targeting the OSC rather than the DOJ, AFL hoped to secure a more formal review of the DOJ’s internal practices. This shift from seeking records from the DOJ to seeking an investigation from the OSC is the critical pivot in the case, moving the inquiry from the executive branch’s duty to produce documents to the legislative branch’s investigative body’s duty to conduct inquiries.

## Development of the Dispute: DOJ's Production of Records Versus OSC's Disclaiming Authority

The dispute moved forward with the OSC declining to investigate the alleged policy. In doing so, the OSC relied on the specific provisions of 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). The agency’s position was that its authority to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings was contingent upon the judicial inquiry and process specified in the FOIA statute. Essentially, OSC asserted that it lacked the statutory power to investigate an arbitrary withholding unless a prior lawsuit and court order satisfied the strict predicates set out in the statute, such as an award of attorney’s fees and a specific judicial inquiry into the arbitrariness of the withholding. AFL responded by arguing that § 1216 mandated OSC investigations independently of FOIA. AFL contended that even if the conditions for § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) were not met, § 1216 required an investigation of the conduct.

This standoff created the procedural posture of the case. The OSC’s refusal was based on legal constraints it perceived within the broader administrative enforcement framework. When the OSC responded to AFL’s lawsuit, it informed AFL that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) did not require an investigation of the alleged FOIA policy because the necessary predicates regarding court orders and attorney fees were absent. The dispute then migrated to the District Court, where the merits of this interpretation became the first major hurdle to clear. The case is therefore not simply about whether the emails were withheld (DOJ was already producing them), but about whether OSC has a statutory mandate to investigate the *policy* of withholding and whether AFL has standing to sue for that inaction.

## Summary of the District Court’s Initial Rulings on the Merits Versus Procedural Dismissal

The District Court’s analysis of the statutory framework was nuanced, acknowledging a tension between the broad language of the CSRA and the specific requirements of the FOIA enforcement mandate. The court concluded that 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) did not limit the OSC’s power to investigate alleged FOIA violations under 5 U.S.C. § 1216. The court acknowledged that the OSC’s decision not to investigate was based on a "legal error" regarding the relationship between these statutes. This ruling suggested that the statutory framework in § 1216 granted OSC broader investigative powers than the specific FOIA enforcement provision allowed them to limit.

However, the District Court also recognized the practical reality of the OSC’s authority under section 1216(c). Despite agreeing that OSC was potentially in legal error by refusing to investigate, the court ruled it could not order the agency to do so because section 1216(c) committed the decision to conduct an investigation to OSC’s discretion. The court therefore remanded the case to the OSC for further consideration. After OSC again declined to investigate, AFL moved for entry of final judgment. Ultimately, the District Court dismissed the remaining claims to conclude the case for lack of a viable remedy against the agency’s discretion. The appellate review is conducted de novo, as is standard practice for review of the dismissal rulings.

# Procedural Posture and the Standing Threshold

## The District Court's mixed rulings: compliance with statute versus OSC discretion

To understand why the appellate court focused on standing rather than the merits of the FOIA policy dispute, students must first understand how the district court struggled to reconcile two statutory sources of authority: the Federal Civil Service Reform Act and the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). The Office of Special Counsel (OSC) is authorized under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 to investigate arbitrary withholding of FOIA information. However, FOIA’s enforcement section, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), establishes a specific trigger for OSC investigations, requiring that a court find the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously in withholding records. In this case, the district court faced a contradictory statutory landscape. While the district court concluded that OSC had erred in believing § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) limited its authority under § 1216, it simultaneously recognized that § 1216(c) vested significant discretion in the OSC regarding whether to open an investigation into a specific alleged violation. The court found that even if OSC could legally investigate (because § 552 does not limit § 1216), the decision to actually launch an investigation remained within the agency’s discretion. Consequently, the court could not compel OSC to investigate on the grounds that OSC had a legal duty to do so, as long as it was allowed to choose not to act.

## Remand to OSC and the subsequent final refusal to investigate

Following this nuanced ruling, the district court entered a remand to the OSC to allow it to reconsider its decision under the correct legal framework regarding the interplay between the FOIA statute and § 1216. When the agency subsequently declined to investigate the alleged FOIA policy again, the district court treated this as an exercise of permissible discretion, leading to a dismissal of AFL’s claims for failure to state a claim on the merits. This final refusal created the necessary procedural posture for the appeal. Now that OSC had made a final decision not to investigate, the circuit court’s review was no longer about interpreting the administrative record de novo on a motion for summary judgment, but rather about whether the plaintiff had legally provable standing to seek relief at all. The district court’s dismissal for failure to state a claim effectively acted as a final judgment, permitting the appellate court to consider the broader constitutional threshold of Article III standing that had not been fully litigated below.

## Appellate jurisdiction and the order for supplemental briefing on Article III standing

In the appellate phase, the Court of Appeals operated on the principle identified in *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env't*, which mandates that federal courts decide issues of subject-matter jurisdiction before addressing the merits of a case. Standing is a jurisdictional requirement; without it, the federal court lacks the power to hear the case at all. Although neither the parties nor the district court explicitly raised the Article III standing issue during their initial pleadings, the appellate court identified it as a potential barrier to reviewing the case. To ensure a proper record on this threshold question, the court ordered supplemental briefing solely on the standing issue. This procedural step underscores the priority of jurisdiction in federal litigation: even if a plaintiff correctly identifies a bureaucratic failure or statutory non-conformity, the Supreme Court’s precedent dictates that the court must first determine if the Constitution permits it to intervene. This approach prevents the federal judiciary from wasting its resources on non-justiciable controversies.

## The constitutional basis for standing: Article III 'Case or Controversy' requirement

The discussion of standing in this chapter is grounded in the "Case or Controversy" requirement of Article III, § 2 of the U.S. Constitution. As articulated in *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, standing is not a creature of discretion but of constitutional limitation. To satisfy this requirement, a plaintiff must demonstrate an actual injury that is concrete and particularized, actual or imminent, caused by the defendant’s action, and likely to be redressed by a favorable judicial decision. In the context of administrative law, the injury must be an invasion of a legally protected interest. Importantly, standing is a threshold inquiry; if the plaintiff fails to prove one of the three core elements of *Lujan*—injury, causation, or redressability—the case is dismissed for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, regardless of how reasonable the plaintiff’s substantive legal theories or arguments against the agency might be. Therefore, the appellate court in *Greer* focused here not on whether the OSC violated the law, but on whether AFL was a proper plaintiff to bring the action.

## The difficulty of standing claims against administrative agencies and discretionary decisions

AFL attempted to establish standing based on at least two theories, yet both failed under the rigorous standards of *Lujan*. First, AFL argued that the OSC’s refusal to investigate caused an informational injury because DOJ had not produced records AFL sought under FOIA. The court rejected this on causation grounds, noting that the failure to produce records was a direct act of the DOJ, not the OSC. Speculating that an OSC investigation would lead the MSPB to mandate sanctions, which would lead the DOJ to turn over records, creates a chain of causation that relies on independent actors making arbitrary decisions. This violates the established rule that causation cannot rely on speculation about unfettered choices made by actors not before the court.

AFL’s second theory rested on the specific informational right denied under section 1216, asserting that an investigation should provide notices required under section 1214 for prohibited personnel practices. However, the court recognized that OSC has discretion regarding procedural requirements under title 5 in section 1216 investigations. Because there is no statutory entitlement to receive section 1214 notices in a section 1216 investigation, the plaintiff sustained the injury of being "procedurally right but substantively deprived" without a concrete interest affected by the procedural violation. The court cited *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*, reiterating that a procedural right in a "vacuo" without a threatened concrete interest does not confer standing. This highlights the particular difficulty in establishing standing against federal agencies where their decision-making processes often involve discretionary denial of relief, requiring plaintiffs to show a tangible injury rather than a grievance with policy or procedure.

## The court's reversal of course: moving from merits determination to jurisdictional dismissal

Because AFL could not satisfy the constitutional requirements for standing, the Circuit Court vacated the district court’s judgment. This decision marks a shift in the appellate posture from a review of the merits—whether OSC acted arbitrarily or whether the FOIA policy was lawful—to a jurisdictional determination. Even though the district court had ruled on the merits or the failure to state a claim, the appellate court concluded that reaching those substantive conclusions was impermissible without standing. Consequently, the Court of Appeals remanded the case with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. For a law student, this disposition is significant because it demonstrates that the "merits" of a case against a federal agency are secondary to the "standing" of the plaintiff. Without a judicially justiciable injury, an administrative enforcement decision—even one claimed to be arbitrary or non-compliant with the statute—cannot be adjudicated in federal court.

# Article III Standing Analysis: Injury, Causation, and Redressability

## The Article III standing triad: injury in fact, causation, and redressability (*Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*)

The doctrine of Article III standing acts as a jurisdictional gatekeeper, ensuring that federal courts remain the primary forum for the resolution of actual disputes between parties. As established by *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, an association claiming such standing must demonstrate three distinct elements: (1) injury in fact, which must be concrete, particularized, and actual or imminent; (2) causation, or a causal connection between the plaintiff's injury and the allegedly unlawful conduct of the defendant; and (3) redressability, meaning it is likely that a favorable decision would redress the injury. In *AMerica First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the D.C. Circuit emphasized that these requirements are jurisdictional and must be satisfied before the court can reach the merits. Because the parties initially failed to raise standing, the Court of Appeals reserved the analysis until supplemental briefing, noting that neither the text of the *Lujan* framework nor the *Steel Co.* interpretation of subject matter jurisdiction imposes greater burdens than the Constitution itself. The inquiry remains focused on whether the specific interests of the plaintiff are affected in a legally protected manner.

## Theory 1: Informational injury regarding DOJ's FOIA withholding (Injury and Causation)

America First Legal Foundation (AFL) advanced its first standing theory by alleging an informational injury related to the denial of Freedom of Information Act records by the Department of Justice (DOJ). Specifically, AFL claimed that the Office of Special Counsel's (OSC) refusal to investigate DOJ's policies prevented AFL from obtaining the discovery it requested under FOIA. Under the relaxed standing requirements applicable to procedural injuries, a plaintiff must still show that a violation of a procedural right resulted in injury to a concrete, particularized interest. In this context, AFL asserted that the "deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest" was insufficient to grant standing. Consequently, AFL attempted to frame its standing claim by arguing that OSC’s inaction directly caused the failure of DOJ to respond to its FOIA request. The D.C. Circuit addressed whether this causal link was sufficient to satisfy the Article III triad.

## Analysis of speculative causation: the chain involving MSPB and DOJ choices (*FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*)

The Court of Appeals rejected AFL's first standing theory due to fatal problems with causation and redressability regarding the informational claim. The opinion explains that the injury from DOJ's refusal to disclose records is plainly traceable to DOJ, not OSC. To support the claim that OSC was the proximate cause, AFL required speculation about the choices of independent government actors not before the court. The court noted that OSC's investigation could have led to a corrective action or disciplinary proceeding, which in turn would have been considered by the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB). Following this potential action, DOJ might then have decided to produce the records. The court held that this speculative chain involving unconstrained agency actors creates an insufficient causal link. A favorable judgment in this case would not "very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress" the informational injury, as judicial relief generally binds the parties before the court, not actors who were not involved in the litigation (*Haaland v. Brackeen*).

## Theory 2: Procedural injury regarding OSC's failure to enforce (Procedural Right in Vacuo)

AFL's second standing theory relied on its argument that OSC violated a procedural right by failing to conduct a mandatory investigation under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) of the Civil Service Reform Act. The Court addressed this through the lens of procedural standing, referencing *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*. This precedent establishes that a deprivation of a procedural right is insufficient for Article III standing without some concrete interest affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo. For standing to exist when alleging a procedural violation, the plaintiff must show that the statutory right was designed to protect a threatened concrete interest and that the violation caused an actual or imminent injury to that interest. Because the right to an investigation under § 1216 did not inherently secure a specific concrete benefit to AFL without further analysis, the requirement of a concrete injury remained unmet, rendering the procedural claim outside jurisdiction.

## Latticeley/Carson precedent on OSC 5 U.S.C. § 1214 notice requirements under § 1216 investigations

In evaluating the concrete nature of the procedural injury, the Court examined the statutory scheme governing OSC investigations. AFL argued that OSC’s failure to investigate meant it did not provide the notices and information required under the analogous provisions of § 1214, which governs investigations into prohibited personnel practices. However, the Court looked to *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*, where other circuit courts held that OSC’s decision whether to follow § 1214's procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary. Because § 1216 does not explicitly mandate that the OSC must engraft § 1214 notice requirements onto investigations of FOIA withholding cases, AFL could not claim an entitlement to receive specific information from OSC. The opinion clarifies that without a statutory mandate or an express entitlement to receive notice in a § 1216 context, the obligation to provide notice is discretionary, not a rigid procedural entitle, and thus does not support standing.

## Redressability failure: the lack of entitlement to OSC investigative findings under § 1216

The Court further analyzed redressability based on the lack of entitlement to OSC investigative findings under § 1216. Even if OSC were compelled to investigate, AFL did not contend that OSC must provide specific information under § 1214 procedures during a § 1216 proceeding. Without a statutory mandate to provide findings, a judicial order mandating an investigation would not be reasonably likely to redress AFL's second alleged injury. The opinion emphasizes that redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not merely on whether its rationale might persuade actors outside the immediate litigation. Since AFL could not demonstrate that a court order would reasonably induce OSC to provide the requested information, or that the investigation itself would yield the records desired from DOJ, the judicial remedy would fail to function as required for redressability.

## The legal principle of agency discretion (*Heckler v. Chaney*) and judicial compulsion limits

Beyond specific statutory interpretations, the opinion relied on the broader principle of agency discretion derived from *Heckler v. Chaney*. In *Heckler*, the Supreme Court established that an agency's decision not to investigate or enforce is often entitled to judicial deference and not easily compelled in the absence of clear statutory language. Applying this principle, the Court noted that speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion creates a barrier to standing. A favorable judgment cannot be assumed to compel OSC to initiate discipline or sanctions against DOJ employees. The Court's reasoning underscores that courts generally may not compel agency enforcement, which further limits the possibility of redressability for claims challenging the agency's refusal to act. Consequently, rather than a concrete injury traceable to the defendant, the injuries alleged were conjectural and hypothetical, falling short of the "actual or imminent" requirement of Article III.

## Disposition

The D.C. Circuit held that AFL lacked Article III standing to pursue its claims against OSC. Because the essential requirements of injury, causation, and redressability were not met under the established framework, the appellate court vacated the district court's judgment. The final disposition remanded the case with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject matter jurisdiction, reinforcing the doctrine that the federal judiciary cannot exercise power over disputes that do not involve a real controversy between the parties.

# Disposition and Significance

## Vacatur of the District Court Judgment and Remand with Instructions to Dismiss for Lack of Subject-Matter Jurisdiction

The appellate court rendered its decision based strictly on a threshold constitutional question rather than the underlying administrative merits. Because the Court concluded that America First Legal Foundation (AFL) failed to establish Article III standing, it held that the district court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction. This finding necessitated the vacatur of the district court's previous judgment, which had addressed the case on the merits. The appellate court issued a clear instruction to remand the case with specific orders: the lower court was to dismiss the suit because the complaining party could not demonstrate a case or controversy under the Constitution. For law students, this disposition highlights a critical procedural hierarchy in federal litigation. Courts are obligated to decide jurisdictional issues, such as standing, before addressing substantive claims. Here, the legal dispute regarding the Office of Special Counsel's (OSC) authority to investigate FOIA violations was irrelevant, as the appellate panel determined that the plaintiff was not a proper party to initiate the lawsuit. Consequently, the disposition resolves the appeal by effectively closing the litigation at the jurisdictional stage, sending a signal to litigators that constitutional barriers must be cleared regardless of the statutory strength of the complaint.

## Implications for the OSC's Discretionary Enforcement Power

The opinion underscores the limits of judicial intervention in administrative enforcement, specifically regarding an agency's discretionary power. Although the Civil Service Reform Act and FOIA appear to empower the OSC to initiate proceedings, the court relied on general administrative law principles to analyze standing. Specifically, the panel cited *Heckler v. Chaney* to emphasize that courts generally may not compel agency enforcement actions. In this specific instance, even though the statute utilized mandatory language like "shall," the court found that the causal link required for standing was broken by the OSC's decisional process. A judicial order mandating an investigation would not guarantee a specific outcome because the OSC retains discretion in whether to pursue corrective actions and disciplinary proceedings. The implication for litigation is that plaintiffs cannot rely on statutory mandates alone to bypass the presumption of agency enforcement discretion. To succeed in a challenge regarding OSC inaction, a plaintiff must demonstrate that the resulting action (or inaction) causes concrete harm that a judicial order would likely redress, cutting through the uncertainty of the agency's future choices.

## Limitations on Non-Government Plaintiffs Seeking OSC Investigations of Administrative Policies

America First Legal Foundation’s failure to state a claim establishes significant limitations on non-government plaintiffs attempting to compel administrative agencies to investigate other government policies. The court rejected the idea that a citizen organization could assert standing merely by virtue of its interest in FOIA compliance or personnel practices. The opinion notes that the "discretionary" nature of the OSC’s decision to provide notices meant that AFL had no concrete informational interest in the investigation itself due to the lack of an entitlement to the information under section 1216. This decision suggests a narrow path for challengers who wish to utilize OSC investigative powers. Specifically, standing is not available to those who seek to sue third-party agencies (like the Department of Justice) for policies (like a refusal to search emails) unless they have a direct, concrete interest that the OSC investigation would necessarily and likely fulfill. This limits the utility of OSC as a target for standing when the underlying violation involves other agency refusals to produce documents, reinforcing the general rule that courts cannot adjudicate abstract disagreements about agency management of compliance.

## The Distinction Between Statutory Interpretation and Constitutional Adjudication in Administrative Suits

The appellate court's reasoning distinguishes between the analytic tools required for constitutional adjudication versus statutory interpretation. At the motion-to-dismiss stage, the court applied Article III constitutional standards to strip the plaintiff of the right to pursue a statutory cause of action. Because AFL could not prove Article III standing, the panel stated explicitly that they need not decide whether the injuries were procedural or substantive, nor do they need to interpret whether section 552(a)(4)(F)(i) limits OSC power under section 1216. This illustrates the judicial economy principle that constitutional jurisdictional bars trump statutory interpretation questions. In administrative suits, courts remain "gatekeepers," ensuring that disputes are sufficiently concrete before engaging in complex statutory construction. The decision emphasizes that a plaintiff cannot use a court to resolve statutory disputes regarding administrative enforcement mechanisms if the complaint itself fails to cross the constitutional threshold of a tangible injury, regardless of how clear the statutory text might appear to be.

## Rejection of the Specific FOIA Injury Theory Versus Procedural Injury Theory as Equally Failing to Meet Standing

The opinion analyzes two distinct standing theories to demonstrate their respective failures to meet Article III requirements. AFL argued first that the failure to investigate prevented them from receiving records denied by the Department of Justice under FOIA. The court rejected this because the informational injury was caused by DOJ, not OSC, and the link was too speculative—that OSC would investigate, then MSPB would sanction, then DOJ would comply. Citing recent precedent, the court held that causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the choices made by independent actors not before the courts. Second, AFL argued it suffered an injury because OSC would issue notices required under section 1214. The court rejected this by finding that OSC sections 1216 investigations do not legally entail providing notice requirements. The opinion explicitly states that AFL does not contend OSC must engraft section 1214's procedural requirements onto section 1216, meaning there was no legally protected interest in the notice itself. Both theories failed: the first due to broken causation (too many speculative actors), and the second due to a lack of entitlement to the specific procedural benefit requested.
