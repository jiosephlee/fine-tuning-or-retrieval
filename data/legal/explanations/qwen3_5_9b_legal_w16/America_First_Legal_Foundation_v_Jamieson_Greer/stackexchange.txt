Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### When does a 'procedural right in vacuo' fail to confer Article III standing?
Question:
I'm reading the second section where the court discusses Article III standing. The court cites *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.* to say that a 'procedural right in vacuo' is insufficient. AFL argues it has a procedural right to an investigation under § 1216. How does this doctrine work specifically when the defendant (OSC) is statutorily mandated to investigate FOIA violations, but the plaintiff's injury is non-concrete? Does the 'concrete interest' requirement mean the plaintiff must have a private concrete interest unrelated to the procedural right itself?
Answer:
### Answer

The doctrine of the “procedural right in vacuo,” derived from *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. 488 (2009), dictates that a statutory procedural right alone cannot confer Article III standing unless it protects a threatened "concrete interest." Under the analysis in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, 2025 WL ______ (D.C. Cir. Oct. 3, 2025), a plaintiff must show that the procedural violation resulted in injury to a concrete interest; a mere procedural deprivations without a protected underlying substantive interest is insufficient.

In *America First*, the Court applied this standard by rejecting two distinct theories of injury alleged by AFL to demonstrate why OSC’s failure to investigate was a "procedural right in vacuo."

**1. Procedural Rights Must Protect Concrete Interests**
The opinion emphasizes that while courts "relax—while not wholly eliminate[ing]—the issues of imminence and redressability," plaintiffs still must show the procedural violation resulted in injury to a "concrete, particularized interest." *Cf. Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ.*, 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005). It is not enough to show a procedural violation (OSC denying an investigation under § 1216); AFL had to show that the procedure itself protected an interest that OSC’s violation concretely harmed.

**2. Rejection of Interest in Information Disclosure**
AFL argued OSC’s refusal violated a concrete interest in receiving FOIA records. The Court rejected this because the informational injury was caused by DOJ, not OSC, and OSC’s alleged investigation would only materially affect the outcome through a speculative chain of events:
> "This requires speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit (the MSPB and DOJ)... And it requires speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion... AFL has not shown that a favorable judgment here ‘would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress’ its informational injury."

Because the chain of causation was too speculative, and redressability depends on how a judgment binds the parties rather than persuading independent actors, the procedural right failed to protect a concrete interest traceable to OSC.

**3. Rejection of Interest in Investigation Notices**
AFL also claimed an informational interest in the notices and information OSC would provide during a § 1214-style investigation. However, the Court found this alleged interest was not concrete. The opinion noted that AFL did not contend that OSC must give notice under § 1214 for a § 1216 investigation, acknowledging that such decisions remain "discretionary" per *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*, 633 F.3d 487 (6th Cir. 2011). Without a statutory entitlement to the information, the alleged injury was intangible and could not support standing.

**Direct Answer to Your Question**
The requirement of a "private concrete interest" does not mean the interest must be unrelated to the procedural right, but rather that the procedural right must protect a concrete interest that is *actually affected* by the violation. In *America First*, the *procedural* right (§ 1216 investigation) was found to protect neither the requested records (because the injury flowed from DOJ) nor the investigation notices (because they were discretionary). Thus, standing failed because the procedural right stood alone, not protecting any specific, enforceable concrete interest of AFL.

### Why is the causal chain between an OSC investigation and DOJ record production considered too speculative?
Question:
In the second standing theory analysis, the court outlines a speculative chain: OSC investigates -> OSC seeks corrective action -> MSPB sanctions employees -> DOJ produces records. The court cites *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.* to dismiss this. If OSC is the defendant in the lawsuit, why does the court allow the possibility of DOE/MSPB discretion to break the causal chain? Does a violation of the law (in DFA investigation) need to compel the other parties to act immediately, and does OSC lack the enforcement power to do so unilaterally?
Answer:
Great question, though it is important to note a little confusion in the premise: in the opinion provided, this specific chain of causation regarding DOJ record production is actually analyzed under the **first standing theory**, not the second. The first theory concerns the plaintiff's interest in obtaining records from DOJ, while the second theory concerns informational interests in the OSC investigation itself. The majority opinion dismisses the first standing theory because it suffers from fatal causation and redressability problems, citing the very speculative chain you describe.

Here is why the court finds this causal chain too speculative to satisfy Article III standing requirements.

**1. The Necessity of Concrete Causation and Redressability**
To establish Article III standing, the plaintiff must show the injury was caused by the defendant and is likely to be redressed by a favorable decision (*Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife*). Where standing is based on a procedural violation of a statutory right, courts relax the standard for imminence and redressability, but not for actual injury or causation (*Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*). In this case, AFL claims that OSC’s failure to investigate DOJ created a sexual injury (roughly caused by DOJ withholding records) and an information injury (lack of investigation reports). The court is required to extend the chain of causation only as far as judicial relief could reach the defendant (*Haaland v. Brackeen*).

**2. Independent Actors and Speculative Chains (*FDA*)**
The speculative nature of the chain breaks at the point where control leaves the defendant. The opinion explicitly notes that if OSC investigates, it might seek corrective action, but then the MSPB might sanction employees. Finally, DOJ might produce records. The court finds this speculative because it requires predictions about "unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts"—specifically the MSPB and DOJ—which are government agencies not subject to the remedy sought against OSC. As the opinion cites *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.*, "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the choices made by independent actors not before the courts." A judgment ordering OSC to investigate cannot bind or compel MSPB or DOJ to act.

**3. OSC’s Discretion and the Limits of Enforcement (*Heckler*)**
You asked why the court allows the possibility of discretion to break the chain. The answer lies in the nature of OSC's statutory authority. While OSC is a defendant, it is an investigative agency whose enforcement power is not unilateral. The court cites *Heckler v. Chaney* to support the principle that courts "generally may not compel [OSC's] enforcement discretion." Because OSC is not required to pursue corrective action unilaterally, a court order mandating an investigation does not guarantee that OSC will agree to seek sanctions or discipline. If OSC retains the discretion to investigate without seeking sanctions, the plaintiff cannot show that a judgment here would redress the alleged injury.

**4. Redressability Turns on Binding the Parties (*Haaland*)**
Finally, the court notes that redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether the rationale of the judgment "might persuade actors who are not before the court" (*Haaland v. Brackeen*). Even if OSC is required to investigate under 5 U.S.C. § 1216, a court order mandating that investigation does not bind MSPB or DOJ. AFL's plea that OSC must immediately compel DOJ to produce records ignores that OSC lacks the unilateral power to do so and that the relief required (sanctions leading to production) involves steps outside the court's reach over OSC alone.

In short, the court allows the possibility of MSPB and DOE discretion breaking the chain because Article III standing prohibits speculative attribution of cause to independent actors. Since the OSC lacks the unilateral enforcement power to bind DOJ or the MSPB without the specific triggers (like a court order or a proactive choice) that OSC can decline to exercise, the chain remains too attenuated to cure the lack of redressability.

### Why does OSC's discretion over § 1214 notice requirements in § 1216 cases prevent informational standing?
Question:
AFL's second claim rests on the idea that it is 'informationally entitled' to OSC notices if OSC were investigating under § 1214. However, the court notes OSC's discretion to ignore § 1214's procedural requirements when acting under § 1216. If OSC has discretion to not provide notices, AFL argues it has no injury. Why does having the *possibility* of not receiving notes constitute no injury of fact? If the statute mandates OSC to investigate, shouldn't the potential realization of rights be the injury?
Answer:
The bottom line is that while § 1216 may mandate an *investigation*, it does not mandate adherence to § 1214's *notice procedures*, because that discretion prevents a concrete informational interest.

To establish "informational standing"—standing based on being denied information a statute entitled you to receive—a plaintiff must demonstrate a statutory entitlement to that specific information. *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see *America First Legal Found. v. Greer*, 2025 WL ___, at *11 (D.C. Cir. Oct. 3, 2025). The District of Columbia Circuit held in this case that OSC’s decision to apply § 1214 notice requirements when acting under § 1216 is discretionary, meaning no absolute entitlement exists for those notices.

Here is why that discretion kills your standing argument:

### 1. The "Process" is Discretionary, Not Mandatory
You correctly note that § 1216(a)(3) states OSC "shall ... conduct an investigation." However, § 1216(c) dictates the *manner* of the investigation: OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action... in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." While this suggests § 1214 procedures could apply, the *Carson* precedent cited in the opinion clarifies that applying § 1214's procedural requirements in a § 1216 context is at OSC's "discretion." *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*, 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011).

This creates a crucial distinction: Soliciting information (investigation) is different from the procedure used to solicit it (notice requirements). Because § 1216(c) creates no statutory "mandate" for the provision of § 1214 notices itself, OSC can lawfully skip them.

### 2. Informational Standing Requires a Legal Right to Information
A plaintiff claiming informational injury must show they have been denied information they have a "legally protected right" to. *America First Legal Found.*, 2025 WL ___, at *11* (citing *Pub. Citizen v. DOJ*, 491 U.S. if 440, 448–50 (1989)). If OSC has the discretion to ignore § 1214 notices, AFL has no concrete, particularized right to those notices in the first place. As the opinion states:
> "Absent any alleged entitlement to receive information under section 1214, AFL lacks a concrete injury sufficient to support its second standing theory." *America First Legal Found.*, 2025 WL ___, at *11*.

### 3. Redressability Depends on a Controllable Outcome
Finally, even if AFL had a vague interest in the investigation, articulating a right to the *outcome* of that investigation (notice) fails the redressability prong. Article III requires that a favorable judgment be "reasonably likely to redress" the injury. *America First Legal Found.*, 2025 WL ___, at *11* (citing *Hecate Energy*, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025)).

A judicial order compelling OSC to investigate under § 1216 would not redress the alleged injury (lack of notices) because OSC retains discretion to deny those notices regardless of the investigation mandate. The opinion explicitly notes:
> "Likewise, insofar as OSC need not provide section 1214 notice requirements in a section 1216 investigation, a judicial order mandating such an investigation would not be reasonably likely to redress AFL’s second alleged injury." *America First Legal Found.*, 2025 WL ___, at *11*.

In short, since the agency retains the choice to withhold the notices, their absence is not the violation of a tangible right. Without a right to the notice, there is no concrete informational injury, and without an injury, there is no standing.

### How does the *Hecate Energy* test on redressability apply to this standing claim?
Question:
The opinion references *Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC* (2025) regarding redressability, stating a judgment must 'very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress' the injury. We've previously seen redressability arguments where the defendant has the discretion to act. Here, if OSC wins or loses, a court order 'may compel next steps' by OSC, but OSC's discretion hides between OSC and the redressable injury. Is *Hecate Energy* clarifying that courts cannot force OSC to use enforcement powers against other agencies?
Answer:
Yes, your analysis is correct. The opinion in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* applies the *Hecate Energy* redressability test to hold that a judgment compelling OSC to investigate would not satisfy Article III standing because the chain of causation relies on "unfettered choices made by independent actors" and OSC's own discretionary authority.

Here is how the opinion grounds its application of *Hecate Energy* in the text provided:

### 1. The *Hecate Energy* Standard
The opinion explicitly adopts a stringent redressability standard, quoting *Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC*, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025): a plaintiff must show that a favorable judgment "would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress" their injury. Under this test, the *Greer* court found that AFL failed to prove the likelihood of redress.

### 2. Application to OSC’s Discretion
Your observation regarding OSC's discretion is central to the court's reasoning. The door is not "open" just because OSC statutorily has the power to investigate; the court considers OSC's enforcement power to be discretionary.

The opinion cites *Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. 821 (1985), noting it requires courts to refrain from compelling an agency to exercise its enforcement powers. Regarding AFL's first standing theory (that an OSC investigation would lead to DOJ producing records), the court states:
> "And it requires speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel."

This confirms that *Greer* aligns with the principle that a judicial order cannot force OSC to utilize its investigative powers if those powers are discretionary.

### 3. Reliance on Independent Actors
Furthermore, the court notes that even if OSC acted, the causal chain would likely stall at other agencies (DOJ and MSPB). The opinion explicitly states:
> "Given this speculative chain, AFL has not shown that a favorable judgment here 'would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress' its informational injury."

The court relies on *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.*, 602 U.S. 367 (2024), which holds that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." Since DOJ and MSPB are not parties to the lawsuit, a judgment against OSC cannot redress injuries to DOJ withholding records or MSPB sanctions.

### 4. The Second Injury Claim (Information from OSC)
The court applies the same logic to AFL's second theory: that OSC investigates an FOIA withholding but fails to provide subsequent notices or information under section 1214. Even if an investigation were mandatory under section 1216, the court notes that OSC's decision regarding section 1214 notice requirements is discretionary. Therefore, "a judicial order mandating such an investigation would not be reasonably likely to redress AFL’s second alleged injury."

### Conclusion
The *Hecate Energy* standard does not clarify that courts cannot force OSC to use enforcement powers; rather, it clarifies that they cannot force OSC to use those powers in a way that relies on **discretionary choices** by OSC or **unfettered choices** by non-parties (like the MSPB or DOJ). Because the chain from "OSC investigation" to "redressed injury" depends on these unfettered choices, *Hecate Energy* forbids the conclusion that the plaintiff would be redressed by a judgment.

### Why do standing issues preclude the court from ruling on the substantive 'shall' language in 5 U.S.C. § 1216?
Question:
The district court had already ruled that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC power under § 1216. The appellate court said it didn't need to decide this because AFL lacks standing. If OSC is statutorily forbidden from refusing to investigate, would AFL have a cause of action? Is the court preempting its own interpretation of the statute because standing doctrine is a jurisdictional limit? Why is the statutory interpretation not enough to grant standing if I have the right to relief?
Answer:
**TL;DR:** Standing is a constitutional requirement that exists *separate* from statutory interpretation. Even if a statute contains mandatory language like "shall," the Constitution requires a concrete injury, causation, and redressability before a court will entertain the case. In this opinion, the D.C. Circuit ruled that regardless of what § 1216 means, AFL could not show that OSC's decision not to investigate caused AFL a redressable injury. Because the court lacks jurisdiction to hear the case, it cannot rule on the substantive "shall" language until the standing barriers are cleared.

### Standing is a Jurisdictional Threshold

The short answer to your question is that **standing is the key to unlocking the courtroom door.** While 5 U.S.C. § 1216 may contain a "shall" command, Article III standing is a constitutional prerequisite that controls whether a court can exercise its power at all.

As the D.C. Circuit explains in *America First Legal Foundation v. Greer*, standing doctrine follows from the "Case or Controversy" requirement of Article III, § 2. A plaintiff must demonstrate three elements: (1) a concrete injury in fact; (2) a causal connection between the injury and the defendant; and (3) redressability (the likelihood that a favorable decision will fix the problem). *Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992). These are constitutional hurdles, not just hurdles in statutory construction. Even if the statute grants you a procedural right, that does not grant you a "right to relief against the government" absent a constitutional case.

### The Opinion’s Application of Standing Doctrine

In *Am. First Legal Found. v. Greer*, the court rejected AFL’s two theories of injury. This demonstrates why the substantive "shall" discussion is irrelevant at this stage.

**1. Causation and Redressability are Aggregate Barriers**
AFL argued that OSC’s refusal to investigate under § 1216 caused them to harm because DOJ withheld information. However, the court found this causal chain too speculative. As the opinion notes:
> "Any informational injury from DOJ’s refusal to disclose agency records is plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC... This requires speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit (the MSPB and DOJ)... and it requires speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion... which the courts generally may not compel."

The court applied the principle that causation cannot rely on speculation about independent actors (*FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*). Therefore, even if § 1216 said "shall," a ruling compelling OSC to investigate would not necessarily result in AFL getting the FOIA records they wanted. Because a favorable judgment would not redress the specific injury claimed, standing fails.

**2. Procedural Rights Cannot Exist in a Vacuum**
AFL also argued that OSC deprived them of a procedural right to an investigation. The court clarified that a procedural right cannot grant standing without a substantive interest:
> "Where plaintiffs allege injury resulting from violation of a procedural right... the injury must be... concrete, particularized interest." *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009).

AFL acknowledged cases holding that OSC has discretion under § 1216 regarding whether to follow § 1214 notice requirements (*Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*). Because AFL had no established entitlement to receive information under § 1216 investigations, they lacked the "concrete injury" necessary to trigger standing.

### Why Statutory Interpretation Alone Does Not Grant Standing

You asked if having a "right to relief" under the statute was enough to grant standing. The answer is generally **no**.

In this opinion, the core issue is that "shall" language defines the *scope of the statute’s obligation*, not the scope of the judiciary’s authority. The court explicitly reserved the substantive question:
> "Because AFL lacks Article III standing, we vacate the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction."

The appellate court did not decide whether § 1216(a)(3) mandates an investigation regarding FOIA violations. It refused to decide because the Constitutional threshold for the lawsuit—Article III standing—had not been met. The Supreme Court has long held that writs of prohibition (standing) prevent questions about issue construction from being heard. *Citizens for Const. Integrity v. Census Bureau*, 115 F.4th 618, 625 (D.C. Cir. 2024).

### Bottom Line for Students

The distinction you need to keep in mind is:
1.  **Standing (Providing the Keys):** Is there a concrete injury, did the defendant cause it, and can a win fix it? (Article III)
2.  **Merits (Entering the House):** Does the statute actually say "shall"? What is the agency's authority?

Because the D.C. Circuit found that AFL could not prove the keys fit the lock (standing), they did not need to ask the agency how the statute works (substantive interpretation).

### What is the relationship between 'independent actors' and OSC's role in redressing FOIA requests?
Question:
The court repeatedly mentions that DOJ cannot be bound by OSC's judgment. The text cites 'principles' from *Haaland v. Brackeen* (2023) regarding injunctive relief not binding non-parties. Since ACJ is suing OSC, not DOJ, does this mean OSC's enforcement power is limited to internal discipline? Does the absence of oversight power over DOJ create the 'redressability' barrier, or does the lack of private enforcement against a federal body create a constitutional limit on adjudication generally?
Answer:
This concern gets at the heart of the Article III standing analysis in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, specifically regarding the redressability prong. The court holds that while OSC has a statutory role investigating FOIA violations, a lawsuit to compel OSC cannot redress an injury caused by the federal agency (DOJ) because the relief sought cannot bind the DOJ in this context.

The relationship between "independent actors" and OSC's enforcement power is defined by the limitations of Article III standing, not necessarily by OSC's statutory authority itself. As Judge Katsas explains, the plaintiff (AFL) contends that OSC’s failure to investigate under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 deprived them of FOIA records. However, the court finds that any informational injury from the withholding of records is "plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC." Because the injury stems from DOJ's withholding, a favorable judgment here (compelling OSC to investigate) does not constitute redress unless that judgment can compel DOJ to produce the records.

The opinion addresses your specific question about the "redressability barrier" through the lens of independent actors and the binding nature of judgments. The court cites *Haaland v. Brackeen* to clarify a fundamental limitation: "redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale 'might persuade actors who are not before the court.'" In this case, DOJ is an "independent actor" not a party to the lawsuit involving OSC.

Consequently, the court ceases from a chain of speculation to establish causation. Even if OSC were to initiate an investigation, that could lead to an action by the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB), which could then *potentially* lead to DOJ producing the records. Judge Katsas notes that such a reliance on choices by independent actors makes the causal connection "too speculative and attenuated." To support this, the opinion relies on *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine, 602 U.S. 367*, holding that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts."

Therefore, the "redressability barrier" you identify is the constitutional limit of the court's authority to adjudicate a case. It is not merely a question of OSC's internal power, but an Article III limitation preventing a court from using its judgment over OSC to remedy an injury inflicted by the DOJ. Since the court cannot force DOJ to produce records in this suit, the judgment cannot remedy AFL's alleged injury, resulting in a dismissal for lack of standing.
