Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### When does a 'procedural right in vacuo' fail to confer Article III standing?
Question:
I'm reading the second section where the court discusses Article III standing. The court cites *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.* to say that a 'procedural right in vacuo' is insufficient. AFL argues it has a procedural right to an investigation under § 1216. How does this doctrine work specifically when the defendant (OSC) is statutorily mandated to investigate FOIA violations, but the plaintiff's injury is non-concrete? Does the 'concrete interest' requirement mean the plaintiff must have a private concrete interest unrelated to the procedural right itself?
Answer:
### Answer

The doctrine of the “procedural right in vacuo,” derived from *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. 488 (2009), dictates that a statutory procedural right alone cannot confer Article III standing unless it protects a threatened "concrete interest." Under the analysis in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, 2025 WL ______ (D.C. Cir. Oct. 3, 2025), a plaintiff must show that the procedural violation resulted in injury to a concrete interest; a mere procedural deprivations without a protected underlying substantive interest is insufficient.

In *America First*, the Court applied this standard by rejecting two distinct theories of injury alleged by AFL to demonstrate why OSC’s failure to investigate was a "procedural right in vacuo."

**1. Procedural Rights Must Protect Concrete Interests**
The opinion emphasizes that while courts "relax—while not wholly eliminate[ing]—the issues of imminence and redressability," plaintiffs still must show the procedural violation resulted in injury to a "concrete, particularized interest." *Cf. Ctr. for Law & Educ. v. Dep’t of Educ.*, 396 F.3d 1152, 1157 (D.C. Cir. 2005). It is not enough to show a procedural violation (OSC denying an investigation under § 1216); AFL had to show that the procedure itself protected an interest that OSC’s violation concretely harmed.

**2. Rejection of Interest in Information Disclosure**
AFL argued OSC’s refusal violated a concrete interest in receiving FOIA records. The Court rejected this because the informational injury was caused by DOJ, not OSC, and OSC’s alleged investigation would only materially affect the outcome through a speculative chain of events:
> "This requires speculation about choices by two government agencies not parties to this lawsuit (the MSPB and DOJ)... And it requires speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion... AFL has not shown that a favorable judgment here ‘would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress’ its informational injury."

Because the chain of causation was too speculative, and redressability depends on how a judgment binds the parties rather than persuading independent actors, the procedural right failed to protect a concrete interest traceable to OSC.

**3. Rejection of Interest in Investigation Notices**
AFL also claimed an informational interest in the notices and information OSC would provide during a § 1214-style investigation. However, the Court found this alleged interest was not concrete. The opinion noted that AFL did not contend that OSC must give notice under § 1214 for a § 1216 investigation, acknowledging that such decisions remain "discretionary" per *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*, 633 F.3d 487 (6th Cir. 2011). Without a statutory entitlement to the information, the alleged injury was intangible and could not support standing.

**Direct Answer to Your Question**
The requirement of a "private concrete interest" does not mean the interest must be unrelated to the procedural right, but rather that the procedural right must protect a concrete interest that is *actually affected* by the violation. In *America First*, the *procedural* right (§ 1216 investigation) was found to protect neither the requested records (because the injury flowed from DOJ) nor the investigation notices (because they were discretionary). Thus, standing failed because the procedural right stood alone, not protecting any specific, enforceable concrete interest of AFL.
