Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### What is the relationship between 'independent actors' and OSC's role in redressing FOIA requests?
Question:
The court repeatedly mentions that DOJ cannot be bound by OSC's judgment. The text cites 'principles' from *Haaland v. Brackeen* (2023) regarding injunctive relief not binding non-parties. Since ACJ is suing OSC, not DOJ, does this mean OSC's enforcement power is limited to internal discipline? Does the absence of oversight power over DOJ create the 'redressability' barrier, or does the lack of private enforcement against a federal body create a constitutional limit on adjudication generally?
Answer:
This concern gets at the heart of the Article III standing analysis in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, specifically regarding the redressability prong. The court holds that while OSC has a statutory role investigating FOIA violations, a lawsuit to compel OSC cannot redress an injury caused by the federal agency (DOJ) because the relief sought cannot bind the DOJ in this context.

The relationship between "independent actors" and OSC's enforcement power is defined by the limitations of Article III standing, not necessarily by OSC's statutory authority itself. As Judge Katsas explains, the plaintiff (AFL) contends that OSC’s failure to investigate under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 deprived them of FOIA records. However, the court finds that any informational injury from the withholding of records is "plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC." Because the injury stems from DOJ's withholding, a favorable judgment here (compelling OSC to investigate) does not constitute redress unless that judgment can compel DOJ to produce the records.

The opinion addresses your specific question about the "redressability barrier" through the lens of independent actors and the binding nature of judgments. The court cites *Haaland v. Brackeen* to clarify a fundamental limitation: "redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not whether its rationale 'might persuade actors who are not before the court.'" In this case, DOJ is an "independent actor" not a party to the lawsuit involving OSC.

Consequently, the court ceases from a chain of speculation to establish causation. Even if OSC were to initiate an investigation, that could lead to an action by the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB), which could then *potentially* lead to DOJ producing the records. Judge Katsas notes that such a reliance on choices by independent actors makes the causal connection "too speculative and attenuated." To support this, the opinion relies on *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine, 602 U.S. 367*, holding that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts."

Therefore, the "redressability barrier" you identify is the constitutional limit of the court's authority to adjudicate a case. It is not merely a question of OSC's internal power, but an Article III limitation preventing a court from using its judgment over OSC to remedy an injury inflicted by the DOJ. Since the court cannot force DOJ to produce records in this suit, the judgment cannot remedy AFL's alleged injury, resulting in a dismissal for lack of standing.
