Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### Why does OSC's discretion over § 1214 notice requirements in § 1216 cases prevent informational standing?
Question:
AFL's second claim rests on the idea that it is 'informationally entitled' to OSC notices if OSC were investigating under § 1214. However, the court notes OSC's discretion to ignore § 1214's procedural requirements when acting under § 1216. If OSC has discretion to not provide notices, AFL argues it has no injury. Why does having the *possibility* of not receiving notes constitute no injury of fact? If the statute mandates OSC to investigate, shouldn't the potential realization of rights be the injury?
Answer:
The bottom line is that while § 1216 may mandate an *investigation*, it does not mandate adherence to § 1214's *notice procedures*, because that discretion prevents a concrete informational interest.

To establish "informational standing"—standing based on being denied information a statute entitled you to receive—a plaintiff must demonstrate a statutory entitlement to that specific information. *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. 488, 496 (2009); see *America First Legal Found. v. Greer*, 2025 WL ___, at *11 (D.C. Cir. Oct. 3, 2025). The District of Columbia Circuit held in this case that OSC’s decision to apply § 1214 notice requirements when acting under § 1216 is discretionary, meaning no absolute entitlement exists for those notices.

Here is why that discretion kills your standing argument:

### 1. The "Process" is Discretionary, Not Mandatory
You correctly note that § 1216(a)(3) states OSC "shall ... conduct an investigation." However, § 1216(c) dictates the *manner* of the investigation: OSC "may investigate and seek corrective action... in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved." While this suggests § 1214 procedures could apply, the *Carson* precedent cited in the opinion clarifies that applying § 1214's procedural requirements in a § 1216 context is at OSC's "discretion." *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*, 633 F.3d 487, 495–96 (6th Cir. 2011).

This creates a crucial distinction: Soliciting information (investigation) is different from the procedure used to solicit it (notice requirements). Because § 1216(c) creates no statutory "mandate" for the provision of § 1214 notices itself, OSC can lawfully skip them.

### 2. Informational Standing Requires a Legal Right to Information
A plaintiff claiming informational injury must show they have been denied information they have a "legally protected right" to. *America First Legal Found.*, 2025 WL ___, at *11* (citing *Pub. Citizen v. DOJ*, 491 U.S. if 440, 448–50 (1989)). If OSC has the discretion to ignore § 1214 notices, AFL has no concrete, particularized right to those notices in the first place. As the opinion states:
> "Absent any alleged entitlement to receive information under section 1214, AFL lacks a concrete injury sufficient to support its second standing theory." *America First Legal Found.*, 2025 WL ___, at *11*.

### 3. Redressability Depends on a Controllable Outcome
Finally, even if AFL had a vague interest in the investigation, articulating a right to the *outcome* of that investigation (notice) fails the redressability prong. Article III requires that a favorable judgment be "reasonably likely to redress" the injury. *America First Legal Found.*, 2025 WL ___, at *11* (citing *Hecate Energy*, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025)).

A judicial order compelling OSC to investigate under § 1216 would not redress the alleged injury (lack of notices) because OSC retains discretion to deny those notices regardless of the investigation mandate. The opinion explicitly notes:
> "Likewise, insofar as OSC need not provide section 1214 notice requirements in a section 1216 investigation, a judicial order mandating such an investigation would not be reasonably likely to redress AFL’s second alleged injury." *America First Legal Found.*, 2025 WL ___, at *11*.

In short, since the agency retains the choice to withhold the notices, their absence is not the violation of a tangible right. Without a right to the notice, there is no concrete informational injury, and without an injury, there is no standing.
