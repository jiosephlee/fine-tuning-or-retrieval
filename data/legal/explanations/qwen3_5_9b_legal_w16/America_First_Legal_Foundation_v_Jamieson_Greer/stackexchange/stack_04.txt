Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

### How does the *Hecate Energy* test on redressability apply to this standing claim?
Question:
The opinion references *Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC* (2025) regarding redressability, stating a judgment must 'very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress' the injury. We've previously seen redressability arguments where the defendant has the discretion to act. Here, if OSC wins or loses, a court order 'may compel next steps' by OSC, but OSC's discretion hides between OSC and the redressable injury. Is *Hecate Energy* clarifying that courts cannot force OSC to use enforcement powers against other agencies?
Answer:
Yes, your analysis is correct. The opinion in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* applies the *Hecate Energy* redressability test to hold that a judgment compelling OSC to investigate would not satisfy Article III standing because the chain of causation relies on "unfettered choices made by independent actors" and OSC's own discretionary authority.

Here is how the opinion grounds its application of *Hecate Energy* in the text provided:

### 1. The *Hecate Energy* Standard
The opinion explicitly adopts a stringent redressability standard, quoting *Hecate Energy LLC v. FERC*, 126 F.4th 660, 667 (D.C. Cir. 2025): a plaintiff must show that a favorable judgment "would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress" their injury. Under this test, the *Greer* court found that AFL failed to prove the likelihood of redress.

### 2. Application to OSC’s Discretion
Your observation regarding OSC's discretion is central to the court's reasoning. The door is not "open" just because OSC statutorily has the power to investigate; the court considers OSC's enforcement power to be discretionary.

The opinion cites *Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. 821 (1985), noting it requires courts to refrain from compelling an agency to exercise its enforcement powers. Regarding AFL's first standing theory (that an OSC investigation would lead to DOJ producing records), the court states:
> "And it requires speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel."

This confirms that *Greer* aligns with the principle that a judicial order cannot force OSC to utilize its investigative powers if those powers are discretionary.

### 3. Reliance on Independent Actors
Furthermore, the court notes that even if OSC acted, the causal chain would likely stall at other agencies (DOJ and MSPB). The opinion explicitly states:
> "Given this speculative chain, AFL has not shown that a favorable judgment here 'would very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress' its informational injury."

The court relies on *FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med.*, 602 U.S. 367 (2024), which holds that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." Since DOJ and MSPB are not parties to the lawsuit, a judgment against OSC cannot redress injuries to DOJ withholding records or MSPB sanctions.

### 4. The Second Injury Claim (Information from OSC)
The court applies the same logic to AFL's second theory: that OSC investigates an FOIA withholding but fails to provide subsequent notices or information under section 1214. Even if an investigation were mandatory under section 1216, the court notes that OSC's decision regarding section 1214 notice requirements is discretionary. Therefore, "a judicial order mandating such an investigation would not be reasonably likely to redress AFL’s second alleged injury."

### Conclusion
The *Hecate Energy* standard does not clarify that courts cannot force OSC to use enforcement powers; rather, it clarifies that they cannot force OSC to use those powers in a way that relies on **discretionary choices** by OSC or **unfettered choices** by non-parties (like the MSPB or DOJ). Because the chain from "OSC investigation" to "redressed injury" depends on these unfettered choices, *Hecate Energy* forbids the conclusion that the plaintiff would be redressed by a judgment.
