# America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer: The 'Procedural Right in a Vacuum' Doctrine in Action

Katsas Circuit Judge’s decision in *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* offers a potent reminder that statutory mandates for administrative oversight do not bypass the rigors of Article III standing. The D.C. Circuit has long served as a balking gatekeeper for agencies and plaintiffs, particularly in cases where the plaintiff seeks relief from a federal agency based on the actions of another. Here, the D.C. Circuit held that the America First Legal Foundation (AFL) lacked Article III standing to compel the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate the Department of Justice’s handling of a Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request. While the litigation involves the OSC’s statutory mandate to investigate arbitrary withholdings under 5 U.S.C. § 1216, the D.C. Circuit concluded that the plaintiffs’ claims failed because the relief sought was too conjectural. This decision underscores a strict application of the *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* principle that a "procedural right in vacuo" is insufficient to confer standing, effectively dampening third-party efforts to police agency transparency through judicial enforcement of administrative procedures.

## The Statutory Mandate and the Standing Barrier

The case arose from a dispute over how the DOJ handled emails regarding attorney recusal. AFL first sought these records under FOIA. While the DOJ eventually produced the records, AFL remained dissatisfied with the agency’s stated policy of not searching employee emails without consent. Consequently, AFL turned to the Office of Special Counsel, the independent agency tasked with ensuring a fair Civil Service. Under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), OSC is empowered to investigate alleged arbitrary or capricious withholdings under FOIA.

Influenced by the lack of disciplinary action in the FOIA proceedings, AFL argued that OSC had an affirmative duty to launch an investigation under § 1216, regardless of whether the court ordered production in the underlying FOIA suit. The district court agreed that OSC may have acted incorrectly in refusing to investigate but dismissed AFL’s suit, remanding for reconsideration. The District Court essentially held that while OSC might have erred, the decision to investigate remained within OSC’s discretion. When OSC declined to investigate again, AFL returned to court. While the D.C. Circuit did not reach the merits of whether OSC had a statutory duty to investigate under § 1216, it concluded that AFL lacked the constitutional standing to bring the case at all.

The court applied the strictures of *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env't*, emphasizing that standing is strictly jurisdictional. Judge Katsas noted the two pathologies at play: first, that AFL claimed the OSC violated a procedural right, invoking the *Summers* doctrine of procedural standing; second, that the relief sought relied on speculative chains of causation where OSC’s decision would influence the choices of independent actors. The court identified the core constitutional hurdle as the "injury in fact" requirement, insisting that the injury must be concrete and particularized, not conjectural.

## Procedural Rights and Redressability

Central to the opinion is the rejection of AFL’s primary argument that the violation of a statutory right to an investigation constituted a concrete injury. The opinion relies on the *Summers* test, which recognizes that a procedural right alone is insufficient without a concrete interest that is affected by the deprivation. The court characterized AFL’s claim as a "procedural right in a vacuo," a phrase that did the heavy lifting for the court’s rejection of the standing claims.

Judge Katsas analyzed two specific theories AFL might use to establish standing. The first claimed that OSC’s failure to investigate prevented AFL from receiving the DOJ records in the first place. The court dismantled this theory based on causation and redressability principles, noting that the informational injury regarding the withheld records was traceable to the DOJ, not OSC. Furthermore, even if OSC had investigated, there would be a speculative link between OSC’s investigation and DOJ eventually complying with FOIA. Any causal chain requiring speculation about the "unfettered choices" of the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) or the DOJ, or how OSC exercises enforcement discretion, is generally insufficient to create standing. The opinion cites *Heckler v. Chaney*, reminding readers that administrative discretion rarely supports judgments that can redress private grievances absent a clear entitlement.

The second theory relied on the fruits of the OSC investigation itself. AFL argued that an investigation would provide them with notices required under § 1214, which AFC claims is a procedural right. However, the court found that § 1216 investigations do not create an entitlement to follow the procedural notice requirements mandated for personnel practice investigations under § 1214. Consequently, the court found no concrete injury because OSC was never statutorily obligated to provide those specific notifications in a section 1216 context.

## Doctrinal Implications and Future Litigation

The holding in *Jamieson Greer* has significant downstream effects for transparency advocates and organizations relying on OSC’s watchdog function. By treating procedural violations without concrete injuries as jurisdictionally barred, the D.C. Circuit forecloses a specific litigation strategy where plaintiffs bypass direct agency accountability by pushing OSC investigations. It forces plaintiffs to seek relief directly against the withholding agency—often the DOJ in cases like this—rather than relying on OSC as an intermediary enforcement mechanism. This suggests that claims based purely on the violation of a statute’s procedural mandate, even one that sounds mandatory like "shall conduct an investigation," may be too attenuated to satisfy the rigorous *Lujan* causation inquiry.

Practically, this decision will likely curb the ability of third-party organizations to enforce institutional transparency mandates through procedural standing alone. Litigants attempting to compel administrative agencies to "work harder" on policy issues or withholdings will find themselves at the mercy of standing doctrine if they cannot demonstrate a direct, non-speculative injury. The court’s reliance on *Summers* and *All. v. Hippocratic Med.* reinforces a high bar for redressability where the injury is derivative of another agency’s non-participation or independent decision-making.

Ultimately, *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* reasserts the separation of powers' boundaries on judicial enforcement of agency discretion. While OSC possesses broad powers under 5 U.S.C. § 1216 to investigate civil service misconduct, the D.C. Circuit confirms that this power cannot be weaponized by third parties through the courts unless a concrete injury is directly tied to the OSC’s refusal to act. For the doctrine of standing, this leaves a nagging question: how much power does Congress intend to grant OSC if the agency can ignore the public interest? Yet, until standing is resolved, such questions remain purely political rather than judicial. The D.C. Circuit has drawn the line: Article III limits access even to the most robust enforcement mechanisms.



# Speculative Causation as the New Redressability Standard: Learning from FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine

For the plaintiffs challenging agency inaction, the road to the federal courts has just become significantly narrower. In *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the D.C. Circuit offered a stinging rejection of Article III standing that serves as a cautionary tale for would-be litigants relying on procedural triggers to access judicial review. America First Legal Foundation (AFL) sought to compel the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate the Justice Department’s arbitrary handling of FOIA requests. While the D.C. Circuit explicitly declined to decide the merits of OSC’s alleged statutory error, the court disposed of the entire case on standing grounds, utilizing a particularly tight reading of *Steel Co.* and a new, post-*FDA* reliance on redressability doctrine.

The decision matters because it tightens the "speculative causation" stringency in the administrative context. For years, plaintiffs have argued that procedural violations of statutes like FOIA create standing if they are sufficiently tied to a concrete interest. The district court had already found OSC made a legal error by declining to investigate, but the appellate court summarized the sweep of administrative injuries by declaring the causal chain too risky. By pivoting heavily on *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, the court signaled that any remedial path relying on another branch of government remains unbound by the court’s power.

## The Speculative Chain of Lay Outcomes

The crux of the opinion lies in how the court analyzed the first prong of AFL’s standing claim: the desire to receive the DOJ records they sought in the first place. AFL argued that without an OSC investigation, there would be no subsequent disciplinary action, and without discipline, DOJ might not release the documents. This created a three-step chain of potential events: OSC investigates, the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) sanctions employees, and DOJ then releases the records.

The D.C. Circuit dismantled this chain using a recent holding from *FDA*. In *FDA*, the Supreme Court had emphasized that "causation generally cannot rely on speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts." Judge Katsas applied this to the administrative dance between agency inaction and congressional enforcement mechanisms. An order against OSC does not bind the MSPB, nor does it bind DOJ. Because the ultimate goal (getting the records) depends on the choices of two independent actors not before the court—the MSPB and DOJ—the redressability requirement fails.

This reliance on *FDA* is significant. The court did not just note that other actors were involved; it treated their "unfettered choices" as a jurisdictional cutoff. Even if OSC is legally bound to start a § 1216 investigation, the heating of facts required to make the final redress compelling cannot be guessed. The court wrote that AFL asks the court to speculate "about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion, which the courts generally may not compel." In a procedural standing case, this creates a double barrier. The violation of the procedural right must be linked to a concrete injury, but then the redress of that right cannot bounce off another agency that isn't bound by the judgment.

## Doctrinal Implications for Procedure-Based Claims

Beyond the statutory mechanics of the OSC case, the doctrinal reach of this decision concerns the "procedural right" strand of standing. *Summers v. Earth Island Institute* established that violating a procedural right without a concrete interest is insufficient. AFL argued that the statutory requirement for an OSC investigation created the procedural right that could save its standing claim.

Judge Katsas confirmed that *Summers* still applies in the formative sense that ":". Of course, he noted that mere violations of a procedural rule don't count. However, the court noted that the informational interest supposed to underpin that right—notice of investigation status—was not even guaranteed by the statutory text. This leaves litigants with a conceptual bottleneck: they need a concrete interest to be protected, but they cannot raise that interest if the only defendant before the court (OSC) cannot control the outcome of that interest.

This creates a particularly troubling下游 effect for administrative law lawyers. In cases where statutes grant investigative powers to agencies but leave the ultimate remedy to a board or another agency, standing becomes nearly impossible to plead. The court's refusal to reframe the statutory scheme implies that plaintiffs must have a direct line of causation from the defendant to the injury, or the court refuses to receive the case. This disallows the court to act as a review forum for "programmatic" grievances where the agency is simply one link in a bureaucracy.

## Downstream Effects on Agency Accountability

The ramifications of *AFL v. Jamieson Greer* extend beyond the OSC and FOIA world. If the D.C. Circuit holds that redressability depends on "independent actors not before the court," it severely impacts plaintiffs who want to hold mid-level agencies accountable for broader patterns of misconduct. In *FDA v. Alliance*, the court found that federal judges are often powerless to dictate the outcome of executive actions when the remedy flows through other agencies. That precedent now sweeps broadly across the administrative state. It suggests that without a direct mandate to the defendant agency, claims must rely on the defendant agency's own leverage over their clients, supervisors, or witnesses.

It also suggests that a court's inability to write a judgment against an independent actor (like MSPB in this case) is not a textual issue but a constitutional one. The court here said the "redressability generally turns on how a judgment binds the parties." Because the judgment would not physically bind the MSPB or DOJ, the redressability is zero. If you are trying to sue an individual or agency for how they handle documents and you want an investigation to prompt a third party to act, you will lose if you frame your claim as "negligence by the primary actor." You are stuck fighting the penalties of the real harm being caused by the other side.

In *AFL v. Jamieson Greer*, the court used Article III standing to draw a sidewall in the fight against information withholding. While not deciding whether OSC should investigate, the decision limits the judicial toolbox to reach these violations. The court forced the dismissal not because OSC was too powerful, but because it was too distant from the成果转化 that plaintiffs claimed to need. For the Volokh Conspiracy or Lawfare readership, this is another example of the political question revolution quietly encroaching on case-and-controversy doctrine.



# ## Statutory Turfs: When Standing Bars a Push for OSC Accountability Under FOIA

**By [User Name]**

The intersection of the Freedom of Information Act and the Civil Service Reform Act often creates a bureaucratic free-for-all, but recently, the membership of meaning in a federal court seemed to close a significant door. *America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer* is a case about oversight. It involves a pressure group seeking to compel the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate alleged violations of public transparency laws. While the attorneys for the America First Legal Foundation argued that OSC possessed a clear statutory duty to investigate the Department of Justice over alleged arbitrary withholding of emails, the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals sidestepped the statutory debate entirely. In a decision written by Judge Katsas, the court dismissed the case on Article III standing grounds, effectively leaving the statutory question of OSC’s investigatory power moot for this plaintiff. This ruling is significant not merely for dismissing a FOIA special plaintiff, but for the way it reinforces the structural limits on enforcing administrative discretion through litigation.

## The Statutory Turfs Between FOIA and the CSRA

To understand the stakes, one must appreciate the procedural tussle the plaintiff attempted to mount over. The Civil Service Reform Act empowers the Office of Special Counsel to investigate allegations of misconduct, including specific forms of government withholding. Under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), OSC is tasked with investigating allegations concerning arbitrary or capricious withholding of information prohibited under the FOIA. This seems like a robust mechanism for holding agencies accountable for transparency failings.

However, the Freedom of Information Act itself contains a specific provision that triggers OSC’s investigative arm: 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F). This section states that if a court denies a FOIA claim, awards attorneys' fees, and specifically questions whether the agency "acted arbitrarily or capriciously," OSC is then required to initiate a disciplinary proceeding. The plaintiff in *AFL v. Jamieson Greer* tried to bridge the gap between these two statutes. They argued that if § 1216 meant OSC was obligated to investigate *any* arbitrary withholding, OSC could even act independently of the specific trigger conditions in § 552(a)(4)(F). Conversely, the Office of Special Counsel argued that § 552(a)(4)(F) limited its power, implying that if § 552 conditions weren't met, OSC didn't owe an investigation.

The District Court had already carved through this statutory thick-wood. It concluded that § 552(a)(4)(F) does not limit OSC’s power under § 1216, meaning OSC arguably had the authority to investigate even if the specific judicial findings required by the FOIA statute weren't present. However, because OSC retained discretion on whether to pursue enforcement and because the District Court found the OSC decision to be primarily discretionary under § 1214, it dismissed the case for failure to state a claim. But to the D.C. Circuit, statutory interpretation wasn't the final frontier.

## Standing as the Ultimate Gatekeeper

The D.C. Circuit used the doctrine of Article III standing to finish the job, declaring that the lawsuit could not proceed regardless of the statutory landscape. Under *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, a plaintiff must show an injury in fact, a causal connection between the injury and the challenged conduct, and a likelihood that the injury would be redressed by a favorable decision. This case struggled at the injury and redressability stages.

The plaintiff claimed an injury in denial of information, specifically information obtained through the OSC investigation. Judge Katsas noted that a plaintiff claiming informational standing suffers a concrete injury only if they were denied information they had a "statute entitled him to receive." Here, the court rejected the argument that § 1216 created such an entitlement. Even if the OSC were obligated to investigate, the investigation yields information primarily for the agency and the public, not necessarily the specific requester. To allow the suit to stand simply because FOIA disclosure was denied by the Department of Justice, noting that the OSC investigation didn't control that dropdown, would distort standing doctrine.

The causation point proved fatal. The court observed that the injury from not producing records (the FOIA denial) was caused by the DOJ, not the OSC. To get OSC to produce the records, the plaintiff would need to prove that OSC might discipline the DOJ employees (step one), which might then lead the MSPB to understand the violation (step two), which might then force DOJ to release the documents (step three). As the court noted, this chain requires speculation about unfettered choices by independent actors not before the court. When causation relies on "unfettered choices made by independent actors," standing evaporates.

## Analytical Implications and Downstream Effect

This precedent has tangible repercussions for overlaying the CSRA and FOIA enforcement. It highlights a critical limitation in how public rights can be enforced through administrative oversight agencies. While the statutory text of § 1216 might mandate an investigation, the court's reasoning suggests that without a private right to "receive" the investigation's specific benefits, the duty remains un-enforceable by a private party. This distinction reinforces the concept found in *Citizens for Constitutional Integrity v. Census Bureau*, where procedural rights cannot be used as a substitute for substantive interests unless the defendant's conduct directly causes the tangible harm.

There is a doctrinal tension here between the "non-debusability" of administrative investigations and the need for judicial review. The D.C. Circuit relies heavily on *Heckler v. Chaney*, reminding us that enforcement decisions by agencies are presumptively unreviewable. This creates a difficult posture for transparency advocates: they can request documents directly via FOIA, but suing OSC for failing to enforce the agency's statutes becomes non-cognizable if the harm is attributed to the agency's own withholding rather than the enforcement body's inaction.

Currently, the D.C. Circuit's decision leaves the statutory question open for future litigation, but it closes the door for this specific plaintiff. The District Court had previously held that OSC errors do not yield a concrete injury without some threatened concrete interest. If the plaintiff wishes to overcome the standing rule seen here, they must reframe their grievance to show that the OSC’s inaction directly harmed their financial or legal rights, not just their request to see records. Until then, § 1216 remains a mandate for OSC, but a mandate that is difficult to enforce in court unless the facts present a sufficiently direct, non-speculative injury. This leaves the turf between FOIA and OSC largely unconquered by litigation strategy, but it also suggests that statutory duties to investigate are less "justiciable" than they might appear on their face.
