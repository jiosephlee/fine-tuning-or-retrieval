Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: "America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer"

Chapter 3: # Article III Standing Analysis: Injury, Causation, and Redressability

## The Article III standing triad: injury in fact, causation, and redressability (*Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*)

The doctrine of Article III standing acts as a jurisdictional gatekeeper, ensuring that federal courts remain the primary forum for the resolution of actual disputes between parties. As established by *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, an association claiming such standing must demonstrate three distinct elements: (1) injury in fact, which must be concrete, particularized, and actual or imminent; (2) causation, or a causal connection between the plaintiff's injury and the allegedly unlawful conduct of the defendant; and (3) redressability, meaning it is likely that a favorable decision would redress the injury. In *AMerica First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer*, the D.C. Circuit emphasized that these requirements are jurisdictional and must be satisfied before the court can reach the merits. Because the parties initially failed to raise standing, the Court of Appeals reserved the analysis until supplemental briefing, noting that neither the text of the *Lujan* framework nor the *Steel Co.* interpretation of subject matter jurisdiction imposes greater burdens than the Constitution itself. The inquiry remains focused on whether the specific interests of the plaintiff are affected in a legally protected manner.

## Theory 1: Informational injury regarding DOJ's FOIA withholding (Injury and Causation)

America First Legal Foundation (AFL) advanced its first standing theory by alleging an informational injury related to the denial of Freedom of Information Act records by the Department of Justice (DOJ). Specifically, AFL claimed that the Office of Special Counsel's (OSC) refusal to investigate DOJ's policies prevented AFL from obtaining the discovery it requested under FOIA. Under the relaxed standing requirements applicable to procedural injuries, a plaintiff must still show that a violation of a procedural right resulted in injury to a concrete, particularized interest. In this context, AFL asserted that the "deprivation of a procedural right without some concrete interest" was insufficient to grant standing. Consequently, AFL attempted to frame its standing claim by arguing that OSC’s inaction directly caused the failure of DOJ to respond to its FOIA request. The D.C. Circuit addressed whether this causal link was sufficient to satisfy the Article III triad.

## Analysis of speculative causation: the chain involving MSPB and DOJ choices (*FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*)

The Court of Appeals rejected AFL's first standing theory due to fatal problems with causation and redressability regarding the informational claim. The opinion explains that the injury from DOJ's refusal to disclose records is plainly traceable to DOJ, not OSC. To support the claim that OSC was the proximate cause, AFL required speculation about the choices of independent government actors not before the court. The court noted that OSC's investigation could have led to a corrective action or disciplinary proceeding, which in turn would have been considered by the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB). Following this potential action, DOJ might then have decided to produce the records. The court held that this speculative chain involving unconstrained agency actors creates an insufficient causal link. A favorable judgment in this case would not "very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress" the informational injury, as judicial relief generally binds the parties before the court, not actors who were not involved in the litigation (*Haaland v. Brackeen*).

## Theory 2: Procedural injury regarding OSC's failure to enforce (Procedural Right in Vacuo)

AFL's second standing theory relied on its argument that OSC violated a procedural right by failing to conduct a mandatory investigation under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) of the Civil Service Reform Act. The Court addressed this through the lens of procedural standing, referencing *Summers v. Earth Island Institute*. This precedent establishes that a deprivation of a procedural right is insufficient for Article III standing without some concrete interest affected by the deprivation—a procedural right in vacuo. For standing to exist when alleging a procedural violation, the plaintiff must show that the statutory right was designed to protect a threatened concrete interest and that the violation caused an actual or imminent injury to that interest. Because the right to an investigation under § 1216 did not inherently secure a specific concrete benefit to AFL without further analysis, the requirement of a concrete injury remained unmet, rendering the procedural claim outside jurisdiction.

## Latticeley/Carson precedent on OSC 5 U.S.C. § 1214 notice requirements under § 1216 investigations

In evaluating the concrete nature of the procedural injury, the Court examined the statutory scheme governing OSC investigations. AFL argued that OSC’s failure to investigate meant it did not provide the notices and information required under the analogous provisions of § 1214, which governs investigations into prohibited personnel practices. However, the Court looked to *Carson v. U.S. Off. of Special Couns.*, where other circuit courts held that OSC’s decision whether to follow § 1214's procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary. Because § 1216 does not explicitly mandate that the OSC must engraft § 1214 notice requirements onto investigations of FOIA withholding cases, AFL could not claim an entitlement to receive specific information from OSC. The opinion clarifies that without a statutory mandate or an express entitlement to receive notice in a § 1216 context, the obligation to provide notice is discretionary, not a rigid procedural entitle, and thus does not support standing.

## Redressability failure: the lack of entitlement to OSC investigative findings under § 1216

The Court further analyzed redressability based on the lack of entitlement to OSC investigative findings under § 1216. Even if OSC were compelled to investigate, AFL did not contend that OSC must provide specific information under § 1214 procedures during a § 1216 proceeding. Without a statutory mandate to provide findings, a judicial order mandating an investigation would not be reasonably likely to redress AFL's second alleged injury. The opinion emphasizes that redressability turns on how a judgment binds the parties, not merely on whether its rationale might persuade actors outside the immediate litigation. Since AFL could not demonstrate that a court order would reasonably induce OSC to provide the requested information, or that the investigation itself would yield the records desired from DOJ, the judicial remedy would fail to function as required for redressability.

## The legal principle of agency discretion (*Heckler v. Chaney*) and judicial compulsion limits

Beyond specific statutory interpretations, the opinion relied on the broader principle of agency discretion derived from *Heckler v. Chaney*. In *Heckler*, the Supreme Court established that an agency's decision not to investigate or enforce is often entitled to judicial deference and not easily compelled in the absence of clear statutory language. Applying this principle, the Court noted that speculation about how OSC might choose to exercise its enforcement discretion creates a barrier to standing. A favorable judgment cannot be assumed to compel OSC to initiate discipline or sanctions against DOJ employees. The Court's reasoning underscores that courts generally may not compel agency enforcement, which further limits the possibility of redressability for claims challenging the agency's refusal to act. Consequently, rather than a concrete injury traceable to the defendant, the injuries alleged were conjectural and hypothetical, falling short of the "actual or imminent" requirement of Article III.

## Disposition

The D.C. Circuit held that AFL lacked Article III standing to pursue its claims against OSC. Because the essential requirements of injury, causation, and redressability were not met under the established framework, the appellate court vacated the district court's judgment. The final disposition remanded the case with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject matter jurisdiction, reinforcing the doctrine that the federal judiciary cannot exercise power over disputes that do not involve a real controversy between the parties.
