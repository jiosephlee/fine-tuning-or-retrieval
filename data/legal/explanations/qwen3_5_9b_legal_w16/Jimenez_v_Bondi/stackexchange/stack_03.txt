Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Why does the failure to formally report persecution to the government prevent proving the government is 'unable or unwilling' to protect?
Question:
The court cites Aviles-Gonzalez regarding reporting requirements. Jiménez worked for the government and had contacts but didn't report formally. If a law enforcement officer told them to leave during a conversation, why does that not qualify as 'evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures' to prove the government is 'unable or unwilling'?
Answer:
In immigration law, claiming that a foreign government is "unable or unwilling" to protect an applicant requires evidence that the government knew of the threat and failed to react. The Tenth Circuit in *Jimenez v. Bondi* applies a rigorous notice and technical requirement to this element, distinguishing between isolated police interactions and evidence of systemic government failure.

**Notice Creates the Opportunity to Respond**
The core doctrine cited by the court is that an analysis of a government’s willingness or ability to protect must consider whether the government had notice of the alleged persecution and an opportunity to respond (*Singh*, 130 F.4th at 861). The *Jimenez* opinion explicitly adopts the reasoning from *Aviles-Gonzalez*: "Without such notice, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped." This creates an evidentiary hurdle for petitioners; failure to report past persecution undercuts the claim because the record cannot otherwise establish that the government possessed the power to protect them but chose not to.

Jimenez specifically challenged this requirement by arguing that he should not be held to the burden because reporting would have been futile. However, the court emphasized that Jimenez and his father possessed contacts within the Colombian government and worked for the justice system. "Petitioners' efforts to reach out to the government for help were minimal, if any," the court noted. Because Jimenez did not formally report the threats despite his employment connections, the court could not determine if the government would have intervened had he sought it, satisfying neither the notice nor the opportunity to respond prongs.

**Individual Apathy vs. Systemic Failure**
Regarding your question about the conversation with the law enforcement officer, the *Jimenez* distinction relies on the requirement for evidence of "systemic, rather than individual, failures." The court quashed the record when it considered Petitioners tried to rely on the officer's advice to leave as proof of government unwillingness. Relying on *Osorio-Morales v. Garland* (72 F.4th 738), the court wrote: "Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim."

A statement from a single officer does not equate to the constitutional or systemic character necessary to deny the state's general ability to protect asylum-seekers, especially when the record elsewhere suggests the government was actively trying to address the underlying crime. The opinion notes that the Colombian government "had attempted several searches and seizures to address the ongoing criminal conduct" at the property in question. Therefore, the government's indicated responsiveness to the property crime undermined the argument that it was *unable* to control its security apparatus generally.

**Conclusion**
Ultimately, the failure to formally report prevents proving "unable or unwilling" because the doctrine requires the government to be the *subject* of the inquiry before it can be found to have failed. Without a report, the record lacks the evidentiary capacity to show the government *could* have helped but *would not*. Additionally, isolated conversations with officers, without further corroboration of systemic behavior, are legally insufficient to establish that the state is unwilling to protect its citizens.
