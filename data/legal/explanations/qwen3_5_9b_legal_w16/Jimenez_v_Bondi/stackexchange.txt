Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: "Jimenez v. Bondi"

### Why do legal threats and emotional distress not constitute 'past persecution' under the INth Circuit?
Question:
I am reading the section on 'Past Persecution' and I am confused about the threshold. The court cites Karki and Wiransane saying threat alone is insufficient. But the petitioners suffered physical stress and panic attacks. If donors suffered these, why did the court say threats alone don't equal persecution, even if they are 'unfortunate circumstances'?
Answer:
The confusion stems from a misunderstanding of the 10th Circuit's definition of "persecution" under the INA. As the *Jimenez* opinion clarifies, the law does not categorize "emotional distress" or "threats" as persecution as a matter of course, regardless of how frightening they are to the individual.

**The Standard: More Than "Unfortunate Circumstances"**
The court relies primarily on *Wiransane v. Ashcroft* and *Karki v. Holder* to establish the substantive threshold for persecution. *Jimenez* explains that persecution "requires the infliction of suffering or harm upon those who differ in a way regarded as offensive and must entail more than just restrictions or threats to life and liberty." (*Jimenez*, citing *Wiransane*). Crucially, the 10th Circuit distinguishes between "unfortunate circumstances" and legally definable persecution.

**Why Emotional Distress Alone Is Insufficient**
You noted that the petitioners suffered "physical stress and panic attacks." While the court acknowledges these suffering, it applies the precedent from *Karki* which established that "threats alone are insufficient to constitute persecution." The *Jimenez* opinion explicitly extends this logic to emotional harm:
> "As physically stressful or psychologically unsettling some events may be, we have rarely, if ever, granted asylum based on threats and emotional or psychological harm alone." (*Jimenez*, citing *Karki*).

The court points out that petitioners conceded they suffered "no lasting physical harm." In the 10th Circuit, without physical injury or a significant restriction on liberty, emotional harm resulting from threats is generally weighed as a "consequence" of the environment rather than proof of persecution itself. To elevate this to persecution, the harm must be "offensive" and more than mere restrictions.

**Application to This Case**
The court distinguishes this case from even more severe fact patterns where threats were rejected. Citing *Chhetri v. Rosen* (four telephone threats) and *Carias-Mejia v. Garland* (threats with machetes and robbery), the *Jimenez* opinion notes that these involved "more extreme fact patterns of inflicted harm" than the facts in this case, yet were still insufficient.

Because the petitioners in *Jimenez* did not escape physical harm, the court concluded that their "threats and emotional or psychological harm alone" did not satisfy the "offensive" harm standard required for a finding of past persecution.

### How does the 'central reason' test distinguish between political opinion and professional conflict in a mixed-motive case?
Question:
In the 'Nexus' section, the court says political opinion cannot be incidental. Jiménez was targeted for investigating a narco base, not just for being in the Liberal party. How do we legally determine when a 'mixed motive' of political affiliation vs. professional involvement (like Don Rafa case) fails to meet the 'on account of protected ground' requirement?
Answer:
In *Jiménez v. Bondi*, the Tenth Circuit clarifies that the "central reason" test functions as a filter within the nexus requirement. When a foreign national claims persecution based on political opinion that is intertwined with professional activity, the government assailant’s motive must be scrutinized to ensure the political opinion was a principal driver of the harm, rather than merely incidental.

The legal standard requires that to establish the nexus between persecution and a protected ground, the applicant must prove the protected ground was **"at least one central reason" for the persecution.** *Rivera-Barrientos v. Holder*, 666 F.3d 641, 646 (10th Cir. 2012); *Jiménez*, 24-9573, at *10 (quoting Rivera-Barrientos & Dallakoti v. Holder*, 619 F.3d 1264, 1268 (10th Cir. 2010)).

Crucially, the court held that political opinion "cannot be incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate to another reason for harm." *Jiménez* at *10*. While persecutors may have **mixed motives**, the protected ground must remain a primary factor. The opinion notes that in mixed-motive cases, the persecutor must be motivated by at least one protected ground that is a **central reason** for the inflicted harm. *Id.*; *O.C.V. v. Bondi*, No. 23-9609, 2025 WL 2447603, at *6 (10th Cir. Aug. 26, 2025)*.

In applying this to the facts, the court distinguished the **political affiliation** from the **professional involvement** via the timing and content of the threats. Petitioners were Liberal party members, yet the court found they were primarily persecuted for Jimenez’s role as a law student and provisional lawyer who investigated a narco-trafficking base. The critical evidence showing political opinion was not a "central reason" included:
1.  **Content of Threats:** The threats specifically valued Jimenez as a "snitch" for the prosecutor's office and for being responsible for search and seizures of Don Rafa's property. *Jiménez* at *11*.
2.  **Timing and Escalation:** Petitioners received initial political threats before the property dispute, but those were not linked to Don Rafa’s group. The escalated, life-threatening violence occurred only after Jimenez investigated Don Rafa’s rented property. *Id.*
3.  **Motivation:** The court found the retaliators were motivated by Jimenez’s professional action (threat to Don Rafa’s revenue and security) rather than Jimenez’s opposition to the President or the Narco leader. *Id.*

Therefore, to survive the "central reason" test in a mixed-motive scenario where political opinion overlaps with professional conduct, an applicant must show that the **professional activity itself did not trigger solely retaliation for professional reasons, but that the harm was fueled by the political dimension of that activity.** In *Jiménez*, because the persecutors acted protectively in response to a threat against Don Rafa's operation initiated by the victim’s professional capacities, the court concluded the political opinion was merely "tangential and subordinate to the main reason for harm." *Jiménez* at *11.*

### Why does the failure to formally report persecution to the government prevent proving the government is 'unable or unwilling' to protect?
Question:
The court cites Aviles-Gonzalez regarding reporting requirements. Jiménez worked for the government and had contacts but didn't report formally. If a law enforcement officer told them to leave during a conversation, why does that not qualify as 'evidence of systemic, rather than individual, failures' to prove the government is 'unable or unwilling'?
Answer:
In immigration law, claiming that a foreign government is "unable or unwilling" to protect an applicant requires evidence that the government knew of the threat and failed to react. The Tenth Circuit in *Jimenez v. Bondi* applies a rigorous notice and technical requirement to this element, distinguishing between isolated police interactions and evidence of systemic government failure.

**Notice Creates the Opportunity to Respond**
The core doctrine cited by the court is that an analysis of a government’s willingness or ability to protect must consider whether the government had notice of the alleged persecution and an opportunity to respond (*Singh*, 130 F.4th at 861). The *Jimenez* opinion explicitly adopts the reasoning from *Aviles-Gonzalez*: "Without such notice, there is no way to know how the police would have reacted or whether the government would have helped." This creates an evidentiary hurdle for petitioners; failure to report past persecution undercuts the claim because the record cannot otherwise establish that the government possessed the power to protect them but chose not to.

Jimenez specifically challenged this requirement by arguing that he should not be held to the burden because reporting would have been futile. However, the court emphasized that Jimenez and his father possessed contacts within the Colombian government and worked for the justice system. "Petitioners' efforts to reach out to the government for help were minimal, if any," the court noted. Because Jimenez did not formally report the threats despite his employment connections, the court could not determine if the government would have intervened had he sought it, satisfying neither the notice nor the opportunity to respond prongs.

**Individual Apathy vs. Systemic Failure**
Regarding your question about the conversation with the law enforcement officer, the *Jimenez* distinction relies on the requirement for evidence of "systemic, rather than individual, failures." The court quashed the record when it considered Petitioners tried to rely on the officer's advice to leave as proof of government unwillingness. Relying on *Osorio-Morales v. Garland* (72 F.4th 738), the court wrote: "Although police apathy can indicate a government’s unwillingness or inability to protect an applicant, a one-off conversation with an unhelpful officer does not necessarily show that a government is ‘unable or unwilling’ to protect a victim."

A statement from a single officer does not equate to the constitutional or systemic character necessary to deny the state's general ability to protect asylum-seekers, especially when the record elsewhere suggests the government was actively trying to address the underlying crime. The opinion notes that the Colombian government "had attempted several searches and seizures to address the ongoing criminal conduct" at the property in question. Therefore, the government's indicated responsiveness to the property crime undermined the argument that it was *unable* to control its security apparatus generally.

**Conclusion**
Ultimately, the failure to formally report prevents proving "unable or unwilling" because the doctrine requires the government to be the *subject* of the inquiry before it can be found to have failed. Without a report, the record lacks the evidentiary capacity to show the government *could* have helped but *would not*. Additionally, isolated conversations with officers, without further corroboration of systemic behavior, are legally insufficient to establish that the state is unwilling to protect its citizens.

### What specific evidence supports a finding of 'safe internal relocation' if the threat involves high-level narco-trafficking leaders?
Question:
The 'Safe Relocation' section says Petitioners could relocate. They lived in the countryside for 3 weeks. But Don Rafa was associated with the President. Why does a 3-week stay in the countryside sufficient to negate a fear of relocation, and does the presence of family in Colombia always guarantee safety under ICT standards?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, 10th Cir. (Oct. 7, 2025), the Tenth Circuit found that the decision regarding safe internal relocation was supported by specific factual evidence despite the involvement of high-level perpetrators. The court applied the standard that an applicant must demonstrate they cannot avoid future persecution by relocating, noting that the presence of family alone does not automatically guarantee safety under ICT standards.

The opinion identifies four key pieces of evidence supporting the safe internal relocation finding:

1.  **Actual Experience of Safety:** The most critical evidence cited is the Petitioners' own conduct. As the court notes, "Petitioners safely lived with Jimenez’s mother in the Bogota countryside for three weeks before deciding to relocate to the United States." This demonstrates that the specific area (the countryside/neighborhood) where they intended to move was capable of providing safety relative to the immediate threats.
2.  **Specificity of the Threat:** The court distinguishes between threats to move out of a specific community versus threats to leave the country entirely. The opinion states: "Recall that the men who threatened Petitioners told them to move out of the neighborhood; they did not threaten the family to move out of the country." Because the threats were geographically limited, relocation within Colombia to a different neighborhood remained a viable option.
3.  **Family Stability:** The court pointed to the stability of the Petitioners' support network. It noted that "Petitioners’ family and relatives continue to live in Colombia unharmed." While the opinion elsewhere clarifies that "the presence of family in the home country does not always establish safety," the court rejected the petitioners' argument that relatives could also be targeted based on hypothetical hardships.
4.  **Government Ties:** The opinion observed that "Jimenez’s father still works for the criminal justice system." This fact was used to counter the argument that the state was completely unable or unwilling to intervene, suggesting that while systemic issues existed, relocation was feasible because the petitioners possessed some degree of connection to state resources.

Ultimately, the court concluded that the rationale for denying asylum was not that Don Rafa or his associates were untouchable, but that the Petitioners failed to prove they could not safely relocate. As the district court reasoned, despite the "ties to the president of Colombia," the Petitioners could "mitigate any potential for future persecution by safely and reasonably relocating within Colombia" because the persecutors did not issue a national exile order and the Petitioners had proven they could survive in a Colombian jurisdiction for three weeks without violence.

### Why does denying asylum automatically result in denying withholding of removal according to Carias-Mejia?
Question:
In the conclusion, the court says failing asylum standards means failing withholding because withholding is a 'higher standard'. But earlier, the court said asylum is discretionary while withholding is mandatory if a refugee. If asylum is discretionary, why can't someone get withholding if they can't meet Asylum's discretion but meet Withholding's strict fear?
Answer:
The vertex of your confusion lies in the relationship between the **discretionary nature** of asylum relief versus the **mandatory nature** of withholding of removal and the comparision of their underlying legal standards.

According to *Jimenez v. Bondi* (citing *Carias-Mejia v. Garland*), the denial of asylum does not automatically preclude withholding. However, in this specific circuit, the opinion holds that because withholding is a "higher standard" than asylum, failure to meet the evidentiary bar for asylum makes it legally impossible to meet the bar for withholding.

### 1. Mandatory Relief Requires Meeting the "Higher Standard"
You correctly observe that *Jimenez* (Section II.B) notes that:
1.  **"Asylum is a discretionary form of relief"** which may be granted to eligible noncitizens.
2.  **"Withholding of removal" is mandatory** *if* the applicant is prevented from removal due to persecution on account of protected grounds.

However, the opinion makes a critical distinction regarding the burden of proof. As explicitly stated in the Conclusion (Section III):
> "[...] the Board of Immigration Appeals' denial of Petitioners' asylum claims... does not qualify for asylum... failing to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution under asylum standards, they **necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal**."

### 2. The Relationship Between the Standards
The "higher standard" referenced in *Jimenez* is the evidentiary threshold required to prove fear of persecution. While the opinion does not explicitly define the probability required for withholding here, it relies on *Carias-Mejia* (citing *Uanreroro*) to establish that withholding demands a stricter showing of fear than asylum does.

The petitioners failed to prove they suffered harm or risked persecution sufficient to meet the "well-founded fear" requirement for asylum. Under the logical framework applied in the Tenth Circuit (cited in *Jimenez* Conclusion):
*   If you cannot prove a fear of persecution sufficient for the **lower standard** (Asylum).
*   Then you effectively cannot prove the fear necessary for the **higher standard** (Withholding).

### 3. Application to Petitioners' Claims
The opinion grounded this conclusion in the facts:
*   **Past Persecution:** The petitioners failed to prove past persecution rose to the level of persecution (Section II.C).
*   **Future Fear:** The petitioners failed to establish a well-founded fear of future persecution (Section II.E).

Because they "fail[ed] to demonstrate a well-founded fear of persecution under asylum standards," the court concluded they "necessarily fail to meet the higher standard for withholding of removal." The court reasoned that if the record is insufficient to satisfy the relatively lower discretionary hurdle of asylum, it is certainly insufficient to satisfy the strictly higher mandatory hurdle of withholding.

### Summary
While you are correct that withholding is mandatory if the criteria are met, the criteria for withholding involve a stricter proof of fear than asylum. *Jimenez* reasons that a failure to prove the lower-level fear for asylum is evidence that the applicant cannot satisfy the higher-level fear requirement for withholding. Therefore, denying asylum effectively precludes withholding in cases where the petitioner fails to establish the necessary fear at the asylum level.

### How does the 'Administrative Record' restriction limit the 10th Circuit from reviewing the 'Right to Counsel' claims raised in the BIA?
Question:
The petitioners claimed they were denied due process for lack of counsel at the IJ level, but they waived the appeal on this issue and limited their appeal to asylum. The court relies on § 1252(b)(4)(A) to limit review to the administrative record. Since the record doesn't include oral arguments about counsel, how can the court confirm the BIA's factual findings regarding those issues?
Answer:
In *Jimenez v. Bondi*, the 10th Circuit did not rely on the administrative record restriction of 8 U.S.C. § 1252(b)(4)(A) to limit its review of the Right to Counsel claim. Instead, the court did not review the claim at all because the petitioners **waived** that issue entirely.

The opinion clarifies the scope of the appeal in Section II, stating explicitly: "Petitioners appeal the BIA’s decision denying their applications for asylum and withholding of removal... They do not appeal the denial of protection under the CAT or their due process claim." Because the petitioners "waived the appeal on this issue" and limited their filing to asylum grounds, the Right to Counsel claim was not before the appellate court.

Consequently, the administrative record restriction—which limits review to "the administrative record on which the order of removal is based"—operates as a barrier to developing new evidence regarding the asylum claims the petitioners *did* raise (such as past persecution and nexus). However, regarding the due process claim, the limitation was procedural waiver, not documentary sufficiency. The court did not need to "confirm the BIA’s factual findings regarding those issues" because the petitioners chose not to contest the BIA’s rejection of the claim in the appellate record.
